Covalency control of photomagnetic relaxation in a manganese(II) photoswitch
Authors: Sandra Kronenberger, Robert Naumann, Christoph Förster, Jan Klett, Dieter Schollmeyer, Luca M. Carrella, Eva Rentschler, Katja Heinze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Switching the electronic spin state in molecules by light is achieved via chemical bond reshuffling or light-induced excited spin state trapping. These procedures either require large-amplitude nuclear motions or cryogenic temperatures limiting their application in data storage or quantum computing devices. Here we show that a carbene manganese(II) complex undergoes light-induced excited spin state trapping at 141 K in solution and 112.5 K in the solid state. Irradiation quantitatively switches from low- to high-spin in solution and in the solid. The lifetime of the metastable high-spin state extrapolated from experimental data amounts to over 1 million years at 77 K. Key to success for the very long lifetime is the high reorganization energy due to high metal–ligand bond covalency, and hence, a large barrier height and width for the spin relaxation, paving the way for photo-switchable magnetic materials operating at ambient temperature.

Main

Modern information technology depends on controlling physical properties (electrical, magnetic and optical) to store and process data. To meet demands for device miniaturization, researchers are exploring single molecules as information units. Photoswitches, which can reversibly change properties when exposed to light, are a key focus. A promising direction is controlling the spin state of molecules with magnetic bistability by light. Spin states of organic molecules have been switched by light from singlet closed shell (S = 0) to diradicaloid states (S = 1) with a change of spin of ΔS = 1 using configurational isomerization (cis-trans isomerization) or a chemical reaction (bond homolysis or pericyclic reactions)1,2. Both processes typically require major nuclear reorganization. However, the diverse spin states of transition metal complexes open an even richer portfolio of potential photoinduced spin-switches, including materials with larger spin state changes of ΔS = 2. A combination of photoinduced trans–cis isomerization in the ligand with a change of coordination number3 (coordination-induced spin state change)4 or ligand substitution at nickel(II), iron(III) or molybdenum(III) centres led to bistable systems with ΔS = 1 (refs. 3,5,6). Again, these spin state switching processes are associated with large-amplitude nuclear motions.

By contrast, octahedral metal complexes with electron configurations d4–d7 and a suitable ligand field strength can undergo thermal spin crossover (SCO)7,8 from the low-spin (LS; maximum number of paired d electrons) to the high-spin (HS; maximum number of unpaired d electrons) state without bond breaking/bond formation or large-amplitude nuclear motions. The pre-requisite is a carefully designed ligand field strength so that the zero-point energy difference between the HS and LS states ΔE0HL = EHS – ELS (Fig. 1a) is positive and of the order of the thermal energy kBT. The thermal LS → HS transition is an entropy-driven process with ‘softer’ degrees of freedom in the HS state owing to its increased intramolecular vibrations being thermally accessible. Transition temperatures T1/2, where LS and HS fractions are equal, have been reported between T1/2 = 100 and T1/2 = 350 K. SCO with LS to HS transitions comprising ΔS = 2 can also be induced by light. This phenomenon was first observed in 1982, when laser excitation of LS iron(II) complexes transiently populates the HS state at room temperature18. Only 2 years later, the photogenerated HS state was trapped at cryogenic temperatures, establishing the phenomenon as light-induced excited spin state trapping (LIESST)19. So far, this phenomenon has been restricted to d6-iron(II) and d5-iron(III) complexes20–22. With the LS → HS transition, the Fe–L bond lengths expand, with ΔdFeL ≈ 0.21 and 0.13 Å for iron(II) and iron(III) complexes, respectively20,21. This nuclear rearrangement is much less pronounced than for isomerization reactions or bond dissociations, but is sufficiently large to induce an appreciable activation barrier Ea,HL to retard the relaxation of the metastable HS state to the LS ground state (Fig. 1a).

Figure 1a illustrates a minimalistic description of the LIESST effect for an octahedral d5 metal complex system, for example iron(III) or manganese(II): excitation of the LS ground state (2T2, S = 1/2) with light populates an excited doublet charge transfer or ligand field state (2CT or 2T1, S = ½) with the same multiplicity as the ground state. Intersystem crossing (ISC) furnishes an intermediate spin state (4CT or 4T2, S = 3/2), which can either relax back to the ground state (ISC′) or undergo another ISC′′ to the final HS state (6A1, S = 5/2). In addition to the double ISC yielding ΔS = 2, internal conversions (ICs) can transiently populate further intermediate excited states on the way to the HS state. Such potential additional intermediate excited states are omitted in Fig. 1b for clarity reasons. In most cases, observation of the LIESST effect requires cryogenic temperatures to trap the HS state20–22. Polypyridine iron(II) complexes possess HS → LS barriers of a few hundred wavenumbers (Ea,HL ≤ 0.09 eV) as determined by variable-temperature transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy in solution23. This small barrier does not trap the HS state despite the weak electronic coupling between the HS and LS potentials of a few wavenumbers only (HAB = 4–6 cm−1, estimated according to a semi-classical Marcus theory approach)23.

The characteristic temperature TLIESST denotes the limit temperature above which a photomagnetic effect in a material is erased by warming the material from very low temperatures at a rate of 0.3 K min−1 (ref. 20). LIESST has been observed only for iron(II) and iron(III) complexes, which show thermal SCO in the solid state. The highest TLIESST value has been reported for a macrocyclic dicyanido d6 iron(II) complex with TLIESST = 132 K in the solid state24,25. As TLIESST cannot exceed T1/2, high TLIESST values require high T1/2 values, preferably above room temperature. High T1/2 values in turn can only be achieved by stabilizing the LS state (destabilizing the HS state), that is, by increasing the zero-point energy difference ΔE0HL.

However, larger energy differences ΔE0HL′ concomitantly increase the HS → LS relaxation rates owing to the resulting smaller and narrower barrier Ea,HL′, according to the so-called inverse energy gap law26,27 as illustrated in Fig. 1b, mitigating the observation of LIESST. This dilemma can be circumvented by simultaneously increasing the distortion (ΔQHL′), that is, the bond length changes associated with the spin transition (but without large-amplitude nuclear motions such as bond breaking), in addition to the large zero-point energy difference ΔE0HL (Fig. 1c). However, a limit for bond length changes of ΔdML ≈ 0.21 Å seems to be reached with iron(II) SCO systems20,21.

Results and discussion

The covalency concept

Our design strategy for a strongly distorted HS state at high energy, that is, large ΔE0HL and large ΔQHL values (Fig. 1b,c), is based on strongly σ-donating carbene ligands combined with a manganese(II) central ion forming highly covalent Mn–C bonds. This contrasts the commonly employed more ionic FeII–L and FeIII–L bonds. The higher Mn–L covalency increases the σ-bonding strength, resulting in substantial destabilization of the eg* orbitals relative to the t2g orbitals and consequently leads to a higher-energy HS state. With the d6 manganese(I) complex [Mn(pbmi)2]+, we already succeeded in destabilizing higher-energy metal-centred states by increased Mn–L covalency to such an extent that a very long-lived luminescent excited state was achieved ((pyridine-2,6-diyl)bis(3-methylimidazol-2-ylidene), pbmi)28. On the basis of these results, we now hypothesized that d5 manganese(II) ions could also form sufficiently strong covalent bonds with carbenes that destabilize the HS state (large ΔE0HL′) and concomitantly impose large Mn–C bond lengths in the HS state (large ΔQHL′) by populating high-energy Mn–L σ-antibonding orbitals in the HS state. In an extreme case, Mn–L bonds might even break. Yet, Mn–L bond dissociation will be prohibited by installing rigid tridentate ligands with five-membered chelate rings. These rigid ligands should also lead to steeper potential wells, that is, larger force constants in the LS and HS states. The proposed sufficiently large nuclear reorganization energy λHL′ (Fig. 1c) without ligand dissociation should increase and broaden the HS → LS relaxation barrier, that is, slow down thermally activated relaxation and tunnelling processes. Computational modelling of 1 in its LS and HS states (LS-1 and HS-1) using density functional theory (DFT; B3LYP functional) suggests that LS-1 represents the ground state as expected (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Tables 1–4). Although energy differences of states with different multiplicity are highly functional-dependent35–38, the employed B3LYP functional typically favours the HS state39. Hence, the LS state is confidently assigned as the ground state. In LS-1, the dx2–y2-derived eg* orbital lying in the MnC4 plane is even higher in energy than the dz2 orbital pointing along the N–Mn–N axis by 0.83 eV, so that population of this orbital in the HS state would imply a large energy penalty. Moreover, geometries of LS and HS states are generally well reproduced by DFT calculations. Importantly, the Mn–C and Mn–N bond lengths in the LS-1 and HS-1 states increase from 1.996 and 1.983 Å to 2.275 and 2.384 Å, respectively (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 1). The calculated differences thus amount to ΔdMnC,DFT = 0.279 Å and ΔdMnN,DFT = 0.401 Å, respectively. In particular, the elongation of the Mn–N bond’s ΔdMnN substantially surpasses the analogous expansion of iron(II) and iron(III) SCO complexes20,21. Hence, our molecular design based on metal–ligand covalency, as seen in the energies of the dx2–y2 and dz2 molecular orbitals (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4), should horizontally shift the HS potential surface to higher displacements of QHL, thus increasing ΔQHL (Fig. 1c).

Synthesis and characterization

The manganese(II) complex 1 is obtained by oxidation of the known parent manganese(I) complex cation28 with ferrocenium hexafluorophosphate40 as a green solid at 293 K (Supplementary Fig. 1). Magnetic susceptibility (χm) measurements of the powder (2–300 K, χmT = 0.50 cm3 K mol−1) (Supplementary Fig. 2) confirms the LS state of 1 over this temperature range. The high-temperature χmT value exceeds the spin-only value of χm,soT = 0.37 cm3 K mol−1, as expected for a LS d5 electron configuration and found previously for the LS d5 complexes K4[Mn(CN)6]·3H2O and K3[Fe(CN)6]41. The resulting g = 2.3 value fits to g values observed by electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy for substituted LS manganocenes at cryogenic temperatures34. 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy in solution (298 K) (Supplementary Figs. 3–10) and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD; 91 K, without irradiation) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1) of 1 further confirm that the LS state of 1 is stabilized over the HS state as predicted from the strong σ-donating character of the carbenes. The experimentally determined Mn–L bond lengths agree well with those calculated by DFT for LS-1 (Supplementary Table 1). The infrared (IR) and Raman vibrational frequencies calculated for LS-1 also fit very well with the experimental data (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6), for example, the totally symmetric breathing mode (a1, compared with Fig. 1, QHL(a1)) of LS-1 is found at 118 cm−1 in the Raman spectrum and calculated at 112 cm−1 by DFT (scaled by 0.98). Consequently, the calculations describe the structure and flexibility of LS-1 reasonably well. The green colour of LS-1 arises from intense absorption bands at 431 and 590 nm, which are composed of spin-allowed metal-to-ligand charge transfer (2MLCT; manganese-to-pyridine) transitions according to time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT)42,43 calculations (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 7). Heating of solid LS-1 to 515.5 K does not change its colour (Supplementary Fig. 12), confirming that thermal SCO to the HS state is absent up to this temperature, as the HS state is expected to show different absorption bands (Supplementary Table 8). This further underlines the strong destabilization of the HS state as predicted.

Photoswitching in solution

Time-resolved absorption spectroscopy of LS-1 was performed at 293 K in MeCN solution (Fig. 2d) to explore the excited state dynamics and the ground state recovery after light excitation. After excitation of 1 with 590-nm light, the characteristic absorption bands of the LS state diminish (ground state bleach, GSB). No excited state absorption (ESA) bands are observed on this timescale. The LS ground state recovery observed at the GSB wavelengths follows a monoexponential rate law with an unusually long lifetime τHL(293 K) = 87 µs. For comparison, the lifetime for the HS → LS ground state recovery of LS polypyridine iron(II) complexes after light excitation is in the lower nanosecond range, between 292 and 235 K (ref. 23). The absence of ESA bands and the long lifetime suggest population of the HS state. TD-DFT calculations42,43 on geometry-optimized HS-1 indeed predict weaker transitions in the visible spectral region (Supplementary Table 8). Consequently, an appreciable activation barrier exists that decelerates the HS → LS recovery.

This HS → LS activation barrier Ea,HL was experimentally determined by variable-temperature TA spectroscopy with λexc = 590 nm in MeCN between 273 and 308 K. The slope of the corresponding Arrhenius plot (Fig. 2e and equation(1)) gives Ea,HL = 0.36 eV (2,900 cm−1). A comparably high barrier of 0.33 eV was observed for a trigonal prismatic iron(II) complex showing a HS → LS transition associated with a coupled breathing and trigonal twisting reaction coordinate44.

However, the 0.36 eV barrier surpasses that of classical LS polypyridine iron(II) complexes by factors of 3.8–9.4, while the pre-exponential factor AHL = 2.2 × 1010 s−1 is very similar to the AHL of polypyridine iron(II) complexes23. The activation barriers of typical iron(II) complexes showing thermal SCO in solution are also smaller (350–1,500 cm−1), while the pre-exponential factors are in a similar range27. Extrapolation of the HS-1 lifetime to 77 K from the Arrhenius plot gives τHL(77 K) > 106 a (Supplementary Fig. 13), which suggests potential applications in data storage using molecular LS-1/HS-1 at lower temperatures.

The Arrhenius equation (equation (1)) can be related to semi-classical Marcus theory (equations (2) and (3))23. Assuming reasonable limiting values for the reorganization energy λHL (Fig. 1c), the electronic coupling constant HAB, which defines the electronic communication between the HS and LS states, can be estimated. As HAB is proportional to λHL1/4 (equation (3)), varying the reorganization energy λHL produces only small effects on HAB. Using λHL,lower = 14,100 cm−1 reported for the structural analogue [Fe(tpy)2]2+ (2,2′:6′,2′-terpyridine, tpy)23 as the lower estimate and a tripled value of λHL,upper = 42,300 cm−1 as the upper limit (based on the larger bond length changes of 1) yields HAB = 11–14 cm−1 in a narrow range. Although this electronic coupling is somewhat larger than that determined for iron(II) complexes (4.2–6.2 cm−1)23, it is still small compared with the overall barrier height Ea,HL = 2,900 cm−1. The exceptionally favourable HS lifetimes of 1 suggest continuous irradiation experiments at lower temperatures to trap the HS state (LIESST effect). Cooling a solution of 1 in 2-MeTHF/MeCN (29:1 v/v) to 95 K sharpens the UV–visible absorption bands, resulting in a noticeable colour change from green to red, but does not change the overall band pattern (Supplementary Fig. 14). Thus, 1 remains in its LS state. Irradiating a frozen solution of LS-1 at 130 K with a 560-nm LED (Supplementary Fig. 15) for 2 min produces a colour change from red to faint orange (Fig. 3a) and decreases the 2MLCT absorption bands. This faint orange colour of the HS state persists for many hours at temperatures below 95 K. From the parameters derived by TA spectroscopy (Fig. 2d,e), the extrapolated HS state lifetime amounts to τHL(77 K) > 106 a (Supplementary Fig. 13). Such a huge lifetime at this temperature cannot be reliably determined experimentally within a reasonable timeframe. Performing an analogous experiment at 130 K (560-nm excitation for 2 min) also forms the orange-coloured HS complex according to UV–visible absorption spectroscopy (Fig. 3a). At this temperature, the LS complex re-forms quantitatively from the HS complex without any side reactions, as seen from the isosbestic points at 306 and 320 nm in the spectra series (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 16). The process follows an exponential rate law with τHL(130 K, solution) of 2.9 h (Fig. 3a, inset). The ultrafast dynamics from the photoexcited LS to the HS state was probed by femtosecond (fs)-TA spectroscopy of 1 in MeCN with 430- and 590-nm light excitation (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 17). The observed dynamics are essentially independent of the excitation energy (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19), suggesting that the initial decay after high-energy excitation is faster than the temporal resolution of our instrument. In the following, we refer to the data from excitation with 590 nm. In addition to the slow LS recovery at 293 K (τHL set as infinite in this ultrafast experiment), rich dynamics were observed on the (sub-)picosecond timescale with τ1,2,3,4(293 K) = 0.25, 1.0, 9.0 and 44 ps, respectively, obtained by global analysis (Supplementary Fig. 19). Clearly, a simple kinetic model involving only two excited states in addition to the LS and HS states as depicted in Fig. 1b is insufficient in this case. At 233 K, the time constants were determined as τ1,2,3,4(233 K) = 0.22, 1.1, 8.5 and 141 ps (Supplementary Fig. 20). The fitted lifetimes τ1–τ3 are independent of temperature within experimental uncertainty, while the lifetime τ4 shows a strong temperature dependence.

Variable-temperature fs-TA spectra obtained between 233 and 293 K (Supplementary Fig. 20) reveal that the state-to-state process associated with τ4 is thermally activated with a barrier of Ea(τ4) = 0.11 eV (885 cm−1) and a pre-exponential factor A(τ4) = 1.60 × 1012 s−1 according to an Arrhenius analysis (equation (1) and Fig. 3c). This pre-exponential factor A(τ4) surpasses AHL associated with the HS → LS relaxation with double spin change ΔS = 2 by two orders of magnitude, suggesting a much stronger electronic coupling. Using very large and small estimates of the reorganization energy λ(τ4) = 14,100–42,300 cm−1 yields HAB(τ4) = 90–120 cm−1 in a relatively narrow range (equation (3)). Hence, this thermally activated state-to-state transition associated with τ4 is an adiabatic process, for example, an ISC with ΔS = 1 and sufficiently strong spin–orbit coupling or an IC without spin change (ΔS = 0) (Supplementary Fig. 21). Using DFT (excited state) optimizations and TD-DFT methods, we were able to identify and optimize several excited states with doublet, quartet and sextet multiplicities (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). However, their individual roles in the excited state dynamics cannot be inferred from the current experimental and computational data alone. A qualitative scheme of the excited state dynamics accounting for the identified processes and barriers is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 21. Future experiments on the ultrafast timescale using X-ray spectroscopies in solution or in the solid state to infer spin state, electronic nature and bond lengths of the various involved intermediate excited states will allow a more detailed assignment of the observed individual processes45–47. The quantum yield of the overall light-induced LS → HS process was estimated as approximately 33% using a spectrum of the HS state extracted from the fs-TA data (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23). Hence, losses occur during the picosecond dynamics from the initially excited 2MLCT states along the way to the final HS state (Supplementary Fig. 21).

The TLIESST value for 1 in solution was determined by absorption spectroscopy at 590 nm after conversion to the HS state by 560 nm irradiation for 2 min at 95 K. Warming to 100 K with a rate of 0.5 K min−1 was followed by warming at a rate of 0.3 K min−1 to 180 K. This procedure17 gave a TLIESST(solution) of 141 K (Fig. 3d). This key figure-of-merit of the manganese(II) complex 1 surpasses that of iron complexes previously reported24,25. The present complex class based on manganese and carbenes capable of LIESST pave the way towards switchable materials at ambient temperatures, which is challenging to achieve with iron-based complexes, which were the focus of intense SCO/LIESST research for over four decades.

Photoswitching in the solid state

Photoswitching of 1 via LIESST was also investigated in the solid state with microcrystalline solid material. A solid sample of 1 was cooled to 10 K in the SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device) magnetometer giving χmT < 0.50 cm3 K mol−1 (Fig. 4a) confirming the presence of LS-1. This sample was irradiated with 590-nm light (Supplementary Fig. 15) at 10 K in the SQUID magnetometer until the magnetization was saturated (compared with 2 h in our SQUID setup) (Fig. 4a). The susceptibility temperature product reached a plateau at χmT = 3.29 cm3 K mol−1, confirming the light-induced spin-state change with approximately 57% conversion (based on g = 2.3) in our setup. The temperature TLIESST(solid) of 112.5 K (determined with a heating rate of 0.4 K min−1) obtained for the solid (Fig. 4b) is lower than the value determined in solution: a TLIESST(solution) of 141 K, probably because the solid state favours the compact LS state over the expanded HS state. With a higher warming rate of 2.5 K min−1, the value increases to a TLIESST(solid) of 118 K (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). The steep relaxation curves (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 25) substantiate cooperative effects for the HS → LS relaxation in the solid state. Relaxation curves obtained after illumination at 10 K and warming to selected temperatures (95, 100, 105 and 110 K) gave the sigmoidal decays (Supplementary Fig. 26), confirming a self-accelerating process in the solid state25. The measured decays further substantiate that no photodamage occurs under these conditions. Structural reports on the metastable HS state prepared by LIESST are restricted to iron(II) SCO complexes irradiated at T ≤ 40 K (refs. 48–56). To investigate the molecular structure of the HS state HS-1 in the solid state, which cannot be prepared by thermal SCO (Supplementary Fig. 12), photochemical experiments were conducted in crystallo. A single crystal of LS-1 was cooled to 91 K and irradiated with 560 nm, resulting in a colour change of the crystal to pale-orange–yellow (Supplementary Fig. 28). A collection of XRD data at T = 91 K confirmed the integrity of the single crystal and the preservation of the space group P21/n. Under constant irradiation, satisfactory XRD data could be obtained for HS-1. Both independent cations in the asymmetric unit display changes in geometry as compared with those of the LS structure, indicative of the HS state (Supplementary Table 1). The average Mn–C/Mn–N bond lengths of 1 at 91 K increased from 1.995(1)/1.955(2) Å (LS-1) to 2.277(2)/2.332(2) Å (HS-1), respectively (Fig. 2c). These bond length changes observed in the solid state agree well with the computationally predicted ones modelled in a continuum solvent and confirm that the HS state is strongly expanded (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Table 1). The calculated differences of ΔdMnC,DFT = 0.279 Å and ΔdMnN,DFT = 0.401 Å reproduce the average differences ΔdMnC,exp = 0.282 ± 0.002 Å and ΔdMnN,exp = 0.377 ± 0.003 Å measured in the solid state very well. This fully supports our design concept that larger zero-point energy differences, ΔE0HL, together with larger distortions, ΔQHL, can achieve LIESST behaviour at higher temperatures (Fig. 1c).

Conclusion

LIESST was restricted to iron(II) and iron(III) complexes, with the highest TLIESST value reported in 2001 as 132 K. Now, a carefully designed LS carbene manganese(II) complex 1 with covalent metal–ligand bonding and a strongly distorted high-energy HS state exhibits LIESST in solution and in the solid state. In solution, the HS state forms quantitatively under visible light irradiation. The extrapolated lifetime of the HS state at 77 K amounts to over 1 million years. The TLIESST value of 141 K exceeds previously reported TLIESST values of iron complexes. The photomagnetic effect even persists at room temperature with a microsecond lifetime. The principles uncovered in the present study unlock opportunities for achieving stable and robust spin-state switching without bond dissociation, exploiting the LIESST effect under ambient conditions using metal ions beyond iron, paving the way for device applications57,58 and single molecule information storage59.

Methods

General synthetic procedures

[H2pbmi]Br2, MnBr2 and ferrocenium hexafluorophosphate were purchased from TCI, Sigma-Aldrich and BLDpharm, respectively. CH3CN/CD3CN, diethylether, toluene and THF were distilled from CaH2, sodium and potassium, respectively. Dry CH2Cl2 and 2-MeTHF were purchased (AcroSealTM) and degassed by bubbling argon through the solvent before use. THF was stored over pre-activated 3 Å molecular sieves. Other reagents were used as received from commercial suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich, Fisher Sci., TCI and Acros). All reactions and measurements were performed under a water-free argon atmosphere if not stated otherwise. A glovebox (UniLab/Mbraun: Ar 5.0, O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm) was used to store and weigh sensitive compounds for synthesis, as well as to prepare samples for spectroscopic and analytical measurements.

Elemental analysis

Elemental analysis was performed by the Mikroanalytisches Labor Kolbe, c/o Fraunhofer Institut UMSICHT, Oberhausen, Germany.

NMR spectra

NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance NEO or a Bruker Avance DRX 400 spectrometer at 400 MHz (1H), 100 MHz (13C{1H}) and 376.5 MHz (19F). Resonances for 1H and 13C{1H} are reported in parts per million versus the solvent signal as an internal standard (CD3CN (1H δ = 1.94 ppm and 13C{1H} δ = 1.3 ppm, δ = 118.3 ppm))60. Resonances for 19F nuclei are reported relative to C(35Cl)2(37Cl)F (δ = 0 ppm). Calibration of the spectrometer before the measurement was performed with α,α,α-trifluorotoluene in CDCl3 (δ = −63.9 ppm). Multiplicities are abbreviated as follows: s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; dd, doublet of doublet. See Extended Data Fig. 1 for the 1H and 13C-NMR assignment.

IR spectra

IR spectra were recorded on an Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer with an ATR (attenuated total reflection) unit containing a diamond crystal inside an argon-filled glovebox. The intensities are qualitatively indicated as weak (w), medium (m), strong (s) and very strong (vs).

Raman spectra

Raman spectra (λexc = 1064 nm) were measured on a Nicolet 5700 FTIR spectrometer combined with a NXR 9650 FT-Raman module equipped with a 1,064-nm laser (laser power 20–1,500 mW; resolution 2 cm−1), a microstage microscope and an NXR Genie Ge-detector using single crystals or crystalline powders in glass capillaries (under inert gas). The intensities are qualitatively indicated as weak (w), medium (m), strong (s) and very strong (vs).

ESI+ mass spectra

ESI+ mass spectra were recorded on an Agilent 6545 QTOF-MS spectrometer.

UV–visible–NIR absorption spectra

UV–visible–near infrared (NIR) absorption spectra were recorded on an Agilent Cary 5000 spectrometer using 1.00-cm quartz cells (Starna) equipped with Schott valves. Measurements at low temperatures were carried out using a liquid nitrogen-cooled cryostat Optistat DN from Oxford Instruments equipped with a low temperature 1-cm quartz cuvette (Hellma). Baseline corrections for low temperature measurements were performed for all temperatures. Shoulders are abbreviated as sh.

HS-1 to LS-1 decay at 130 K via UV–visible–NIR absorption spectroscopy

A 0.13 mM solution of 1 in 2-MeTHF/MeCN (29:1, v/v) was cooled to 130 K, followed by irradiation at λexc = 560 nm with a high-power LED from Prizmatix (UHP-T-560-DI; output power 2.6 W) (Supplementary Fig. 15) for 2 min to ensure full LS-1 to HS-1 conversion (Fig. 3a). UV–visible–NIR absorption spectra were measured over time to observe the conversion from HS-1 to LS-1. The evolution trace (Fig. 3a) at λobs = 590 nm was fitted with a monoexponential function.

TLIESST experiment via UV–visible–NIR absorption spectroscopy

A 0.14 mM solution of 1 in 2-MeTHF/MeCN (29:1, v/v) was cooled to 95 K (Supplementary Fig. 14), followed by irradiation at λexc = 560 nm with a high-power LED from Prizmatix (UHP-T-560-DI; output power 2.6 W) (Supplementary Fig. 15) for 2 min to ensure full LS-1 to HS-1 conversion. The temperature was then increased to 100 K (0.5 K min−1) and kept at 100 K for 10 min. With a rate of 0.3 K min−1, the temperature was increased to 180 K. The conversion from HS-1 to LS-1 was observed by following the absorbance at λobs = 590 nm (Fig. 3d). The full UV–visible-NIR absorption spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 14) after the TLIESST experiment was measured at 293 K.

Solid state spin-state switching

A crystalline, powdery sample of 1 was sprinkled over a piece of paper. The powder was pressed with a spatula on the paper to cover the paper with the sample. A round disc of 1-cm diameter was cut out from the paper. The disc was placed in a MicrostatN cryostat from Oxford Instruments. To ensure thermal conductivity, the disc was covered with 2-methylpentane before cooling to 77 K. The disc was irradiated at 77 K with either a λexc = 560 nm high-power LED from Prizmatix (UHP-T-560-DI; output power 2.6 W) (Supplementary Fig. 15) or with a white light Seeker 4 mini torch from Olight, which leads to the formation of the HS state. The HS state was erased by increasing the temperature to T > 130 K leading to recovery of the LS state.

Thermal SCO

Crystalline powder of 1 was filled into a glass capillary under inert atmosphere, followed by an increase in temperature in a melting point apparatus from Gallenkamp until 540.5 K. No colour change was observed upon temperature increase, although the dark solid showed signs of decomposition at T > 515 K. Consequently, 1 shows no thermal SCO from LS to HS under these conditions.

SCXRD

Intensity data for crystal structure determination were collected with a STOE STADIVARI diffractometer from STOE and CIE GmbH with an Oxford Cryostream 700 using Mo-Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). The diffraction frames were integrated using the STOE X-Area61 software package and were corrected for absorption with STOE LANA62,63 of the STOE X-Area software package by scaling of reflection intensities followed by a spherical absorption correction. The structures were solved with SHELXT64 and refined by the full-matrix method based on F2 using SHELXL65 of the SHELX66 software package and the ShelXle67 graphical interface. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, while the positions of all hydrogen atoms were generated with appropriate geometric constraints and allowed to ride on their respective parent atoms with fixed isotropic thermal parameters. Co-crystalized acetonitrile has been constructed with the DSR plugin68 in ShelXle67.

The irradiation experiment on the X-ray diffractometer was carried out by exposing a single crystal (which was kept in a Parabar 10312 cryoprotection oil from Hampton research) at 91 K to a λexc = 560 nm LED (Prizmatix UHP-T-560-DI; output power 0.5 W). A colour change from dark red to pale orange was observed within 10 min of irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 28). To ensure complete LS-1 → HS-1 conversion before data collection, the XRD measurement was initiated after 2 h of continuous irradiation. The crystal was kept under constant irradiation throughout the data collection. After completion of the XRD experiment, the light source was switched off and a photograph was immediately taken, which still showed the characteristic pale-orange colour of the HS-1 state. Subsequently, the sample was allowed to warm to 293 K, resulting in an immediate conversion to the dark-coloured LS-1 complex. After re-cooling to 91 K, a final photograph of the crystal of LS-1 was recorded (Supplementary Fig. 28).

SQUID measurements

Sample preparation for magnetic studies was carried out under inert atmosphere in a glovebox under Ar. Magnetic susceptibility measurements were performed with a Quantum Design MPMS3-EC SQUID magnetometer in direct current and settle mode on powdered microcrystalline samples under an applied magnetic field of 1 T.

Variable temperature susceptibility data were collected in a temperature range of 2–300 K. The powdered microcrystalline sample (11 mg) was placed in a gelatine capsule mounted on a plastic straw. The temperature-dependent magnetic contribution of the holder (capsule and straw) was experimentally determined. The obtained raw voltages of the sample and background measurements were subtracted from each other with the help of the SquidLab69 routine for MATLAB70. Fitting of the corrected raw voltages gave the raw susceptibility data, which were corrected for the underlying diamagnetism using Pascal’s constants71.

For the photomagnetic experiments, approximately 0.4 mg of powdered microcrystalline sample was placed into the quartz-assembly of the magneto-optic-holder (Quantum Design) equipped with a Mountain Photonics Hyperchromator light source (Supplementary Fig. 15). Background treatment was done analogously, as described above. To determine precisely the amount of sample used in the photomagnetic setup, variable-temperature susceptibility data were collected from 300 to 10 K without irradiation and compared with the previously obtained susceptibility data of the standard measurement. The actual sample mass was determined to be 0.47 mg and 0.40 mg, respectively, to obtain the susceptibility values of the standard measurements.

Irradiation experiments in the SQUID magnetometer with a photomagnetic setup were performed at λexc = 590 nm (Mountain Photonics Hyperchromator, 6 nm full width at half maximum) (Supplementary Fig. 15) at 10 K for 2 h and monitored by continuous DC measurements. The coupled light causes a small heat input and leads to a small deviation between the actual temperature at the sample and the measured temperature by the thermometers located at the chamber wall. The temperature at the sample was corrected by comparing the susceptibility values after 2 h, while irradiating and immediately after switching off the light source. The temperature deviation between sample and thermometers was determined to be approximately 1.6 K, and the temperature on the sample during irradiation was corrected for each irradiation experiment in the range of 11.5–11.7 K. After the sample (0.47 mg) was irradiated for 2 h, variable-temperature susceptibility data were collected in the temperature range 10–300 K with a heating rate of 2.5 K min−1 in DC settle mode. The experiment was repeated with less sample (0.40 mg) and a slower heating rate of 0.4 K min−1.

Relaxation studies were conducted with the photomagnetic setup. After irradiating for 2 h at around 10 K (see above) using a λexc = 590 nm excitation, the light source was switched off and the sample was heated with the maximum rate of 30 K min−1 to the desired temperature of 90 K, 95 K, 100 K, 105 K and 110 K, respectively. After stabilization of the temperature, continuous DC measurements were done until the magnetic moment reached a plateau. Thereafter, the sample was heated to 300 K before the next experiment to avoid any remanent effects. The temperature at which the relaxation studies was performed was altered in the following order: 100 K, 110 K, 95 K, 105 K and 100 K to avoid any undesired effects. At room temperature, a χmT-value of 0.50 cm3 K mol−1 is observed, which slightly exceeds the spin-only value of 0.38 cm3 K mol−1 for one S = 1/2. The value remains nearly unchanged upon cooling. Only at low temperature a small decrease is observed, which can be due to the saturation of magnetization, spin–orbit coupling and/or small intermolecular exchange interactions. The χMT versus T curve can be simulated satisfactorily by applying a g value of 2.30.

fs-TA experiments

fs-TA experiments were conducted using a Helios pump–probe setup from Ultrafast Systems paired with a regeneratively amplified 1,030-nm laser (Pharos, Light Conversion; 1,030 nm, <175 fs, 2 mJ). The effective laser repetition rate of 2 kHz was set via an internal pulse picker. A small portion of the 1,030-nm fundamental was directed to the optical delay line and was subsequently used to generate broadband probe light by focusing the beam onto a sapphire for measurements in the visible-NIR range (450–915 nm). In the UV–visible spectral range (330–500 nm), the second harmonic was focused onto a second sapphire instead of the fundamental. The pump pulse was generated with an optical parametric amplifier (Apollo Y, Ultrafast Systems) and the beam diameter at the sample was adjusted to 100–150 µm at the sample to assure homogeneous excitation of the observation volume, which is defined by the probe diameter (approximately 25 µm). The sample solutions were measured under argon atmosphere in a 1-mm quartz cuvette. To generate spectra that cover the whole spectral region from 350 nm to 915 nm, the UV–visible and visible-NIR part of the TA spectra were recorded separately under identical conditions and were combined by matching the relative band maxima of both spectral regions with the corresponding TA spectra providing spectra at a 1-ns delay time. For the latter, the entire spectral range can be measured simultaneously. Preprocessing of the data, including chirp and baseline correction, has been performed using the Surface Xplorer 4.3.0 software from Ultrafast Systems. The open source Python-based data analysis tool KiMoPack 7.4.972 was employed for global analysis of the TA data. For the global fit, data points before 350 fs were cut-off to avoid additional components due to coherent artefacts.

LIESST quantum yield

The LIESST quantum yield was obtained from the ratio (Supplementary Fig. 23) of the bleaching (at 570 nm) after full conversion to HS-1 (300 ps after excitation) and the initial bleaching (200 fs upon excitation). In the investigated system, negative signals are exclusively caused by depopulation of the ground state (GSB). Hence, the loss of the negative signal is directly related to the ground state recovery. However, this value must be considered as an upper limit for the LIESST quantum yield, as the bleaching is superimposed in the TA spectrum with ESA. Although we know from the experiment ‘HS-1 to LS-1 decay at 130 K via UV–visible-NIR absorption spectroscopy’ (vide supra) that HS-1 shows only negligible absorption features in the visible spectral region, we cannot exclude that the initially populated 2MLCT state possesses a more pronounced ESA, reducing the observed bleaching, which in turn would lead to an underestimation of the initial depopulation of the ground state.

TA spectroscopy

TA spectroscopy experiments were carried out using a modified version of the described fs-TA spectroscopy setup. For this purpose, the Eos add-on has been employed, which uses a photonic crystal fibre-based supercontinuum laser as the probe light source. In contrast to the femtosecond measurements, the pump–probe time delay is controlled electronically with a time resolution of <1 ns. The effective laser repetition rate was set to 1 kHz. Fitting and averaging of the nanosecond decay traces and averaging of the TA spectra was performed with Mathematica 14.3.

Variable temperature TA measurements

Variable temperature TA measurements were carried under nitrogen atmosphere in an Optistat DN cryostat from Oxford Instruments. The TA setup was employed as described for the room temperature measurements (fs- and ns-TA experiments). The effective laser repetition rate was set to 1 kHz for all TA spectroscopy experiments.

DFT calculations

DFT calculations were carried out using the ORCA program package73 (version 5.0.3). Geometry optimizations were performed by using unrestricted Kohn–Sham orbital DFT and tight convergence criteria (keywords ‘tightscf’ and ‘tightopt’). All calculations were performed using the B3LYP functional74–76 using Ahlrichs’ polarized valence triple-zeta basis set (def2-TZVP)77,78 employing the RIJCOSX approximation (keyword ‘RIJCOSX’)79,80 and the auxiliary basis set (SARC/J)81. Relativistic effects were calculated at the zeroth order regular approximation (keyword ‘ZORA’) level77 using relativistically adjusted basis sets. To account for solvent effects, a conductor-like screening model (keyword ‘CPCM’) modelling MeCN was used in all calculations82,83. Atom-pairwise dispersion correction was performed with the Becke–Johnson damping scheme (keyword ‘D3BJ’)84,85. Explicit counter ions and/or solvent molecules were not taken into account. The 4MLCT state was geometry optimized using def2-SVP74 as a basis set and the 4MC starting geometry with contracted Mn–Npy(1,2) bond lengths of 1.5 Å. A numerical frequency calculation confirmed that the optimized geometry corresponds to a minimum structure on this level of theory. Increasing the basis set to def2-TZVP followed by optimization of the def2-SVP-optimized geometry converged to the 4MC state, so that the 4MLCT state was not accessible with the larger basis set, even with reduced step sizes. The excited doublet and sextet MLCT states 2MLCT(3) and 6MLCT(1) (keywords ‘iroot 3’ and ‘iroot 1’, respectively) were geometry optimized using the number of roots keyword ‘nroots 7’ (2MLCT(3)) and ‘nroots 5’ (6MLCT(1)) respectively, following the root (keyword ‘followiroot true’) in combination with the same configuration as above (keywords ‘tightopt’, ‘tightscf’, ‘RIJCOSX’, ‘CPCM(acetonitrile)’, ‘ZORA’ and ‘D3BJ’). The 6MLCT(1) geometry optimization was based on TD-DFT calculations from the HS-1 geometry, whereas the 2MLCT(3) geometry optimization was based on the TD-DFT calculations from the LS-1 geometry. TD-DFT calculations were performed using the TDA approximation, which is set as a default in ORCA 5.0.3. Numerical frequency calculations using the same keywords (‘iroot n’, ‘nroot n’ and ‘followiroot true’) as in the geometry optimizations confirmed the minimum structures, respectively. The reported calculated IR and Raman frequencies were obtained from a numerical frequency calculation with the keyword ‘elprop Polar 1’ without a solvent model. The resulting frequencies were scaled by a factor of 0.98. The charge transfer number analyses of the fifty TD-DFT-calculated transitions (keyword ‘nroots 50’) were done using TheoDORE 3.286–88. The reported calculated transition energies are shifted by 0.4 eV and 0.1 eV to lower energies to match the experimental absorption spectra for LS-1 and HS-1, respectively (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). For charge transfer number analysis, the respective complex cation was divided into three fragments: the manganese centre, the two pyridines and the four carbenes.
