Beyond transfusions and transplants: genomic innovations rewriting the narrative of thalassemia
Authors: Liangbin Shi, Xili Yan, Yanwei Xia, Yingdi Zhao, Xiuling Zhu, Qiang Li, Zhiliang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThalassemia is a globally prevalent inherited blood disorder that usually leads to severe complications and even premature death due to impaired hemoglobin synthesis. The conventional treatment approach encompasses a range of interventions, including red blood cell transfusions, iron chelation therapy, splenectomy, and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. However, transfusion-induced iron overload and the limitation of graft matching have emerged as significant clinical impediments. In recent years, with the advent of precision medicine and translational research, the treatment of thalassemia has undergone a paradigm shift toward stem cell gene therapy, gene editing combined with nanodelivery, and pharmacogenomics-guided, personalized treatment regimens. In preclinical and early-phase clinical trials, these approaches have demonstrated efficacy in modulating hemoglobin gene expression and reversing ineffective hematopoiesis. Consequently, this review explores the constraints imposed by conventional therapeutic approaches and the advancements in the field of gene therapy for thalassaemia. It elucidates the mechanisms of gene editing and the potential of stem cell therapies. Furthermore, the discourse encompasses the advancement of primary prevention strategies, including genetic testing and prenatal screening, in the context of reducing morbidity. It is our hope that this review will provide the latest clues and insights in gene therapy for the effective management of thalassemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06548-y.Keywords: Thalassemia, Molecular mechanisms, Diagnosis, Gene editing

Introduction

Thalassemia is a specific subtype of hemoglobinopathy caused by mutations affecting alpha- or beta-globin chain synthesis [1, 2]. The condition is categorized into two main types based on the affected globin chain: alpha-thalassemia and beta-thalassemia, with clinical severity further classified as mild, intermediate, or severe. Mild thalassemia typically presents with nonspecific or no symptoms and is often diagnosed via blood tests demonstrating mild anemia. Thalassemia intermedia manifests clinically significant anemia without reaching the severity of thalassemia major. Thalassemia major, the most severe form, is defined by profound anemia, growth retardation, hepatosplenomegaly, and skeletal abnormalities such as classic craniofacial deformities. Patients with thalassemia major require lifelong transfusion regimens to manage complications [3, 4]. Additionally, based on transfusion requirements, thalassemia is categorized into transfusion-dependent (TDT) or non-transfusion-dependent (NTDT) [5]. The central pathophysiological mechanism of thalassemia involves premature destruction of erythroid precursors, leading to ineffective hematopoiesis and compromised production of mature red blood cells [2].

Epidemiological data indicate that thalassemia is prevalent worldwide, with a high prevalence of carriers and affected individuals in endemic regions [6, 7]. Approximately 1–5% of the global population harbors thalassemia-associated mutations, and an estimated 60,000 newborns globally, particularly in low-resource settings, are diagnosed with clinically significant thalassemia syndromes annually [8]. Mitigating this public health burden necessitates population-level preventive measures, including systematic identification of beta-thalassemia carriers, genetic counseling, and prenatal diagnosis. These strategies are essential for curtailing the burden of thalassemia, particularly in regions with a high disease burden [9].

Types of thalassaemia

Alpha-thalassemia

Alpha-thalassemia is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by microcytic hypochromic anemia, with a clinical spectrum ranging from asymptomatic presentations to mild anemia and, in severe cases, fatal hemolytic complications. Hemoglobin, a tetrameric protein responsible for oxygen transport from the lungs to tissues, is structurally impaired in alpha-thalassemia. Normal hemoglobin is composed of two alpha-globin chains paired with two beta-globin chains [10, 11]. The alpha-globin gene cluster is located on chromosome 16 (16p13.3), approximately 150 kb from the telomere [12]. This cluster includes three functional genes: the embryonic alpha gene (HBZ), the fetal/adult alpha genes (HBA1 and HBA2), as well as the pseudogene HBM and the functionally uncharacterized theta gene (HBQ) [13]. The expression of alpha-globin genes is regulated by four highly conserved non-coding sequences (MCS-R1 to MCS-R4) located approximately 10–48 kb upstream of the gene [11]. Among these enhancers, MCS-R2 (HS-40) is the most efficient regulatory element for alpha-globin expression [14] (Fig. 1).

The high prevalence of alpha-thalassemia carrier frequency (> 1%) in tropical and subtropical regions is partly due to the selective advantage of alpha-thalassemia carriers in areas with past or present epidemics of falciparum malaria. Furthermore, two clinically significant disorders-hemoglobin H disease and hemoglobin Bart’s hydrops fetalis syndrome-are predominantly associated with individuals with compound heterozygous and homozygous genotypes [15, 16].

Hemoglobin H disease

Alpha-thalassemia, particularly Hb H disease, exhibits high prevalence in the Chinese population. It arises from biallelic deletions of the HBA2 and HBA1 genes on one chromosome combined with a deletion or point mutation in the HBA2 or HBA1 gene on the other chromosome, producing two subtypes of Hb H disease: deletional and non-deletional. Common deletional mutations in the Chinese population include --SEA,-α3.7, and -α4.2, while non-deletion-type point mutations include --SEA/αCSα, --SEA/αQSα, and --SEA/αWSα. Both subtypes disrupt alpha-globin synthesis, compromising hemoglobin assembly and culminating in Hb H disease [17, 18]. Clinical manifestations encompass anemia with variable Hb H levels, splenomegaly, jaundice, and-in pediatric patients-growth retardation alongside complications including infections, leg ulcers, and folic acid deficiency. Secondary iron overload frequently develops in older patients. Non-deletional Hb H variants are generally clinically more severe than deletional variants [19, 20]. In southern China, Hemoglobin Constant Spring (Hb CS) is the predominant non-deletional Hb H variant [21].

Hemoglobin bart’s hydrops fetalis syndrome

Hydrops fetalis associated with hemoglobin Bart’s (BHFS) is a life-threatening form of alpha-thalassemia with high-risk complications for both the mother and fetus. The molecular basis of alpha-thalassemia most frequently involves large deletions disrupting production of one or more alpha-globin chains. While less frequent, point mutations also contribute to alpha-thalassemia pathogenesis. Both genetic lesions lead to deficient alpha-globin synthesis, which prevents normal pairing with beta-globin chains and promotes excessive γ4 tetramer (Hb Bart’s) formation [11, 22].

The pathogenesis of BHFS involves hemolysis of unstable Hb Bart’s-containing erythrocytes and tissue hypoxia due to impaired oxygen release by γ4 tetramers. This induces systemic hypoxia, causing anemia, edema, and multiorgan dysfunction [10]. Affected fetuses succumb to intrauterine demise between 23 and 38 weeks gestation or postnatally [23]. The complex pathophysiological changes affect multiple organs and systems, including the cardiovascular system, liver, and lungs, and manifest as characteristic symptoms of cardiac failure and prolonged intrauterine anemia. These symptoms include marked hepatosplenomegaly, an enlarged fetal heart, delayed cerebral development, skeletal and cardiovascular malformations, and placental thickening [24, 25]. BHFS significantly endangers pregnant women, increasing the risk of maternal complications such as preeclampsia, obstructed labor, and abnormal bleeding. Managing BHFS poses a significant challenge in the prevention and treatment of birth defects [23, 26]. Currently, only a small number of children worldwide survive with intensive life-support measures and blood transfusion therapy. Therefore, this review primarily focuses on the pathogenesis and treatment of β-thalassemia.

Genotype and phenotype

The severity of the α-thalassemia phenotype is directly correlated with the number of nonfunctional alpha-globin gene copies in an individual [27]. Normal human cells typically contain four functional alpha-globin genes, corresponding to an αα/αα genotype. When one alpha gene becomes nonfunctional (manifesting as genotypes -α/αα, ααT/αα, or αTα/αα), it is categorized as silent α-thalassemia, which is often asymptomatic and detectable only through genetic testing. Individuals with genotypes αCSα/αα or αQSα/αα may exhibit hematological characteristics of microcytic hypochromic anemia. When two alpha-globin genes are nonfunctional (genotypes --/αα, -α/-α, -α/ααT or αTα/αTα), it is classified as α-thalassemia minor, also known as mild α-thalassemia. Affected individuals are generally asymptomatic, with characteristic microcytic hypochromic anemia evident in hematological examinations. When three alpha genes are nonfunctional (genotypes --/-α or --/αTα), individuals exhibit symptoms of Hb H disease and, in some cases, may require transfusion therapy. Additionally, certain cases with genotype αTα/αTα, such as αCSα/αCSα, αQSα/αQSα, and αCSα/αQSα, may also present with Hb H disease. Typically, non-deletion Hb H disease (--/αTα) is more severe than deletion-type Hb H disease (--/-α). Complete loss of function in all four alpha-globin genes (genotype --/--) results in Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis syndrome (Table 1) [20, 28].

Beta-thalassemia

Beta-thalassemia is a complex autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis and hemolysis. It is caused by reduced production of β-globin chains due to mutations or deletions in the HBB gene, leading to iron overload, anemia, and dysregulation of iron metabolism. Additionally, partial loss of β-globin function (β+) or complete loss (β0) mutations or deletions in the HBB gene [26, 29].

The beta-globin gene cluster, located on chromosome 11, comprises HBE1, HBG2, HBG1, HBD/HBB, and ψβ-pseudogenes, which are regulated by the Locus Control Regions (LCR) in a sequential 5′-ε-Gγ-Aγ-δ-β−3′ transcription. The LCR contains five DNase I hypersensitive sites (HS1 to HS5)(Fig. 2A), which play a critical role in the regulation of hemoglobin genes. The hemoglobin conversion process from HbF (α2γ2) to HbA (α2β2) involves a sophisticated regulation mechanism that is important for disease diagnosis and understanding the function of hemoglobin genes(Fig. 2B) [30].

(B) Translational expression of hemoglobin transitions from HbF (α2γ2) to HbA (α2β2)

The LCR also plays a pivotal role in the targeting of the control region of the beta-globin locus to the gamma-globin gene in adult erythrocytes through chromatin looping, mediated by protein complexes containing LDB1, LMO2, GATA1, TAL1, and E2A (Fig. 3). These regulatory mechanisms are of significance in the context of thalassemia diagnosis and potential therapeutic targeting for gene activation in β-thalassemia [31, 32].

HBB gene mutation

Beta-thalassemia predominantly arises from point mutations or small fragment deletions in the HBB gene, occasionally involving large segment deletions. Large segmental deletions of the gamma-globin gene that do not affect its function may lead to increased expression of the gamma-globin gene, subsequently resulting in elevated HbF levels in individuals carrying this deletion. This condition is referred to as HPFH or δβ-thalassemia and is characterized by less severe symptomatology. Over 350 mutations in genes contributing to β-thalassemia have been documented, with the majority of the point mutations occurring in functionally significant regions of the globin genes, such as in HBB exons and introns [33]. Mutations can be further categorized into point mutations (single nucleotide substitutions or oligonucleotide insertions/deletions), transcriptional mutations (promoter regulatory elements and 5’ UTR mutations), and mutations that lead to aberrant mRNA translation (start codon, unintentional, shifted codon, missense mutations, and premature termination mutations) based on their impact on the function of the beta-globin gene. This categorization also encompasses mutations that affect messenger RNA (mRNA) processing, including cryptic splice sites, splice junctions and consensus sequences, polyadenylation, and other 3’UTR mutations, thereby impairing the regulation of HBB at multiple stages of gene expression-from transcription to RNA processing and translation of beta-globin mRNA [34].

Common β + abnormal genes include IVS-II- 654, −28, βE, −29, −30, −32, CAP, and IVS-I-5, which affect mRNA splicing and gene transcription, resulting in the slowed synthesis of beta-globin peptide chains. In particular, oligonucleotides targeting the HBB gene IVS2-654 (C > T, which is common in East Asia) were found to produce an aberrant 5’ splice site in intron 2 of the precursor mRNA or a cryptic 3’ splice site that was activated upstream. These oligonucleotides were also found to restore correct splicing [35, 36]. Approximately half of the non-deletion mutations completely inactivate the beta-globin gene, leading to β0-thalassemia. β0 abnormal genes, such as CD41-42, CD17, CD71-72, Int, CD31, CD14-15, IVS -I- 1, CD43, and CD27-28, are mutated to form a terminator, rendering them incapable of producing beta-globin chains [37, 38]. The researchers achieved efficient and seamless correction of the common CD41/42ΔTTTC mutation in β-thalassaemia patient-specific iPSCs from Southeast/South Asia through an HDR-mediated editing strategy using high-fidelity CRISPR/Cas9 in combination with single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotides (ssODNs) and small molecules [39]. About half of the non-deletion mutations completely inactivate the beta-globin gene, failing to produce beta-globin and resulting in β0-thalassemia. In the Chinese population, there are eight relatively common HBB mutations, accounting for over 95% of β-thalassemia mutations. These mutations are denoted according to the HGVS nomenclature as follows: HBB: c. 124_127delTTCT, HBB: c. 52 A > T, HBB: c. 316–197 C > T, HBB: c. −78 A > G, HBB: c. 216_217insA, HBB: c. 79G > A, HBB: c. 92 + 1G > T, HBB: c. −79 A > G [13, 40, 41].

Genotypes and phenotypes

In the absence of pathological conditions, the typical human beta-globin genotype is βN/βN. Individuals affected by β-thalassemia manifest varying degrees of severity: (1) Asymptomatic β-thalassemia, marked by the genotype β++/βN and normal or near-normal erythrocyte parameters, typically necessitating confirmation through molecular diagnosis; (2) Mild β-thalassemia, also known as β-thalassemia trait (TT), with genotypes β0/βN or β+/βN, characterized by small red blood cells, reduced hemoglobin content, and elevated HbA2 values. This condition is generally asymptomatic and does not require treatment, though it may occasionally lead to mild anemia due to sporadic abnormalities in red blood cell morphology; (3) Intermediate β-thalassemia, present in β-thalassemia heterozygotes with genotypes β+/β + or β+/β0, exhibits fluctuating symptom severity. Patients may experience mild to moderate anemia, which may not require continual transfusion but could necessitate intermittent transfusions under specific conditions (e.g., infection, surgery, or pregnancy). This condition is also known as NTDT. With aging, bone marrow enlargement can lead to stunted growth, bone irregularities, infertility, and numerous other complications; (4) Severe β-thalassemia, also known as Cooley’s anemia, is associated with the most severe phenotype of β-thalassemia, occurring in individuals with homozygous β0/β0 or compound heterozygous β+/β0 genotypes. Typically manifesting in early infancy to childhood (between 6 months and 2 years of age), patients present with severe anemia and heart failure, which worsens over time and necessitates regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy to sustain life(Table 1). Additionally, individuals with β-thalassemia major face an elevated risk of liver cancer [42, 43].

Diagnosis of thalassaemia

Laboratory diagnosis

Thalassemia can be diagnosed using various laboratory methods, including DNA analysis, prenatal testing, and hematological phenotyping. The alpha/beta-globin chain synthesis ratio is measured using automated hematology analyzers, hemoglobin electrophoresis, and high-performance liquid chromatography, with consideration given to the individual’s iron metabolism status. Initial qualitative and quantitative Hb analysis is typically conducted using protein-based gel electrophoresis, high-performance liquid chromatography, capillary zone electrophoresis, and isoelectric focusing [44, 45]. However, definitive diagnosis necessitates DNA testing to identify the genotype of an individual, detect point mutations, and ensure the accuracy of hematological phenotyping. Hematological phenotypic screening involves measuring erythrocyte parameters (MCV and MCH) and analyzing hemoglobin composition, including the detection of abnormal hemoglobin and quantification of HbA2 and HbF. Findings are closely associated with the number of mutations in the alpha gene and reflect the predicted reduction in alpha-chain synthesis for each mutation. Studies on individuals with specific mutations have indicated a tendency toward reduced MCV, MCH, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC). Additionally, some individuals carrying specific mutations may exhibit characteristic abnormal hemoglobin variants, such as Hb CS and Hb E [4, 46, 47].

Molecular diagnostics

The prevalence of point mutations in the HBB gene and large deletions is dominant in beta-thalassemia. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), reverse dot blot hybridization, PCR-based real-time multicolor melting curve analysis, and Sanger sequencing, are crucial but have limitations [48, 49]. For instance, PCR-based methods may overlook rare and novel thalassemia variants, while next-generation sequencing (NGS) has significantly improved thalassemia screening programs but still faces challenges in accurately reading long and highly homologous regions like the alpha-globin gene [50]. Gap polymerase chain reaction (Gap-PCR) and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification are commonly used for detecting deletion mutations, each with its respective limitations [51].

In response to these challenges, the Comprehensive Thalassemia Allele Analysis (CATSA), utilizing long-read sequencing technology on the PacBio platform, has been validated for its clinical utility in screening thalassemia carriers. CATSA offers significant advantages over PCR-based methods, as it enables the analysis of the globin gene in a single long-read segment and facilitates the direct detection of both common and rare variants, including alpha-globin gene triple duplications and large deletions. However, the clinical feasibility of CATSA for prenatal diagnosis remains underexplored. Meanwhile, the combined analysis of NGS and Gap-PCR has proven to be an effective screening method for thalassemia, providing a crucial hematological profile that forms the basis for further diagnosis and treatment [52–54].

Prevention and screening for thalassaemia

Thalassaemia screening is essential to identify carriers, prevent misdiagnosis of anaemia, provide genetic counselling and reduce disease transmission. Target populations include individuals of African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, or Southeast Asian descent; those with iron therapy-resistant microcytic anaemia; a family history of thalassaemia; or unexplained anaemia. Prospective parents with these risks require carrier testing - partner testing is mandatory if one parent is a carrier [9].

Screening should take place before conception or early in pregnancy to support genetic counselling and prenatal diagnosis (by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis). Newborn screening, which is not universally standardised, uses haemoglobin electrophoresis, HPLC or IEF to detect variants such as Hb Bart’s. For adults/children with microcytic anaemia or risk factors, assessment follows a stepwise approach: CBC, iron studies, haemoglobin electrophoresis and confirmatory genetic testing for α- and β-globin mutations [55]. Current methods rely on haematological/molecular testing for population-specific mutations, while future strategies may include affordable genetic testing for broader carrier identification.

Treatment for thalassemia

Traditional treatment of beta-thalassemia

Common treatments for thalassemia, like regular blood transfusions, iron chelation, and sometimes the surgery to remove the spleen, are really important for managing the condition. But if you give too many blood transfusions over time, even though they’re necessary to keep patients with TDT alive, it can cause too much iron to build up in different organs. This leads to problems in multiple organs and serious complications [56]. These complications can include heart failure because iron builds up in the heart, liver problems from too much iron, issues with hormones and growth because of iron in the pituitary gland, and bone diseases caused by too much iron [42, 57].

Hepcidin is a core peptide hormone that regulates iron homeostasis. It maintains this balance by inhibiting intestinal iron absorption, macrophage iron release and hepatocyte iron storage [58]. Its expression is affected by iron storage, hypoxia and other factors [59]. However, patients with β-thalassaemia have reduced hepcidin expression due to the inhibition of erythropoietic activity, which exacerbates iron overload [60]. Iron chelation therapy (e.g., deferiprone or deferoxamine) is a key tool for reducing iron overload and forms a complementary strategy alongside iron-regulated therapy, termed ‘source control (hepcidin-regulated iron intake) + terminal clearance (chelator iron removal)‘ [61–63]. This strategy can be used to manage iron overload in patients with thalassaemia. However, the choice of chelating agent must be tailored to the individual patient, and patient compliance affects the effectiveness of iron control [54, 58].

Splenectomy is sometimes used to reduce the need for blood transfusions in TDT patients who can’t have regular transfusions. However, it can cause problems like infections and lung issues, which limits its use [64, 65]. Traditional treatments like blood transfusions, iron chelation, and splenectomy are important, but challenges like iron buildup and complications mean more research and careful thought are needed in treatment.

Gene therapy

Currently, gene therapy for β-thalassemia is split into two main types: gene editing and gene replacement. The idea is to use gene therapy along with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) to fix the anemia caused by thalassemia and stop making faulty red blood cells. With better gene editing tools, the success rate has gone up, and new detection methods help check if the edited cells are safe, including checking for unintended changes and big deletions in the DNA. However, using gene therapy with HSCT comes with risks, like treatment-related deaths, the transplanted cells failing, or the transplanted cells attacking the recipient’s body (GVHD), which can be serious, especially if the donor isn’t a close blood relative [66]. Using different types of stem cells, like cord blood and peripheral blood stem cells, has been successful and is better than the traditional bone marrow approach. Using matched family donors has also worked well. Having more places to get stem cells from (umbilical cord blood and peripheral blood) has made it easier to find patients for transplants. Also, using unrelated donors and half-matching donors has made it easier to find suitable matches, which helps patients and improves transplant success rates [67, 68].

Gene editing

Gene editing for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) has gotten better over time. Instead of using older tools like zinc finger nuclease (ZFN) technology, scientists now often use transcription activator-like effector nuclease (TALEN) technology or clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) technology [69, 70]. CRISPR is easier to design and cheaper compared to older methods. It makes precise cuts in the DNA, which the cell’s repair systems fix, either correcting the mutation or adding a small change to increase fetal hemoglobin. They use a virus called lentivirus to add a healthy HBB gene into these cells in a lab [71]. To make sure the edits don’t happen in the wrong places, new tools have been developed [72, 73].

When someone has both alpha and beta-thalassemia, the body makes less of both alpha and beta-globin chains. This helps balance the globin chains and reduces the amount of free alpha-globin, which is good for symptoms [74]. Inhibiting the HBA gene (which makes alpha-globin) is a good treatment idea because it helps balance the globin chains [75]. Studies on K562 cells show that deleting HbA2 and fixing HBB (the beta-globin gene) is a promising approach. This method reduces cell toxicity, making it safer for treatments. Overall, these strategies can help improve the lives of people with beta-thalassemia by balancing globin chains and reducing harmful substances in the blood [76].

In the Phase III CLIMB THAL-111 trial of EXA-CEL, a CRISPR/Cas9 gene therapy for beta thalassemia involving 52 patients, 91% achieved transfusion independence, with an Hb level of 13.1 g/dL and an HbF level of > 94%. No off-target effects were observed [77]. In the Phase I/II CTX001 trial (NCT03655678), one beta thalassaemia patient became transfusion independent after 18 months with HbF at 99% and Hb at 13.1 g/dL. One sickle cell disease patient experienced no vaso-occlusive crises for 16.6 months and had normalised haemolytic markers within 19 days post-injection. This marks a significant therapeutic breakthrough (Table 2) [78].

Cases related to gene editing

CRISPR/Cas9-derived single nucleotide base editors

Base editing (BE) technology represents a cutting-edge genome editing method designed to enable the precise incorporation of specific point mutations into the genome. This technology offers the advantage of reducing DNA damage and enhancing editing efficiency without relying on DSB-mediated and HDR-mediated editing techniques [81]. BE involves the fusion of catalytically inactivated forms of Cas9 (dCas9) or nickase-Cas9 (Cas9n) with deaminases, which selectively deaminate specific DNA bases [35, 82]. Base editing can be categorized based on the type of enzyme catalyzing the deamination: cytidine base editors (CBE) and adenine base editors (ABE) [83, 84]. CBEs convert cytosine to uracil, which is then read as thymidine by the cell, resulting in a C-G to T-A transition [85, 86]. On the other hand, ABE has a special ability to turn A-T pairs into G-C pairs during DNA repair without making double-strand breaks. This avoids the problems like toxicity that can happen with Cas9. While using a more accurate ABE, like ABEmax, reduces the chance of random edits, ABE still has limits. It struggles with unintended edits and isn’t efficient enough for treating blood diseases, so more improvements are needed [87].

ABE is a more advanced form of gene editing compared to CBE (cytidine base editor). Unlike CBE, ABE does not cause insertions or deletions, making it less likely to introduce unintended changes in the DNA. ABE has shown effectiveness in correcting a specific mutation linked to beta-thalassemia, specifically the IVS1-110 (G > A) mutation in the eastern Mediterranean region [88, 89]. However, ABE7.10, an earlier version of ABE, faced challenges in editing efficiency, particularly at difficult sites in human cells. To overcome this limitation, researchers evolved ABE7.10 into ABE8s by creating a library of adenosine deaminase variants. This improvement allowed ABE8s to achieve up to 60% efficiency in editing the promoter regions of the gamma-globin genes (HBG1 and HBG2) in human CD34 + cells. This enhanced editing capability enables sustained production of fetal hemoglobin, addressing the anemia associated with beta-thalassemia [90]. Moreover, a new version of ABE called ABE8e-SpRY was created. Using its RNP complex, they successfully fixed Hb E and IVS II-654 mutations in CD34 + HSPCs from patients. These results show how ABE can be used to fix genetic blood disorders by editing HSPCs [91].

Subsequent engineering efforts have led to the development of the adenine-to-cytosine base editor (ACBE), which includes high-precision ACBE-Q variants capable of achieving precise A-to-C substitutions with minimal Cas9-independent off-target effects [92]. Another promising approach to overcoming previous CRISPR/Cas9 limitations is the prime editor (PE), which shares features with conventional CRISPR/Cas9 but offers enhanced specificity and safety. By avoiding double-strand breaks, PE can integrate all possible base substitutions with a significantly lower likelihood of off-target mutations compared to HDR-mediated genome editing used by base editors (BEs), making PE a preferred choice for therapeutic gene editing [93].

Recent research has demonstrated that the transformer base editor (tBE)-mediated disruption of the BCL11A binding motif at the HBG1/2 promoter stimulates fetal hemoglobin in healthy individuals and beta-thalassemia patients’ HSPCs. No detectable off-target mutations were observed at the DNA or RNA level. Furthermore, tBE-mediated activation of gamma-globin expression was precise, leading to sustained repair of HSPCs. These findings demonstrate the safety and effectiveness of tBE-mediated editing as a treatment strategy for β-hemoglobinopathies [82, 94]. The use of a cytidine base editor (hA3A-BE3) in the HBG1/2 promoter region introduces HPFH-associated mutations at specific temperatures, significantly enhancing HbF levels in cultured hematopoietically differentiated healthy and thalassemia patient-derived stem cells. The A3A(N57Q)-BE3 system restored globin chain balance in thalassemia patient-derived stem cells by introducing a mutation at the + 58 site of the BCL11A erythroid enhancer. Introducing mutations upstream of the HBG1/2 gene using CBEs or ABEs can reactivate embryonic hemoglobin expression in anemic patient-derived stem cells by disrupting the LRF binding site or recruiting KLF1 transcriptional activators (Table 3). These findings underscore the potential clinical applications of base editing technology for treating genetic defects in hemoglobin-related diseases [95, 96].

Gene replacement therapy

Gene replacement therapy delivers a functional HBB gene to the patient’s hematopoietic stem cells using lentiviral-mediated gene editing tools. This approach directly repairs the HBB gene in situ, increasing beta-globin expression in red lineage cells. This method is more direct than ex vivo repair, enabling precise in vivo gene correction [114]. Zynteglo, developed by Bluebird, is a pioneering lentiviral gene therapy and the first of its kind for treating β-thalassemia [115]. It is indicated for both adult and pediatric patients requiring regular blood transfusions. Zynteglo employs a single-dose, disposable gene therapy product. Each dose is derived from the patient’s CD34 + bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, which are genetically modified in vitro for autologous transplantation. In vivo, these modified stem cells differentiate into erythrocytes that produce functional hemoglobin, restoring HbA levels and correcting the alpha/beta-globin balance in transfusion-dependent thalassemia patients, thereby treating β-thalassemia [114–116].

The two studies on lentiviral gene therapy (Beti-cel) for β-thalassemia differed in terms of trial phase and patient population. A phase 3 trial (NCT03207009, n = 18) involving patients with severe genotypes (e.g. β0/β0) demonstrated a TI of 89% and long-term safety (median follow-up: 47.9 months) [79]. A real-world study (n = 8; non-β0/β0) showed a TI of 100% and maintained Hb levels above 11 g/dL, confirming the effectiveness of Beti-Cel [80](Table 2). However, safety concerns remain regarding the integrative activity of lentiviral vectors and potential long-term silencing of transgene expression in hematopoietic stem cells. These issues necessitate long-term investigation and follow-up to ensure safety and efficacy.

Strategies for HbF activation

HPFH mutation achieves HbF reactivation

The conversion of hemoglobin from HbF to HbA depends on the suppression or silencing of the paralogous homologous HBG1/2 genes. HPFH is a syndrome characterized by elevated levels of HBG, which, when co-inherited with β-thalassemia, can transform hemoglobinopathies into benign disorders with mild clinical phenotypes [35]. HPFH is typically classified into two categories: deletion and non-deletion (Fig. 4). The deletion type HPFH alters the interaction between the LCR and the globin genes due to the deletion of a large segment of the β-globin gene cluster (HBG1-HBBP1-HBD-HBB). For example, the HPFH1-7 type involves a deletion length of 12.9–106 kb [117–119].

Schematic representation of common deleterious and non-deleterious mutations in the β-globin motif

Many non-deletion HPFH mutations arise from point mutations or small deletions in the HBG1/2 promoter region, which elevate HbF by disrupting repressor protein binding (e.g. BCL11A, ZBTB7A) or enhancing activator binding [120, 121] (Table 3). Notable variants include − 117 G > A, −114 C > T, −114 C > A, −114 C > G, and 13-bp deletions, as well as −195 C > G, −196 C > T, −197 C > T, −201 C > T, and − 202 C > T [120]. HPFH-associated mutations, such as −117 G > A, −114 C > T, −114 C > A and − 114 C > G, can effectively induce γ-globin gene expression by remodelling the binding sites of transcription factors. This significantly increases the level of HbF [122]. Therapeutic HbF levels (≥ 20%) were achieved in differentiated erythrocytes by gene editing of the − 196 C > T mutation in β039 thalassaemia HSPCs [123]. However, clinical trial data on the direct editing of HPFH mutations is still limited, with most studies in the preclinical stage. Moreover, specific point mutations, such as −567 T > G, the − 113 A > G mutation (which creates a GATA1 binding site) [120, 124], the − 198 T > C mutation producing a KLF1 binding site [125], and the − 175 T > C mutation create a TAL1 binding site that increases HbF expression (Fig. 4) [126]. Advances in genome editing tools have enabled attempts to correct disease mutations or replicate naturally occurring or HPFH-like mutations. At present, most studies are still in the early stages, either in animal experiments or in the first stages of clinical trials. The long-term benefits and risks of these techniques still need to be validated in large patient cohorts [118].

Modifying factors of HbF

Novel research on HbF regulators has identified key genetic loci and transcription factors as potential targets for genome editing [30]. Intergenic regions like XMN1-HBG2 (11p15.4), BCL11A (2p16.1), and HBS1L-MYB (6q23.3) harbor SNPs associated with elevated HbF levels [97], such as rs7482144 in XMN1-HBG2 (C > T substitution) and rs66650371/rs9399137 in HBS1L-MYB (3-bp deletions). These variants enhance HbF by modulating promoter activity or mRNA stability, alleviating anemia severity in β-thalassemia [98, 99].

Trans-acting factors play a pivotal role in HbF regulation. BCL11A, a repressor of γ-globin, binds to the HBG promoter and recruits the NuRD complex for silencing [100, 101]; editing its erythroid enhancer (+ 58 site) reactivates HbF [102]. In the CLIMB SCD-121 clinical trial, researchers genetically edited the erythroid-specific BCL11A enhancer in the haematopoietic stem cells of patients with sickle cell anaemia (SCD). This achieved an editing efficiency of 82.6% and increased HbF levels by 43.2% [78]. In the NCT04211480 clinical trial, two children with thalassaemia who carried the β0/β0and β+/β + genotypes achieved transfusion independence through the disruption of the GATA1 binding domain at the BCL11A erythrocyte enhancer + 58 locus using the CRISPR technique. Gene editing efficiency was consistently above 85%, corresponding to a haemoglobin level of 14–15 g/dL [102]. ZBTB7A/LRF represses γ-globin via a BCL11A-independent NuRD complex [103, 104], while KLF1 promotes β-globin expression and suppresses γ-globin by activating BCL11A/LRF [105]. HRI regulates HbF through the eIF2αP-ATF4 pathway, inhibiting BCL11A/MYB transcription [127–129] (Fig. 5).

Transcriptional regulators of γ-globin. Transcription factors linked to the β-globin gene cluster are depicted, with red arrows indicating activation and black arrows indicating repression

Erythroid-specific factors like GATA1 (via GATA1/FOG1/Mi2 complex) [106], NF-E4 [107], TAL1 [107], and zinc finger proteins (LYAR, ZNF410) also modulate HbF. LYAR binds to the Aγ-globin promoter [108], while ZNF410 reduces CHD4 expression to upregulate γ-globin [109]. HIC2 represses BCL11A [110], and HES6 regulates the GATA1-FOG1 complex [111]. Nuclear factors such as NF-Y [112] and NFIA/X [113]compete for promoter binding, influencing γ-globin activity. Collectively, these regulators form a complex network for HbF reactivation in gene therapy (Table 4). Most of these modifiers are in preclinical studies and are expected to provide safe and cost-effective treatment options for β-thalassemia patients in the future.

Thalassemia treatment

Nanotechnology

Researchers have focused on developing nanotechnology-based artificial hemoglobin to address the limitations of burdensome transfusion therapies. This is achieved by integrating hemoglobin enzymes, such as catalase and superoxide dismutase, into nano-complexes to produce biodegradable polymeric membrane-like artificial red blood cells. These constructs can function as oxygen carriers and antioxidants, depending on the specific conditions. Additionally, various nanoparticles have been explored as nano-chelators and nanocarriers for use in conjunction with gene therapy and gene editing techniques. Depending on their size and properties, these nanoparticles have successfully enabled iron chelation in thalassemia patients, HbF induction, reduction of hemolysis, and knockout of defective genes [130].

In a study using a specific mutation in β-thalassemia as a model disease, an assay was developed to detect IVS110 point mutations (A > G retrotransposons). This was achieved by designing probe-gated mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MCM-41) loaded with reporter fluorescein molecules. The target mutation produced distinct fluorescence signals, enabling the detection of the mutant sample [131]. In another study, a non-enzymatic sensor based on NiTe nanorods was developed to quantify hemoglobin in blood samples from anemic pregnant women, demonstrating the potential of nanorods for electrochemical hemoglobin analysis in thalassemic patients [132]. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology, combined with the supramolecular nano substrate-mediated delivery (SNSMD) strategy, has been used to knock out the defective HBB gene in various hemoglobinopathies. In vivo studies in sickle cell mice have shown proliferative efficacy, suggesting that this approach could be applied to treat thalassemia and other hemoglobinopathies [133] (Table 4).

Medication

Luspatercept, a novel fusion protein, blocks transforming growth factor-β superfamily erythropoiesis inhibitors, thereby inhibiting ineffective hematopoiesis, promoting erythropoiesis maturation, and alleviating the transfusion burden in these patients. As the first erythropoietic maturation agent to enter clinical trials, luspatercept has been approved for the treatment of thalassemia major (TM) in China. This drug not only significantly and consistently reduces the transfusion burden in TM patients but is also effective for β-thalassemia patients with TDT and NTDT, consistently alleviating anemia symptoms with mild and predictable side effects [60, 134]. Hydroxyurea, a cost-effective alternative to transfusion and iron-chelating therapies, has been widely used in β-thalassemia treatment. It primarily increases HbF expression by upregulating γ-mRNA, which enhances the expression of GATA2—a fetal hemoglobin gene associated with apoptosis and the cell cycle—while suppressing the expression of the GATA-1 gene [135]. Thalidomide, on the other hand, is an effective, well-tolerated, and low-toxicity drug that enhances the expression of GATA-1 and KLF1 in erythroid progenitor cells. It induces HbF expression via the HBG gene, reducing the accumulation and deposition of alpha-globin chains [137]. These properties make thalidomide a convenient, effective, and affordable treatment for thalassemia patients who are ineligible for allogeneic stem cell transplantation [138]. The combination of thalidomide with hydroxyurea in the treatment of β-thalassemia has been shown to increase Hb levels and reduce the transfusion burden in patients with refractory TDT [136, 137]. Studies have shown that thalidomide induces HbF through epigenetic regulation and that CRISPR activates the γ-globin gene through gene editing. These two mechanisms act synergistically in the treatment of β-thalassemia via a pharmacogenomic mechanism. Together, they target the core pathology of the globin chain imbalance [138]. Additionally, domestic researchers have found that certain Chinese herbal ingredients, such as astragalus, tortoise shell, angelica sinensis, and cinchona alkaloids, can upregulate the expression of the HBG gene and promote the synthesis of HbF [139, 140].

Perspective

As a serious hereditary blood disorder, β-thalassemia has long relied on red blood cell transfusion, iron chelation therapy and allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation to sustain life, but the iron overload caused by transfusion dependence and transplantation mating limitations have not yet been fundamentally resolved. In recent years, with the breakthroughs in precision medicine and translational research, the therapeutic paradigm has gradually shifted to pharmacogenomics-guided individualised regimens, the combined application of gene editing technology and nano-delivery systems, and stem cell-mediated gene therapy strategies - in which tools such as CRISPR/Cas9 and base editing are useful for the in situ repair or repair of HBB mutations. in situ repair of HBB mutations or precise regulation of BCL11A enhancers by tools such as CRISPR/Cas9, base editing, etc. have demonstrated the potential for remodelling normal haematopoiesis in preclinical models and early clinical trials.

Notably, basic research on the mechanisms of gene expression regulation has revealed new therapeutic targets: abnormal histone modifications are a key link between developmental defects and thalassaemia. The mechanism by which they silence the γ-globin gene is an important breakthrough for HbF activation [141–143]. Another area of focus is the so-called ‘super-enhancers’, which are specific DNA regions that enhance gene activity. For instance, the α-globin super-enhancer in mice comprises sections named R1 to R4 [144]. Together, these sections control the production of alpha-globin. Similarly, enhancers in the β-globin region play a key role in controlling thalassaemia symptoms, although the exact mechanism remains unclear. While the precise mechanism of action of β-globin region enhancers remains to be analysed in depth, their role in symptom regulation provides theoretical support for screening gene editing targets.

Currently, stem cell-based gene therapy programs are gradually overcoming the bottlenecks of hematopoietic stem cell in vitro expansion efficiency, editing accuracy and immunogenicity through the closed-loop strategy of ‘editing-differentiation- transplantation’. These cutting-edge technologies integrating genome editing, epigenetic regulation and nano-delivery are not only dedicated to creating more efficient and economical individualised therapeutic solutions, but also expected to fundamentally reverse the ineffective haematopoietic pathology of β-thalassemia by reshaping the globin gene expression network. With the accelerated advancement of preclinical research into translational medicine, these breakthroughs may revolutionise the treatment of thalassaemia in the next 5–10 years, and bring tens of millions of patients around the world a cure from blood transfusion dependence.
