Epidemiology, resistance profiles, and risk factors of multidrug- and carbapenem-resistant Serratia marcescens infections: a retrospective study of 242 cases
Authors: Hong Zhu, Fengyan Li, Xiaoli Cao, Yan Zhang, Chang Liu, Yuxin Chen, Han Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerratia marcescens is an opportunistic pathogen increasingly associated with healthcare-associated infections and rising antimicrobial resistance. The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and carbapenem-resistant S. marcescens (CRSM) presents significant therapeutic challenges. However, data on the clinical and microbiological analyses of these infections remain limited.MethodsThis retrospective study included 242 hospitalized patients with S. marcescens infections admitted to Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, China, from January 2012 to December 2020. Clinical characteristics, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and resistance patterns were analyzed. Patients were categorized based on infection site (urinary tract infection [UTI] vs. bloodstream infection [BSI]) and resistance phenotype (MDR and CRSM). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed according to CLSI 2024 guidelines. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify independent risk factors associated with BSI, UTI, and CRSM.ResultsMDR isolates exhibited significantly lower susceptibility to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones compared to non-MDR isolates, although amikacin and sulfamethoxazole retained moderate activity. Isolates from blood samples showed higher susceptibility to most antibiotics than urinary isolates, particularly imipenem (23.5% vs. 3.2%). Multivariate analysis identified prior hospitalization as an independent risk factor for S. marcescens BSI (OR = 2.8, P = 0.048), while kidney disease (OR = 4.2, P < 0.001) was independently associated with UTIs. Pulmonary infection (OR = 3.2, P = 0.020) and prior exposure to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes (OR = 3.7, P = 0.031) were independent predictors of carbapenem-resistant infections. Patients with MDR bacteremia had a markedly higher 28-day mortality (50.0%) than non-MDR cases (12.0%). The 28-day mortality rate for CRSM infections was 28.6%.ConclusionThis study reveals substantial differences in antimicrobial susceptibility between MDR and non-MDR S. marcescens isolates and highlights amikacin and sulfamethoxazole as potential treatment options against MDR strains. Didentified clinical risk factors—including prior hospitalization, kidney disease, and extensive antibiotic exposure—underscore the importance of early risk stratification and optimized antibiotic stewardship.Keywords: Serratia marcescens, Multidrug resistance, Carbapenem resistance, Bloodstream infection, Urinary tract infection, Risk factors

Introduction

Serratia marcescens, a member of the Enterobacterales family, is an opportunistic pathogen increasingly implicated in healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) [1]. It is commonly isolated from the respiratory tract, urinary tract, bloodstream, and surgical sites, particularly in immunocompromised or critically ill patients [2]. While traditionally considered of low virulence, S. marcescens has emerged as a significant nosocomial pathogen due to its intrinsic resistance to multiple antimicrobial agents and its ability to acquire additional resistance determinants under selective pressure [3, 4]. Of particular concern is the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and carbapenem-resistant S. marcescens (CRSM) strains, which pose serious therapeutic challenges and are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stays [5]. The effectiveness of carbapenems, once regarded as last-resort agents against Gram-negative infections, is now increasingly compromised in S. marcescens [6, 7]. However, data remain limited regarding resistance pattern differences between isolates from distinct infection sites, such as bloodstream infections (BSIs) and urinary tract infections (UTIs), as well as the clinical factors associated with resistance phenotypes.

Previous studies on S. marcescens have often focused on outbreak settings or single infection types, with relatively few providing comprehensive analyses of antimicrobial resistance patterns, infection site–specific characteristics, and patient-related risk factors in a large clinical cohort [8, 9]. Understanding the risk factors and resistance profiles associated with MDR and CRSM is critical for guiding empirical antibiotic therapy, informing infection control strategies, and improving patient outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) describe the clinical and microbiological characteristics of S. marcescens infections in a tertiary hospital in China; (2) compare antimicrobial susceptibility profiles between MDR and non-MDR isolates, as well as between bloodstream and urinary isolates; and (3) identify independent risk factors associated with BSIs, UTIs, and carbapenem resistance in patients infected with S. marcescens infection.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study included 242 hospitalized patients with S. marcescens infections admitted to Nanjing Drum Tower hospital, a 3,000-bed tertiary care teaching hospital with over 218 intensive care unit (ICU) beds in Jiangsu, China between Jan, 2012 and Dec, 2020. Patients were enrolled based on microbiological confirmation of S. marcescens isolated from various clinical specimens. To ensure that cases met standardized infection definitions for UTI and BSI, two independent infectious disease physicians reviewed each patient’s electronic medical record (EMR) using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior reviewer. Demographic and clinical information—including age, sex, comorbidities, infection site, prior hospital exposure, invasive procedures, antimicrobial usage history (Patients who received at least one dose of a broad-spectrum antibiotic within 30 days prior to the isolation of S. marcescens were considered to have antibiotic exposure), and clinical outcomes—was extracted from the EMRs using a standardized data collection form. All data were de-identified and cross-checked by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Jiangsu province, China. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the need for informed consent was waived by the same ethics committee (Approval Number: 2021 − 363).

Microbiological identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

All S. marcescens isolates were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the VITEK 2.0, bioMérieux/broth microdilution system and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (2024 edition) [10]. Comparative AST analysis was conducted to evaluate susceptibility profiles between MDR vs. non-MDR isolates and blood vs. urinary isolates. Antibiotics tested included β-lactams (ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, piperacillin/tazobactam), carbapenems (imipenem), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin, tobramycin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), and sulfamethoxazole.

Definitions

Patients were stratified into subgroups according to the site of infection (UTI, BSI) and antimicrobial resistance phenotype, including MDR and CRSM. MDR was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [11], while CRSM was defined as resistance to imipenem. UTI was defined as the isolation of S. marcescens from a midstream or catheterized urine sample with significant growth (≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL) in the presence of urinary symptoms or pyuria [12]. BSI was defined as at least one positive blood culture for S. marcescens in patients exhibiting signs of systemic infection [13]. Pulmonary infection was defined based on clinical diagnosis plus radiologic evidence (e.g., new infiltrate) and positive respiratory culture consistent with S. marcescens [14]. Broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was defined as receipt of at least one dose of a third-generation cephalosporin, carbapenem, or β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor within 30 days prior to culture positivity [15]. Clinical improvement was defined as resolution of infection symptoms and signs, normalization of inflammatory markers, and no requirement for antibiotic escalation. Cure was defined as sustained resolution without recurrence during hospitalization [16].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To identify potential risk factors for BSI, UTI, and CRSM infections, univariate logistic regression analysis was first performed. Variables with a P value < 0.1 were further entered into multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent risk factors. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Overall susceptibility differences between MDR and Non-MDR isolates

There is a stark contrast in antimicrobial susceptibility between MDR and non-MDR S. marcescens isolates. Non-MDR isolates exhibited high susceptibility rates to all tested antibiotics (above 78.0% for most), whereas MDR isolates showed low susceptibilities for most agents ranging from 11.8 to 36.3%. Among aminoglycosides, amikacin retained moderate activity against MDR isolates (68.2% susceptibility) and remained highly activity against non-MDR strains (96.2%). In contrast, tobramycin and gentamicin exhibited low susceptibility toward MDR isolates (11.8% and 23.5%, respectively), while maintaining over 78.0% susceptibility in non-MDR isolates. β-lactam antibiotics, including ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and cefepime, showed poor activity against MDR strains (susceptible rates: 5.9–22.7%), but were highly activity in non-MDR strains (susceptibility: 94.3–97.9%). Similarly, aztreonam and piperacillin/tazobactam had limited activity in MDR isolates (resistance: 31.8% for both), yet demonstrated over 90.0% susceptibility in non-MDR isolates. Imipenem showed moderate activity against MDR strains (36.3% susceptible), with complete susceptibility observed in the non-MDR group. Sulfamethoxazole displayed relatively good activity among MDR isolates (68.2% susceptible) and excellent activity in non-MDR isolates (98.1%). Fluoroquinolones, including ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, were largely ineffective against MDR strains (susceptibility: 17.6% and 18.2%), but remained activity in non-MDR isolates with susceptibility rates exceeding 92.0% (Table 1).

Comparative antibiotic susceptibility of S. marcescens isolates from bloodstream and urine infections

A comparative analysis of S. marcescens isolates revealed that strains from urinary samples exhibited relatively higher susceptibility than those from blood ones toward most of the antibiotics (Table 2). As shown, ceftriaxone had lower susceptibility in blood isolates (75.8%) compared to urine (84.4%). Ceftazidime and cefepime were more activity in urinary isolates (93.7% and 87.3%) than blood (both 79.4%). Aztreonam showed high susceptibility across both groups (77.1% in blood vs. 88.9% in urine). Notably, imipenem was significantly more activity in urine isolates (92.1%) than blood isolates (70.6%). However, piperacillin/tazobactam displayed a striking difference, with only 31.4% susceptibility in blood isolates compared to 82.5% in urine. Furthermore, sulfamethoxazole maintained high activity in both sources (100.0% in blood, 96.8% in urine). And fluoroquinolones, including ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, were also more activity in urinary isolates (88.9% and 90.5%) than in blood isolates (78.1% and 80.0%), though both remained reasonably active overall.

Comparative characteristics of UTI, BSI, and MDR infections caused by S. marcescens

A total of 242 hospitalized patients with S. marcescens infections were included in this study. The majority were male (n = 165, 68.2%) and aged over 60 years, with a mean age of 60 years. Patients were distributed across multiple departments, including general surgery (n = 32, 13.2%), urology (n = 28, 11.6%), intensive care unit (ICU) (n = 29, 12.0%), cardiothoracic surgery (n = 16, 6.6%), and geriatrics (n = 15, 6.2%). Common underlying conditions included hypertension (n = 89, 36.8%), diabetes mellitus (n = 55, 22.7%), malignancies (n = 65, 26.9%), kidney disease (n = 58, 24.0%), and cerebrovascular disease (n = 56, 23.1%). The average length of hospital stay was 13.5 days. Clinical outcomes were as follows: 19 patients died, 100 showed improvements, 94 were cured, and 29 were discharged or transferred.

Among the 63 patients with UTIs, the mean age was 67.9 years, and 71.4% were male. Frequent comorbidities included kidney disease (11.1%), hypertension (28.6%), and cerebrovascular disease (25.4%). Notably, 90.6% of patients had an indwelling urethral catheter during hospitalization, suggesting a predominantly catheter-associated infection profile. However, only 23.4% of S. marcescens isolates were obtained directly from urinary catheter specimens; the remaining isolates were recovered from midstream or other urine samples. The most frequently identified co-infecting organisms were Escherichia coli (22.2%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (16.7%).

Among the 33 patients with S. marcescens BSIs, 30.3% had primary BSIs, while 69.7% were secondary to other infections, most notably pneumonia (18.9%). Invasive procedures were frequently observed, including indwelling urethral catheterization (54.1%) and central venous catheterization (44.2%). The 28-day all-cause mortality rate for bacteremia was 21.2%, with markedly higher mortality among patients with MDR bacteremia (50.0%) compared to those with non-MDR infections (12.0%).

In the MDR group (n = 31), the mean age was 62 years. The most common invasive interventions included indwelling urethral catheterization (90.5%), central venous catheterization (81.0%), mechanical ventilation (66.7%), and tracheotomy (47.6%). Most patients had received more than three classes of antibiotics (71.4%) and/or carbapenems (61.9%). The average ICU stay duration among MDR patients was 23.5 days. And the 28-day mortality rate for CRSM infections was 28.6%.

Prior hospitalization as an independent risk factor for Serratia marcescens bloodstream infection: a multivariate analysis

Univariate analysis identified several potential risk factors for S. marcescens BSI, including prior hospitalization, blood transfusion, vascular catheter use, and prior use of carbapenems, glycopeptides, or ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics. However, multivariate logistic regression revealed that prior hospitalization was the only independent risk factor (OR: 2.847, 95% CI: 1.010–8.021, P = 0.048) (Table 3).

Admission to Intensive

care unit (ICU)

Univariate analysis identified several potential risk factors for S. marcescens BSI, including prior hospitalization, blood transfusion, vascular catheter use, and prior use of carbapenems, glycopeptides, or ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics. However, multivariate logistic regression revealed that prior hospitalization was the only independent risk factor (OR: 2.847, 95% CI: 1.010–8.021, P = 0.048) (Table 3).

Kidney disease was the independent risk factor for Serratia marcescens urinary tract infections: insights from a multivariate analysis

Univariate analysis revealed that advanced age, hypertension, kidney disease, prior surgery, blood transfusion, invasive procedures (e.g., catheterization, mechanical ventilation), and prior use of multiple antibiotics were significantly associated with S. marcescens UTIs. However, multivariate analysis identified kidney disease as a strong independent risk factor (OR = 4.154). Interestingly, Prior use of carbapenems was independently associated with a reduced risk of S. marcescens UTIs (OR = 0.214, 95% CI: 0.078–0.591, P = 0.003) (Table 4).

Analysis of the risk factors for patients with Serratia marcescens utis infection

Prior exposure to multiple antibiotic classes and pulmonary infection are associated with carbapenem resistance in S. marcescens

Univariate analysis identified several potential risk factors associated with CRSM infection, including ICU admission, pulmonary infection, blood transfusion, vascular catheterization, central venous catheterization, mechanical ventilation, prior glycopeptide use, and exposure to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes. However, multivariate analysis demonstrated that pulmonary infection (OR = 3.164, P = 0.020) and prior exposure to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes (OR = 3.738, P = 0.031) were the only independent predictors (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides data on the clinical characteristics, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and risk factors associated with S. marcescens infections in hospitalized patients over a nine-year period. Our findings highlight the growing clinical challenge posed by MDR and CRSM, and emphasize the importance of early identification of high-risk populations and rational antibiotic use.

Our antimicrobial susceptibility analysis revealed a significant disparity between MDR and non-MDR S. marcescens isolates. Non-MDR isolates retained high susceptibility to most antibiotics, whereas MDR strains exhibited widespread resistance, particularly to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones. Notably, amikacin and sulfamethoxazole maintained moderate activity against MDR strains, suggesting they may be viable treatment options in resource-limited settings or when carbapenem resistance is present. These findings are consistent with prior reports indicating that amikacin is among the few agents with retained activity against MDR S. marcescens [17–20].

Moreover, isolates from urinary sources demonstrated generally higher susceptibility to antibiotics compared to those from BSIs. This discrepancy may reflect differences in host immune responses, bacterial burden, or antibiotic exposure at the infection site. Blood-derived isolates, often associated with severe infections and prior invasive procedures, may harbor more resistant clones selected under antibiotic pressure [21]. The significantly lower susceptibility of bloodstream isolates to imipenem and piperacillin/tazobactam supports this hypothesis and underscores the need for cautious empirical therapy in patients with suspected bacteremia [22, 23].

We found that the majority of patients were elderly males, this is consistent with prior reports that older adults are more susceptible to opportunistic infections due to immunosenescence and increased exposure to healthcare settings [24]. The distribution of cases across diverse departments, particularly surgical wards and the ICU, underscores the nosocomial nature of S. marcescens and its ability to affect a wide range of patient populations [25, 26]. Furthermore, the common comorbidities in our study have been previously associated with increased infection risk and adverse clinical outcomes. These conditions may compromise immune responses or require frequent medical interventions, creating opportunities for pathogen colonization and invasion [27]. UTI patients were older, predominantly male, and frequently had urinary catheters, supporting the role of catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI) as a major risk factor. The frequent detection of S. marcescens in urinary catheter specimens further suggests a strong link between biofilm formation and catheter colonization, a well-documented pathogenic mechanism for this organism [28, 29]. In contrast, BSIs were often secondary to other infections, with pneumonia being the most common source [30]. This finding reflects the invasive potential of S. marcescens in critically ill patients and aligns with studies indicating that pneumonia and catheter-related infections are leading sources of Gram-negative bacteremia in hospital settings [31, 32]. Furthermore, the majority of MDR patients had been exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics, including carbapenems and ≥ 3 antibiotic classes. This aligns with previous study that highlights prior antibiotic exposure as a key driver of resistance selection in S. marcescens and other Enterobacterales [33].

Multivariate analysis identified prior hospitalization as an independent risk factor for S. marcescens BSIs. Hospitalization may increase exposure to invasive procedures and antibiotic pressure, facilitating colonization and subsequent bloodstream invasion [34]. Similar associations have been reported in studies of other nosocomial Gram-negative pathogens [35].

For UTIs, kidney disease was independently associated with S. marcescens UTI. Chronic kidney disease may impair local urinary immunity and promote colonization by opportunistic organisms [36]. Interestingly, prior carbapenem exposure was associated with a reduced risk of UTI in our cohort, potentially reflecting prior eradication of susceptible strains or selective pressures that favored colonization by non-urinary-tropic organisms. This finding warrants further investigation.

In addition, pulmonary infection and prior exposure to ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics emerged as independent risk factors for CRSM infection. The lungs represent a complex ecological niche, where suboptimal antibiotic penetration, prolonged ventilation, and polymicrobial colonization may facilitate the selection of resistant strains [37]. Meanwhile, extensive prior antibiotic use exerts strong selective pressure, favoring the emergence of CRSM [38]. These results align with prior studies linking antimicrobial overuse with carbapenem resistance in Enterobacteriaceae [39].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limits our ability to infer causality. Second, data were derived from a single tertiary center, which may not be generalizable to other settings. Third, molecular data on resistance mechanisms (e.g., presence of carbapenemase genes) were not available and should be included in future studies to better understand the genetic basis of resistance.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant differences in antimicrobial susceptibility between MDR and non-MDR S. marcescens isolates, with MDR strains exhibiting extensive resistance and limited treatment options. Amikacin and sulfamethoxazole retained moderate activity against MDR isolates. Susceptibility varied by infection site, with urinary isolates generally more susceptible than bloodstream isolates—except for aminoglycosides, to which bloodstream isolates showed relatively higher sensitivity. Multivariate analysis identified prior hospitalization and kidney disease as independent risk factors for BSIs and UTIs, respectively. Interestingly, prior carbapenem exposure was associated with a lower risk of UTI, whereas pulmonary infection and prior use of three or more antibiotic classes were linked to CRSM. These findings highlight the importance of targeted surveillance, early risk stratification, and rational antibiotic use in combating resistance. Further studies are needed to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance and to improve treatment strategies for MDR and CRSM infections.
