Healthcare leader perspectives on strategies to implement and sustain interventions that promote employee wellbeing and reduce burnout
Authors: Rafaela Avallone Mantelli, Chloe Glaros, Steven Lockhart, Hillary Sinn, Anthony Edelblute, Michael Henry, Katherine Reed, Caroline Tietbohl, Marc Moss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurnout and psychological distress among healthcare professionals are associated with adverse patient outcomes, workforce turnover, and substantial costs to health systems. Although a range of wellbeing interventions have demonstrated effectiveness, healthcare organizations continue to face challenges in implementing and sustaining these programs at the organizational level. Understanding leadership perspectives on implementation is critical to informing effective, system-level approaches to clinician wellbeing.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with senior healthcare leaders across the United States, including Chief Wellness Officers, Chief Medical Officers, and senior nursing leaders. Participants were purposively sampled to represent diverse health systems and professional roles. Interviews were conducted between March and November 2024, audio recorded, professionally transcribed, and analyzed using thematic analysis. An iterative coding process was employed, with investigator triangulation used to enhance trustworthiness.ResultsTwenty-one healthcare leaders participated in the study. Four interrelated themes were identified: (1) professional wellbeing was viewed as fundamentally dependent on organizational and structural drivers rather than individual-level wellness efforts alone; (2) visible leadership presence and frontline engagement were perceived as critical to the credibility and effectiveness of wellbeing initiatives; (3) implementation required ongoing navigation of tensions between heterogeneous individual clinician needs and organizational constraints; and (4) sustaining wellbeing initiatives depended on framing them in ways that aligned with institutional priorities, often through a business case emphasizing retention, cost, and quality of care. Leaders consistently noted that symbolic or individual-focused interventions were insufficient without addressing workload, staffing, and operational inefficiencies.ConclusionsSenior healthcare leaders view clinician wellbeing as a shared organizational responsibility requiring structural change, leadership engagement, and strategic alignment. Effective implementation of wellbeing initiatives depends on integrating interventions into existing workflows, addressing systemic barriers, and articulating their value in organizational terms. These findings underscore the importance of systems-based approaches to sustaining clinician wellbeing within complex healthcare environments.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14479-1.

Background

Repeated exposure to extreme work-related stressors and limited adaptability to high-pressure environments can lead to significant psychological distress among healthcare professionals (HCPs) [1, 2]. Burnout syndrome is a work-related disorder that is common among this population and is associated with multiple deleterious patient outcomes including: diminished quality of care, increased medical errors, and even higher 30-day patient mortality [3–8]. One notable consequence of burnout is elevated turnover rates [9], which imposes substantial costs on health systems [10, 11] associated both with the cost of training a new professional and with negative effects on patient-care [12]. In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, rising turnover linked to psychological distress has further underscored the urgency of addressing this healthcare crisis. Although healthcare leaders play a central role in addressing workforce burnout, leadership itself is a demanding role and emerging literature suggests that leaders may also experience significant burnout, which can affect their capacity to effectively support clinician wellbeing and organizational culture [13].

As the awareness of burnout and psychological distress in HCPs has increased [1], a variety of promising wellbeing interventions have been proposed and studied [14–17]. For example, we developed a creative arts therapy (CAT) intervention and demonstrating that this intervention was feasible, acceptable, and effective in reducing psychological distress symptoms in healthcare professionals [18]. Additionally, several positive effects of our CAT intervention persisted up to 12 months after participants completed the program [19]. However, healthcare systems are unsure how to most effectively disseminate and implement these wellbeing interventions that may require dedicated time for the HCP and trained facilitators with unique skills. Regardless of the specific intervention, addressing clinician burnout is most effective when approached as a shared responsibility between healthcare systems and individual providers [20]. Prior research has emphasized that leadership and organizational strategies play a critical role in addressing clinician burnout, with evidence suggesting that supportive leadership practices and system-level interventions can improve workforce engagement, reduce burnout, and enhance patient care outcomes [21, 22].

To identify ways to enhance the implementation of these wellbeing programs, we conducted semi-structured interviews with healthcare administrators to identify actionable and sustainable approaches to implement wellbeing programs for healthcare workers.

Methods

Study design & participants

We conducted a descriptive qualitative study using 21 semi-structured individual interviews and inductive thematic analysis from March 2024 to November 2024 [23]. Participants were purposefully sampled from Chief Wellness Officers (CWO), Chief Nursing Officers, and Chief Medical Officers (or equivalents) from different health systems across the country. Another form of purposive sampling, limited snowball sampling, was employed when participants recommended additional healthcare leaders with relevant experience [24]. Recruitment occurred through members of the national Collaborative for Healing and Renewal in Medicine (CHARM), as well as locally with leadership at the University of Colorado and surrounding Denver hospitals. All participants received a postcard consent before the start of activities. We developed a semi-structured interview guide that reflected our primary research question: how to best incorporate behavioral health interventions for HCPs into diverse healthcare systems (Supplemental Material). This study was approved by the Colorado Institutional Review Board (24–0469). Each participant was compensated with a $75-dollar electronic gift card for their time after the completion of the interview.

Data collection & analysis

Study personnel with experience in qualitative research conducted interviews based on participant availability. Interviews lasted approximately 45 min, were conducted on Zoom, audio recorded, and professionally transcribed. Transcripts were then checked for accuracy and entered into ATLAS.ti software for data management. Interviews were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis [25], in which themes were generated from the data rather than guided by a predefined theoretical framework. Three study team members iteratively reviewed transcripts and inductively generated a list of codes. They met to develop an initial shared codebook, followed by additional rounds of transcript review and codebook refinement until code saturation was reached. Code saturation was determined when review of additional transcripts did not yield new codes or meaningfully expand the existing codebook [26]. Coded data was analyzed both within and across cases to identify major themes, with the team meeting frequently to compare interpretations and discuss emerging patterns, with the final themes established through consensus-based discussion among investigators. Consistency was ensured by having a subset of the coded transcripts re-reviewed by an alternate member of the coding team. Several strategies were used to enhance the trustworthiness of our findings [27]. Credibility was supported through investigator triangulation and iterative team discussions during coding and theme development. Transferability was addressed through purposive sampling of leaders from diverse roles, institutions, and geographic regions. Dependability was strengthened through systematic codebook development and consistent application of codes across transcripts. Confirmability was promoted by grounding interpretations in participants’ verbatim quotations and maintaining an audit trail of analytic discussions and coding decisions.

Results

A total of 21 senior healthcare leaders participated in the interviews, representing diverse organizational roles and geographic regions across the United States. The sample included 10 physician wellbeing leaders, such as Chief Wellness Officers, Chief Well-Being Officers, and Medical Directors of Wellbeing; 6 Chief Medical Officers or equivalent from both academic and community health systems; 5 senior nursing leaders, including Chief Nursing Officers, Chief Nurse Executives, or equivalent. Participants represented institutions distributed across the West (n = 9), Midwest (n = 2), South (n = 4), and East (n = 6) regions, spanning academic medical centers, integrated delivery networks, and large health systems. Collectively, these individuals provided broad insight into the design, implementation, and sustainability of clinician wellbeing initiatives across varied healthcare environments.

Across interviews, four overarching themes emerged: (1) professional wellbeing was viewed as fundamentally dependent on organizational support rather than individual wellness efforts alone; (2) leadership involvement at the frontline was perceived as critical to the credibility and success of wellbeing initiatives; (3) efforts to improve wellbeing required balancing heterogeneous individual needs with organizational constraints, a process leaders described as inherently challenging; and (4) promoting and sustaining wellbeing initiatives required framing them in ways that resonated with institutional priorities, often through a business case. These themes are illustrated in Fig. 1, which depicts the interrelated domains identified by healthcare leaders as shaping the implementation and sustainability of wellbeing initiatives within complex healthcare systems. Importantly, these domains function as an integrated system, with the tension between individual clinician needs and organizational constraints permeating and influencing all domains. Professional wellbeing requires organizational support more than individual wellness efforts

Across interviews, participants emphasized that “wellness” was a broad concept encompassing professional wellbeing, individual health behaviors, and expressions of appreciation. Although leaders described implementing initiatives across all three domains—such as job support programs, mental and physical health resources, and appreciation or community-building activities—they consistently prioritized structural drivers of professional wellbeing over individual-level wellness offerings.

Participants commonly described appreciation efforts such as free food, coffee, or wellness events as meaningful symbols of acknowledgment, yet insufficient to address the deeper drivers of strain when implemented in isolation. Several leaders expressed concern that individual-focused wellness efforts could inadvertently shift responsibility onto clinicians rather than addressing systemic contributors to burnout. One participant articulated this tension clearly:

A lot of those individual-driven efforts continue to feel like the blame, the burden rests on the shoulders of an individual when truly it is a problem of a system. A system that’s not just the institution, it’s the system that extends nationally. And if you don’t fix the systems issues, you’re not actually going to solve the problem. - Respondent 2: Chief Wellness Officer

While many interviewees acknowledged the value of individual-focused programs—such as counseling services, mental health apps, or training in stress management and self-compassion—they emphasized that even well-designed offerings could falter if clinicians lacked the time or capacity to engage. As one leader explained:

You can make a respite room, but if the nurses don’t have time to take a break to use the respite room, it doesn’t help. People are individualized. You have to provide a menu of options. – Respondent 17: Senior Nurse Executive

You can make a respite room, but if the nurses don’t have time to take a break to use the respite room, it doesn’t help. People are individualized. You have to provide a menu of options. – Respondent 17: Senior Nurse Executive

These accounts underscored the complexity of designing interventions that were both accessible and responsive to diverse workforce needs. Interviewees instead emphasized the importance of “fixing the problem” (Respondent 11: Chief Medical Officer) by improving staffing, reducing throughput pressure, and enabling clinicians to perform their work effectively, rather than adding optional wellness activities to already constrained schedules.

Participants frequently cited structural initiatives—such as improving documentation processes, optimizing electronic medical records, or supporting career development programs—as more impactful than short-term wellness perks. Leaders described these changes as particularly meaningful because they supported a core professional value shared across disciplines: the ability to provide high-quality patient care. As one nursing leader reflected, burnout was driven less by the absence of perks and more by the inability “to take good care of patients,” noting that what many employees wanted most was “to go home at the end of the day and feel like they’d taken good care of patients” (Respondent 21: Chief Nursing Officer).

Leadership involvement on the ground matters for any institution

At the same time, participants consistently emphasized that meaningful progress required both leadership engagement and insight from frontline staff. Leaders described the importance of aligning wellbeing initiatives with institutional missions while ensuring clinicians felt their voices were heard and that there was a clear mechanism for feedback and dialogue.

Several interviewees highlighted the importance of visible, on-the-ground leadership engagement in building trust and signaling that wellbeing efforts addressed real organizational challenges rather than serving as symbolic gestures. As one senior nursing leader described:

Our leadership all the way to the top, to the president of the company, is really there visible at the frontlines. It’s being present. It’s being accessible, and then you really hear the stories, and you see the obstacles and the burns, and you understand what it is our frontlines need—not necessarily what they’re asking for. – Respondent 10: Chief Nursing Officer

Our leadership all the way to the top, to the president of the company, is really there visible at the frontlines. It’s being present. It’s being accessible, and then you really hear the stories, and you see the obstacles and the burns, and you understand what it is our frontlines need—not necessarily what they’re asking for. – Respondent 10: Chief Nursing Officer

Interestingly, interviewees representing institutions both with and without a designated Chief Wellness Officer (CWO) described remarkably similar priorities, challenges, and values. Participants at institutions with CWOs often described formalized structures—such as dedicated offices, committees, and cross-campus coordination—to support wellbeing initiatives. One leader explained that their university had “a very active office for wellness,” (Respondent 17: Senior Nursing Executive) with counseling resources, programs for staff and families, and leadership roles elevated during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

However, participants at institutions without a CWO described comparable commitments and challenges, with differences emerging more in scale and resources than in underlying philosophy. Across both types of institutions, leaders described the pandemic as a pivotal event that intensified burnout, normalized conversations about mental health, and strengthened the moral imperative to act. Improvements in mental health–related stigma were frequently noted, with one leader explaining that while stigma “probably still exists…I think it’s definitely less,” (Respondent 17: Senior Nursing Executive) in part because organizations had normalized access to counseling services and mental health tools.

Despite these advances, sustaining wellbeing efforts remained challenging across institutions of all sizes. Participants emphasized the need for broad collaboration across departments and persistent communication to maintain momentum and credibility over time.

Success requires balancing individual needs and organizational priorities, which is difficult to achieve

Leaders described wellbeing implementation as requiring navigation of two parallel sets of barriers: wide variation in individual clinician needs and persistent organizational constraints related to funding, staffing, and sustainability. Participants emphasized that no single solution fit all clinicians and that meaningful progress required both adequate resources and a clear “seat at the leadership table” (Respondent 2: Chief Wellness Officer) to ensure ownership, accountability, and alignment with institutional priorities.

The most frequently cited barrier was participation. Leaders described a culture of endurance among some clinicians, in which patient care was prioritized over personal wellbeing and support was perceived as unnecessary. Several interviewees noted that wellness events were often attended by nonclinical staff, while clinicians cited time pressure as a primary barrier to engagement. One leader explained:

The nonclinical folks show up, and the clinicians are like, ‘I’m too busy. I’ve got to take care of somebody first before I take care of myself. – Respondent 10: Chief Nursing Officer

The nonclinical folks show up, and the clinicians are like, ‘I’m too busy. I’ve got to take care of somebody first before I take care of myself. – Respondent 10: Chief Nursing Officer

Time constraints compounded these challenges even among clinicians motivated to engage. Participants repeatedly emphasized the importance of “bringing the wellness to them” (Respondent 06: Chief Wellness Officer) by embedding offerings into existing workflows or designing flexible, brief interventions. One leader vividly described this reality:

We have nurses dialing into wellness sessions literally on their wheeled computers—the cows—pausing to give medications, and then coming back when they can. That’s the reality. If you don’t bring the wellness to them, it’s just not going to happen. – Respondent 02: Chief Wellness Officer

Cost and staffing were also persistent obstacles. Some organizations had strong leadership support but insufficient full-time equivalents to execute programs, while others faced pressure to demonstrate return on investment before receiving resources. Leaders expressed frustration with the expectation that wellbeing work be accomplished for free, particularly in the context of widespread staffing shortages.

Finally, interviewees highlighted the challenge of prioritization amid heterogeneous workforce needs. While many emphasized the importance of embedding wellbeing into organizational strategy to signal commitment, determining which initiatives should take precedence was difficult. Participants acknowledged that different specialties, disciplines, and individuals required different approaches, creating ongoing tension between individualized support and broader systemic improvements.

Convincing leadership and promoting success of wellness initiatives requires making a business case

Participants emphasized that wellbeing initiatives gained meaningful traction only when anchored in organizational strategy and framed in ways that resonated with leadership priorities, often through a combination of compelling stories, relevant data, and opportunities for leaders to witness frontline realities firsthand. Most leaders described a dual strategy that paired narrative accounts with institutional metrics such as retention, turnover, patient experience, financial performance, and burnout measures.

You need the story to get at their heart, and then you need the data to back it up to show that it actually is effective. Numbers matter, but why this really matters—how it changes the workplace life—that’s what makes people pay attention. – Respondent 09: Chief Medical Officer

You need the story to get at their heart, and then you need the data to back it up to show that it actually is effective. Numbers matter, but why this really matters—how it changes the workplace life—that’s what makes people pay attention. – Respondent 09: Chief Medical Officer

Participants noted that data alone rarely persuaded executives without clear articulation of goals, expected outcomes, and relevance to institutional priorities. Leaders also emphasized the importance of alignment among senior stakeholders, describing greater success when multiple executives supported wellbeing proposals.

Across interviews, participants stressed the value of having leaders witness frontline conditions directly. Bidirectional communication was described as essential for addressing mistrust and countering cynicism among staff who felt administrators were disconnected or unresponsive. Participants also described the importance of tying wellbeing initiatives to strategic planning and long-term organizational outcomes. Some embedded wellbeing explicitly into strategic plans, while others worked with financial leaders to model the long-term costs of burnout relative to investments in system-level change. Although interviewees expressed openness to external programs, they emphasized the need for clear evidence of value, cost-effectiveness, and alignment with organizational needs, reiterating a preference for systemic solutions over time-limited individual-level interventions unless embedded within a broader wellbeing strategy.

Finance is kind of the king in all of this. If we’re going to bring something in from the outside, we need to understand what problem it’s solving, what the return is, and whether it actually reduces burden instead of adding more work. – Respondent 06: Chief Wellness Officer

Discussion

In this qualitative study of senior healthcare leaders from diverse U.S. health systems, we found strong consensus that clinician wellbeing efforts must prioritize organizational and structural drivers of burnout over traditional individual-level wellness activities. Leaders emphasized that persistent workload pressures, inefficiencies in care delivery, and limited staffing capacity remain central barriers to improving professional wellbeing. While participants acknowledged the value of symbolic efforts such as appreciation events or wellness rooms, they consistently noted that these strategies are insufficient when clinicians lack the time, resources, or workplace conditions needed to deliver high-quality patient care. Prior research has also highlighted important limitations of institutional wellbeing programs, noting that initiatives focused primarily on individual resilience or symbolic gestures may raise ethical and practical concerns, particularly when incentives shift responsibility for wellbeing onto employees rather than addressing organizational factors [28]. Taken together, these findings highlight that meaningful progress in clinician wellbeing requires embedding wellness into the operational fabric of health systems rather than offering isolated activities that place responsibility on individuals.

This study extends the growing literature on clinician burnout by shifting focus from the experiences of frontline clinicians to the perspectives of those responsible for designing and resourcing organizational wellbeing strategies. At the same time, leadership itself is a demanding role, and evidence suggests that healthcare leaders may also experience burnout, an often underrecognized challenge that may influence their ability to sustain wellbeing initiatives and foster supportive organizational cultures [29]. Prior research has documented high burnout prevalence [1, 2, 20], its association with poor patient outcomes [4, 5], and the promise of interventions such as creative arts therapy [16–19] or stress-reduction programs [14, 30]. However, far fewer studies have explored, at a national level, how health system leaders conceptualize wellbeing, define barriers to implementation, and approach decisions regarding investment in external programs. Our results build upon earlier work by illustrating the tension between system-level constraints and individualized needs, and by identifying specific challenges that leaders encounter when integrating wellbeing initiatives into complex healthcare environments [15, 30]. The findings also reinforce previous research demonstrating that health systems increasingly recognize burnout as an institutional responsibility rather than a deficit in individual resilience [1, 10, 31] and they provide novel insight into how organizational priorities shape the feasibility, sustainability, and perceived value of programs like CAT interventions.

This study has several strengths. The sample included leaders from multiple regions, system types, and professional backgrounds, providing a broad view of organizational priorities and constraints across the U.S. healthcare landscape. Using rigorous thematic analysis, an iterative codebook development process, and multiple forms of triangulation strengthened the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. The interviews were conducted during a period of sustained national attention to clinician wellbeing, increasing the timeliness and relevance of the perspectives shared.

There are also limitations. Although participants represented diverse roles, they may not fully reflect the perspectives of leaders in smaller or resource-limited health systems, where viability of wellbeing programs may differ. Recruitment through national networks such as CHARM could have introduced selection bias toward leaders already engaged in wellbeing work. Self-reported organizational practices may not capture the full range of operational barriers or may overestimate program success. As with most qualitative studies, findings are not intended to be generalizable but rather to provide depth of understanding that can guide future implementation strategies. Finally, clinicians themselves were not interviewed in this study; pairing leadership perspectives with frontline experiences in future research would provide a more complete picture of implementation feasibility.

These findings point toward several actionable pathways for improving implementation of clinician wellbeing interventions. Health systems may benefit from embedding wellbeing into formal strategic plans, allocating protected resources and personnel, and developing mechanisms for frontline staff to participate in decision-making. Programs such as creative arts therapy may be most effective when integrated into existing workflows, offered in shorter or modular formats, and supported by visible leadership engagement. Strengthening the business case for wellbeing—through improved data collection on staffing outcomes, turnover, patient experience, and cost savings—may help ensure sustained investment. Future research should evaluate hybrid models that combine system-level improvements with targeted, evidence-based interventions and should examine implementation strategies that reduce participation barriers for busy clinicians. Future work could also apply established implementation science frameworks, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) or Proctor’s implementation outcomes framework, to more systematically evaluate how wellbeing interventions are adopted, implemented, and sustained across healthcare systems. As healthcare systems continue to confront burnout and workforce instability, deploying organizationally aligned, accessible, and data-driven wellbeing initiatives will be essential for supporting the long-term health of both clinicians and the patients they serve.
