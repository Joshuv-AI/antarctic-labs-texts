Factors Associated With Default of Treatment and Follow-Up Among Patients With Cancer: A Cross-Sectional Study From a Lower-Middle-Income Country
Authors: Deepak Sundriyal, Mayank Kapoor, Preeti Antil, Sweety Gupta, Parmod Kumar, Ujjawal Kumar Shriwastav, Amit Sehrawat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEDesirable outcomes during the treatment of patients with cancer require adherence to evaluation, treatment, and follow-up. This study aimed to evaluate the factors associated with default in patients with cancer.MATERIALS AND METHODSWe included patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of cancer who defaulted during evaluation, treatment, and follow-up. All patients’ detailed demographic and clinical data were recorded. Those lost to follow-up were interviewed telephonically. The factors associated with default were noted. Descriptive statistics were used to compute the demographic and clinical characteristics.RESULTSIn total, 172 patients were included. Geriatric and female patients were 38.9% and 37.2%, respectively. Fifty-eight percent of the patients lived in rural areas, whereas 45.9% were illiterate. More than one third (34.3%) of the patients who defaulted received the curative-intent treatment, whereas 62.1% of patients had a good performance status immediately before default. The average distance traveled by the patients to reach the cancer facility was 143 ± 13.15 km. The most common reasons for default were the lack of social support, financial constraints, difficulty in commuting, and too sick to come in 45 (26.2%), 35 (20.3%), 28 (16.3%), and 23 (13.4%) patients, respectively. Twenty-nine (16.9%) patients reported more than one reason for default.CONCLUSIONLack of social support, financial constraints, transportation barriers, and inadequate counseling by the health care provider serve as barriers to uninterrupted cancer care. These factors are often interrelated and can be attenuated by adopting the hub and spoke model. Simultaneously, a good relationship between the patient and the health care provider fostered by appropriate counseling is imperative to increase adherence to cancer treatment and improve the outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer has emerged as a major social, economic, and public health burden. Twenty million new cases and 9.7 million deaths were reported in the year 2022, as per the International Agency for Research on Cancer.1 The estimated number of cancer cases in India for the year 2022, as per the National Cancer Registry Program and the National Center for Disease Informatics and Research of the Indian Council of Medical Research, was 1.461 million. Furthermore, it has been predicted that cases in India will increase to 2.08 million by 2040.2 Detailed evaluation of a patient suspected of cancer is a time-consuming process. Similarly, systemic therapy in cancer is usually a prolonged process and requires multiple visits, investigations, and regular follow-ups. The success of cancer care services largely depends upon adherence to the treatment and follow-up. However, nonadherence or default among patients with cancer is a commonly observed phenomenon and is a major area of concern. Default can cause disease progression, treatment failure/relapse, decrease in the quality of life, and drug resistance, ultimately leading to increased morbidity, financial toxicities, and mortality, thus adversely affecting clinical outcomes. Default also leads to confusion, burden, and demotivation for the treating physician who has to revamp the treatment.3

There have been previous studies of default of treatment among patients with cancer, with the prevalence ranging from 15% to 21%. Most of these studies have been carried out in developed countries and thus do not represent the true magnitude and disparities associated with the default of treatment in a resource-constraint setting.8-11

WHO advocates that interventions leading to adherence to the treatment can positively affect the population's health. We therefore aimed to identify the factors associated with default during evaluation, treatment, and follow-up among patients with cancer and to outline the circumvention.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Research Review Board and Ethics Committee approved the protocol for this study. This was a prospective study carried out between June 2021 and December 2022. We included patients of all age groups in the evaluation, treatment, or follow-up stage with a definitive diagnosis of cancer. Patients registered at the outpatient and inpatient facilities of the medical oncology department who met the inclusion criteria were included.

We defined default as

Skipping a scheduled visit during evaluation for more than 2 weeks.

Skipping a scheduled visit during evaluation for more than 2 weeks.

Skipping a scheduled chemotherapy session by more than the duration between chemo sessions (week, 2 weeks, or 3 weeks).

Skipping a scheduled chemotherapy session by more than the duration between chemo sessions (week, 2 weeks, or 3 weeks).

Lost to follow-up (skipped two or more scheduled consecutive visits with no further intention of continuing treatment).

Lost to follow-up (skipped two or more scheduled consecutive visits with no further intention of continuing treatment).

Patients were persuaded to visit the facility for a detailed interview. Those who refused to come were interviewed telephonically. All patients’ detailed demographic and clinical data were recorded. The factors associated with default were noted. Descriptive statistics were used to compute the demographic and clinical characteristics.

RESULTS

We included 172 patients during the study period. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics. The mean age of the patients was 52.02 ± 2.4 years, ranging from 12 to 94 years. This included seven pediatric patients and 67 patients of geriatric age group. Female patients constituted 37.2% (64) of the study group. Close to 40% (70) of the patients belonged to home state, the rest being outstationed. Illiterate patients without formal education accounted for 45.9% (79) of the study participants. Fifty-eight (100) percent of the patients lived in rural areas. Patients had to travel, on average, 143 ± 13.15 km, ranging from 4 to 500 km, to reach the cancer facility. The most alarming observation was that 34.3% (59) of the patients who defaulted were receiving the curative-intent treatment, whereas 62.1% (107) patients had a good performance status.

DISCUSSION

The present study analyzed the factors associated with default among patients with cancer reported at our institute. The lack of social support (no family member, friend, or relative available to accompany them to the hospital), financial distress, and the difficulty in commuting from a far-off place have emerged as the main reasons for default among patients with cancer. Datta et al cited a lack of support from family and friends as a barrier to accessing timely cancer care services and making the right decisions in their study from a cancer center in east India.12,13 The lack of social support originated as the fear of losing daily wages by the only earning family member or attending to the other vulnerable family members.

The rising financial burden of cancer treatment serves as another important factor for nonadherence. The complexity of the treatment, including surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, leading to multiple hospital visits, becomes the major driving force for the increased economic crisis. Paradigm shifts in the standard of care with the advent of immunotherapeutic agents and biologics add to the misery. Many patients with cancer now live longer, with an additional need to monitor the disease and its sequelae. Compared with other patients, those with a diagnosis of cancer have increased out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure on frequent imaging and other investigations and the late or lasting treatment-related effects even during the follow-up phase.14 Moreover, in India, many patients with cancer and their caregivers are the sole daily wage earner in the family. They are likely to report sickness absenteeism at the workplace during the treatment period. In addition, the cost of transportation from a far-off place to the cancer institute further magnifies the financial effect of cancer care. The insurance coverage in India is low, which is more pathetic in rural areas. Even after the availability of a publicly funded health insurance program, Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, which provides access to cashless surgical, medical, and radiation therapy for cancers, the financial burden remains high as many of the factors mentioned above run collaterally.15 An Australian systemic review reported the frequency of nonadherence to cancer treatment as 7% as an effect of financial toxicity.16 A study of the health care expenditure burden among nonelderly cancer survivors in the United States revealed that 21.6% of the individuals with a high OOP expenditure reported nonadherence to the necessary medical care.14 Although many studies in India cite the OOP expenditure on cancer treatment, none of them outline their effect on nonadherence.17-20

Close to 16% of our patients cited difficulty in finding the transport for commuting as the reason for default. It has been observed that patients with cancer living in geographically rural areas tend to present with a more advanced stage, higher mortality, and frequent default during active treatment and follow-up. Moreover, the paucity of public transport facilities, limited access to private vehicles, and an increased travel distance to the cancer care facility serve as the potential barrier for default. The nonavailability of medical, radiation, and surgical oncologists in rural settings prompts patients with cancer in these areas to travel greater distances to access specialized care, further doing collateral damage to the patients already facing transportation barriers. The suffering was further amplified by the multiple outpatient visits for repeated investigations, consultations, and multiple hospitalizations during treatment.21-24 Besides financial toxicity, arranging transportation and accommodation has been cited as a barrier to cancer care access by Kuru et al25 in their study from northeast India.

Our Institution (All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Rishikesh) is a tertiary care teaching and referral center situated in a lower-middle-income country. The institute caters to 80% of the rural population including remote and hilly areas. The catchment area of our center is enormous, and there is an absence of a dedicated public cancer facility delivering multimodality services (medical oncology, radiation oncology, and surgical oncology) in the adjoining rural and urban areas. This is represented by the observation that 124 (72%) patients in our study had to travel more than 100 km to reach the cancer facility. The average distance traveled to reach the cancer facility was 143 km.

The hub and spoke model is a patient-centric approach providing comprehensive, financially viable, and easily accessible cancer care services. This model comprises a network of hospitals on two levels. State cancer institutes and tertiary care centers providing cancer services serve as anchor establishments (hubs) offering infrastructure, human expertise, and comprehensive services. These are supported by next-level establishments (spokes) comprising state medical colleges and district hospitals providing essential, in-continuity, or supportive care services. Spokes provide the manpower and facilities to treat common cancers and for supportive care and are fittingly connected to a nearby hub. This yields a robust network comprising a leading center and adjoining satellite centers. This model has been used in many health care establishments and may serve to overcome the barriers related to financial issues, transportation, and social support. It is aptly suited for a resource-constrained geographical area.26,27

A sizable number of patients (13.4%) were too sick to come for the treatment. Most of these patients had advanced-stage diseases, compromising their general well-being. Another critical factor affecting treatment adherence is the health care team's counseling (clinician and ancillary staff) regarding the treatment and follow-up schedules. Patients with cancer and their caregivers should be counseled in a compassionate manner regarding the plan of evaluation, treatment, adverse effects arising out of it, outcome, and follow-up schedule. It is challenging but crucial to make the patients and caregivers understand the significance of treatment and follow-up by proper counseling for adherence and desirable outcomes.28,29

Less common factors observed related to default were resorting to another physician or alternative or indigenous treatment, lack of understanding, refusal of treatment, and a life event like marriage or death. Forty-six percent of our patients were illiterate. This may be why these patients need insight and an understanding of the gravity of the disease, leading to nonadherence. It is imperative to know that appropriate counseling and a good patient and HCP team relationship can circumvent most of these factors. Patient and HCP team unison on a shared understanding of goals leads to increased patient satisfaction and treatment adherence. Moreover, social workers and oncology nurses can play a colossal role in bridging the gap between the treating physician and the patient by providing more face time and apt counseling, thus leading to a better adherence.30,31

In conclusion, identifying the reasons for defaulting to cancer treatment and follow-up is the first step toward improving treatment adherence. The most common factors associated with default are lack of social support, financial constraints, transportation barriers, and inadequate counseling by the health care provider. Many of these issues complement each other and can be attenuated by adopting the hub and spoke model. A good relationship between the patient and the health care provider fostered by appropriate counseling regarding insight into the disease is critical to increasing adherence to cancer treatment and improving the outcome.
