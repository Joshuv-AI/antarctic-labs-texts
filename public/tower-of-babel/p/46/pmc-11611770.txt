Host ANGPTL2 establishes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and resistance to immune checkpoint therapy
Authors: Shinsei Yumoto, Haruki Horiguchi, Tsuyoshi Kadomatsu, Taichi Horino, Michio Sato, Kazutoyo Terada, Keishi Miyata, Toshiro Moroishi, Hideo Baba, Yuichi Oike
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUse of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as cancer immunotherapy has advanced rapidly in the clinic; however, mechanisms underlying resistance to ICI therapy, including impaired T cell infiltration, low immunogenicity, and tumor “immunophenotypes” governed by the host, remain unclear. We previously reported that in some cancer contexts, tumor cell‐derived angiopoietin‐like protein 2 (ANGPTL2) has tumor‐promoting functions. Here, we asked whether ANGPTL2 deficiency could enhance antitumor ICI activity in two inflammatory contexts: a murine syngeneic model of colorectal cancer and a mouse model of high‐fat diet (HFD)‐induced obesity. Systemic ANGPTL2 deficiency potentiated ICI efficacy in the syngeneic model, supporting an immunosuppressive role for host ANGPTL2. Relevant to the mechanism, we found that ANGPTL2 induces pro‐inflammatory cytokine production in adipose tissues, driving generation of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in bone marrow and contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and resistance to ICI therapy. Moreover, HFD‐induced obese mice showed impaired responsiveness to ICI treatment, suggesting that obesity‐induced chronic inflammation facilitated by high ANGPTL2 expression blocks ICI antitumor effects. Our findings overall provide novel insight into protumor ANGPTL2 functions and illustrate the essential role of the host system in ICI responsiveness.Keywords: ANGPTL2, cancer immunity, chronic inflammation, immune checkpoint inhibitor, MDSC

INTRODUCTION

Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies represent a revolutionary approach that has shown great clinical benefit against multiple tumor types, such as melanoma, NSCLC, and RCC. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 However, only a subset of patients and cancer types respond positively to ICI therapy. 2 , 7 As ICI therapies, including treatment with anti‐PD‐1 or anti‐PD‐L1 Abs, reinvigorate exhausted CD8+ T cells, it is generally accepted that T cell infiltration of the tumor microenvironment influences ICI therapy efficacy. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Recent studies propose that immunologically, tumors can be “hot” or “cold”; hot tumors, which are characterized by T lymphocyte infiltration, are associated with greater ICI efficacy, while cold tumors, in which T cells are absent or excluded from the tumor microenvironment, are relatively unresponsive to ICI. 13 , 14 However, several studies indicate that even tumors with high CD8+ T cell infiltration may not respond to ICI, 15 , 16 , 17 suggesting that T cells play a key role but are not sufficient for ICI responsiveness. Many studies have focused on the relationship between ICI responses and other immune‐related activities within tumor microenvironment, such as natural killer cell infiltration, 18 high immunogenicity, 19 , 20 antigen presentation sufficiency, 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 and its heterogeneity. 13 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Moreover, other host systems, such as metabolic regulation, nervous system activity, hormones, and the microbiome, reportedly impact tumor immunophenotypes. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Another particularly relevant host factor is the systemic impact of obesity on tumor development. 40 , 41 Obesity can cause chronic low‐grade inflammation, which promotes immune cell activation, secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, and immune cell recruitment to sites of inflammation, contributing to tumor development. 42 , 43 Thus, defining how cellular and molecular mechanisms associated with obesity modulate antitumor immune responses in the tumor microenvironment could suggest novel approaches to combat ICI resistance.

Previously, we and others identified a family of secretory proteins structurally similar to angiopoietin and designated them angiopoietin‐like proteins (ANGPTLs). 44 , 45 We previously showed that one of those proteins, ANGPTL2, promotes cancer progression in some contexts. Specifically, tumor cell‐derived ANGPTL2 promotes tumor cell migration and invasion by activating downstream signaling through integrin α5β1, accelerating metastasis in lung and breast cancers and osteosarcoma. 46 , 47 , 48 We also previously demonstrated that tumor cell‐derived ANGPTL2 renders colorectal cancer cells resistant to chemotherapy and accelerates tumor cell proliferation. 49 , 50 More recently, we reported that tumor cell ANGPTL2 signaling contributes to tumor immune evasion in a murine kidney cancer model. 51 These findings suggest overall that autocrine ANGPTL2 signaling in tumor cells functions in tumor progression. By contrast, we have also reported that ANGPTL2 contributes to maintenance of tissue homeostasis by promoting adaptive inflammation and subsequent tissue reconstruction, while excess ANGPTL2 signaling leads to chronic inflammation and irreversible tissue remodeling, promoting development and progression of various metabolic diseases. 52 For example, obesity‐related increases in ANGPTL2 expression in white adipose tissue promotes chronic inflammation in those tissues and obesity‐related systemic insulin resistance. 53 However, it is unclear whether ANGPTL2‐mediated chronic inflammation in the host regulates ICI efficacy.

Here, analyzing a murine syngeneic colorectal cancer model we report that host ANGPTL2 expression contributes to immune evasion and resistance to anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 Abs. Moreover, parallel evaluation of a mouse model of HFD‐induced obesity suggests that obesity‐induced ANGPTL2 expression in adipose tissues establishes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, leading to ICI resistance. Our findings suggest that the host plays an essential role in modulating ICI responsiveness and may guide design of immune‐based treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

BALB/c and C57BL/6N mice were purchased from CLEA (Japan). Angptl2 +/− mice 53 were back‐crossed to a BALB/c strain for 10 or more generations. Eight to ten‐week‐old littermate mice were used for all experiments. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experimentation of Kumamoto University.

Cell culture

CT26 cells, purchased from ATCC, were cultured in RPMI‐1640 (Wako) supplemented with 10% FCS, penicillin, and streptomycin (Wako) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Tumor transplantation and sample collection

CT26 cells (5 × 105) in 50 μL PBS were injected s.c. into both flanks of 8–12‐week‐old mice. Tumor volume was measured with calipers and calculated using the formula: width2 × height × 0.523. For survival analysis, to minimize suffering, mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1,500 mm3. Tumors <50 mm3 were considered completely regressed. For ICI treatment, mice were treated with polyclonal rat IgG (BioXcell) or anti‐PD‐1 (BioXcell)/PD‐L1 (BioXcell) Abs (4 mg kg−1 each) on the day stated in respective figures and legends. For the HFD‐induced obesity model, mice were fed either ND (CE‐2; CLEA) or HFD (HFD‐32; CLEA) for 6 weeks, starting at 6 weeks of age, and then were challenged with CT26 cells, followed by ICI treatment (8 mg kg−1 each).

For flow cytometric analysis, >50 mg of tumor tissue was minced into 2–3 mm3 pieces and digested 30 min with 1 mg/mL collagenase D and 0.5 mg/mL dispase on a shaking 37°C incubator. To assess the tumor microenvironment in WT and Angptl2 −/− mice, we pooled tumor samples from approximately 10 tumors, passed tissue through a 100 μm cell strainer by mechanical disruption, and treated cells with red blood cell lysis buffer (0.15 M NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.1 mM EDTA). The cell suspension was centrifuged and suspended in MACS buffer (Miltenyi Biotec). Bone marrow cells were obtained from femur and tibia of tumor‐bearing mice. To analyze Angptl2 mRNA expression in tumors or stromal cells, tumor stromal cells were isolated from three pooled tumor specimens by MACS using a CD45 and CD31 MicroBead kit and a tumor‐associated fibroblast isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Isolated cells were subjected to qRT‐PCR analysis. Tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells were isolated from tumor tissues by MACS using a CD8a microbeads kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and subjected to qRT‐PCR analysis.

Bone marrow‐derived MDSC culture and treatment

To generate bone marrow‐derived MDSCs, we isolated bone marrow cells from femur and tibia of naive WT mice, filtered them through a 100 μm cell strainer, and cultured cells as reported 54 in RPMI‐1640 medium (Wako) supplemented with 10% FCS, penicillin, streptomycin, 20 ng/mL IL‐6 (216‐16; PeproTech), and 20 ng/mL GM‐CSF (576304; BioLegend). Three days later, we added an equal volume of complete medium containing 20 ng/mL IL‐6 and 20 ng/mL GM‐CSF, and on day 6, collected cells and subjected them to flow cytometry analysis. For ANGPTL2 treatment, 10 μg/mL rANGPTL2 protein was added on days 0 and 3.

Isolation, culture, and treatment of CD8 + T cells

CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleen of naive WT mice by MACS using a pan T cell isolation kit and a CD8a microbeads kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were then cultured 16 h in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS, penicillin, and streptomycin (Wako), and the Dynabeads mouse T‐activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. After removing the Dynabeads, cells were treated 6 h with vehicle or rANGPTL2 (10 μg/mL), and T cell activation was measured by qRT‐PCR analysis.

Immunofluorescence

For histological analysis, tumor samples were embedded in OCT compound, frozen in hexane with dry ice, sectioned with a cryostat, and fixed in pre‐cold acetone for 20 min. Immunohistochemical staining for mouse ANGPTL2 was carried out using a Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) kit (Perkin Elmer), as described. 55 Samples were incubated overnight with anti‐ANGPTL2 Ab (1:100, made in house) 56 at 4°C. Nuclei were counterstained 20 min with DAPI. Images were obtained using a BZ‐X710 microscope (Keyence). For CD31 immunofluorescence, after fixation, samples were blocked for 20 min in 5% goat serum at room temperature and incubated with anti‐CD31 Ab (1:100; BD Pharmingen) overnight at 4°C. Cells were then incubated 60 min with Abs conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 at room temperature. Nuclei were DAPI‐stained for 20 min. Areas of CD31+ vascularization were quantified by assessing three to four areas from each tumor using BZ‐X analyzer version 1.4.1.1 (Keyence).

Flow cytometry

Cells were suspended in MACS buffer and incubated with anti‐CD16/32 mAb (101310; BioLegend) for Fc receptor blocking. Cells were then stained with the following fluorochrome‐conjugated Abs: PerCP/Cy5.5 anti‐CD45 (103131; BioLegend), APC anti‐CD45 (103111; BioLegend), V421 anti‐CD3 (100336; BioLegend), PE/Cy7 anti‐CD11b (101215; BioLegend), PE anti‐CD4 (553049; BD Biosciences), APC anti‐CD8 (100712; BioLegend), FITC anti‐CD11b (101206; BioLegend), PE/Cy7 anti‐MHC II (107630; BioLegend), V421 anti‐CD64 (139309; BioLegend), PE anti‐Ly6g (127607; BioLegend), V421 anti‐Ly6C (128032; BioLegend), PerCP/Cy5.5 anti‐B220 (103236; BioLegend), FITC anti‐CD45 (103108; BioLegend), and APC anti‐Gr‐1 (108412; BioLegend). Stained cells were analyzed by BD FACSVerse (BD Bioscience). Data analysis was undertaken using FlowJo software (Treestar).

Total RNA extraction and real‐time qRT‐PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). DNase‐treated RNA was reversed transcribed with a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio). The PCR products were analyzed using a Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (Takara Bio). The PCR primer sequences (forward and reverse, respectively) were: 5′‐GGAGGTTGGACTGTCATCCAGAG‐3′ and 5′‐GCCTTGGTTCGTCAGCCAGTA‐3′ for mouse Angptl2; 5′‐AAGTCGGAGGCTTAATTACACATGT‐3′ and 5′‐CCATTGCACAACTCTTTTCTCATTC‐3′ for mouse Il6; 5′‐TCCAGGATGAGGACATGAGCAC‐3′ and 5′‐GAACGTCACACACCAGCAGGTT‐3′ for mouse Il1b; 5′‐CATCCACGTGTTGGCTCA‐3′ and 5′‐GATCATCTTGCTGGTGAATGAGT‐3′ for mouse Ccl2; 5′‐AAGCCTGTAGCCCACGTCGTA‐3′ and 5′‐GGCACCACTAGTTGGTTGTCTTTG‐3′ for mouse Tnf; 5′‐GCCTCCTGTTCTACAAGTGGAAG‐3′ and 5′‐ACTGGCAGTTCCACCTGTCTGT‐3′ for mouse Csf1; 5′‐AACCTCCTGGATGACATGCCTG‐3′ and 5′‐AAATTGCCCCGTAGACCCTGCT‐3′ for mouse Csf2; 5′‐GCCACGGCACAGTCATTGA‐3′ and 5′‐TGCTGATGGCCTGATTGTCTT‐3′ for mouse Ifng; and 5′‐TTCTGGCCAACGGTCTAGACAAC‐3′ and 5′‐CCAGTGGTCTTGGTGTGCTGA‐3′ for mouse 18s. Relative transcript abundance was calculated after normalization to 18s mRNA.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were undertaken using GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software). Statistical parameters and methods are reported in respective figures and legends. Results with p values <0.05 were considered significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). Comparisons between two groups were carried out using an unpaired two‐tailed t‐test. For comparisons with two or more independent variable factors, we used two‐way ANOVA/mixed‐effects analysis. Survival rate was analyzed by log‐rank test. Fisher's exact test was used to compare complete regression rates of CT26 tumors. To determine statistical outliers, we used the Robust Regression and Outlier Removal (ROUT) method (Q = 1%) prior to data analysis.

RESULTS

Antitumor therapy with ICIs is potentiated in Angptl2‐deficient mice

To determine whether ANGPTL2 activity in the host functions in tumor development, we evaluated a murine syngeneic cancer model. In tumors, tumor cell‐intrinsic ANGPTL2 expression contributes to tumor progression, 51 while ANGPTL2 expression by CAFs in the tumor microenvironment is tumor‐suppressive. 51 , 57 Therefore, to distinguish ANGPTL2 function in the host from that in the tumor microenvironment, we evaluated formation of tumors in mice from CT26 murine colon carcinoma cells, which do not express ANGPTL2. Also in this model, we confirmed that ANGPTL2 is not expressed in stromal CAFs, immune cells, or endothelial cells found in the tumor microenvironment (Figure S1). After CT26 cell injection into the flanks of WT or Angptl2 −/− mice, we observed no significant differences in tumor volume (Figure S2A,B) or mortality (Figure S2C) between genotypes, suggesting that host ANGPTL2 is dispensable for CT26 cell tumor growth. Then, to assess a potential association between host ANGPTL2 expression and ICI effects, after confirming tumor formation in both genotypes on day 7, we administered anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐PD‐L1 Abs i.p. to mice on days 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, and 17 and monitored tumor volume (Figure 1A). Overall, Angptl2 −/− mice showed decreased tumor volume and weight relative to WT mice (Figure 1B–E). Moreover, 2 of 10 (20%) tumors in WT mice showed complete regression, while 11 of 15 (73%) tumors in Angptl2 −/− mice were completely regressed (Figure 1F). Moreover, survival of Angptl2 −/− mice bearing CT26 tumors was significantly prolonged compared with that of WT tumor‐bearing mice (Figure 1G). These findings suggest overall that host ANGPTL2 expression attenuates ICI efficacy, leading to resistance to immunotherapy.

Host ANGPTL2 deficiency antagonizes immunosuppression in tumor microenvironment

We next addressed potential immunoregulatory mechanisms underlying acquisition of ICI resistance in the presence of ANGPTL2. Given that CD8+ T cell activity is critical for ICI antitumor effects, 8 we isolated tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells from Angptl2 −/− and WT mice bearing CT26 tumors and that had undergone treatment with anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐PD‐L1 Abs and evaluated expression of Ifng and Tnf, both of which function in T cell activation (Figure 2A). Tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells from Angptl2 −/− mice showed greater Ifng and Tnf expression than did cells from comparable WT mice, indicative of enhanced T cell activation (Figure 2B). Next, to determine if ANGPTL2 directly activated T cells, we stimulated CD8+ T cells isolated from spleen of naive, WT mice with either rANGPTL2 or vehicle in vitro and assessed Ifng and Tnf expression. Expression of both was comparable (Figure S3A), suggesting that ANGPTL2 may block T cell activation by modulating components of the tumor immune microenvironment. To test this hypothesis, we undertook flow cytometry analysis of cells from tumor tissues of WT and Angptl2 −/− mice to characterize immune cell subpopulations. Because tumors seen in Angptl2 −/− mice described above were substantially smaller or undetectable at time points in which tumors in WT mice had reached the end‐point, we undertook flow cytometry analysis of 10 or more tumors from mice of both genotypes to compare the tumor microenvironment (Figure 2A). Quantification of immune cell distribution across cell clusters revealed a comparable number of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells in Angptl2 −/− and WT mice; however, relative to WT mice, tumors from Angptl2 −/− mice showed significantly decreased infiltration of both PMN‐MDSCs (Gr‐1+CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chigh, which are a major fraction of MDSCs) and M‐MDSCs (Gr‐1+CD11b+ Ly6G+Ly6Clow, which are a minor fraction of MDSCs) (Figure 2C). These findings suggest that activation of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells in Angptl2 −/− mice may be due to decreased infiltration of PMN‐MDSCs. Given that MDSCs originate from common myeloid progenitors in bone marrow and are recruited to tumor sites by chemokines secreted from both cancer and tumor stromal cells, 58 , 59 , 60 we collected bone marrow from WT and Angptl2 −/− mice in our model (Figure 2A), and observed that the PMN‐MDSC fraction was significantly reduced in bone marrow of Angptl2 −/− relative to WT mice (Figure 2D). However, the percentage of M‐MDSCs in Angptl2 −/− and WT bone marrow was comparable (Figure 2D). These observations suggest that host ANGPTL2 expression supports PMN‐MDSC generation in bone marrow, creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

ANGPTL2 deficiency decreases inflammation of adipose tissue

Next, to determine whether ANGPTL2 directly enhances MDSC generation or maturation, we investigated ANGPTL2 effects on MDSC differentiation. To do so, we prepared primary bone‐marrow‐derived MDSCs derived from naive, WT mice in the presence of rANGPTL2 in vitro and undertook flow cytometric analysis. We observed comparable generation of PMN‐MDSCs and M‐MDSCs between rANGPTL2‐stimulated and unstimulated cells (Figure S3B). These findings suggest that ANGPTL2 does not directly impact MDSC generation but that host ANGPTL2 expression contributes to MDSC generation and differentiation. Obesity‐related chronic inflammation in adipose tissues reportedly promotes differentiation of bone marrow progenitors into MDSCs through activity of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. 59 , 61 We previously reported that ANGPTL2 is very highly expressed in adipose tissues and that high ANGPTL2 expression in visceral adipose tissues enhances chronic adipose tissue inflammation in mice and humans. 53 Thus, we hypothesized that ANGPTL2 induces pro‐inflammatory mediators in adipose tissues that drive PMN‐MDSC generation in bone marrow. To test this, we assessed levels of transcripts encoding pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in eWAT from Angptl2 −/− and WT mice in our tumor model. Based on qRT‐PCR analysis, expression of Il6, Tnf, and Csf2 in eWAT from Angptl2 −/− mice was lower than that seen in eWAT from WT mice (Figure 2E). The eWAT from Angptl2 −/− mice also showed modest but not significant decreases in Il1b, Ccl2, and Csf1 relative to WT mice (Figure 2E). Overall, these results suggest that ANGPTL2 expressed in adipose tissues induces pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, which may drive PMN‐MDSC generation in bone marrow.

Tumor angiogenesis is essential for immune cell recruitment. As we previously reported that ANGPTL2 activates endothelial cell motility, 53 we asked whether the extent of the CT26 tumor vasculature differed in WT compared to Angptl2 −/− mice. Immunofluorescence staining for CD31 revealed comparable tumor angiogenesis between genotypes (Figure S3C,D), suggesting that vascular changes potentially mediated by ANGPTL2 do not impact MDSC populations.

Obesity impairs responsiveness to ICI treatment

We previously reported that obesity and obesity‐related stress increase ANGPTL2 expression in adipose tissues. 53 Given findings reported above that Angptl2 −/− mice show improved responsiveness to ICI treatment and lower pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression in adipose tissues, we hypothesized that obesity‐induced chronic inflammation facilitated by excess ANGPTL2 expression might attenuate ICI antitumor effects. To test this, we used an HFD‐induced obesity mouse model, in which chronic inflammatory responses in liver and adipose tissue lead to low‐grade systemic inflammation. 62 , 63 , 64 To establish the model, we fed WT mice a HFD or ND for 6 weeks and then implanted s.c. mice with CT26 cells (Figure 3A). Over the 6‐week period, HFD mice gained more weight than did ND mice (Figure 3B). At week 6, we assessed Angptl2 expression in eWAT from both HFD and ND mice and observed increased Angptl2 expression in HFD mice (Figure 3C). Next, to assess ICI effects in this model, we injected CT26 cells into the flanks of both HFD and ND mice (Figure 3D) and administered anti‐PD‐1 and PD‐L1 Abs i.p. to both groups on days 7, 9, and 11 (Figure 3D). Tumor tissues were isolated each group on day 12, and HFD mice showed an increase in weight of s.c. grafted CT26 cells compared to the ND group (Figure 3E,F), suggesting that the therapeutic effects of ICI is attenuated by obesity‐induced ANGPTL2 expression.

To further assess mechanisms underlying HFD‐induced resistance to ICI therapy, we undertook flow cytometry analysis of CT26 tumors from HFD‐ and ND‐fed mice 12 days after cell implantation (Figure 3A). The proportion of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and monocytes was comparable in both groups (Figure 3G). However, the proportion of PMN‐MDSCs significantly increased in tumors from HFD versus ND mice, although the proportion of M‐MDSCs was comparable between groups (Figure 3G). Furthermore, mice fed a HFD showed increased numbers of MDSCs, especially PMN‐MDSCs, in bone marrow relative to ND mice (Figure 3H), suggesting that obesity‐induced chronic inflammation promotes PMN‐MDSC differentiation in bone marrow, resulting in ICI resistance.

Angptl2‐deficient mice fed HFD show decreased ICI resistance relative to WT counterparts

Given that HFD‐induced obesity enhances not only ANGPTL2 expression but that of numerous inflammatory cytokines, we asked whether ANGPTL2 impacts ICI responsiveness in an HFD‐induced obesity murine model. To do so, we fed WT or Angptl2 −/− mice HFD for 6 weeks (Figure 4A). Over that period, Angptl2 −/− and WT mice showed comparable weight gain (Figure 4B). Then at week 6, we injected CT26 cells into the flanks of these mice, administered anti‐PD‐1 and PD‐L1 Abs i.p. to both groups on days 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, and 17, and monitored tumor volume (Figure 4C). Angptl2 −/− mice fed HFD showed decreased tumor growth relative to HFD‐fed WT mice (Figure 4D,E). Also, the complete regression rate of CT26 tumors was significantly higher in HFD‐fed Angptl2 −/− versus WT mice (11 of 14 [79%] vs. 2 of 17 [12%]) (Figure 4F). Moreover, survival of tumor‐bearing mice was significantly prolonged in HFD‐fed Angptl2 −/− versus WT mice (Figure 4G). These findings suggest that host ANGPTL2 expression attenuates ICI efficacy under conditions of obesity‐induced chronic inflammation, supporting a critical role for host ANGPTL2 on development of obesity‐associated resistance to immunotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Here, we show that ANGPTL2 deficiency in the host potentiates responsiveness to anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 treatment in a murine syngeneic tumor model. We also found that host ANGPTL2 deficiency decreased PMN‐MDSC infiltration of tumor tissues and also decreased PMN‐MDSC generation in bone marrow. Moreover, our data suggests that obesity‐induced chronic inflammation, which is facilitated by obesity‐induced excess ANGPTL2 expression, attenuates ICI antitumor effects. We conclude that ANGPTL2 expressed in adipose tissues induces pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, driving generation of PMN‐MDSCs in bone marrow and contributing to establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and resistance to ICI therapy (Figure 5).

(C) In obesity, upregulated ANGPTL2 expression in adipose tissues facilitates chronic adipose tissue inflammation, promoting MDSC differentiation in bone marrow and contributing to ICI resistance. We previously reported that ANGPTL2 is expressed in both the tumor microenvironment and in tumor cells, and that the latter promotes cancer progression in some contexts and enhances tumor cell migration and invasion, tumor angiogenesis, and tumor cell proliferation. 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 65 , 66 More recently, we reported that ANGPTL2 signaling in tumor cells contributes to tumor immune evasion in a murine kidney cancer model, 51 suggesting that autocrine ANGPTL2 signaling by tumor cells functions in tumor progression. On the other hand, tumor stroma‐derived ANGPTL2 contributes to tumor suppression by enhancing antitumor immune responses, 57 , 67 suggesting a dual function of ANGPTL2 signaling in cancer pathology depending on context. Here, to assess how host ANGPTL2 expression impacts ICI therapy, we chose a CT26 syngeneic model in which ANGPTL2 is not expressed in either tumor or stromal cells. Using those mice, we show potentiation of antitumor ICI therapy in Angptl2‐deficient mice, indicating that host ANGPTL2 blocks ICI efficacy, leading to resistance to ICI.

In this study, Angptl2‐deficient mice treated with ICI showed suppressed tumor development compared to similarly treated WT mice in a syngeneic tumor model. Antitumor effects of Angptl2 loss were not seen between genotypes in the absence of ICI treatment, possibly because ANGPTL2 loss alone cannot promote an effective antitumor immune response due to overriding activity of immune checkpoint molecules.

Relevant to clinical applications, it is proposed that turning “cold” into “hot” tumors enhances the clinical benefits of immunotherapy. 13 , 68 Cold tumors are marked by scarcity of T cells and immunosuppression mediated by tumor‐associated macrophages, T regulatory cells, and MDSCs. 68 In particular, MDSC infiltration of the tumor microenvironment interferes with anticancer effects of ICIs. 69 Furthermore, meta‐analysis of a human clinical study showed that elevated numbers of circulating MDSCs were an independent indicator of poor outcomes in subjects with solid tumors. 70 Recently, therapeutic anticancer approaches targeting MDSC activation have received attention; for example, inhibition of MDSC activity, migration, or differentiation enhances ICI effects in a murine model of melanoma, RCC, colorectal cancer, rhabdomyosarcoma, head and neck cancers, prostate cancer, and glioma. 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 Moreover, treatment of murine breast cancer with ferrimagnetic vortex‐domain iron oxide nanorings significantly increased CTL infiltration of tumors and decreased MDSC infiltration, effects that synergized with anti‐PD‐L1 antibodies. 77 Moreover, in the COMBAT trial, treatment of metastatic PDAC with the CXCR4 antagonist plus pembrolizumab synergistically increased infiltration of tumors by CD8+ effector T cells and reduced MDSC density in tumors. 78 Given that host ANGPTL2 promotes PMN‐MDSC differentiation and ICI resistance, the ANGPTL2–PMN‐MDSC axis could be targeted by novel therapeutics to improve antitumor immunity.

Although healthy adults show few MDSCs in peripheral blood, conditions associated with chronic inflammation, such as autoimmune disease or cancer, result in aberrant and sustained myelopoiesis as well as MDSC generation. 59 Relevant to mechanism, signaling factors released from chronic inflammatory sites promote MDSC differentiation in bone marrow. 43 , 59 , 61 , 79 , 80 , 81 Overall, such activities inhibit T cell function in tumors and at inflammation sites. 59 , 82 , 83 Indeed, we observed increased numbers of PMN‐MDSCs in bone marrow from HFD‐induced obese mice, linking chronic inflammation with MDSC generation. We also show increased Angptl2 transcript levels in mesenteric white adipose tissues in HFD‐fed obese mice and observed decreased Il6, Tnf, and Csf2 expression in eWAT and decreased numbers of PMN‐MDSCs in bone marrow from Angptl2 −/− mice. We also previously reported that ANGPTL2 deficiency attenuates HFD‐induced expression of pro‐inflammatory factors, indicative of decreased adipose tissue inflammation: relevant to mechanism, in cases of severe obesity, ANGPTL2 is abundantly expressed in adipocytes, and high ANGPTL2 levels lead to vascular inflammation through the integrin α5β1–nuclear factor‐κB pathway. 53 ANGPTL2 also promotes migration of circulating monocytes directly into adipose tissue through inflamed blood vessels and enhances production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and others) in monocytes or differentiated macrophages through integrin α5β1. 53 , 84 These observations suggest that obesity and obesity‐induced ANGPTL2–integrin α5β1 signaling induce pro‐inflammatory signaling that promotes PMN‐MDSC differentiation in bone marrow. Thus, we propose that blocking ANGPTL2–integrin α5β1 signaling could antagonize development of adipose inflammation and subsequent MDSC generation.

Chronic inflammation reportedly contributes to development and progression of several cancers, among them, gastric, esophageal, colorectal, liver, pancreatic, bladder, and lung cancer. 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 Although obesity causes chronic subclinical inflammation, 89 , 90 , 91 its effect on anticancer immunotherapies is controversial. For example, recent studies report that the benefits of ICI therapy are attenuated in patients with melanoma and NSCLC showing severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2). 92 , 93 Nonetheless, others even reported that high BMI is positively associated with improved progression‐free survival or overall survival in patients with melanoma, NSCLC, and RCC following ICI with Abs to PD‐1, PD‐L1, or CTL‐associated antigen‐4 (CTLA4), 92 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 , 99 a phenomenon called the “obesity paradox”. Importantly, although obesity is primarily diagnosed using BMI cut‐offs, as BMI cannot distinguish skeletal muscle from adipose tissue, BMI reportedly misclassifies <30% of individuals, 100 suggesting body fat status determinations based on BMI may not reflect systemic chronic inflammation. Other studies report that assessing obesity by BMI alone leads to incorrect assessment of ICI efficacy due to the lack of longitudinal BMI assessment, the differences regarding the type of administered ICIs, and the comparison between weight‐based and fixed dose ICIs. 101 , 102 Notably, one study reported that visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation were significant prognostic factors in patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma receiving ICI therapy, and that the prognostic impact of visceral adiposity, not BMI, depended on systemic inflammation status. 103 In addition, a recent study of RCC revealed that obesity decreased efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 therapy in humans and orthotopic murine models. 104 Furthermore, several studies report that nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, which is often marked by chronic sterile inflammation, limits immunotherapy efficacy against hepatocellular carcinoma. 105 , 106 Nonetheless, few reports have focused on a potential association between chronic inflammation and ICI efficacy. Thus our demonstration that HFD‐induced obese mice showing chronic inflammation acquire tolerance to ICI therapies provides novel insight into how the host regulates antitumor immunity and ICI responsiveness.

In summary, our findings strongly suggest that host ANGPTL2 promotes secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, which facilitate PMD‐MDSC differentiation in bone marrow and establish an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, resulting in resistance to ICI therapy. Our findings suggest that the host plays an essential role in modulating ICI responsiveness and may guide design of immune‐based treatments.
