Characterization of the AP2/ERF gene family in Fritillaria thunbergii and functional evaluation of FtAP2/ERF-61 in conferring tolerance to drought and salt stress
Authors: Jia Liu, Kejie Zhang, Xiaoxiao Sheng, Zixuan Zhu, Qiang Yuan, Hui Wang, Ning Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe APETALA 2/ethylene-responsive element binding factors (AP2/ERFs) are crucial modulators of plant development and stress responses. Fritillaria thunbergii is an important medicinal herb with considerable therapeutic efficacy. However, despite its importance, no systematic identification and functional characterization of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii has been reported to date.ResultsThis study identified a total of 71 AP2/ERF genes in F. thunbergii and classified them into 11 subgroups, with genes in the same subgroup exhibiting similar motifs and domains. Expression analysis revealed that these genes displayed distinct expression patterns under salt and drought stress, and Gene Ontology annotation indicated their involvement in stress responses and hormone signaling. Further analysis demonstrated that FtAP2/ERF-61 responded to mannitol, NaCl, ethylene, abscisic acid, and jasmonic acid. The FtAP2/ERF-61 protein consisted of 352 amino acids, contained a conserved AP2/ERF domain, was localized to the nucleus, and possessed transcriptional activation activity. Overexpression of FtAP2/ERF-61 in Arabidopsis thaliana significantly enhanced germination rates and growth status under salt and osmotic stress, while reducing hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde accumulation, and upregulating the expression of stress-responsive genes and antioxidant enzyme genes.ConclusionThis study provided the systematic identification of AP2/ERF family members in F. thunbergii and demonstrated that FtAP2/ERF-61 conferred dual stress tolerance by activating the ROS-scavenging system and stress-responsive genes, thereby offering new genetic resources and a theoretical basis for molecular breeding of stress-tolerant medicinal plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09538-y.Keywords: AP2/ERF transcription factor, Fritillaria thunbergii, Drought stress, Salt stress, ROS homeostasis

Introduction

Plants encounter complicated and ever-changing environmental difficulties as they develop and mature [1]. Several abiotic stresses, including salinity and aridity, present significant threats to plant survival and yield. Salt stress raises soil solution osmotic pressure. This hinders water absorption in plants and induces ionic toxicity, hence disrupting normal physiological and biochemical processes within them [2]. Drought stress leads to water scarcity in plants, adversely impacting essential physiological activities like photosynthesis and respiration, hence constraining plant growth and development [3]. Soil salinization, a worldwide environmental concern, is a principal cause in soil degradation. Soil salinization has deteriorated about 20% of arable land around the world [4]. The deposition of salts diminishes soil microbial variety and biological activity, hence impeding crop growth and agricultural advancement. Furthermore, traditional engineering methods for soil improvement, such as freshwater leaching for salt suppression and subsurface drainage, are costly, unsustainable, and can cause secondary salinization [5–7]. Therefore, conducting in-depth research on the mechanisms plants use to respond to abiotic stresses and exploring stress-resistant gene resources to breed stress-resistant plant varieties are of great significance for ensuring sustainable agricultural development.

Within the realm of plant biology, the AP2/ERF gene family is an important family of transcription factors (TF) [8]. Its members are integral to activities like plant development, hormone signal transduction, and responses to abiotic stressors [9]. AP2/ERFs have a conserved AP2 domain that exhibits a characteristic 3D conformation with one α-helix and three β-sheets, which is implicated in DNA binding [10]. This domain can selectively attach to the target genes. The AP2/ERF gene family can be subdivided into subfamilies, including AP2, RAV, DREB, ERF, and Soloist, based on the quantity and arrangement of the AP2 domains [11, 12]. There are two AP2 domains in the AP2 subfamily and one AP2 domain and one B3 domain in the RAV subfamily. The DREB and ERF subfamilies both have a single AP2 domain. The Soloist group also only has one AP2 domain, but its members have very little in common with members of the DREB and ERF subfamilies. They also have the weakest evolutionary ties to other subfamilies [13]. Despite the fact that members of different subfamilies exhibit considerable functional differences, they all make significant contributions to the adaptability of plants.

Plant development, morphogenesis, and responses to abiotic stresses are all dependent on the presence of members of the AP2/ERFs family [14]. There have been studies conducted on members of the AP2/ERF family in a number of different plant species, including A. thaliana [15], soybean [16], maize [17], eggplant [18], and Coix lacryma-jobi [19]. Recent investigations have concentrated on the involvement of the AP2/ERFs in plant stress resistance mechanisms, amid the swift advancement of biotechnology. AP2/ERF significantly influences plant responses to abiotic stressors, hence augmenting tolerance to conditions such as salinity and drought. In A. thaliana, the AP2/ERF member WRI1 establishes an indirect link between the abscisic acid and sugar signalling pathways, influencing the sensitivity to abscisic acid and osmotic stress [20]. In soybean, GmDREB1 forms heterodimers by interacting with GmERF008 or GmERF106, which in turn enhance the downstream genes and thereby enhances soybean drought resistance [21]. Osmotic resistance in tomatoes is negatively modulated by the AP2/ERF member SlERF.B1. Its overexpression heightens vulnerability to salt and drought stressors [22]. In eggplant, silencing SmRAV1 reduces its tolerance to salt stress, while silencing SmERF1 significantly enhances its sensitivity to salt stress [18]. In Coix lacryma-jobi, the AP2/ERF members ClWRI1-18 and ClWRI1-20 collaboratively modulate various hormonal and stress pathways in reaction to environmental salinity and drought, therefore augmenting stress resilience and preserving cell membrane integrity [19]. Consequently, it is clear that the AP2/ERFs are crucial in assisting diverse plants in managing abiotic stressors.

Fritillaria thunbergii, belonging to the Liliaceae family and Fritillaria genus, is a herbaceous medicinal plant [23]. It has notable effects such as clearing heat, resolving phlegm, dispersing swellings, and eliminating abscesses [24]. In recent years, compound preparations with F. thunbergii as the main ingredient have demonstrated promising application prospects in the adjuvant treatment of respiratory diseases and certain tumours, leading to a continuous increase in clinical demand [25]. To meet market needs, large-scale cultivation of F. thunbergii has been carried out in provinces such as Zhejiang, Jiangsu, and Anhui in China. F. thunbergii is vulnerable to abiotic challenges during its growth, including elevated temperatures and dryness, leading to yield variability and quality deterioration [26]. The AP2/ERF gene family is a fundamental regulatory element in plants’ responses to abiotic stresses and is also involved in developmental processes like seed germination and bulbs enlargement, rendering it a crucial focus for research aimed at enhancing variety and breeding for stress resistance in F. thunbergii [27]. However, current research on the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii is relatively scarce. Comprehensive investigation into the structure, function, and mechanism of action of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii in reaction to external stressors will elucidate the molecular basis of F. thunbergii’s environmental adaptation and furnish a theoretical foundation for employing genetic engineering techniques to develop F. thunbergii varieties with improved stress resistance.

Despite extensive studies on AP2/ERFs in model plants and major crops, no systematic identification or functional characterization of this gene family has been reported in F. thunbergii. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms by which AP2/ERFs regulate drought and salt tolerance in this medicinal plant remain completely unknown. In this study, we therefore aimed to: (i) systematically identify and characterize the AP2/ERF family members in F. thunbergii using bioinformatics approaches; (ii) analyze their expression profiles under various abiotic stresses and hormone treatments; and (iii) functionally evaluate a candidate gene, FtAP2/ERF-61, through heterologous expression in yeast and Arabidopsis. This study presents the analysis of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii and the discovery of FtAP2/ERF-61 as a previously uncharacterized dual regulator of drought and salt tolerance acting through ROS homeostasis and stress-responsive gene activation, thereby offering novel genetic resources and theoretical foundations for molecular breeding to enhance stress resistance in F. thunbergii.

Materials and methods

Plant growth conditions and stress treatments

The Fritillaria thunbergii material used in this study was the commercial cultivar ‘Zhebeimu No. 1’. This cultivar is one of the elite lines currently recommended and widely cultivated in the main production regions of Zhejiang Province, China, and is characterized by stable agronomic traits, high contents of bioactive constituents, and broad adaptability. The plant material was provided by Pan’an City, Zhejiang Province, and was taxonomically verified as F. thunbergii by Professor Bing Yu of Zhejiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Voucher specimens (No. ZJ00100193) were deposited at the Zhejiang Institute for Food and Drug Control, Hangzhou, China. An artificial climate room with 16 h of light at 25 °C and 8 h of darkness at 18 °C was used to cultivate F. thunbergii. Three-month-old seedlings were drenched with 200 mM mannitol and 200 mM NaCl as abiotic stress treatments. The concentrations were selected based on preliminary experiments (100–300 mM gradients) (Fig. S1). Additionally, these concentrations are consistent with previous studies on osmotic and salt stress in related species and other medicinal plants [28, 29]. F. thunbergii seedlings were given 100 mg/L abscisic acid solution, 10 mg/L methyl jasmonate solution, and 100 mg/L ethephon solution as hormone treatments.

In this investigation, A. thaliana lines of the Columbia (Col-0) ecotype were used. A. thaliana seeds were sown on 1/2-Murashige and Skoog (MS) solid semi-medium after being sterilized for five minutes each with 75% (v/v) ethanol and 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite [30]. Seven days later, well-grown A. thaliana seedlings were transferred to pots and watered every three days. For seed germination assays, treatment dosages of 200 mM NaCl and 350 mM mannitol were used [31]. Four-day-old A. thaliana seedlings raised in normal conditions were moved to stress media containing 200 mM mannitol or 100 mM NaCl for seven days in order to perform root growth tests. Drought stress was applied to four-week-old plants of A. thaliana, and the plants that were not given water for 12 d were used for biochemical analysis. Four-week-old plants of A. thaliana were grown in 200 mM NaCl solution for 5 d, after which samples were taken for physiological studies. All samples were obtained from a minimum of three biological replicates.

Identification of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii

The transcriptome data used in this work was submitted to NCBI (SRR31178335 to SRR31178358). Coding sequences (CDS) and protein sequences were extracted from the sequencing data after genes containing the AP2 domain (PF00847) were found in the Pfam annotation file. These candidate genes were searched for the presence of a complete domain using the hidden markov model (HMM) profiles from the Pfam database (http://pfam.janelia.org/search/sequence), SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/), and NCBI Conserved Domain Search (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) [32]. Genes without a full AP2 domain were excluded. Genes with one AP2 domain and one complete B3 domain (PF02362) were found to belong to the RAV subfamily. The ExPASy was used to determine the genes’ amino acids, Open Reading Frame (ORF) length, molecular weight (Mw), and isoelectric point (pI) [33].

Phylogenetic tree and analysis of conserved motifs for the FtAP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii

The AtAP2/ERFs sequences were acquired from the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1361313/) [15]. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was generated utilizing the neighbour-joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11.0 software, incorporating 1000 bootstrap repetitions. Conserved motifs of FtAP2/ERF were discovered using the Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME) online program [34].

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

For expression profiling of FtAP2/ERF genes in different tissues and under stress treatments, total RNA was extracted from three biological replicates per sample. Total RNA was isolated from F. thunbergii samples utilizing a plant total RNA extraction kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer was used to measure the amount of extracted RNA. cDNA synthesis was conducted with the UnionScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Gene-specific primers for each of the 71 FtAP2/ERF genes were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Table S1). qRT-PCR was conducted in a 35 µL reaction mixture including 20 µL of 2 × qPCR SYBR Mix, 1 µL of each primer, 2.5 µL of cDNA template, and 10.5 µL of ddH2O. The reaction comprised a two-step amplification process: pre-denaturation at 97 ℃ for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 97 ℃ for 20 s and annealing/extension at 57 ℃ for 35 s [21]. A melting curve analysis was performed to verify amplicon specificity (Fig. S2). The F. thunbergii 18s-rRNA gene was used as the internal reference. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. For tissue expression analysis, the expression level in leaf tissue was set as the calibrator; for stress treatments, the expression level in untreated control seedlings was set to 1.0. All reactions were performed in three technical replicates for each of the three biological replicates.

For hormone treatment analysis of FtAP2/ERF-61 expression, the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative expression levels. The expression level at 0 h (untreated control) for each hormone treatment was set to 1.0, and the expression levels at other time points (3, 6, 9, 12, 24 h) were calculated relative to the corresponding 0 h sample.

For expression analysis of stress-responsive genes in transgenic Arabidopsis, Col-0 and FtAP2/ERF-61 overexpression lines (OE#1 and OE#2) were grown under controlled conditions as described in section "Plant growth conditions and stress treatments". For each line and treatment, three independent biological replicates were collected. The AtActin gene (AT2G37620) was used as the internal reference. The genes analyzed included ROS-scavenging enzyme genes and stress-responsive genes. For each gene, the expression level in untreated Col-0 plants was set to 1.0, and all other samples were normalized accordingly.

GO annotation analysis

The protein sequences of FtAP2/ERFs underwent BLAST searches against the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot database using the NCBI website. GO annotation was conducted with TBtools, setting the parameters MinWeightCov to 0.33 and MaxEvalue to 1e-5 [35].

Correlation analysis of FtAP2/ERF with hormone signalling pathway genes

To explore potential associations between FtAP2/ERFs and genes involved in abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ETH) signaling pathways, we performed correlation analysis based on expression data under salt and drought stress conditions. The genes participating in each hormone pathway were identified by Pfam domain search and NCBI CDD validation. Spearman correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using R software for each pair of FtAP2/ERF gene and hormone pathway gene. Only gene pairs with |r| > 0.6 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Correlation heatmaps and network visualizations were generated using the OmicStudio tool.

Assays for transcriptional activation and subcellular localization

Subcellular localization was assessed using a modified version of Baros’s method [36]. The ORF of FtAP2/ERF-61 gene (without stop codon) was amplified from the pTOPO-Blunt Vector and inserted via homologous recombination (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) into the pCambia1305-GFP vector, which contains a CaMV 35 S-driven GFP reporter. The resulting constructs were delivered into Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells by Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101, and the cells were incubated at 22 ℃ in darkness for 48 h. Fluorescence signals were then observed under a confocal microscope (LSM780, Zeiss) according to Baros [36].

The FtAP2/ERF-61 was cloned into the pGBKT7-7 vector for transcriptional activation tests. The negative control was the empty pGBKT7-7 vector, whereas the positive controls were pGBKT7-T and pGBKT7-53. Yeast transformation was performed using the lithium acetate method. The transformation products were plated on synthetic drop out medium lacking tryptophan (SD/-Trp) and incubated at 30 ℃ for 3–5 d. Three independent colonies from each transformation were selected and resuspended in sterile water. To evaluate transcriptional activation activity, serial dilutions of each yeast suspension were spotted onto SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade medium, and the plates were incubated at 30 ℃ for 3–5 d.

Yeast transformation and stress tolerance assay

The coding sequence of FtAP2/ERF-61 was amplified and cloned into the pPIC9 expression vector. The recombinant plasmid pPIC9-FtAP2/ERF-61 and the empty pPIC9 vector (negative control) were transformed into strain GS115 [35]. The yeast transformants were grown in buffered methanol-complex media (BMMY) until their optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.0. The culture underwent serial dilution employing a gradient dilution method (100, 10− 1, 10− 2). Equal amounts of the diluted cultures were applied on yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) plates supplemented with either 1.5 M mannitol or 1.0 M NaCl. For 48–72 h, the plates were incubated at 30 ℃. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Generation of transgenic A. thaliana

To construct the overexpression vector, the full-length CDS of FtAP2/ERF-61 was ligated into the pCAMBIA1305 vector using homologous recombination (ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The recombinant plasmid was verified by sequencing and then transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Seeds of A. thaliana ecotype Columbia (Col-0) were surface-sterilized, sown on 1/2 MS semi-solid medium, and cultured at 22 ℃ under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod for 7 d, after which they were transplanted to potting mix for further growth. Genetic transformation was performed using the floral dip method [37]. Briefly, A. tumefaciens GV3101 harboring the recombinant plasmid was cultured in LB liquid medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin and 20 mg/L rifampicin at 28 °C to the logarithmic phase. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000 g for 10 min and resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 of 0.8. The primary inflorescences of approximately 4-week-old plants at the initial flowering stage were immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension for 30 s with gentle agitation. After dipping, the plants were covered to maintain humidity and incubated in darkness for 24 h, then returned to normal light conditions. A second dipping of newly emerged inflorescences was performed 7 d later. T0 seeds were harvested, dried, and selected on 1/2 MS plates containing 70 mg/L kanamycin. After 10 d, seedlings exhibiting green true leaves and normal root elongation were transplanted to soil. Genomic DNA was extracted from leaves of each candidate plant, and PCR validation was performed using gene-specific primers and vector backbone primers. Positive plants were allowed to self-pollinate to produce T1 seeds. T1 seeds were again selected on kanamycin-containing plates, and the resulting seedlings were transplanted to soil and confirmed by PCR. After seed collection from the positive plants, this screening procedure was repeated to obtain T2 plants. The expression level of FtAP2/ERF-61 in the homozygous lines was examined by qRT-PCR, and the two independent lines with the highest expression levels (OE‑FtAP2/ERF-61#1 and OE‑FtAP2/ERF-61#2) were selected for subsequent phenotypic analyses.

Assessment of physiological and biochemical parameters

Commercial test kits (Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) were used to measure the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; G0168W48) and malondialdehyde (MDA; G0109W), and catalase (CAT; G0105W) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX; G0203W). Frozen tissue (0.2 g) was homogenized in 2 mL of the extraction buffer (provided in the kit) using a tissue grinder. After centrifuging the homogenate, the supernatant was gathered for additional biochemical examination. To further validate H2O2 accumulation, A. thaliana seedling leaves were stained using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining kit [37].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently performed three times, representing three biological replicates. For each biological replicate, sample measurements included three technical replicates. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0. Prior to analysis of variance, normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference test was employed to determine significant differences between groups (P < 0.05).

Results

Identification of AP2/ERF genes in F. thunbergii

A total of 71 AP2/ERF gene family members were identified in F. thunbergii via HMM profile searches (PF00847) combined with homology-based approaches. Detailed information on the protein length, pI, Mw, and subcellular localization of these FtAP2/ERFs is provided in Table S2. The coding sequences of the 71 FtAP2/ERF genes have been provided in Table S3. The protein lengths spanned from 110 to 598 amino acids, and the predicted Mw varied from 12,004.35 Da (FtAP2/ERF-4) to 66,296.09 Da (FtAP2/ERF-58). The pI values of all FtAP2/ERFs varied from 4.57 (FtAP2/ERF-4) to 9.98 (FtAP2/ERF-40). The results of the subcellular localization prediction showed that 49 genes were located in the nucleus, whereas 22 genes were discovered in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus (Table S2).

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of FtAP2/ERFs

Using 71 FtAP2/ERFs and 145 AtAP2/ERFs sequences, a NJ phylogenetic tree was created in order to deduce the evolutionary relationships between A. thaliana and F. thunbergii (Fig. 1). Based on the established AtAP2/ERFs classification, the FtAP2/ERFs were categorized into four subfamilies: ERF, DREB, AP2, and RAV. There were 35 members of the ERF subfamily, 22 members of the DREB subfamily, 12 proteins with two AP2 domains that belonged to the AP2 subfamily, and two members of the RAV subfamily, which was identified by one AP2 and one B3 domain.

Conserved motif analysis of FtAP2/ERFs

An investigation of the conserved domain architecture of FtAP2/ERF was conducted on 71 proteins using MEME online tools, leading to the discovery of ten motifs (Fig. 2; Fig. S3). The findings indicated that genes within the same subfamily had almost comparable motif compositions and arrangements, while notable differences were observed among subfamilies. Motif 1, motif 2, and motif 3 were observed in nearly all genes, excluding those in the RAV and AP2 subfamilies. Notably, members of the RAV subfamily exclusively comprised motif 1 and motif 3. Some sequences of the A1, A4, and A5 subgroups within the DREB subfamily contained motif 8, and most A4 subgroup harboured motif 7. The AP2 subfamily exhibited a distinct motif profile compared to the other subfamilies. All AP2 subfamily members contained motif 3 and motif 5. Except for FtAP2/ERF-62, all AP2 proteins also harboured motif 1. Motif 6 was absent in FtAP2/ERF-62, FtAP2/ERF-64, and FtAP2/ERF-65. Eight AP2 subfamily members contained motif 4, four contained motif 9, and three contained motif 10. In summary, the FtAP2/ERF subfamilies exhibit both conserved and divergent motif features. Figure 2 illustrates the conserved domains of FtAP2/ERFs. All members of the FtAP2/ERFs contained an AP2 domain. Furthermore, RAV subfamily members had a B3 domain, while all AP2 subfamily members featured two AP2 domains.

FtAP2/ERF gene expression profiling in different tissues and stress responses

To infer the potential biological roles of the FtAP2/ERF gene family, we compared expression between bulb and leaf tissues of F. thunbergii. Among the 71 annotated FtAP2/ERF genes, 13 were detected exclusively in bulbs, while 24 were specific to leaves (Fig. 3A). Twenty genes, including FtAP2/ERF-11, -30, -47, -49, and − 61, were highly and stably expressed in both bulb and leaf tissues.

The FtAP2/ERF gene expression pattern. A The FtAP2/ERF gene expression patterns in different tissues. B The FtAP2/ERF gene expression patterns after treatment with 200 mM mannitol and 200 mM NaCl. The scale bar displayed the genes’ level of expression

To investigate the responses of FtAP2/ERFs to salt stress and drought stress, we treated F. thunbergii with 200 mM mannitol and 200 mM NaCl, subsequently measuring the expression of FtAP2/ERFs (Fig. 3B). Under salt stress, 37 genes were markedly up-regulated, while 21 genes were down-regulated. Notable up-regulated expression was observed for members including FtAP2/ERF-9, -20, -53, and − 61. Drought treatment elevated the expression of 39 genes, while repressing that of 22. A core set of 10 genes (FtAP2/ERF-9, -19, -20, -23, -24, -25, -53, -57, -61, and − 66) exhibited concordant up-regulation under both stresses, while that of 18 genes was consistently down-regulated. Conversely, 8 genes displayed opposite trends between salt and drought stresses. Notably, the expression of FtAP2/ERF-61 increased under both salt and drought stresses, and was present in both bulbs and leaves.

GO annotation of FtAP2/ERFs

A total of 71 FtAP2/ERFs were annotated, resulting in 15 GO terms categorized into 3 molecular functions, 2 cellular components, and 10 biological processes (Fig. 4A). According to molecular function analyses, all 71 genes were associated with DNA binding and transcription factor activity (GO:0003677), and sequence-specific DNA binding (GO:0003700), while 30 were related to transcription regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding (GO:0000976). Under cellular components, 71 genes were designated to the nucleus (GO:0005634) and 30 to integral component of membrane (GO:0016021). Under biological processes, most genes were anticipated to participate in stress responses and hormone signaling. Specifically, 16 genes were annotated to the ethylene-activated signaling pathway (GO:0009873), 16 to defence response (GO:0006952), 13 to jasmonic acid and ethylene-dependent systemic resistance (GO:0009861), and 12 to salicylic acid mediated signaling pathway (GO:0009863). In addition to the aforementioned annotations, 10 to the regulation of seed development (GO:0080050) and 11 to the regulation of flower development (GO:0009909), and 10 to regulation of pectin biosynthetic process (GO:1900030).

Correlation analysis between FtAP2/ERFs and hormone signaling-related genes

To preliminarily examine the prospective roles of FtAP2/ERFs in the ABA, JA and ETH signaling pathways, we analysed gene expression within these three hormonal signaling pathways in F. thunbergii under salt and drought stresses, aiming to assess the correlations between FtAP2/ERFs and genes associated with these three plant hormone signaling pathways. Three genes were found to be involved in the ABA transduction route (FtPYL, FtSnRK2, and FtABF) and four in the ABA biosynthesis pathway (FtZEP, FtNCED, FtABA3, and FtABA4), as illustrated in Fig. 4B and D and Fig. S4. In the JA signaling pathway, five genes (FtLOX, FtAOS, FtAOC, FtOPR3, and FtOPCL1) were related to JA biosynthesis, along with the other four (FtJAR1, FtCOI, FtJAZ, and FtMYC2). In the ETH synthesis and transduction pathways, three genes (FtSAM, FtASC5, and FtACO) were involved in ETH biosynthesis, while four (FtETR, FtCTR, FtEIN2, and FtEIN3) participate in ETH-induced responses. We selected 10 FtAP2/ERF genes (FtAP2/ERF-9, -19, -20, -23, -24, -25, -53, -57, -61, and − 66) with significantly up-regulated expression under both drought and salt treatments, and determined their correlations with genes from the three hormone signaling pathways. Correlation analysis revealed that FtAP2/ERF-61 exhibited statistically significant associations with multiple genes involved in ABA, JA, and ETH signaling pathways (Fig. 4B-D, Table S4). Among the 10 stress‑responsive FtAP2/ERFs, FtAP2/ERF-61 showed the highest average Spearman correlation coefficients with these hormone pathway genes (average r = 0.97 for ABA, 0.96 for JA, 0.96 for ETH). These co‑expression patterns suggest potential crosstalk between FtAP2/ERF-61 and these hormone signaling cascades under salt and drought stress conditions. Consequently, we selected FtAP2/ERF-61, which exhibited high expression in bulbs and leaves and was activated by salt and drought conditions, for further examination.

Identification and structural analysis of FtAP2/ERF-61

The CDS sequence of FtAP2/ERF-61 measures 1059 bp, encoding 352 amino acids, with a relative Mw of 39226.47 Da and pI of 8.59. The anticipated secondary structure consists of random coils, α-helices, and extended strands. These structures further bend and fold through various secondary bonds to form a specific tertiary structure (Fig. S5). The hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity of the FtAP2/ERF-61 protein were determined using ProtScale, which found that the hydrophilic regions were greater than the hydrophobic ones, showing that the protein is hydrophilic. Analysis of the signal peptide with SignalP-5.0 indicated that FtAP2/ERF-61 is a non-secretory protein with no signal peptide. Transmembrane structure analysis indicated that FtAP2/ERF-61 is a non-transmembrane protein. Subcellular localization of FtAP2/ERF-61 was determined through transient expression in N. benthamiana epidermal cells, and revealing that FtAP2/ERF-61 was localized in the nucleus (Fig. 5A). To assess whether FtAP2/ERF-61 possesses transcriptional activation activity, the recombinant pGBKT7-FtAP2/ERF-61 vector was transformed into yeast strain Y2H-Gold. As shown in Fig. 5B, yeast cells harboring pGBKT7-FtAP2/ERF-61 grew on SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade selective medium, similar to the positive control, whereas the negative control (pGBKT7) did not. This result indicates that FtAP2/ERF-61 possesses transcriptional activation activity. FtAP2/ERF-61’s expression pattern under hormone therapies was also identified and it reacted to ABA, JA, and ETH (Fig. 5C).

Stress analysis of FtAP2/ERF-61 in yeast transformants

To assess whether FtAP2/ERF-61 can enhance stress tolerance in a heterologous system, we overexpressed FtAP2/ERF-61 in the yeast strain GS115 (Fig. 6). In the control YPD medium, both the empty vector control group and the FtAP2/ERF-61-overexpressing transformants exhibited similar growth (Fig. 6A). However, under osmotic stress (1.5 M mannitol), the FtAP2/ERF-61 transformants displayed better growth than the control group, particularly at the 10− 1 dilution (Fig. 6B). Similarly, under salt stress (1.0 M NaCl), the survival rate of FtAP2/ERF-61 transformants was significantly higher than that of the control group (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that overexpression of FtAP2/ERF-61 enhances yeast tolerance to osmotic and salt stresses.

Validation of heterologous expression for the salt-tolerance function of FtAP2/ERF-61 in A. thaliana

To confirm the favorable effect of FtAP2/ERF-61 in stress adaptation, we generated independent 2 × 35 S::FtAP2/ERF-61 transgenic overexpression lines (OE-FtAP2/ERF-61#1 and OE-FtAP2/ERF-61#2) in A. thaliana, which harboured the full-length CDS of FtAP2/ERF-61. Using specific primers, we performed PCR identification on transgenic plants and amplified a 1,059 bp DNA fragment in the T2 generation. The gene expression revealed that, compared with the wild type (Col-0), the transcript level of FtAP2/ERF-61 in the leaves of the two overexpression lines was markedly elevated (Fig. S6).

The germination rate of the FtAP2/ERF-61 overexpression lines was much higher under salt stress than that of the Col-0; however, the overexpression lines’ development did not significantly differ from the Col-0 under normal conditions (Fig. 7A-B, Table S5). After being under salt stress for 7 d, the growth condition of Col-0 plants was inferior to that of the overexpression lines (Fig. 7C-D). To quantitatively evaluate growth performance under salt stress, we measured the shoot fresh weight of Col-0 and FtAP2/ERF-61 overexpression lines after NaCl treatment. Under normal conditions, no significant difference in fresh weight was observed among genotypes. However, under salt stress, the two overexpression lines (OE#1 and OE#2) exhibited 14.45% and 12.41% higher shoot fresh weight, respectively, compared with Col-0 (Fig. S7). DAB staining, which detects H2O2 accumulation, showed lighter brown coloration in overexpression line leaves than in Col-0 leaves under salt stress, indicating reduced H2O2 levels (Fig. 7E-F). Furthermore, we assessed the relative activity of APX and CAT in Col-0 and overexpression lines (Fig. 7G-H). Under standard conditions, the growth of the overexpression lines exhibited no significant difference compared to Col-0. Under salt stress, the APX activity of the overexpression system was significantly higher than that of Col-0, increasing by 1.85-fold, and CAT increased by 0.48-fold. In comparison to Col-0, the levels of H2O2 and MDA in the overexpression lines under salt stress diminished by approximately 16.61% and 24.15%, respectively (Fig. 7I-J). The findings demonstrate that the enhanced salt tolerance of A. thaliana resulting from overexpression of FtAP2/ERF-61 is contingent upon its capacity to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS).

Discussion

Fritillaria thunbergii is a highly valued traditional and precious Chinese medicinal herb with extremely high medicinal value [24]. Important new information about the biosynthesis of bioactive chemicals and the regulatory networks governing F. thunbergii’s growth and stress responses has been made possible by multi-omics research [25]. Cloning and identifying potential genes linked to significant characteristics are vital for genetic advancement, new variety breeding, and research on medicinal and nutritional properties.

Identification and classification characteristics of the FtAP2/ERFs

71 F. thunbergii AP2/ERFs were successfully identified through HMM profiles (PF00847) and homology searches in this study. The findings indicate that AP2/ERFs display a comparable distribution pattern in additional plant species. The AP2/ERFs have been identified and examined in many species. The quantity of AP2/ERF members differs across several species, including A. thaliana (145), Oryza sativa (163), Zea mays (214), Brachypodium distachyon (141), Fagopyrum tataricum (134), Panax ginseng (318), Salvia miltiorrhiza (170), Coptis chinensis (96), and Adiantum nelumboides (163) [8, 15, 17, 34, 38–41]. The evolutionary tree of AtAP2/ERFs (Fig. 1) categorizes the FtAP2/ERFs into subfamilies: Soloist, AP2, DREB, RAV, and ERF. The ERF subfamily in F. thunbergii is the most extensive, aligning with research findings from other species [42–44]. Prior studies indicated that the membership of the AP2/ERF superfamily remains largely constant, exhibiting variations in gene counts across the DREB and ERF subfamilies within identical species. The RAV subfamily is a small group with a limited number of members [45–48]. The findings imply a degree of evolutionary conservation within the AP2/ERFs across several species.

The structural domains and motifs of TFs are typically associated with their DNA binding, protein–protein interactions, and transcriptional activity [49]. The nature and quantity of TF motifs dictate their function. In Actinidia eriantha, 10 motifs were identified among 158 AP2/ERFs [50]. All AP2/ERFs, with the exception of AeAP2/ERF032, possessed either motif 1 or motif 2. Similarly, out of 135 AP2/ERFs in Trichosanthes kirilowii, 10 motifs were identified, with motifs 1, 2, and 7 being conserved in most TkAP2/ERF proteins [35]. This investigation detected ten motifs in 71 FtAP2/ERFs, with motifs 1, 2, and 3 found in the majority of members (Fig. 2). The quantity of motifs differed across several subfamilies, potentially linked to their distinct activities.

Motif 1 encompasses the RAYD element, perhaps implicated in protein-DNA or protein-protein interactions owing to the amphipathic characteristics of its core α-helix [51]. Two different bases inside motif 2 are the primary distinction between the DREB and ERF subfamilies [52]. It is possible for motif 2 of the ERF subfamily to bind very specifically to the GC-box element that is located in the promoter regions of target genes when it is stimulated by abiotic stimuli. Conversely, motif 2 of the DREB subfamily can only bind to the DRE/CRT element and the A/GCCGAC element. These elements are connected to drought and cold reactions, respectively, and play a part in how plants deal with stress [53]. Certain genes within the AP2 subfamily possess two instances of motif 1, capable of binding to the GCAC(A/G)N(A/T)TCCC(A/G)ANG(C/T) motif and contributing to plant growth [54]. These findings indicate that the diverse patterns across several subfamilies imply they may fulfill unique functions in plants.

Expression patterns and functions of FtAP2/ERFs

The expression patterns of FtAP2/ERF genes in various organs and under various therapies have been clarified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3). Twenty genes demonstrated significantly elevated expression levels in both tissues, indicating their conserved roles in essential physiological processes of F. thunbergii. Regarding abiotic stress responses, certain genes dramatically rose under salt and drought treatments, whereas those of others were markedly reduced. Moreover, certain genes exhibited patterns of expression that were either comparable to or different from one another in response to salt and drought treatments. In the presence of abiotic stressors, such as osmotic stress, excessive salt, and drought, it is believed that AP2/ERFs play a vital role [55]. The expression of CqERF24 in quinoa was increased in both roots and leaves. CqERF24 overexpression in Arabidopsis increased the transgenic plants’ resistance to drought, while the silencing of CqERF24 in quinoa diminished drought resistance [56]. In maize, ZmEREB57 overexpression lines demonstrated enhanced salt tolerance, whereas CRISPR/Cas9 knockdown lines revealed decreased salt tolerance [56]. Our analysis revealed that numerous FtAP2/ERF genes exhibited responsiveness to salt and drought conditions, underscoring their significant functions in this species’ reactions to abiotic stress. The expression of FtAP2/ERF-61 was elevated under both saline and dry conditions. The fact that the expression patterns of the FtAP2/ERFs are strongly connected with the plant’s capacity to adapt to changes in its environment provides evidence that this gene family plays an important part in the way that F. thunbergii reacts to abiotic challenges.

GO annotation analysis yielded functional predictions for the 71 FtAP2/ERF genes from three aspects (Fig. 4). In terms of their molecular functions, the majority of the genes were found to be associated with DNA binding and the activity of TFs, which indicates that they play key roles in the control of gene transcription. Following an examination of the cellular components, it was discovered that these genes were situated in the nucleus, which is consistent with the functional localization of TFs. According to the examination of biological processes, the majority of the genes were implicated in hormonal and stress responses, including the ETH-activated signaling pathway, defense mechanisms, and responses to salt stress. This provides more evidence of the FtAP2/ERF gene family’s versatility in plant responses to hormone signaling and environmental stresses [57].

The results of correlation analysis between FtAP2/ERF genes and genes related to three pathways (ETH, ABA, JA) showed that 10 FtAP2/ERF genes were connected to hormone signaling pathway genes. These genes also exhibited significant up-regulation under salt and drought treatments. Hormones play a pivotal part in the salt signal cascade during plant salt stress resistance [58]. ABA functions as a primary regulator in how plants react to unfavorable environments, including drought and salinity stress [59]. ABA, for instance, can increase AtCBF4 expression. By blocking AtXERICO, AtCBF4/DREB1D reduces reactions to ABA, osmotic stress, and drought stress while promoting stomatal development [60]. The CaERF14 gene in pepper augments resistance to elevated salinity and drought by modulating the genes associated with ABA production and sensitivity [61]. Overexpression of ZmEREB20 in Arabidopsis delays seed germination under salt stress, increases ABA sensitivity, and controls the expression of genes linked to ABA and gibberellin [62]. There is a close connection between JA, a plant growth regulator, and the resistance of plants to stress [63]. Through the enhancement of the antioxidant enzymes and the levels of osmoregulatory substances, it may minimize plant stress damage [64]. In maize, ZmEREB57 contributes to salt tolerance via modulating the JA signaling pathway and 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid. ZmEREB57 overexpression lines demonstrate enhanced salt tolerance, while CRISPR/Cas9 knockdown lines display diminished salt tolerance [65]. Ethylene is an essential plant hormone that has a considerable impact on the development, and responses of plants to various stresses or conditions [66]. The GmERF3 in soybean improves plant stress tolerance via overexpression by modulating the expression of many genes within the ETH signaling pathway [67]. This study demonstrated that FtAP2/ERF-61 had strong association coefficients with genes associated with three plant hormone signaling pathways: ETH, ABA, and JA. This suggests that by participating in plant hormone signaling pathways, FtAP2/ERF-61 may alter how plants react to abiotic stressors, providing important new information for its functional research.

Characteristics and functional verification of FtAP2/ERF-61

Currently, research on the AP2/ERFs in F. thunbergii is limited. We found and cloned the FtAP2/ERF-61 gene. We examined FtAP2/ERF-61’s hydrophilicity, signal peptide, and transmembrane protein features to prepare for further research into its role in stress resilience. Like other ERF proteins, FtAP2/ERF-61 has an AP2/ERF domain, which shows that it can control several downstream genes. Subcellular localisation and transactivation activity analyses support the nuclear localisation and transcriptional activation capacity of FtAP2/ERF-61 (Fig. 5). This is consistent with its role as a TF that regulates gene expression. In many plant species, ERF proteins are considered key regulators of various aspects of development and the response to stress [42]. According to our research, stressors such as drought and salinity, as well as the presence of ETH, ABA, and JA, increased the expression of FtAP2/ERF-61. This suggests that FtAP2/ERF-61 is involved in stress response pathways and could help explain F. thunbergii’s resilience to stress.

The function of FtAP2/ERF-61 in stress reactions was shown by the stress analysis results of yeast transformants, which showed that overexpression of FtAP2/ERF-61 improved yeast tolerance to salinity and drought (Fig. 6). Further heterologous expression verification in Arabidopsis showed that plants overexpressing FtAP2/ERF-61 exhibited stronger resistance under drought and salt stresses, as evidenced by improved germination rates, better root growth, and reduced water loss rates (Figs. 7 and 8). Our research results indicate that overexpression of FtAP2/ERF-61 in Arabidopsis could increase the activities of CAT and APX while reducing the level of H2O2, thereby endowing transgenic Arabidopsis with enhanced salt tolerance and better osmoregulatory capacity (Figs. 7 and 8). Salinity stress induces an overproduction of ROS, resulting in oxidative stress in plants [68, 69]. The three main enzymes that make up the plant antioxidant system are peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase [70]. Superoxide anions (O2⁻) can be converted by SOD, whereas H2O2 can be scavenged by the enzymes CAT and POD [71, 72]. APX, a well-known H2O2-scavenging guardian, works in concert with other enzymes to maintain the homeostasis of free radical levels in plants [73]. Researchers demonstrated that ERF3 causes stress responses to be upregulated, which lowers ROS levels and raises proline and soluble carbohydrate contents, improving salt tolerance [67]. ERF3 increases proline and chlorophyll levels, activates stress-responsive genes, and decreases stomatal conductance and H2O2 concentration in wheat to promote salt and drought stress responses [28]. Likewise, overexpression of the transgenic tobacco GmERF3 increases the amount of soluble carbohydrates, which increases tobacco’s resilience to aridity and salinity [67]. This research sheds even more light on the significant role that AP2/ERFs play in the development of plant responses to salinity and drought conditions.

In addition, after being subjected to stress, the transcriptional levels of genes that are associated with the scavenging of ROS and the response to stress were dramatically increased in plants that overexpressed FtAP2/ERF-61 (Fig. 9). This supports the idea that FtAP2/ERF-61 gives plants stress tolerance by adjusting how these genes are expressed. Researchers demonstrated that overexpressing CqERF24 in Arabidopsis increases the drought tolerance of transgenic plants. This is accomplished by raising the activities of antioxidant enzymes and activating genes associated with stress [55]. The suppression of SmERF1 results in a large increase in the sensitivity of plants to salt stress, a considerable reduction in the transcriptional expression of marker genes connected to salt stress defense, and a decrease in the activities of catalase and superoxide dismutase [18]. The findings demonstrate that AP2/ERF markedly improves plant stress resistance by modulating the expression of genes associated with ROS scavenging and stress response. In conclusion, it can be concluded that FtAP2/ERF-61 enhances plant resistance to salt and drought by triggering stress-responsive genes and scavenging ROS through the antioxidant enzyme system.

Future perspectives

It should be noted that the analysis of the FtAP2/ERF gene family in this study was based on transcriptomic data rather than whole‑genome data. Due to the unavailability of a publicly accessible reference genome for F. thunbergii, we were unable to perform analyses such as chromosomal localization, genome‑wide synteny analysis, cis‑acting element analysis in promoter regions, or Ka/Ks selection pressure analysis. These analyses are of great significance for a deeper understanding of the evolutionary mechanisms and expansion patterns of the AP2/ERF gene family. With the ongoing genome sequencing of F. thunbergii nearing completion, the above analyses will be performed in the future. Nevertheless, the phylogenetic analysis, conserved motif identification, and expression profiling of tissue specificity and stress responses, all based on transcriptomic data in this study, still provide important preliminary insights into the characteristics and functions of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii. We also acknowledge that the amplification efficiency of qRT-PCR primers was not systematically validated. Although all primer pairs were designed with similar amplicon lengths and annealing temperatures, and melting curves confirmed single specific products, we will include amplification efficiency data in future studies to further ensure data reliability.

Conclusion

This study provided the comprehensive characterization of the AP2/ERF gene family in F. thunbergii, identified a total of 71 FtAP2/ERFs classified into five subfamilies with conserved motif compositions and stress‑responsive expression patterns. This study discovered that FtAP2/ERF‑61 was a nucleus‑localized transcriptional activator that responded to drought, salt, and multiple hormones. Functional validation further demonstrated that FtAP2/ERF‑61 enhanced drought and salt tolerance in yeast and Arabidopsis by upregulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes, reducing the accumulation of H2O2 and MDA, and activating stress‑responsive genes. These findings not only fill a critical gap in understanding of stress responses in F. thunbergii but also provide a valuable genetic resource for molecular breeding of stress‑tolerant medicinal plants.
