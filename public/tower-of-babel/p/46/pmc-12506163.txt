Agronomic parameters and drought tolerance indices of bread wheat genotypes as influenced by well-watered and water deficit conditions
Authors: Md Abu Hena Sorwar Jahan, Md Golam Azam, Mohammed Mohi-Ud-Din, Md Motiar Rohman, Ferdousi Begum, Md Hafizul Haque Khan, Istiak Ahmed, Md Noor Alam Chowdhury, Md Shamsul Alam, Md Robiul Alam, Mohammad Kamrul Hasan, Tasnim Zerin Khan, Ahmed Gaber, Viliam Bárek, Marian Brestic, Akbar Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA primary threat to food security stems from the expanding global population and climate change, which have increased the frequency of droughts. Owing to shifting climatic conditions, abiotic stresses such as severe drought are intensifying, reducing wheat productivity. This study aimed to evaluate the response of elite drought-tolerant wheat genotypes to water deficit stress by analysing agronomic and physio-biochemical traits, with the goal of identifying promising genotypes for breeding.MethodsTwenty wheat genotypes sourced from various national and international drought-tolerant nurseries, including a benchmark variety, were tested under water deficit and well-watered conditions over two consecutive years. The data collected included agronomic traits such as plant height (PH), days to heading (DH), days to anthesis (DA), days to physiological maturity (DPM), canopy temperature, SPAD values at different growth stages, intercepted photosynthetically active radiation above the canopy (IPARAC) and on the ground (IPAR OG), yield stability index (YSI), stress tolerance index (STI), stress index (SI), leaf area index (LAI), spike length (SL), grains per spike (GPS), 1000-grain weight (TSW), grain yield (GY; t/ha), and biomass yield (BY; t/ha).ResultsTo streamline the study, two years of aggregated data were analysed for each parameter. Drought tolerance was assessed based on grain yield, and multitrait genotype‒ideotype distance (MGIDI) indices were employed to select drought-tolerant wheat genotypes. Significant differences were observed among the wheat genotypes across all measured parameters under both conditions. Under normal conditions, correlation analysis revealed that grain yield (GY) and biomass yield (BY) had the strongest positive relationship (r = 0.75**), followed by TSW, LAI, GPS, SL, PH, DPM, and DA. In contrast, under water deficit stress, BY exhibited a notable correlation with plant height (PH) (r = 0.42). Under both irrigated and water deficit stress situations, GY had positive and substantial correlations with PH, DA, DPM, GPS, SL, the STI, and the YSI. Two of the ten main components (PCs) accounted for 52.3% and 50.4% of the overall variation under water deficit and well-watered conditions, respectively. Additionally, the genotypes were separated into three clusters via a cluster heatmap, and the most tolerant genotypes (E38, E40, E41, E35, and E33) were found to be in cluster 3, which revealed their genetic relatedness. Genotypes E9 and E29 were found to be sensitive to water deficit, whereas genotypes E40, E38, and E35 were drought tolerant, according to tolerance indices.ConclusionPlant breeders may find the MGIDI useful for selecting genotypes on the basis of a variety of characteristics because it is a straightforward and robust selection method. Among the 20 wheat genotypes, the most stable and productive were E38, E30, E35, E40, and E34, according to an analysis of MGIDI for diverse settings. This was likely caused by the high MPS (mean performance and stability) of specific traits under different situations. The features that have been identified can be used as genitors in hybridization procedures to create wheat breeding materials that are resistant to drought. The genotypes and features that were found to be drought tolerant could be used to create new genotypes that are resistant to drought stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07355-3.Keywords: Water deficit stress, Correlation analysis, Principal component analysis, Variance components, Cluster heatmap, Tolerance indices, Wheat

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L., 2n = 6x = 42, AABBDD), a vital global commodity crop, underpins numerous value chains. It ranks as the second most widely cultivated cereal crop after rice, with a global annual output exceeding 761 million tons [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa, its role as a staple food has increased significantly due to socioeconomic factors such as urbanization, an increasing population, increasing per capita income, and changing lifestyles [2, 3]. In Bangladesh, which is characterized by a subtropical climate with wet summers and dry winters, wheat is cultivated during the precipitation-free winter season. Cultivation is limited to a few regions with irrigation facilities. Consequently, abiotic stresses such as drought, particularly during the grain-filling stage, are considered the primary cause of reduced grain weight, significantly contributing to the decline in wheat yields in Bangladesh.

Rainfed and irrigated methods are the primary approaches for growing wheat [4]. The yield potential of wheat grown in rainfed systems is constrained by various abiotic factors, particularly heat and water shortages [5]. For example, Zhang et al. [6] reported that drought conditions led to a reduction in grain yield ranging from 30 to 62.75%. Zhang et al. [7] noted that annual yield improvements in key wheat-producing nations such as China, the United States, Mexico, and Turkey are minimal, hovering approximately 1%. In contrast, countries such as Canada, Iran, and Argentina have experienced no increase in wheat yields [8]. Furthermore, wheat yields under drought stress are significantly lower than those under optimal conditions [9], a trend attributed to the reduced production capacity caused by poor crop performance in water-scarce environments. As a result, newly bred wheat varieties face substantial yield declines when cultivated under drought stress [10].

Drought, a major abiotic stress, significantly hinders global agricultural productivity and output, especially in arid and semiarid regions, and poses a serious threat to food security worldwide [11]. It severely impacts wheat production, with potential yields decreasing by 50–90% under dry conditions [12]. During the seedling phase, drought restricts wheat development [13], whereas water shortages during the grain-filling stage sharply decrease grain output [14]. Numerous global studies have confirmed that drought stress substantially diminishes wheat yields. However, traditional crop breeding methods, which target specific agronomic traits, significantly increase wheat productivity in both well-watered and drought-affected environments. Several studies worldwide indicate that drought stress dramatically reduces wheat yields. Drought is one of the most significant natural occurrences that threatens agricultural productivity [15, 16]. To ensure that there is enough food for the growing population during drought circumstances, certain measures should be taken to increase agricultural productivity [17]. FAO report projected that climate change would cause yields of staple crops to decline of 5% by 2050 compared to 2012 [18] To ensure the food security for the growing population during drought circumstances, certain measures should be taken to increase agricultural productivity. Traditional crop breeding methods, which target specific agronomic traits, significantly increased wheat productivity in both well-watered and drought-affected environments.

To assess genotype stability across both stressful and non-stressful conditions, the yield stability index (YSI) was calculated via the approach outlined by Bouslama and Schapaugh [19]. Bidinger et al. [20] introduced the drought response index (DRI) to identify genotypes that are either susceptible or resilient to drought, accounting for yield under drought stress while factoring in variations in flowering times and potential yields in irrigated environments to highlight drought resistance traits. Fernandez [21] developed the stress tolerance index (STI) to classify genotypes based on their performance across different conditions. Gavuzzi et al. [22] proposed the yield index (YI) to evaluate a genotype’s performance under stress relative to the average yield. These indices have been extensively applied to select drought-resistant wheat genotypes [23, 24].

Several statistical methods have already been established to determine and measure the impacts of genotypes and environments by using multiple biological events [25]. Selecting the optimal genotypes may occasionally be challenging because of the principal obstacle of stating the pecuniary worth of traits and altering them into rational profitable increments [26]. Olivoto and Nardino [27] created the multitrait genotype–ideotype MGIDI, which is based on factorial analysis and genotype–ideotype distance, to seize this restriction. The crucial goal of this index is to select advanced genotypes once several attributes have been evaluated. Furthermore, by providing better techniques and genotype selection, MGIDI may facilitate breeding efforts [28].

Owing to the complexity of drought and its interactions with other abiotic stresses, such as heat or varying soil conditions, few detailed studies have fully measured its impact on agriculture. It is crucial to categorize drought stress using scientifically recognized metrics like water potential, matric potential, and field capacity when screening wheat genotypes for drought resistance. According to Taiz [29], severe stress occurs at or below − 1.5 MPa, whereas drought is often described at values below − 0.5 MPa. Water potential (Ψw) represents the energy status of water in the plant-soil system. As soil dries, metric potential (Ψm), which measures the force holding water in the soil matrix, becomes more negative, which has a direct impact on plants’ access to water [30]. In order to imitate moderate to severe drought conditions during genotype assessment, soil moisture is kept between 30 and 50% of field capacity (FC) and permanent wilting point (PWP), which are also frequently used to designate drought levels [31]. For a precise evaluation of genotype performance, these metrics provide uniform, repeatable, and physiologically appropriate drought stress categorization.

To recommend suitable genotypes for cultivation under water deficit and heat stress conditions, an overall understanding of the genetic basis and plant responses to these stresses and their interactions with the environment is required. To achieve these goals, MGIDI was utilized to recognize abiotic-stress-tolerant [32]. This classification approach has proven effective in identifying high-yielding genotypes across diverse conditions in multiple crop species [33–35]. To evaluate genotypes on the basis of agronomic traits and biomass distribution under both drought-stressed and non-stressed conditions for production and breeding purposes, this study examined 20 wheat genotypes under rainfed and irrigated conditions during the Rabi season.

Materials and methods

Genetic components

This study assessed twenty distinct wheat genotypes, including the control variety Prodip, under both irrigated (control) and water-scarce conditions (drought was induced by withholding irrigation). The seeds of these advanced wheat genotypes were developed by the Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute and the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Centre (CIMMYT) via conventional breeding, mutation, and biotechnological methods. These genotypes present various genetic origins. A detailed list of the wheat genotypes, their suppliers, and pedigrees is presented in Table S1.

Soil and the experimental site

The study took place at the experimental field of the Regional Wheat Research Center (RWRC), Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute, located in Joydebpur, Gazipur, Bangladesh, over two consecutive wheat-growing seasons. The site is 8.4 m above sea level at coordinates of 24.09°N latitude and 90.26°E longitude within the Madhupur Tract agroecological zone (AEZ-28). The experiment was carried out during the winter period from November to April. Three soil samples were collected during the study season, and their physicochemical properties were analysed via the standardization methods of Page [36] and Klute [37]. With a bulk density of 0.89 to 15 g cm-3, a permanent wilting point of 8.05%, and a field capacity of 30.1%, the soil is classified as acidic. The soil has a modest amount of organic matter (0.61). Prior to the experiment, the initial state (Table 1) of the experimental soil was determined by studying its physical and chemical characteristics down to a depth of 15 cm.

Details of the design and treatment

The investigation was carried out in the Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute’s (BARI) experimental field in Joydebpur, Gazipur, Bangladesh. Two treatments, well-watered (irrigated) and water deficit (drought), were used in the randomized complete block design (RCBD) experiment. Using a single Coulter hand drill, seeds were seeded in rice fallow Land in sequence with rice crops into four rows 2.5 m in length, separated by 20 cm, and reproduced three times. Since the plants were grown under accessible soil moisture under irrigated conditions, the only source of moisture was the residual moisture of the soil, which was measured at depths of 1–15 cm at 14.5% and 16–30 cm at 15.9%. Table 3 provides information on rainfall during the crop-growing season. In contrast, irrigated (control) plots received three irrigations during the crop season during the crown root initiation, tillering, and grain filling stages. The control plots received enough water in accordance with customary culture practices. To prevent precipitation, the experiment was carried out beneath a rainout cover. To mitigate the impact of groundwater logging during water deficit conditions, three boundary rows of plants were created.

Crop husbandry

The research field was levelled by meticulously harrowing and ladding it before being ploughed with a motorized tiller. The main plots, subplots, and blocks were then marked by hand removal of weeds and remnants from past crops. Fertilizers containing nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), sulfur (S), zinc (Zn), and boron (B) were applied at rates of 100–60–40–20–5–1 kg ha−1 in the forms of urea (46% N), triple superphosphate (50% P2O5), a potash mixture (60% K2O), gypsum (18% S), and boric acid (17% B), respectively. All fertilizers, except for one-third of the nitrogen, were thoroughly incorporated into the soil as the initial dose. The remaining nitrogen was split into two equal portions for application 20 and 50 days after sowing (DAS). Wheat seeds of all the genotypes were sown in rows spaced 20 cm apart at a rate of 120 kg ha−1. Shortly after planting, all the plots received light irrigation to promote uniform germination and seedling growth. Throughout the growing season, the unirrigated plots were kept dry and protected from rainfall by a rainout shelter (made of transparent polythene sheeting) to simulate water-scarce conditions. In contrast, the irrigated plots were watered three times (at 21, 50, and 70 DAS). For up to 15 days after seeding, precautions were taken against the birds. Thirty-five days after sowing, the first weeding was performed, and fifty-five days later, the last was performed. Thirty days after sowing, the fungicide Tilt 250 EC was applied often at 15-day intervals to manage disease and keep the crop free of weeds. As needed, additional multicultural activities were conducted in accordance with the accepted norms.

Data collection

At different points during the crop cycle, information on yield and yield components was gathered. From the date of sowing until 50% of the heads had fully emerged from the flag leaf, the DH was noted. The number of days between the date of planting and the point at which 90% of the plants achieved harvest maturity was known as the days to physical maturity, or DPM. The plant height (PH) of five randomly selected plants was measured in centimeters from the soil surface to the top of the spike. A PAR Ceftometer (LP-80, Decagon device, USA) was used to measure physiological parameters such as intercepted photosynthetically active radiation (IPAR) on a bright, sunny day at anthesis. An infrared thermometer (Model-LT-300, USA) was used to measure the canopy temperature seven days prior to anthesis, during anthesis, and seven days following anthesis. A portable SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Minolta Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was used to measure the SPAD-chlorophyll value by evaluating the chlorophyll content of ten randomly selected plants from each treatment. Measurements were taken at three stages—seven days before anthesis, during anthesis, and seven days after anthesis—and the final SPAD-Chlorophyll value was calculated by averaging readings from the flag leaf at three points: the base, middle, and tip. From the randomly tagged plants across all the genotypes, five spikes were selected, and the number of grains per spike (GPS) was counted and averaged for each genotype. To determine the 1000-grain weight (TGW), 200 seeds from each genotype were counted, weighed in grams via an electric balance, and multiplied by five. The GY was assessed by harvesting grain from the two central rows of each plot, sun-drying it, weighing it in grams, and converting the result to kilograms per hectare on the basis of plot size. For biological yield, three randomly chosen plants per plot were individually weighed before threshing via an electric balance, and their average biomass yield was calculated in grams.

The relative water content (RWC) was determined via the method outlined by Bannister [38] and was calculated via the formula RWC = [(FW - DW)/(TW - DW)] × 100, where FW represents fresh weight, DW denotes dry weight, and TW indicates total weight.

Three indices for drought tolerance and susceptibility were computed for each genotype on the basis of grain yield under water deficit and irrigated conditions, and a specific formula was used to distinguish between genotypes that are tolerant and susceptible to water deficit.

The yield stability index (YSI) was calculated as Ys/Yp [19].

The stress tolerance index (STI) was calculated as Yp × Ys/YP2 [20].

Stress intensity (SI) % = 1 - (YS/YP) × 100.

Here, Yp = yield of the cultivar under normal conditions, Ys = yield of the cultivar under stress conditions, YP = total yield mean under normal conditions, and YS = total yield mean under stress conditions.

In Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Varimax rotation is a statistical technique used to simplify the interpretation of the principal components. While PCA identifies components that explain the maximum variance in the data, the original component loadings may be difficult to interpret due to complex variable associations. Varimax rotation is an orthogonal rotation method that maximizes the variance of squared loadings within each component, resulting in a more distinct structure where each variable tends to load strongly on only one component [39].

This rotation does not change the total variance explained but redistributes the variance among the components to achieve a more interpretable solution. In this study, Varimax rotation was used to clarify the contribution of specific traits to each principal component, thereby helping to identify trait groupings and their relationship with drought tolerance more effectively.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test [40] to validate the assumptions required for analysis of variance. The homogeneity of variances was confirmed with Levene’s test [41] and Bartlett’s test [42]. Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test was applied to detect significant differences between means at the 5% significance level. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated via R version 4.2.0 [43] with the corrplot method [44] to explore relationships between the traits studied, and their significance was evaluated via a t test at the 5% level. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in R, which is based on the correlation matrix, to identify key traits under water deficit and irrigated conditions, retaining principal components (PCs) with eigenvalues exceeding 1. PCA biplots were generated separately for drought and irrigated scenarios via the factoextra package [45] in R. The multitrait genotype–ideotype distance index (MGIDI) [27] was used to determine the top-performing genotype for each environment, requiring meticulous planning for each trait’s application. Data processing and index calculations were performed via the metan 1.12.0 package in R version 4.2.0 [46].

The influence of soil water deficit stress may explain why heading was discovered earlier in the water deficit plots than in the irrigated plots. DA varied between 69 and 83 days for each genotype and was significant in both circumstances (Table S2). Antheses may be a forced reproductive system because they occur earlier in water deficit conditions than in irrigated plots.

All E8 genotypes reached to maturity (DM) between 103 and 105 DAS when there wasn’t enough water, but this wasn’t important when the plants were watered (Table S2). Spikes/m² showed significant change under water shortage but not under irrigation (Table S3). Biological yield (BY) under stress was best in E41 (9.36 t/ha), followed by E40, E33, and E8. No major changes in BY were found under irrigation, possibly due to uniformly favorable growing conditions. SPAD values, showing chlorophyll content, changed significantly under water deficit at 65 DAS (41.50–65.40) and 80 DAS (41.47–51.13), but not at 57 DAS or under irrigation (Tables S4 & S5). These differences were likely genotype dependent.

Index of stress susceptibility (SSI)

Smaller SSI values imply superior stress tolerance, preferring genotypes with steady yields under both stress and non-stress environments. Genotypes E7, E9, E39, E41, and E43 had low SSI values (≤ 1.00), demonstrating decreased susceptibility to stress (Table S4). Genotype E40 exhibited the greatest STI (> 1.00), followed by Prodip, E30, E8, E35, E9, and E23, exhibiting outstanding yield performance under both circumstances (Table S5). Similarly, E9, E39, E41, and E43 preserved ~ 80% yield under stress, as evidenced by their YSI values.

Based on breeder selection and field data, genotypes E8 and E39 fared well under both irrigated and stress situations. Stress lowered grain production by 17–46%, with an average decline of ~ 35% under water deficiency (Table S5). Overall, genotypes Prodip, E8, E9, E23, E30, and E40 were chosen for high yields under both circumstances (STI > 1.00), whereas E9, E39, E41, and E43 (YSI > 100%) and E7, E9, E39, E41, and E43 (SSI < 1.00) exhibited great stability and stress tolerance. STI-selected genotypes are appropriate for drought-prone locations, whereas YSI and SSI-selected genotypes offer potential for use in breeding initiatives aiming at generating drought-tolerant, high-yielding wheat varieties.

Multitrait Genotype–Ideotype distance index (MGIDI)

The MGIDI was employed to identify the best wheat genotype by considering all evaluated traits. Traits are standardized via BLUP to estimate genotype means, followed by factor analysis, and the distance from each genotype to the ideotype is calculated. Examining the factors affecting traits during genotype selection is essential for understanding the results of multienvironment trials. Figures 7A & B show the genotype values for the MGIDI, assuming a 15% selection intensity. E38, E30, E35, E40, E34, and one released variation prodip were the five advanced lines chosen (Fig. 7A & B).

The cut-off point is shown by the MGIDI value of 5.8 (red circle in Fig. 7). Prodip genotypes were more closely aligned with the red circle, which may help explain desirable traits. It would be valuable to explore the performance of genotypes closest to the baseline in future studies, particularly those near the threshold, to gain deeper insights in subsequent research.

The 20 wheat genotypes were categorized using clustering and the MGIDI (Multi-Trait Genotype-Ideotype Distance Index) analysis according to how well they performed in well-watered and water-deficient environments. It’s interesting to note that both approaches consistently identified a number of common genotypes specifically, E30, E35, E38, E40, and E34 as high-performing and stable across contexts. The trustworthiness of the results is reinforced by the consistency of these genotypes across two separate analytical techniques, one based on ideotype-based selection and the other on multivariate clustering of features. Additionally, it demonstrates how resilient these genotypes are to fluctuating moisture levels. They are excellent prospects for next breeding initiatives and multi-environment experiments because of their recurrent selection, which indicates that they have a desirable mix of features that contribute to both yield potential and drought tolerance.

Discussion

Abiotic stressors significantly impact crop production and productivity by inducing unfavourable environmental changes [47]. Among these factors, water deficit stress is a major abiotic factor that adversely affects wheat yield and output globally. Water deficit stress, exacerbated by climate change, is one of the most detrimental factors limiting wheat growth and productivity [48, 49]. It is one of the most harmful weather-related stressors, causing severe damage to wheat, with the extent of crop losses depending on the growth stage and duration of the stress. Water deficit stress impacts more than 42% of wheat yield [50]. The development of genotypes adapted to a wide range of environmental conditions is likely the most effective strategy for mitigating the risks associated with abiotic stressors [51]. Therefore, breeding and selecting water deficit-resistant genotypes is essential to address this challenge. In this study, we evaluated the resilience and performance of 20 wheat genotypes under diverse conditions via ANOVA, correlation analysis, and the MGIDI, which is based on 10 traits and 10 water deficit tolerance indices.

Yield is a complicated and multifaceted feature that is linked to several other related criteria. When these influencing characteristics are properly taken into account, selection will be successful. If so, selection on the basis solely of yield will not be considered valuable [52]. For every observed attribute, the genotypes differed significantly according to the combined analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Table 4).

Significant genetic diversity in terms of wheat production and yield components under both drought-stressed and nonstressed conditions has been reported in field studies by Mwadzingeni [53] and Mathew et al. [54]. For the successful development of wheat cultivars adaptable to various stress conditions, it is essential that the tested genotypes exhibit valuable genetic variability for traits associated with water deficit tolerance. The overall mean performance of the wheat genotypes for the examined agronomic traits under the two moisture regimes is summarized in Tables 5 and 6 and Table S1. The results of this study highlight the substantial impact of water stress on yield and related agronomic traits. However, under moisture stress conditions, certain promising wheat genotypes resistant to water deficit present relatively high grain yields [55, 56]. Box plot analysis revealed the adverse effects of water deficit stress on grain yield and yield-related traits in wheat genotypes (Fig. 1). Water deficit stress negatively influenced all thirteen traits, with biological yield (BY) and grain yield (GY) experiencing significant reductions of 38.7% and 33.32%, respectively (Fig. 1). In summary, wheat yield is highly vulnerable to water deficit stress, indicating that such stress may affect wheat production by altering yield component traits (e.g., BY and GY) at various growth stages. Despite advancements in traditional plant breeding, many commercial wheat cultivars remain sensitive to water deficit [57, 58]. Heritability estimates ranged from moderate (41.5%, GY) to high (94.4%, DH), reflecting differences in the genetic control of various traits. These varying heritability estimates suggest that different traits require distinct breeding strategies for improvement. Similar heritability estimates (ranging from 30 to 95%) for agronomic traits in wheat have been documented in previous studies [54, 59]. The high heritability of these traits underscores their importance for enhancing grain yield (GY) and water deficit tolerance in wheat.

High-potential genotypes that performed well under both water deficit stress and nonstress conditions were identified based on the positive and significant correlation of yield in both environments. Mursalova [60] reported that certain genotypes presented strong positive correlations between grain yield under water deficit stress and nonstress conditions. These findings suggest that yield-related indices, such as PH (plant height), DA (days to anthesis), DPM (days to physiological maturity), GPP (grains/plant), SL (spike length), STI (stress tolerance index), and YSI (yield stability index), are effective criteria for selecting water deficit-resistant genotypes, as they present significant (p < 0.001) correlations with grain yield (GY) in both irrigated and water deficit environments [61]. According to Mursalova et al. [60] and Ayalew [62], these indices are highly effective for identifying superior genotypes across varying water availability conditions. Additionally, grain yield under nonstress conditions was significantly positively correlated with the water deficit stress susceptibility index. This implies that selecting for traits that increase yield potential may increase the susceptibility of genotypes to drought stress [63], whereas selecting for drought tolerance may reduce yield potential under optimal conditions [64].

We employed multivariate approaches to analyse all traits collectively rather than individually. Cluster analysis groups similar genotypes into clusters, with the resulting clusters being distinct from one another. In this study, hierarchical cluster analysis was used to categorize 20 wheat genotypes into three distinct groups based on the contributions of the examined traits (Fig. 4). Clusters I and III represented sensitive and tolerant genotypes, respectively (Fig. 4). Similar classifications of drought tolerance in wheat and genetic divergence through cluster analysis have been reported by Mohi-Ud-Din et al. [13], Islam et al. [52], and Grzesiak [65].

Principal component (PC) analysis is a multivariate statistical technique used to analyse and interpret complex and large datasets. PCs were considered significant only if their eigenvalues exceeded 1. As noted by Kamel et al. [66], this method reduces a large number of correlated variables into a smaller set of components. A PC biplot visually represents variables as lines or vectors, showing their contribution to the total variance, which facilitates informed decision-making on the basis of variable characteristics. An angle between vectors of less than 90 degrees indicates a positive correlation, whereas a 90-degree angle suggests no correlation. A biplot was used to examine correlations among various traits in the experimental data [67]. Figure 5A and B display the PC biplot of wheat genotypes under water deficit stress. The longest vector length in the biplot indicates that yield per plant exhibited the greatest variability, making it the most critical variable for distinguishing wheat genotypes under water deficit stress. While RWC (relative water content) and GPS (grains per spike) presented relatively low variability, traits such as DA (days to anthesis), DH (days to heading), BY (biological yield), and LAI (leaf area index) presented high variability. Under both normal and water deficit conditions, all significant traits negatively influenced the second principal component (PC2), a finding that is consistent with previous research on seedling traits in wheat crops [68]. Principal component analysis (PCA) is also a valuable tool for selecting diverse parents for hybridization and other plant breeding strategies. The projection of genotypes onto PC1 and PC2 aided in identifying distinct parent groups for breeding purposes. The population structure under both water deficit and normal conditions was effectively represented by the genotype patterns of these two principal components [68]. The traits studied are thus recognized as reliable indicators of water deficit tolerance for varietal selection. Varieties exhibiting minimal declines under water deficit stress could serve as standard checks in breeding programs to identify lineages with increased water deficit tolerance. These varieties could also be recommended for cultivation in water deficit-prone regions.

The results are given further confidence by the consistency between the two separate approaches, trait-based clustering and ideotype-focused selection, which also shows how stable these genotypes are under various moisture circumstances. They are attractive choices for next breeding programs and multi-location testing because of their steady selection, which indicates that they possess a desirable trait combination that promotes both high yield and drought resilience.

Conclusions

In order to find drought-tolerant lines, this study evaluated 20 wheat genotypes in both well-watered and water-deficient environments. While some genotypes behaved consistently across contexts, others had varied responses. All characteristics showed significant genetic variability, suggesting a high possibility for choosing drought-tolerant cultivars. Except for CT7DBA, CTA, and CT7DAA PAR80DAS, which rose in water-limited environments, water stress decreased the majority of characteristics. Strong positive correlations and direct impacts on grain yield were demonstrated by traits such as plant height, days to anthesis and maturity, grains per plant, spike length, STI, and YSI, which made them ideal for selection. Under both circumstances, the genotypes E8, E9, E23, E30, and E40 demonstrated greater yield (STI > 1.00). Grain yield’s high heritability and genetic gain point to the possibility of successful selection and additive genetic control. In accordance with ANOVA and STI findings, the MGIDI index was able to identify E38, E30, E35, E40, and E34 as stable, high-yielding genotypes. For more research and possible use in water-limited settings, these genotypes are suggested.
