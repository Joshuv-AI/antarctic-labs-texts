Charge-reversal proteolysis polymers enable tissue-specific STING degradation in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Xu He, Lidong Gong, Juqi Zhang, Xiaocong Pang, Yuhui Li, Wei Wei, Yimin Cui, Zhiqiang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a debilitating disease characterized by autoimmune attack on the joints. The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) is a critical mediator of this pathogenesis. However, its systemic inhibition carries significant toxicity, and its variable nature poses a major challenge for targeting. We developed “smart” therapeutic polymers [charge-reversal proteolysis-targeting chimeras (CreTACs)] that function as precision-guided therapy. These agents remain inactive until they reach the inflamed arthritic joint, where the acidic microenvironment triggers their accumulation and cellular uptake, leading to the specific degradation of STING. In animal models, CreTACs outperformed a standard first-line drug without inducing systemic toxicity. This strategy offers translational potential for precision treatments of RA or other inflammatory diseases.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, progressive bone erosion, and systemic inflammation (1, 2). While therapeutic progress has been remarkable over the past few decades (3, 4), the relapse rate after treatment cessation remains high (40 to 80%) (5), highlighting the persistence of underlying pathophysiological activity. These limitations highlight an urgent need for innovative therapeutic targets that can address the fundamental pathogenic mechanisms of RA.

Emerging evidence suggests that the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway plays a crucial role in autoimmune diseases (6, 7). A recent study has shown that synergistic STING inhibition can significantly reduce dsDNA-induced synovial and cartilage damage in RA models (8). Thus, STING inhibition is a promising therapeutic strategy. However, directly targeting STING is challenging due to multiple human STING variants (9). In clinical settings, five major STING variants have been identified, namely R232 (57.9%), HAQ (20.4%), R232H (13.7%), AQ (5.2%), and R293Q (1.5%) (10). These variants exhibit different responses to STING modulators and distinct distribution patterns among various ethnic groups. In previous drug research and development, the candidate drug DMXAA (5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid or vadimezan) failed during phase III clinical trials (11). This was mainly because it tightly bound to mouse STING but had inadequate binding to human STING, despite the significant sequence and structural similarities between the two species.

To address variant heterogeneity, Proteolysis Targeting Chimera (PROTAC) technology, which employs an “event-driven” degradation mechanism, has attracted significant attention (12–14). This mechanism requires lower affinity and achieves robust activity across protein mutants (15, 16), enabling potent pharmacological effects in proteins with interspecies structural variations or extensive mutational diversity (17, 18). However, a critical challenge that hinders the clinical translation of PROTAC is site-specific delivery or spatial activation to minimize off-target toxicity and enhance therapeutic efficacy in vivo (19).

In this study, we capitalize on the distinct microenvironment in RA-affected joints to develop charge-reversal targeted chimeras (CreTACs) for achieving spatially specific degradation of the protein of interest (POI) at the tissue, cellular, and protein levels. As depicted in Fig. 1, CreTACs were designed using a polymer with the tertiary amine structure of cyclic seven-membered rings (C7A), which have been proven to exhibit strong affinity for the STING protein, and ligands that recruit E3 ligases. In blood circulation (pH 7.4), CreTACs exist as deprotonated nanoparticles, enabling long circulation and minimizing off-target effects. At the arthritic joint (pH 6.5 to 7.0), the CreTAC nanoparticles dissociate into protonated polymers with positive zeta potential, which enhances synovial retention and facilitates internalization into fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) cells with negatively charged membrane surfaces (20). Subsequently, under physiological cytoplasmic conditions (pH 7.2 to 7.4), the CreTAC polymers become deprotonated and are enriched near the STING proton channel through electrostatic binding and hydrophobic effects. Overall, the CreTAC platform uniquely integrates protein degradation activity with delivery capabilities, featuring charge reversal in response to the diverse pH microenvironments in vivo. First, we validated the excessive activation of the STING/p-IRF3 pathway in RA. Next, we synthesized CreTACs and demonstrated their function in vivo and STING-degrading efficacy, offering a promising treatment strategy.

Results

Activation of the STING/p-IRF3 Pathway in Synovial Microenvironment of RA.

Differential expression analysis of genome-wide RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data (GSE77298, GPL570; 7 healthy controls, 16 RA samples (21)) revealed significant STING upregulation in RA (Fig. 1A). RA patients stratified by STING expression level underwent Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This identified significant enrichment of IL-17, NF-κB, and TNF signaling pathways in the high-STING group (Fig. 1B). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) further demonstrated enrichment in interferon-α response, DNA repair, and TNF-α signaling via NF-κB pathways (Fig. 1C), suggesting STING contributes to RA progression through these mechanisms.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of synovial tissues from RA and OA (osteoarthritis) patients (a control condition with distinct pathogenesis (2223, 24) showed elevated STING and p-IRF3 (a key STING pathway effector) expression in RA (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), indicating the STING/p-IRF3 pathway activation. RA is characterized by synovial remodeling driven by FLS proliferation and immune cell infiltration (25). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed increased STING colocalization with the macrophage marker CD68 in RA synovium vs. OA (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), consistent with prior findings (8). STING expression and colocalization with the FLS marker FAPα were also significantly higher in RA, with ~60% colocalization (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D–F). Western blot analysis of primary human FLS confirmed marked upregulation of STING, p-STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3 in RA compared to OA (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). Similar upregulation occurred in FLS from an adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) rat model (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1H).

To investigate STING’s functional role, we generated STING knockout (STING−/−) mice and established a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model (Fig. 1H). STING−/− CIA mice exhibited reduced arthritis incidence (Fig. 1I) and significantly improved clinical scores (Fig. 1 J and K). Histopathology including Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome revealed attenuated synovial hyperplasia, cartilage destruction, and bone erosion in STING−/− CIA mice vs. WT-CIA (Fig. 1L). These results underscore the critical role of STING signaling in RA pathogenesis.

Engineering pH-Responsive CreTACs with Switchable Surface Charge.

CreTACs were designed using a polymer with cyclic seven-membered ring (C7A) tertiary amine structures (PC7A)—which binds STING with high affinity—and E3 ligase-recruiting ligands. This rational design yielded a library of CreTACs with various structure parameters. Through a preliminary screening, three kinds of CreTACs (CreTAC-1, -2, -3) were obtained with different structural architectures (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). These polymers were synthesized via random copolymerization to form PEG-b-P (C7A-r-E3L) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B–E), where PEG is polyethylene glycol, C7A is a 2-(nitroimidazole-1-yl) ethyl methacrylate monomer (a pH-sensitive monomer and STING protein ligand), and E3L is the E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand. A negative control (NC) polymer without STING binding function but with a pH transition point (pHt) comparable to PC7A was also synthesized (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). The structural correctness of the C7A monomer and PC7A polymer was confirmed by 1H NMR (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). As designed, CreTACs exhibited dynamic, pH-dependent charge reversal (Fig. 2A).

Due to tertiary amines in their side chains, CreTACs exhibit pH-responsive protonation/deprotonation. In a low-pH environment, protonation results in CreTAC with a positive zeta potential, which exhibits enhanced cellular internalization. To verify their charge-reversal ability of CreTACs, we used dynamic light scattering. As shown in Fig. 2B, both CreTACs and PC7A exhibited a negative zeta potential under alkaline conditions. This negative charge was attributed to the preferential adsorption of anions in the physiological buffer solution by the PEG shell (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). As the pH decreases, the negative zeta potential of CreTACs reversed into positive zeta potential. Further verification was performed by incubating bovine serum albumin (BSA) with CreTAC nanoparticles at different pH environment. As shown in Fig. 2C, the particle sizes of CreTAC nanoparticles and PC7A are significantly smaller under acidic conditions than under alkaline conditions, indicating acidic-induced dissociation of nanoparticles. When incubated with BSA, the particle size of CreTAC nanoparticles increased significantly at lower pH but hardly changed at higher pH, confirming their charge-reversal ability. In addition, the results of particle size measurement after incubation with BSA indicate that CreTACs are not prone to binding with BSA under high pH conditions, beneficial for its stability in blood circulation.

We also used the fluorescent dye Nile Red to verify the charge-reversal ability of CreTACs at different pH conditions. The pH conditions of 7.4, 6.5, and 7.4 were used to simulate the pH environments in blood circulation, joint inflammatory tissue, and cytoplasm, respectively (Fig. 2D). In the absence of CreTACs, Nile Red showed almost no obvious fluorescence activation, indicating its weak and pH-independent fluorescence. When CreTACs were added, at pH 7.4, Nile Red exhibited obvious fluorescence activation at λex/λem = 360 nm/718 nm and λex/λem = 535 nm/594 nm; while at pH 6.5, Nile Red was hardly activated. Under alkaline conditions, deprotonated CreTACs remain as complete nanoparticles, and Nile Red enters the hydrophobic core via hydrophobic interactions, resulting in enhanced long-wavelength fluorescence. In acidic conditions, the protonated nanoparticles dispersed with adsorption of Nile Red, showing short-wavelength activation. The complete curves of Nile Red under different conditions—with excitation at 360 nm (emission: 400 to 900 nm) and 535 nm (emission: 560 to 900 nm)—are presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B.

Physicochemical Characterization and Protein Degradation Efficacy of CreTAC Nanoparticles.

After verifying the charge-reversal performance, we characterized the conventional physicochemical parameters of CreTAC nanoparticles. Titration results (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) showed that CreTACs and control nanoparticles had similar pH transition points (around 7.1). CreTAC nanoparticles maintained a spherical nanostructure under alkaline conditions and disintegrated into polymer chains under acidic conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Dynamic light scattering (including particle size and polydispersion index) indicated their long-term stability at 4 °C and 37 °C (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D).

To evaluate the capacity of CreTACs in targeting degrading STING proteins, THP-1 cells were treated with negative control, CreTAC-1, CreTAC-2, CreTAC-3, or PC7A for 20 h, followed by cGAMP stimulation for 6 h. Quantitative analysis showed that CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2 induced significant STING degradation, while CreTAC-3 had little activity (Fig. 2 E and F). Due to its lack of significant protein-degrading efficacy, CreTAC-3 was excluded, and CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2 were selected for subsequent experiments. The PC7A/cGAMP-mediated STING degradation was consistent with established mechanisms.

Time-course experiments with CreTAC-1 (0, 1, 6, 12, 18, 24 h) revealed detectable degradation starting at 12 h, with increasing efficacy up to 24 h (Fig. 2 G and H). Dose–response studies in hFLSs demonstrated concentration-dependent (0 to 20 μM) and time-dependent (12 to 24 h) target elimination, with a maximal degradation efficacy (Dmax) of 99.40 ± 0.04% (Fig. 2 I and J).

Tissue Acidic Microenvironment–Activated Charge Conversion of Optimized CreTACs in RA.

To accurately map the synovial pH, we employed a gradient pH-sensitive (GPS) (26) nanoprobe panel in AIA rats. The fluorescent signal pattern confirmed an acidic microenvironment in arthritic joints (pH slightly above 6.5), whereas healthy joints maintained a physiological pH between 7.4 and 7.7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C), a finding consistent with established clinical reports of synovial acidosis in RA (2728, 29). We then verified the pH-responsive design of CreTACs. Titration revealed a sharp pH transition point (pHt) at 7.1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), enabling charge reversal via protonation of its tertiary amines. This property allows CreTACs to specifically respond to the acidic RA synovial milieu (pH 6.5 to 6.8), which results from hypermetabolism and immune infiltration, vs. healthy tissue (pH 7.3 to 7.8). At physiological pH 7.4, CreTAC polymers self-assembled into ~30 nm nanoparticles confirmed by a transmission electron microscope (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Under RA synovial acidosis (pH 6.5 to 6.8), CreTAC nanoparticles rapidly dissociated into sub-5 nm polymers (Fig. 3A). Fluorescent dye-labeled CreTACs demonstrated pH-sensitive “Active/Inactive” behavior (Fig. 3B), achieving >50-fold signal enhancement under acidic vs. neutral conditions (Fig. 3 C and D).

In CIA models, administration of ICG-labeled CreTAC-2 showed pathological joint accumulation within 4 h, persisting at 24 h (Fig. 3 E and F), with negligible signal in healthy joints. A distinct fluorescent signal was observed in the tail region of CIA mice, which corresponded to the site of model induction (subcutaneous injection at the tail base, Fig. 1H). To confirm that this signal originated from the local inflammatory lesion rather than from injection-related artifacts, we administered ICG-labeled CreTACs via an alternative route (intraperitoneal injection). Consistent with the intravenous results, strong tail fluorescence was still evident in CIA but not in normal mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). This confirms that the tail signal reflects targeted CreTAC accumulation at the inflammatory site induced by CIA modeling. Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher accumulation and activation in arthritic vs. normal joints at 24 h (Fig. 3G). Biodistribution revealed expected reticuloendothelial clearance (liver/spleen/lung accumulation; minimal heart/brain deposition), with significant nanoparticle aggregation specifically in arthritic joints (Fig. 3 H and I).

Enhanced Cellular Internalization of Optimized CreTACs Mediated by Protonated Polymer.

After charge reversal in the RA joint microenvironment, CreTAC nanoparticles dissociated into protonated polymers, exhibiting enhanced cellular uptake (Fig. 4A). The protonated configuration enhances the affinity of CreTAC polymers for negatively charged cell membranes via electrostatic interaction, significantly boosting intracellular uptake efficiency. Consistent with this, RA synovial fibroblasts have a stronger negative surface charge and greater uptake potential than OA fibroblasts (Fig. 4B). Cellular uptake efficiency of CreTACs increased progressively with decreasing pH of cell culture medium. The uptake efficiency of CreTACs in lower pH (in the form of protonated polymers) was more than 50% higher than that in higher pH (Fig. 4 C and D).

Further laser scanning confocal microscopy experiments demonstrated efficient cytoplasmic delivery of CreTACs. Colocalization analysis with lysosomes revealed continuous nanoparticle uptake over 16 h, followed by reduced colocalization indicating cytoplasmic release (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). This temporal lysosomal escape profile coincides with the onset of protein degradation activity.

Cytoplasmic Charge Reversion of Optimized CreTACs for Enhanced Affinity for STING.

Following lysosomal escape and cytoplasmic entry, protonated CreTACs polymers undergo deprotonation in the higher-pH environment of the cytosol (Fig. 5A). Zeta potential measurements in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and simulated cytoplasmic fluid (SCF) confirmed the charge reversal of the nanoparticles in the cytoplasmic microenvironment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C). Further validation came from 3D and emission wavelength scanning of Nile Red (λex 360 nm/535 nm; SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D–F).

As STING functions as a proton channel that mediates Golgi-to-cytosol proton efflux, it creates a localized proton-rich microenvironment. Theoretically, this charge gradient promotes CreTAC accumulation near STING, facilitating ligand–receptor binding.

To first confirm the direct binding between CreTACs and STING, we performed cellular experiments. Cellular fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays confirmed CreTAC binding to STING, unlike control nanoparticles lacking this capacity (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). In vitro fluorescence colocalization of CreTACs or PC7A with STING proteins revealed polymer-inducing protein aggregation. In contrast, control polymers did not induce any fluorescence emitted by aggregates (Fig. 5B). The aggregates of CreTACs or PC7A with STING are the key structural prerequisite for targeted STING degradation.

To quantify the binding affinity and dissect the underlying interaction mechanism, we conducted surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays. The results demonstrated that CreTACs and PC7A exhibited strong binding affinities to STING protein, with dissociation constants (KD) of 2.14 × 10−11 M and 5.96 × 10−11 M, respectively (Fig. 5C). To elucidate the interaction mechanism between CreTACs and STING, we compared their binding affinity under different ionic-strength conditions. At pH 7.4, a high-salt environment markedly reduced CreTACs’ affinity for STING (KD = 1.32 × 10−7 M; SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). In contrast, under physiologically normal salt concentrations, binding was substantially stronger (KD = 2.14 × 10−11 M). These results confirm that, beyond its intrinsic ligand–receptor affinity, CreTACs engage STING via electrostatic interactions, as elevated salt concentrations shield these forces and weaken binding (30).

Optimized CreTAC-Mediated STING Degradation via the Ubiquitin–Proteasome Pathway.

According to the above studies, CreTACs were expected to potently degrade STING and block downstream STING/p-IRF3 signaling in human and RA animal models. In AIA rat FLSs, CreTAC-1, CreTAC-2, and STING antagonist H-151 suppressed phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 (Fig. 5 D and E). IF analysis of human RA-FLSs confirmed STING degradation, showing reduced STING fluorescence and suppressed nuclear p-IRF3 translocation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C–E).

To exclude the activation effect by the C7 rings in the scaffolds of CreTACs before protein degradation, human FLSs were treated with PC7A or CreTACs over time (0 to 48 h). CreTAC degradation efficiency increased time-dependently (12 to 24 h), while STING phosphorylation progressively decreased—confirming direct degradation without target activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8F). Time-course analysis showed total STING and p-STING decreased concurrently from early time points with no prior p-STING upregulation, a kinetic profile distinct from STING agonists (SI Appendix, Fig. S8F). Mechanistically, it is plausible that CreTACs trigger rapid STING ubiquitination upon ER binding, intercepting it during ER-to-Golgi transit and driving proteasomal degradation before downstream signaling activation (31, 32). Mechanistic studies showed cotreatment with the proteasome inhibitor PS-341 reversed CreTAC-mediated STING depletion and pathway inhibition (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). IF revealed colocalized STING-cereblon depletion reversible by PS-341 (Fig. 5 F–H), indicating ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS)-dependent degradation via CreTAC-facilitated ternary complex formation.

In RA’s inflammatory cascade, TNF drives FLS activation. Activated FLSs promote inflammation and cartilage damage via chemokines, cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). TNF-α stimulation (4 ng/mL) upregulated STING/p-TBK1/p-IRF3 in human RA-FLSs. CreTACs or H-151 effectively reversed this STING pathway amplification (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D), suggesting CreTACs disrupt the inflammatory loop by degrading STING—potentially countering NF-κB-mediated STING activation.

Optimized CreTACs Suppress STING Signaling and Ameliorate Inflammation in the RA Model.

We evaluated CreTAC therapeutic efficacy in CIA mice (Fig. 6A). CreTAC-1/2 significantly reduced polyarthritis index (PI) scores and joint swelling thickness vs. model controls—effects surpassing methotrexate (MTX), a clinically available drug for RA therapy (Fig. 6 B–D). Histopathology revealed CreTAC-1/2 attenuated synovial hyperplasia and joint inflammation (Fig. 6E), with lower histological scores than both model and MTX groups (Fig. 6 G–I). Safranin O-Fast Green staining confirmed reduced cartilage destruction and preserved tissue integrity in CreTAC-treated mice (Fig. 6F).

Consistent with the findings in murine CIA models, CreTACs also demonstrated potent efficacy in rat AIA models. Treatment significantly ameliorated disease phenotypes, including joint swelling, synovial inflammation, and cartilage destruction (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–C). Crucially, immunohistochemical and western blot analyses of synovial tissues confirmed that this therapeutic effect was mediated by on-target suppression of the STING pathway, as shown by reduced levels of STING protein and inhibited phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D and E). Thus, the data confirm on-target activity through marked STING inhibition within the inflamed joints, underlying the observed therapeutic alleviation of inflammation.

Optimized CreTACs Exhibit Selective Targeting and a Favorable Systemic Safety Profile.

To evaluate potential off target effects, we systematically examined CreTACs’ impact in healthy tissues. In nonarthritic organs where nanoparticles passively accumulate (liver, spleen, lung, kidney), CreTAC treatment did not suppress the low basal levels of STING/p-STING (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F), confirming the absence of unintended pathway inhibition. Notably, in the AIA model, inflammatory disease activity itself induced marked STING overexpression specifically in the spleen; CreTAC treatment effectively normalized this pathological elevation, demonstrating selective correction of disease associated dysregulation while sparing homeostatic signaling. Consistent with this, flow cytometry analysis revealed minimal CreTAC accumulation in noninflamed synovium (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G), supporting its targeting preference for inflamed joints.

To comprehensively assess CreTAC safety, we first examined their impact on immune homeostasis in healthy mice given the same treatment regimen as model mice. For innate immunity, flow cytometry analysis of splenocytes confirmed that CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2 did not significantly alter the frequencies of total myeloid cells (CD45+CD11b+) or macrophage subsets (CD45+CD11b+F4/80+) compared to vehicle controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–D). For adaptive immunity, no statistically significant changes were observed in the proportions of total T cells, B cells, CD4+/CD8+ T cell subsets, activated CD4+ T cells (CD4+CD25+), or regulatory T cells (Treg, CD4+CD25+FOXP3+) in CreTAC-treated groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A–H). All comparisons showed no significant differences (one-way ANOVA), indicating that CreTACs do not disrupt the homeostatic balance of either innate or adaptive immune cell populations in healthy animals. To evaluate the systemic toxicity of CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2, healthy mice were administered different dosages of these compounds. The mice were randomly divided into groups receiving low and high doses of CreTAC-1 (15 mg/kg and 75 mg/kg) and CreTAC-2 (15 mg/kg and 75 mg/kg), along with a normal control group. After 7 d post injection, blood and vital organs were collected for examination. Liver function indicators, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and albumin (ALB); heart function indicators, including creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH); and kidney function indicators, including urea (UREA) and uric acid (UA), as well as total protein (TP), were assessed (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). These indicators showed no significant alterations following treatment with CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2. Subsequently, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney samples were sectioned and H&E stained (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B). The images showed that CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2 did not induce morphological changes or inflammation in these organs. Therefore, CreTAC-1 and CreTAC-2 demonstrate high biocompatibility and do not cause acute damage to major organs.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate aberrant STING activation as a critical driver in RA pathogenesis and a potent therapeutic target. Dysregulated dsDNA/cGAS signaling in RA sustains a harmful positive feedback loop with downstream TNF-α production, worsening inflammation, and promoting disease progression (6, 33, 34). In this study, pathological STING overexpression and dysregulated cGAS/STING pathway were rigorously validated across multiple levels: elevated STING/p-IRF3 signaling was evident in clinical synovial samples from RA patients compared to OA controls, corroborated in STING-knockout rat models, and recapitulated in the CIA model. This multifaceted confirmation underscores the fundamental importance of targeting the cGAS/STING axis for RA therapy. As a more upstream mediator than TNFα within the inflammatory cascade, STING represents a more favorable target for RA intervention. While emerging STING-targeting strategies for RA show promise (8, 3536, 37), most focus on synovial macrophages (22), leaving pathologically critical FLSs—with notoriously poor drug uptake—inadequately addressed. Here, we uniquely identify the pivotal role of STING within FLS for RA pathogenesis and take the cellular uptake of POI degraders into account in the design of CreTACs.

CreTACs effectively degrade diverse STING variants across species. This broad efficacy stems from two key features: 1) As an event-driven strategy, CreTACs require only weak target binding for degradation, accommodating sequence variation. 2) The C7A ligand targets the Glu296-Asp297 site on STING’s α5 helix, a highly conserved region among mammals. This conserved-epitope targeting provides a framework to overcome mutational heterogeneity.

Unlike existing TPD technologies like PROTAC, CreTACs achieve spatially precise, multilevel STING inhibition. CreTACs leverage polymer charge-reversal properties to achieve intelligent targeting at multiple levels: acidic synovial fluid (pH 6.5 to 7.0), synovial cytoplasm (pH 7.0 to 7.4), and the POI (STING as a proton channel). Thus, it maximizes efficacy while minimizing off-target effects and dose-limiting toxicities, especially crucial for STING targeting therapy. CreTACs achieve spatially precise STING degradation (85% reduction in synovial fibroblasts), outperforming MTX in reducing bone erosion (68% reduction, P < 0.001) without causing lymphopenia or liver toxicity. Overall, this platform bridges the gap between PROTAC’s degradation efficacy at protein level and precision delivery at tissue and cell levels, offering a blueprint for next-generation protein degraders.

Our central design idea in this study is to transform a functional polymer into a promising drug candidate called CreTACs, which aims to degrade disease-related POI. Importantly, CreTAC design differs from reported systems using polymeric nanocarriers to deliver PROTACs (which exert proteolysis activity). CreTACs offer several distinct advantages over traditional PROTACs. In terms of candidate drug optimization, CreTACs exhibit greater structural flexibility than PROTACs, allowing for the conjugation of diverse ligand types and adjustment of ligand ratios (38, 39). This study demonstrates the optimized CreTACs with 70 repeating units of C7A and less than 4 conjugates of E3-ligase ligands (by preliminary data). During the formation of a ternary complex, CreTACs, as a polyvalent macromolecule, exhibits a longer-lasting and stronger binding affinity with both the POI and E3 ligase, compared to the bivalent PROTAC. In certain cases, this polymer can even induce the condensation of multiple POIs, thereby enhancing the efficiency of protein degradation.

A key translational advantage is the built-in safety profile. CreTACs’ degradation activity is context-dependent, confined to inflammatory sites (e.g., inflamed joints and spleen in AIA), normalizing pathological STING overexpression without affecting homeostasis in healthy tissues. This specificity, gated by the acidic joint microenvironment, is supported by the absence of acute systemic toxicity or disruption of immune cell homeostasis. These features indicate a wide therapeutic window for CreTACs in RA and other STING-driven diseases.

Furthermore, this approach could be extended to other macromolecules (e.g., polymers, peptides that bind specific proteins. Currently, a wide range of polymers has been reported as effective carriers for delivering active drug molecules into cells. However, most polymers do not possess pharmacological activities themselves, which is likely attributed to their weak protein affinity. The protein degradation strategy proposed in this study does not require strict dependence on high affinity. Even mild binding of CreTAC molecules to the target protein is enough to trigger degradation. Therefore, the CreTAC strategy shows great potential for expanding the possibilities of polymers in the field of medication, offering insights for biomaterials.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG, Melo-PEG), synthesis reagents (Aladdin, Beijing Tide Pharmaceutical), antibodies (Beijing Solarbio), and mice (Beijing Vital River) were used as received. Details are in SI Appendix.

Synthesis of PC7A and CreTACs.

PC7A random copolymers were synthesized via initiators for continuous activator regeneration atom transfer radical polymerization (ICAR-ATRP) polymerization with a monomer C7A-to-PEG initiator molar ratio of 1:70. CreTACs were prepared by conjugating E3 ligase ligand to PC7A through the amidation reaction between amino groups on PC7A and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester groups on the ligand.

Cell Culture and Animal Models.

Human RA FLSs were cultured in DMEM/10% FBS. AIA (SD rats) and CIA (C57BL/6, STING−/− mice) models were established per standard protocols. The study protocol involving human synovial tissues was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Approval No. 2022-R-461-002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Approval No. J2022108).

Nanoparticle Characterization and Cellular Interactions.

Charge reversal properties and fluorescence activation of CreTAC nanoparticles were detected by fluorescence spectrophotometry. Cellular uptake and colocalization with lysosomes were observed via confocal microscopy and quantified by flow cytometry. FRET assays were performed to evaluate interactions between STING protein and nanoparticles.

Binding Affinity and Biodistribution Analysis.

SPR assays were conducted to measure the binding affinity between STING protein and PC7A/CreTACs under different salt conditions. ICG-labeled CreTAC biodistribution was monitored via live imaging (Living Image software for quantification).

Pharmacodynamic and Histopathological Evaluations.

Antiarthritic efficacy of CreTACs was evaluated in AIA rats and CIA mice by monitoring joint swelling. Histopathological changes of joint tissues were assessed by H&E, Safranin O-Fast Green staining. Expression levels of STING and related signaling molecules were detected by IHC, IF staining and Western blot.

Statistical Analysis.

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using appropriate software (details in SI Appendix). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD or SEM. Differences between groups were compared using statistical tests such as the t test or one-way ANOVA, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
