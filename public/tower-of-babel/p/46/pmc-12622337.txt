Embedding genetic testing in the metastatic prostate cancer pathway: understanding patient and physician perspectives
Authors: Ruhee Jain, Amanda Ribbands, Jake Butcher, Sophie Barlow, Jacob Skilling, Andrea Stevens, Katie Pascoe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo understand how genetic (homologous recombination repair [HRR], including BReast CAncer [BRCA]) testing is being embedded in clinical practice and identify testing challenges given global approvals of poly(adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) for metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa).MethodsData from two Adelphi Real World Prostate Cancer Disease Specific Programmes™ (DSPs; 2020 and 11/2022–7/2023) were analyzed and compared using appropriate bivariate tests.ResultsGenetic testing was performed among 45% (855/1899) of patients with mPCa (castration-sensitive [mCSPC]: 36%; castration-resistant [mCRPC]: 49%; HRR genes: 43% [34%; 47%]; BRCA: 42% [34%; 46%]). Testing increased from 2020: HRR genes by 17% (p < 0.01); BRCA by 18% (p < 0.01). HRR gene and BRCA mutations were reported in 16% (mCSPC: 13%; mCRPC: 18%) and 15% (13%; 16%) of patients, respectively. In the 2022–2023 DSP, 56% of physicians indicated they were more likely to conduct HRR gene testing following PARPi availability. Patient refusal, costs, reimbursement issues, low sample availability, and result delays were testing challenges. Among 40% of patients who spoke with physicians about testing, 83% were tested, and 49% reported knowing results helped.ConclusionsUnderstanding physicians’ and patients’ experiences with, and challenges surrounding, genetic testing for PCa may facilitate better clinical-management interactions.KEYWORDS: Metastatic prostate cancer, genetic mutation, testing, real world, DDR, homologous recombination repair, BRCA, PARP inhibitor

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) was the second most frequent cancer (estimated 1.5 million new cases) and the fifth leading cause of cancer deaths (397,000 PCa deaths; 7.3% of all cancer deaths) in males in 118 of 185 countries analyzed in 2022 [1]. With an estimated 299,010 new cancer cases and 35,250 deaths in 2024, PCa also is the second most frequent cancer in the United States, representing 14.9% of all new cancer cases [2].

Between 2014 and 2020, 8% of PCa cases were metastatic PCa (mPCa) at diagnosis, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 37% [2]. A wide array of treatment options for mPCa has been approved over the last decade, and treatment choice depends on whether the disease is castration sensitive (mCSPC) or castration resistant (mCRPC), as well as on individual patient and clinical characteristics [3–6].

DNA-repair anomalies have been identified in 15% to 20% of patients with mPCa, including mutations in homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes (e.g., BReast CAncer [BRCA]1 and BRCA2, ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), and Fanconi anemia complementation group A [FANCA]) [7–9]. Up to 30% of patients with mCRPC have deleterious germline or somatic HRR gene mutations—most frequently in BRCA [9–16], resulting in impactful symptoms, impaired health-related quality of life, and worse outcomes [11,16–20]. Among HRR gene mutations, BRCA mutations are associated with a more aggressive PCa phenotype [11,14,17,19,21–26].

The poly(adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase (PARP) pathway is a potential drug target in mPCa with HRR gene mutations [11,17,19–21,27]. Cells normally use homologous recombination, which is mediated by PARPs, to repair damaged DNA and survive. Tumor cells with HRR gene mutations treated with a PARP inhibitor (PARPi) cannot efficiently and accurately accomplish repairs, leading to tumor cell death [28–30]. Given the global approvals of PARPi for mPCa, exploring physicians’ and patients’ perspectives of genetic testing (see Box 1) is important to facilitate clearer treatment pathways and better patient experiences.

Types of genetic testing

Genomic: analysis of patient’s entire DNA, including germline and somatic DNA, to identify mutations that are present in all cells of the body (germline) or in specific cells (somatic)

Germline: analysis of DNA in all cells of the body, including tumor cells, to identify inherited mutations

Somatic: analysis of tumor-cell DNA to identify mutations present only in tumor cells

Tumor: analysis of tumor-cell DNA to identify mutations that have occurred spontaneously

In this study, use of genetic testing in the management of mCSPC and mCRPC was described and compared using data from an Adelphi Real World (ARW) PCa Wave V Disease Specific Programme™ (DSP). Both physicians’ and patients’ perceptions of testing were analyzed. The primary objectives of the study were to determine at which points in the PCa disease journey genetic testing is conducted and to understand challenges associated with genetic testing. Use of genetic testing in physicians’ treatment decisions and patients’ attitudes to testing also were explored. Using data from a previous survey (ARW PCa Wave IV DSP), changes to genetic testing rates and physicians’ attitudes over time were compared.

Materials & methods

Study design

Data from a survey, which comprised three key phases—a preparatory phase (survey refinement and analysis plan finalization), a data collection phase (assembly of patient demographic and clinical characteristic data, HRR gene mutation [HRRm] testing rates, timing and type of genetic tests, physicians’ attitudes to genetic testing, and patients’ attitudes to genetic testing), and a data processing/analysis phase, were used in this retrospective study (Figure 1) [31].

Data were collected between November 2022 and December 2023.

Research themes

Research questions focused on the following themes: (1) genetic testing rates and physicians’ attitudes about testing, including changes between the ARW PCa Wave IV DSP (data collection: 2020) and ARW PCa Wave V DSP (data collection: 2022–2023); (2) profiles (e.g., demographic and clinical characteristics) of patients with mCSPC or mCRPC who received genetic testing; (3) when in the PCa disease journey physicians perform genetic testing; (4) approaches to genetic testing used by treating physicians and any differences among the different genetic testing approaches across the PCa disease spectrum; (5) use of genetic testing in physicians’ treatment decisions; (6) challenges/barriers to genetic testing faced by physicians at different stages of PCa; and (7) patients’ attitudes to genetic testing.

Data source

The ARW PCa DSP is a large, independent, cross-sectional survey with retrospective elements, completed by physicians and their consulting patients. The DSP methodology has been previously described and validated [31–33], and has been demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [33]. Quantitative and qualitative data collected in the DSPs included largely objective variables and some subjective (more qualitative, opinion-based) variables, as well as clinical information about individual patients, their disease, and their treatment. The DSP was performed in compliance with the European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association Code of Conduct and ARW Standard Operating Procedures for all processes involved, and an exemption for analysis of the data was obtained from Pearl Institutional Review Board (AG9236).

Wave-IV data were collected from January 2020–August 2020. Wave-V data were collected from November 2022 to July 2023 from three sources (Figure 1). The physician survey—a 30-minute, semi-structured online survey exploring attitudes, motivations, and current practices with regards to PCa management—was completed by medical oncologists and urologists. Each physician also completed patient record forms (PRFs) over a series of 25-minute, online sessions, following patient consultations for 12 to 14 consecutive consulting patients who met DSP inclusion criteria (see Study participants)—four patients with mCSPC, eight patients with mCRPC, and up to two additional patients with HRRm mCRPC, generating a larger sample of HRRm mCRPC patients. Data for the mCRPC HRRm oversample were collected after each physician completed eight PRFs for patients with mCRPC, regardless of status. Corresponding patients were invited to complete a patient self-completion (PSC) form on a voluntary basis. The 20-minute, pen-and-paper PSC survey contained questions about patient demographics, symptoms, and current treatment perceptions; genetic testing; and quality of life. Where applicable, all responses to multiple-choice questions per participant were counted. Fully de-identified physician and patient information were aggregated for analysis.

Study participants

A geographically representative sample of medical oncologists and urologists in France, Germany, and the United States who were treating patients with mCSPC and mCRPC was recruited into the ARW Wave V DSP. To participate in the DSP, physicians had to be a specialist in medical oncology or urology and responsible for the management of ≥ 4 patients with mPCa per month. Physicians received fair market value renumeration for participation in the study.

Patients had to be ≥18 years old at data collection, have a physician-confirmed diagnosis of mPCa, and have never participated in a clinical trial to be eligible for DSP inclusion. Patients classified as “HRR tested” had been tested for ≥1 of the following HRR genes—BRCA1, BRCA2, BRCA1 interacting protein C-terminal helicase 1 (BRIP1), cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)12, checkpoint kinase (CHEK)2, FANCA, partner and localizer of BRCA2 (PALB2), RAD51 paralog B (RAD51B) and RAD54-like (RAD54L) for patients with mCSPC, and ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK2, FANCA, PALB2, and histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) for patients with mCRPC. The mCRPC group comprised a sample of a general pseudorandom cohort of mCRPC patients, which were classified either as nonHRRm (either never tested or had a negative HRR test) or HRRm (≥1 confirmed mutation in ≥ 1 of the listed HRR genes), and an oversample of mCRPC HRRm patients, which included patients with a confirmed mutation in ≥ 1 of the listed HRR genes (Figure 1). The mCRPC group included the oversample of mCRPC HRRm patients, except when the oversample cohort likely would have biased the results (e.g., when discussing gene mutation frequency).

Statistical analyses and reporting

Data for the combined mCSPC/pseudorandom mCRPC cohort, which does not include the oversample of mCRPC HRRm patients, are presented throughout, unless specified otherwise. Where specified, analysis was restricted to data available from matched PRFs and PSCs. Descriptive analysis was used to quantitatively describe and summarize data from the ARW PCa DSP databases, without any data manipulation or statistical analysis unless otherwise stated. Mean and standard deviations (SD) and counts (%) were calculated for continuous and categorical outcomes, respectively; in the absence of these values, medians were determined.

Bivariate analyses were conducted to compare an outcome between ≥2 patient groups. For two-group comparisons, T-test was used for continuous outcomes, Fisher’s exact for nominal categorical variables, and Mann-Whitney U test for ordered categorical variables. Differences were considered significantly different at p-values of < 0.05. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine factors associated with outcomes when making comparisons. Covariates were selected using domain knowledge ensuring adequate sample size was available for comparison between groups. Covariates included patient age at data collection, presence of visceral metastases at data collection (vs. no visceral metastases), and presence of metastatic disease at initial PCa diagnosis (vs. non-metastatic disease at initial PCa diagnosis). All analyses with statistical comparisons were performed using IBM® SPSS® Data Collection Survey Reporter Version 6 or later (International Business Machines Corp., New York, USA) was used to generate descriptive data, and STATA version 17.0 was used to conduct statistical comparisons. Where appropriate, statistical analyses were performed. Significance levels are reported as p-values when significant.

Results

Study participants

Data from 170 physicians and 2190 patients from the ARW PCa Wave V DSP were analyzed (Figure 1; Table S1). Physician data analysis sets included all physicians (N = 170), medical oncologists (n = 126) versus urologists (n = 44), and community (n = 107) versus academic (n = 63) physicians. Overall, 164 physicians (96%) reported performing genetic testing, and 129 (76%) and 57 (34%) reported performing germline and somatic HRR gene testing, respectively. Patient data analysis sets (PRF data) included all patients (N = 2190); mCSPC group (n = 637); mCRPC group (n = 1553); pseudorandom mCRPC cohort (n = 1262); mCRPC HRRm oversample cohort (n = 291); and combined mCSPC/pseudorandom mCRPC cohort (n = 1899). Patient perspectives from 549 PSCs, as well as 538 matched PRFs and PSCs, also were analyzed. Data from 179 physicians and 1413 patients in the previous ARW PCa Wave IV DSP (2020) were included in comparative analyses with the ARW PCa Wave V DSP (2022–2023).

Mean (SD [25th, 75th percentiles]) age of all patients was 70.8 (7.98 [67.0, 76.0]) years, and most had an Eastern Cooperative Outcomes Group Performance Score of 0–1 (72%) at data collection and were White/Caucasian (78%) (Table 1). Disease stage at initial diagnosis varied—localized: 28%, locally advanced: 29%, and metastatic: 42%. At data collection, > 80% of patients had ≥3 bone lesions, 36% were classified by their physician as having high-volume disease, and 58% were classified as having high-risk disease.

Testing rates and physicians’ reasons for testing

At the time of the Wave-V survey, 45% of all mPCa patients (mCSPC plus pseudorandom mCRPC cohort, n = 1899) had undergone genetic testing (mCSPC: 36%; mCRPC: 49%). For patients with mCSPC, 34% were tested for HRR gene mutations and 34% for BRCA mutations; mCRPC testing rates were slightly higher (HRR: 47%; BRCA: 46%) (Table 2; Figure 2(A)). In Wave V, more patients were tested if they had private insurance (vs utilized publicly funded healthcare), were treated in academic (vs community) settings, were treated by a medical oncologist (vs a urologist), or were treated by physicians with (vs without) clinical trial experience (Figure 2(B)).

Between Waves IV and V, HRR gene testing across mPCa significantly increased by 17% (26% [364/1413] vs 43% [811/1899]; p < 0.0001), with increases of 11% for mCSPC and 18% for mCRPC (Table 2; Figure 2(A)). HRR gene testing rates numerically increased (statistical testing not performed) most between Waves IV and V in France (mCSPC: 15% vs 27%; mCRPC: 16% vs 39%) and were highest in the United States (mCSPC: 27% and 39%; mCRPC: 37% and 53%). BRCA testing across mPCa increased significantly by 18% between Wave IV (24% [345/1413]) and Wave V (42% [799/1899] (Table 2); p < 0.01), with numerical increases of 13% for mCSPC and 19% for mCRPC. Of note, 56% of physicians in Wave V indicated they were more likely to test for HRR gene mutations following the availability of PARPis.

Patients, regardless of disease status, who received a genetic test were significantly younger, had significantly more severe disease (e.g., increasing prostate-specific antigen [PSA] trend), and had a significantly higher rate of a family history of cancer than those who did not receive a genetic test (Fig S1A).

Several patient characteristics influenced whether physicians requested an HRR gene test (Figure 3(A)). Compared to Wave IV, two factors were significantly stronger drivers of testing, namely “young age [physician defined] at diagnosis” and “progression from an earlier stage to advanced stage” (p = 0.0013 and 0.0014, respectively). When asked what knowing a patient’s HRR gene mutation status helps inform, “treatment decisions” and “genetic risk in prostate cancer in family members” were the factors that significantly increased between waves (p = 0.0390 and 0.0316, respectively) (Figure 3(B)). Family history of breast, ovarian, pancreatic, or prostate cancer were the most common patient characteristics influencing germline HRR gene testing in Wave V, regardless of physician specialty (Figure 3(C)). The most common reason for physicians to conduct biomarker testing in Wave V was to ‘predict patient response to systemic drug treatment (e.g., PARPis) (Figure 3(D)). HRR gene and BRCA mutation positivity rates

In the Wave-V combined mPCa cohort (mCSPC plus pseudorandom mCRPC), 38% of patients tested for an HRRm had ≥1 HRRm (mCSPC: 38%; mCRPC: 38%), and 35% of patients tested for a BRCAm had ≥1 BRCAm (mCSPC: 38%; mCRPC: 35%) (Table 2). Patients with HRRm mCSPC or mCRPC were significantly younger, had significantly more severe disease (e.g., increasing PSA trend), and had a significantly higher rate of a family history of cancer than patients with nonHRRm disease (Fig S1B). Patients with BRCAm mCSPC or mCRPC were significantly younger, had a higher rate of a family history of cancer, and had a higher increasing PSA trend, but significantly fewer bone lesions, than patients with non BRCAm disease (Fig S1C).

Timing of genetic testing

According to patients’ charts, initial genetic testing was most common “at mPCa diagnosis” (36%; range: 34–40%), and few patients (8%; range: 6–9%) had initial testing at progression of last mPCa therapy (Figure 4(A)). However, overall timing of initial testing differed significantly based on practice setting and physician specialty. Patients treated in academic settings were first tested significantly earlier than patients treated in community settings (p < 0.0001), and patients treated by medical oncologists were first tested significantly earlier than patients treated by urologists (p = 0.0249).

Amid the 164 physicians who stated they perform testing, 75% typically tested “at identification of castration resistance,” 59% “at mPCa diagnosis,” and 45% “at initial PCa diagnosis”; 27% reported typically testing when localized disease progressed to locally advanced/metastatic disease (Figure 4(B)).

Overall, 92% of patients received one test (Fig S2A) and 7% received two tests, with most (58%) of the second tests being conducted at “emergence of castration resistance” (Fig S2B). Repeat testing was uncommon, regardless of practice setting, physician specialty, or patients’ number of lines of therapy, and “mCRPC diagnosis” was the most common reason for repeat testing across HRR genes tested.

Types of genetic testing

According to physician-survey data, 95% of the 164 physicians who performed genetic testing reported they “frequently or always” test for both BRCA1 and BRCA2, with most saying that a BRCA test result was essential when considering mCSPC (BRCA1: 63%; BRCA2: 62%) or mCRPC (BRCA1: 87%; BRCA2: 86%) treatment. Among genetic tests recorded in patients’ charts, which could have been conducted at any point during their disease journey, the most common tests/panels across disease states (Figure 5(A)), and physician practice settings and specialties (Figure 5(B)), were “germline BRCA testing” and “tumor BRCA testing.” “Germline BRCA testing” was used significantly more often for patients with mCSPC than mCRPC (48% vs 40%; p < 0.05). Broader HRR gene mutation panel testing was infrequent (mCSPC and mCRPC: 16% each) (Figure 5(A)).

Amid patients who underwent genetic testing (n = 850), HRR genes most frequently tested were BRCA1 (92%; France: 96%, Germany: 92%, United States: 89%) and BRCA2 (79%; France: 90%, Germany: 74%, United States: 74%), followed by ATM (26%; France: 16%, Germany: 21%, United States: 35%) (Figure 5(C)). BRCA testing was conducted for 47% of patients who saw medical oncologists versus 25% who saw urologists, and the difference was more pronounced for mCRPC (52% vs 27%) than mCSPC (37% vs 23%).

Tissue was the most common sample used to perform testing, with no significant differences between patients with mCRPC versus mCSPC (tissue: 59% vs 57%, blood: 43% vs 47%; saliva [oral rinse] or cheek [buccal]: 9% vs 7%).

Use of HRR gene testing in physicians’ treatment decisions

Where data were available allowing accurate determination of the timing of HRR gene testing in relation to treatment among tested patients, an HRRm result was known before starting initial mCSPC treatment for 65% (141/218) of patients and before starting first-line mCRPC treatment for 17% (102/593) of patients (Fig S3).

Some clinical factors were found to significantly influence treatment choice depending upon HRRm status (Tables S2 and S3). For example, among patients with nonHRRm mCSPC, those with no visceral metastases were significantly more likely to receive treatment with ADT alone (25% vs 13%) or NHT ± ADT (50% vs 38%) than those with visceral metastases (p < 0.0001), and those with visceral metastases (vs no visceral metastases) were numerically more likely than to receive a regimen involving chemotherapy (36% vs 17%). Among patients with HRRm mCSPC, use of chemotherapy regimens was slightly higher for those with than without visceral metastases (45% vs 26%).

Some clinical factors also significantly influenced treatment choice for patients with mCRPC depending on HRRm status (Fig S4). For example, among patients with nonHRRm mCRPC, those with visceral metastases were significantly more likely than those without visceral metastases (odds ratio = 3.303; p = 0.001) to receive NHT + chemotherapy ± ADT than NHT ± ADT. Among patients with HRRm mCRPC, those with metastatic disease at initial PCa diagnosis were significantly more likely than those initially without metastatic disease (relative risk ratio = 0.2998, p = 0.010) to receive a PARPi ± ’other’ treatments than a NHT + chemotherapy + ADT.

Physicians reported that the treatment they prescribed as first-line for patients with BRCAm mCRPC was either a PARPi (34%) or a regimen involving chemotherapy (40%). “Health insurance covered the full cost of treatment” was the predominant reason among patients whose HRRm test result was obtained before starting treatment (initial mCSPC treatment: 67%; first-line mCRPC treatment: 71%).

Physician-reported challenges to genetic testing

Noting that HRR gene testing rates increased between DSP Waves IV and V, the main challenges to testing experienced by physicians in both waves were “cost of test is too high” (42% and 38%, respectively), “not reimbursed” (28% and 29%), “inadequate sample available” (21% and 27%), and “time delay in getting test results/turnaround time is long” (27% and 26%) (Table 3). Between Waves IV and V, “patient refusal” significantly increased (12% vs 24%; p = 0.0070), while “not recommended in the guidelines I follow” (20% vs 9%; p < 0.0050) significantly decreased, as challenges (Table 3). “Mutation results are not actionable,” which was not a response option in Wave IV, was a challenge for 21% of physicians in Wave V.

Patient-reported information about genetic testing

Of 538 patients with PRFs and matching PSC forms, 57% did not speak or could not recall speaking with their healthcare provider (HCP) about genetic testing related to their PCa (Figure S5A). Numerically more patients from the United States (63%) than France (45%) or Germany (38%) indicated their HCP had not discussed genetic testing with them. Patients had discussions about genetic testing with medical oncologists more often than urologists (46% and 14%, respectively), and patients who had discussions were commonly younger ( < 75 [47%] vs ≥ 75 [25%] years old). Among 213 (40%) patients who discussed genetic testing with their HCPs, 83% subsequently had a test. The most common reason to get tested, according to approximately half (49%) of patients, was to assess eligibility for certain PCa treatments (Figure S5B)—31% had a genetic test that was recommended by their HCP but did not recall the reason. Only 2% of patients reported testing was not suggested by their HCP but they requested it. Few (6%) patients thought they had a genetic test but did not according to their physician.

According to matched PRF/PSC data, 25 patients at initial mCSPC treatment and 99 at first-line mCRPC treatment had received a genetic test (BRCA1, BRCA2, or other HRR gene), had results known before treatment initiation, and discussed those results with their HCP before treatment initiation (Figure S6). Knowing and discussing patients’ HRRm status before starting treatment impacted physicians’ treatment choice, and choice of treatment was primarily dependent on whether or not “health insurance covered the full cost of treatment” (initial mCSPC treatment: 76%; first-line mCRPC treatment: 68%).

When asked how they felt about having a genetic test done and having the results discussed with them, 42% and 30% of patients, respectively, did not recall having any specific feeling (Figure S5C). Among patients who reported feelings, some positive numerical shifts (e.g., more “relieved” and “reassured,” and less “numb”) were observed between having a test done and discussing results, although some patients reported greater negative feelings (less “hopeful” and more “frustrated”) after discussing their results. Interestingly, differences in feelings between being tested and discussing results varied by country. For example, the proportion of patients who were “hopeful” about being tested and discussing results was similar in France (36% vs 37%, respectively), whereas numerically fewer patients in the United States (23% vs 8%) and Germany (6% vs 2%) felt “hopeful” about being tested versus discussing results. Also, feeling “worried” about being tested versus discussing results was reported more frequently by patients in France (25% vs 8%) and the United States (12% vs 4%) than Germany (14% vs 22%). Overall, 49% of patients reported that knowing the result of their genetic test helped them in some way.

Discussion

Findings from this study of physicians and their consulting patients with mPCa provide useful evidence and insights into a variety of aspects of genetic testing—testing rates, which are increasing but remain suboptimal; characteristics of patients who were tested, including those who had HRR gene alterations, and timing and types of testing, which are somewhat but not wholly consistent with guideline recommendations; the use of testing in treatment choices, which is primarily to inform treatment decisions; challenges to testing, which are not insurmountable; and patients’ feelings about testing, which may help inform physician-patient communications.

As mentioned, germline and somatic mutations in HRR genes render cancer cells vulnerable to PARPis, making precision medicine a valid approach in PCa. Identification of mutated HRR genes in up to 30% of patients with mCRPC [9–16] led to the approval of these targeted therapies, which have the potential to improve survival outcomes [34–36]. Between 2020 and 2021, PARPi use in patients with HRRm mCRPC was approved by regulatory bodies in the United States (rucaparib [37] and olaparib [38]) and Europe, Japan, and China (olaparib [39–41]). Since then, approvals for use of PARPi combinations (e.g., talazoparib/enzalutamide, niraparib/abiraterone acetate + prednisone, olaparib/abiraterone acetate + prednisone) in mPCa prompted updates to guidelines, wherein germline and/or somatic HRR gene panel testing is actively recommended to identify PCa patients who will potentially benefit [3,42–48]. Of note, the ARW PCa DSP Wave-V survey was conducted at a critical pivot point in mPCa management, immediately after initial PARPi approvals and before approval of many PARPi combination treatments.

In the present analysis of data from two separate ARW PCa DSPs conducted in the United States, France, and Germany—three highly medically advanced countries—HRR gene testing almost doubled between Wave IV (2020) and Wave V (2022–2023), overall (26% vs 45%) and in the mCSPC (23% vs 34%) and mCRPC (29% vs 43%) settings. These findings are similar to those from a cross-sectional, international survey of physicians (August to December 2022; United States, EU5, Japan, and China), where proportions of patients with mCRPC recommended for testing increased from 20% before to 50% after PARPi approval and from 25% before to 50% after guideline updates [49]. Findings from other studies also show testing rates have been increasing [24,50–52]. In one study using the US Flatiron Health database [50], the HRR testing rate was 13% among patients with mCRPC between 2013 to 2019; by year, rates were 0% in 2013, 33% in 2014, 41% in 2015, 57% in 2016, 53% in 2017 and 74% in 2018 [50]. In other studies, HRR testing rates were 18.1% among patients with mCRPC from France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom (2020; ARW PCa DSP) [51]; 28% among patients with mCRPC from the United States (2020; ARW PCa DSP) [52]; and 30.4% among US patients initiating first-line mCRPC therapy on or after mCRPC diagnosis (2013 to 2022; Flatiron Health database) [24]. Finally, in the aforementioned international survey (2022), 60% of patients with mCRPC were recommended for HRR gene testing, among whom 65% received HRR gene testing and 60% were recommended solely for BRCA mutation testing [49]. Consistent with other real-world studies [49–51], BRCA and ATM were the most commonly tested HRR genes across analyses, and germline BRCA testing was used significantly more often for patients with mCSPC than mCRPC in the Wave-V survey. More than 90% of physicians who performed genetic testing in the present study reported frequently or always testing for BRCA, saying it was an essential step before starting treatment. Overall, more patients were tested if they had private insurance (vs public healthcare), were treated in academic (vs community) settings, were treated by a medical oncologist (vs urologist), or were treated by physicians with (vs without) clinical trial experience.

In the current study, and in the prior international survey [49], ≥50% of patients with mCRPC were not recommended for and/or did not receive HRR gene testing. Interestingly, though, only 9% of physicians reported that testing was “not recommended in the guidelines”—an 11% decrease from the Wave-IV survey. Additional challenges to testing were financial concerns (e.g., cost, not reimbursed), which were more of a barrier for urologists than medical oncologists; patient refusal, which significantly increased between evaluation periods; low sample availability; and delays in getting results. Prior reports have identified similar and additional patient (racial and geographic location disparities), clinical (patient selection, test timing), and technical challenges (sample selection [blood, tumor, circulating DNA], size, and processing; gene selection; archival sample retrieval/viability; and testing strategies) that contribute to widespread adoption of genetic testing in patients with PCa [36,53,54]. PARPis were not indicated for mCSPC at the time of the Wave-V survey, and physicians were less likely to request genetic testing without an actionable treatment option, likely accounting for the lower rate of HRR gene testing among patients with mCSPC.

According to guidelines, germline HRR testing is recommended in men with a positive family history of PCa or other relevant cancers or known germline variants; a personal history of breast cancer; certain ancestries; and high-risk/very high-risk localized, regional, or mPCa, regardless of family history/ancestry [3,4,42–48]. A positive germline test at initial PCa diagnosis, in addition to identifying whether or not a cancer is hereditary, may help inform treatment decisions later in the treatment pathway [3–6,42,55]. Testing for somatic HRR gene alterations is recommended in patients with mPCa [3,4,42–48] or in patients with localized PCa, only where results can impact clinical decision-making, and upon metastasis and at key stages of progression (e.g., mCRPC diagnosis) because HRR status can change over the disease course [3–6,42,55,56]. Results of somatic testing also are used to inform treatment decisions [57]. Some guidelines include specific recommendations for which HRR genes (e.g., BRCA) and/or panels to evaluate for germline and/or somatic mutations [3,4,43,46,48].

According to Wave-V survey findings, key patient factors driving HRR gene testing were young age (physician defined) at diagnosis, family history of cancer, and disease severity/progression (e.g., mPCa diagnosis, increasing PSA trend, emergence of castration-resistance). Though physicians reported testing 45% of their patients at initial PCa diagnosis, only 31% of patients received initial testing at this timepoint according to patients’ charts; most had been tested by the time of the diagnosis of mCRPC. These findings are in line with at least one analyses where next-generation sequence testing within one year of PCa diagnosis increased from 1.3% (95% CI: 1.3%, 1.3%) in 2015 to 32.7% (95% CI: 32.6%, 32.8%) in 2022 [58]. Of note, timing of the first reported instance of genetic testing for PCa in the present analysis differed significantly based on practice setting, with academic HCPs first testing earlier than community HCPs, and physician specialty, with medical oncologists first testing earlier than urologists.

In the Wave-V survey, approximately 38% of patients in the combined mCSPC/pseudorandom mCRPC cohort who were tested had ≥1 HRR gene mutation, and—in line with prior findings [9–16]—BRCA was most frequently tested (92% of patients), and mutations in BRCA were most commonly identified. Almost all (95%) testing physicians said they “frequently or always” test for BRCA. HRR genes other than BRCA may have been less frequently tested, and corresponding mutations less frequently identified, for a variety of reasons: HRR gene testing beyond BRCA may not have been well established at the time of the survey and/or was not well captured in patient records, possibly because of insufficient knowledge of the prognostic/predictive relevance of nonBRCAm in PCa and/or the lack of treatment options for nonBRCAm mCRPC at the time of the survey; only BRCA testing was recommended for mPCa in some country-specific guidelines; an HRR panel was not available at the time of the survey; among HRR genes, only BRCA testing was available to physicians; physicians believed the poor prognosis associated with PCa was driven by BRCAm versus other HRR gene mutations; small (vs large) next-generation sequencing panels, or only BRCA testing, were preferentially reimbursed; and/or patients had a known family history of a specific BRCA mutation and a BRCA test was ordered to confirm germline BRCA status.

Positivity rates among patients with mCRPC in the present study generally are higher than those from prior analyses (HRRm: 23.7% [US] [50], 24.3% [US] [24], 24.6% [US] [59], 31% [Germany] [16], 33.7% [US, Europe, Japan] [51], and 35.7% [Japan] [60,61]; BRCAm: 9.2% [US] [59], 12.6% [Japan] [60,61], 13.4% [US] [50], and 21% [Germany] [16]). The slightly higher mutation positivity rates reported in this survey suggest that, in real-world settings, physicians may consciously be testing patients more likely to have these genetic mutations due to known risk factors (e.g., younger age, family history, or speed of disease progression). Interestingly, significantly more patients with mCSPC than mCRPC who were tested had an ATM mutation—a finding worthy of further exploration. Consistent with prior reports [50,51,61], patients with mutations were significantly younger than those without mutations, and significantly more patients with mutations had a family history of cancer and were progressing faster (increasing PSA trends) than those without mutations.

Results of genetic testing can greatly impact disease management and, as mentioned, should be an essential part of assessing and treating patients with PCa. Pathogenic HRR gene alterations are associated with early disease onset, aggressive tumors, high recurrence rates, and poor prognosis [11,14,17,19,21–26]. BRCA mutations confer the highest risk for developing aggressive PCa and are associated with the worst outcomes, particularly in the mPCa setting [11,16,19,22,25,26,62–64], and ATM alterations increase the risk of developing PCa [17,20,26,62], Moreover, patients with BRCA and ATM mutations are significantly more likely to die of PCa, be significantly younger when they die, and have a shorter time to death after diagnosis than those without these alterations [17]. HRR gene testing at key points (such as upon metastasis and progression to mCRPC) can help ensure identification of patients who are suitable for treatment with a PARPi. Hence, testing for HRR gene alterations, particularly BRCAm, early and at key decision points in the disease course is important for risk stratification and treatment decision-making, and to inform the risk of familial cancers in patients’ relatives [42,47]. Four in 10 patients discussed genetic testing with their HCPs, and ≥8 in 10 of them subsequently had a test. Future research could explore whether the percentage of patients talking to doctors about genetic testing increases, and whether discussing testing influences test timing and/or leads to altered treatment pathways and patient outcomes.

In the current study, physicians did—in fact—report using genetic testing results to inform treatment decisions, determine risk in family members, and predict the likelihood of progression. In addition to HRR gene mutation status, physicians considered age, metastases at initial diagnosis, and presence of visceral metastases when making treatment decisions. For half of patients, testing was done to determine treatment eligibility. Though the proportion of patients with mCRPC who were prescribed PARPis was low (10%) among patients with mCRPC in the United States, Germany, and France, prescribing rates were higher when HRRm or BRCAm results were known (36% and 34%, respectively) or were known and discussed with patients before initiating first-line treatment (35% and 37%). Notably, 56% of physicians in Wave V indicated they were more likely to test for HRR gene mutations following the availability of PARPis. Examining records from future research, such as the ARW DSP, will be important to assess whether or not the addition of PARPi combinations to the treatment pathway influences treatment choices and patient outcomes. Guidelines are readily adopting these PARPi combinations, so understanding remaining challenges from both the physician and patient perspective will be important.

In addition to the aforementioned challenges with genetic testing, urologists reported deficient educational materials/training and support for patients with regards to genetic testing; these factors were less challenging for medical oncologists. In another survey, lack of time, expertise, and knowledge about testing; low counselor availability; and inadequate co-ordination of referrals also were reported as challenges faced by physicians [65]. At least in part because of the myriad of challenges, and despite guideline recommendations, many patients in the present study who could have benefited from testing were not tested. Moreover, earlier testing—before mCRPC—likely would have increased identification of at-risk patients and helped inform subsequent treatment decisions. Future endeavors should concentrate on raising community physicians’—particularly urologists’—awareness of and education about genetic testing in PCa; appropriate patient selection and timing of testing, with a focus on the importance and advantages of early testing; and the benefit of PARPis for patients with ≥1 HRR gene alteration. These efforts should encourage more testing overall, and in patients with mCRPC specifically, thereby increasing eligible patients’ access to treatment with PARPis.

In summary, our findings indicate a need for solutions from a systems perspective to improve testing operations, boost physician support (educational and logistical), minimize healthcare system costs, and smooth the path for testing reimbursement. In addition, patients undergoing genetic testing likely would benefit from enhanced discussions about genetic testing and emotional support from their HCPs. This knowledge and support could enable patients—if they wish—to be more proactive in their disease management, including becoming better informed about treatment options. Further research into the emotions and attitudes of patients about genetic testing may enhance their willingness to discuss testing with their physician and ultimately undergo testing. For example, according to findings from a cross-sectional, online, quantitative survey [66], while patients with mCRPC—being driven by hope—were highly aware of and willing to explore genetic testing, they required additional information and emotional support to understand the benefits and implications of genetic testing, and make informed decisions regarding its use.

This study has several strengths, including its cross-sectional nature and a well-established, multi-perspective data source that uses a validated methodology, which employs consistent and recognized survey techniques that have been used for > 25 years. DSPs are objective and impartial, real-world data sources with large, statistically robust numbers of patients who represent a wider population profile than clinical trial populations. DSPs also reflect current clinical practice that are indicative of the implementation of guidelines or recommendations. The AWR DSP also has inherent limitations. It does not include patients enrolled in clinical trials and includes data on consulting/presenting patients only (at the time of data abstraction); patients who less frequently consult their HCPs may not be captured. Furthermore, the characteristics of patients included in the DSP may be different from the general mPCa population, so testing may be lower in clinical practices that were not included. The DSP is cross-sectional, captured from the perspective of physicians and their consulting patients, which should be considered when comparing findings from longitudinal studies that capture data from the same patients over time. Responses to questions in the DSP were limited to the options provided; although efforts were made to allow a degree of flexibility by allowing “other” as an option, some alternative responses may not have been captured. Despite the large number of total patients in the mPCa DSP, patients within some sub-cohorts may have been too small for robust estimates. Lastly, recall bias, which is a common limitation of surveys, might also have affected responses of both physicians and patients. While physicians had the ability to refer to patients’ records while completing the PRF, minimizing the possibility of recall bias, the information in the PRF may be limited by the data documented, physicians’ interpretation of the data, or both. Finally, though both physicians’ and patients’ perspectives are reported, we acknowledge that most of our commentary is from PRFs rather than PSC forms and more exploration is possible from the patient perspective. Other survey methods [66] can provide more detailed and qualitative patient perspectives.

Conclusions

Since the introduction and approval of PARPis in mPCa, genetic testing rates in the surveyed countries almost doubled, likely because of adherence to guidelines and improved knowledge about the importance of testing in choosing the optimal treatment. Genetic testing of patients with mPCa influenced physicians’ treatment decisions in the initial years following PARPi availability. As experience with testing improves and result timelines become expedited, genetic profiles are expected to play an even greater role in treatment decisions. Whilst conversations between physicians and patients about genetic testing can increase and improve, patients in Wave V who were tested reported mixed feelings toward testing—some were relieved/reassured, but others were less hopeful and more frustrated. Greater understanding of patients’ experience with testing and the subsequent benefits of treatment options for patients with HRR gene mutations is needed in mPCa.
