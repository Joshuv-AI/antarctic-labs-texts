Clinical and radiographic outcomes of a novel posterior occlusal splint in adults with temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis: a retrospective case series
Authors: Zhenlong Liu, Junxi Feng, Liwen Huang, Yunyi Yuan, Rong Zhang, Xingyang Li, Qingbin Zhang, Antong Wu, Wei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the clinical and radiographic outcomes associated with the use of a novel posterior occlusal splint (nPOS) in adults with temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) and its impact on condylar morphology, as assessed by cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Methods and materialsThis retrospective study included 156 adult patients (24 males, 132 females; mean age: 27.05 ± 9.18 years) diagnosed with TMJ-OA, with 182 osteoarthritis (OA)-like condyles. All patients received treatment with the nPOS between January 2020 and December 2024. Clinical symptoms were evaluated at six time points: T0 (baseline), T1 (1 month), T2 (3 months), T3 (6 months), T4 (12 months), and T5 (24 months) after treatment initiation. CBCT assessments of condylar morphology were performed at T0, T3, T4, and T5. Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired t test, McNemar test were employed to evaluate longitudinal changes in clinical symptoms.ResultsSignificant improvements in maximum mouth opening (MMO) were observed from T2 to T4 (P < 0.01). Significant reductions in spontaneous pain were observed at both T3 and T4 (P < 0.01), while reductions in joint tenderness and joint noises were statistically confirmed at T3 (P < 0.01). No significant changes were noted for intermittent closed lock (ICL), muscle pain, push-back mandibular test, or altered mouth opening patterns (P > 0.01). CBCT showed a significant improvement in condylar cortical score at T3 compared to T0 (P < 0.01).ConclusionIn this observational study, the use of nPOS was associated with improvements in key TMJ-OA symptoms and condylar morphology, especially during the early to mid-treatment phase (3–12 months). These findings warrant validation in randomized controlled trials before clinical adoption. Introduction

Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMD) comprise a group of heterogeneous conditions affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and the associated masticatory muscles. The global prevalence of TMD ranges from 24.3% to 45.7% [1], with rates as high as 70% reported among young adults aged 18 to 40 years [2]. Common clinical manifestations include joint sounds (e.g., clicking or crepitus), pain, and restricted or abnormal mandibular movement [3, 4]. It is well established that prolonged mechanical overloading can trigger adaptive remodeling within the TMJ. This remodeling may lead to alterations in condylar bone morphology and, in some cases, contribute to the onset and progression of TMJ osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) [5, 6]. Notably, in adolescent patients, the incidence of condylar bone resorption has been reported to reach 40.7%, and this rate may be even higher in adults due to longer disease duration and increased joint loading [7]. Therefore, greater attention should be given to the diagnosis and management of this patient population.

The conservative treatment system for TMJ-OA includes physiotherapy, pharmacological interventions, and occlusal splint therapy. Among these, occlusal splint therapy disrupts the pathological neuromuscular feedback loop, optimizes the distribution of occlusal forces, and helps restore the biomechanical balance among the condyle, articular disc, and glenoid fossa [8–10]. Current occlusal splint therapy for TMD primarily encompasses two main categories: stabilizing splints (SS) and anterior restraint splints (ARS). The SS aim to achieve stable, reproducible mandibular positioning and reduce muscle activity. However, they may fail to adequately relieve posterior joint loading in some cases of osteoarthritis. Furthermore, an excessive increase in the anterior vertical dimension can induce mandibular posterior rotation, which increases the risk of long-term occlusal disturbance, particularly in patients with an anterior open bite or severe tooth wear [11]. The ARS attempt to reposition displaced articular discs by guiding mandibular movement; however, they carry a risk of inducing permanent occlusal changes [12]. Consequently, there is a critical clinical need for a device that can effectively reduce joint load while minimizing iatrogenic risks and enhancing comfort, which is particularly important for adult osteoarthritis patients with diminished adaptive capacity. Posterior occlusal splints were first introduced by Gelb in 1991 and have been reported to reduce excessive mechanical stress within the TMJ region, thereby contributing to pain relief [13]. Conventional posterior occlusal splints typically feature a resin connector in the anterior region; however, prolonged use is often associated with mucosal tenderness, speech disturbances, and a pronounced foreign body sensation [14]. To address these issues, a novel posterior occlusal splint (nPOS) was developed, replacing the traditional anterior resin connector with a palatal free archwire. This modification aims to enhance patient comfort while preserving therapeutic efficacy. Our previous research demonstrated that the nPOS may better accommodate the biomechanical requirements of the TMJ by enhancing posterior support, alleviating anterior pressure, reducing intra-articular loading, and decreasing stress on the masticatory muscles [15]. However, adult patients with TMJ-OA often face persistent challenges due to progressive condylar bone resorption, diminished bone remodeling capacity, and limited joint compensatory adaptability—factors that distinguish them from adolescent patients. Effective treatment must therefore achieve a delicate balance between symptom relief and the maintenance of occlusal and articular stability [16, 17]. In this context, it is essential to further investigate the clinical efficacy of the nPOS in the management of adult TMJ-OA patients.

Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical records and CBCT imaging data of adult patients with TMJ-OA who received treatment with the nPOS. The primary aims were to: (1) describe changes of nPOS in alleviating core TMJ-OA symptoms such as pain, joint sounds, and limited mouth opening; (2) assess longitudinal changes through CBCT-based cortical scoring, thereby revealing potential improvements in condylar morphology; and (3) explore the clinical applicability and limitations of the nPOS, ultimately providing evidence-based guidance for individualized treatment strategies in adult TMJ-OA management.

The nPOS is recommended for nighttime use, as it modifies the occlusal relationship to reduce mechanical stress on the temporomandibular joint and masticatory muscles, thereby alleviating joint pain and improving limited mouth opening. Patients are generally instructed to wear the device for approximately 10 h per day, with monthly follow-up visits for assessment and adjustment of splint thickness.

Participants and treatment

This retrospective study included patients diagnosed with TMJ-OA between January 2020 and December 2024 at the Department of Temporomandibular Joint, Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Ethic number: LCYJ20250616006). The nPOS was designed as an improved version of the traditional posterior occlusal splint, sharing the same biomechanical principles and mechanism of action. All patients provided written informed consent for occlusal splint therapy as part of routine clinical care. The fabrication and application of the nPOS followed standard prosthodontic principles using approved dental materials. Inclusion criteria were: age over 18 years (adult) [18]. A confirmed diagnosis of TMJ-OA, established using the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) and corroborated by CBCT imaging analysis according to Ahmad et al. [19, 20], including evidence of osteophytes, erosion, or subchondral sclerosis. And completion of at least 3 months of follow-up following treatment with the nPOS. Exclusion criteria included: presence of congenital craniofacial anomalies or history of maxillofacial trauma, prior treatment with other types of occlusal splints or surgical interventions, and incomplete clinical or imaging data. No NSAIDs, physiotherapy, orthodontic treatment, or intra-articular injections were administered during the study period. Compliance with the prescribed nPOS schedule was monitored at each follow-up visit. Follow-up clinical evaluations were conducted at six time points: T0 (baseline), T1 (1 month), T2 (3 months), T3 (6 months), T4 (12 months), and T5 (24 months). CBCT assessments were performed at T0, T3, T4, and T5 following the initiation of nPOS treatment. Given the retrospective, single-arm design, this study represents a case series aimed at describing outcomes and generating hypotheses, rather than establishing causal efficacy.

Clinical examinations

Data collection included demographic information (age and gender) and clinical symptom assessments obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical records, as well as CBCT imaging data retrieved from the hospital’s imaging database. Clinical symptoms were assessed using standardized criteria previously reported by our group: (1) Joint noises: During mouth opening, closing, and lateral movements, abnormal sounds (clicking or crepitus) were assessed by auscultation and/or palpation. (2) Intermittent closed lock (ICL): The patient reported transient locking during mouth opening that could be self-resolved. (3) Spontaneous pain: Patient-reported joint pain present at rest. (4) Joint tenderness: Finger pressure was applied to the lateral and posterior regions of both TMJs; pain reported by the patient was recorded. (5) Push-back mandibular test: The examiner gently pushed the mandible backward and upward; reproduction or aggravation of joint pain was considered a positive finding, indicating posterior capsular inflammation. (6) Mouth opening pattern: The mandibular movement trajectory during natural maximum opening was observed and classified as linear, deviated, or corrected deviation. (7) Maximum mouth opening (MMO): The vertical distance (mm) between the maxillary and mandibular central incisors was measured using a vernier caliper, repeated three times, and averaged. (8) Muscle pain: Bilateral masticatory muscles (e.g., masseter and temporalis) were palpated and patient-reported pain was recorded. (9) Limited mouth opening: An MMO < 40 mm was defined as restricted mouth opening, consistent with our previous protocol [21].

CBCT imaging analysis

CBCT Imaging acquisition was standardized using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans (NewTom, Italy) with the following parameters: tube voltage of 110 kV, automatic tube current modulation, and an effective exposure time of 3.6 s. Image analysis was conducted using Greenlander software. The severity of TMJ-OA is mainly determined by the degree and extent of bone destructive lesions, referring to the imaging assessment system of Ahmad et al. [19, 20]: Score 0 (None): Intact cortical bone with no visible changes. Score 1 (Mild): Rough or discontinuous cortical bone surface, not reaching cancellous bone. Score 2 (Moderate): Wear of cortical and cancellous bone, depth ≤ 2 mm below cortical surface. Score 3 (Severe): Extensive wear of cortical and cancellous bone, depth > 2 mm below cortical surface. To further quantify the spatial distribution of lesions, the articular surface of the condyle was divided into nine anatomical regions using a grid partitioning method (Fig. 2). The scores from all nine regions were aggregated to calculate the final condylar cortical score, ranging from 0 to 36. Treatment efficacy was assessed dynamically by comparing pre-treatment and post-treatment CBCT imaging analysis score difference (post-treatment score - pre-treatment score). Improvement was defined as a reduction in defect area (Fig. 3); stable condition referred to unchanged lesion extent and grading; and deterioration indicated an increase in lesion area or a higher grade. This multidimensional evaluation model integrates both morphological changes and clinical progression, offering a standardized and objective framework for assessing therapeutic outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables, including MMO and condylar cortical scores, are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine data distribution. To strictly ensure statistical independence and avoid pseudo replication, the patient was defined as the statistical unit of analysis (n = 156). Bilateral condylar cortical scores were aggregated to calculate a total condylar burden score for each participant. This composite metric represents the cumulative osteoarthritic disease load at the patient level, acknowledging that bilateral involvement constitutes a greater total pathological burden than unilateral involvement. Consequently, all longitudinal comparisons and inferential statistics were conducted exclusively on this patient-level dataset, ensuring that the degrees of freedom accurately reflected the number of subjects. The SPSS statistical calculation methods for T0 and T3 of 3.5 CBCT condylar morphology are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. For comparisons of pre- and post-treatment data, paired t-tests were used for normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and changes over time were assessed using the McNemar test. To control for the family-wise error rate across multiple time-point comparisons, a Bonferroni-type adjustment was applied, and a stricter two-tailed P-value of < 0.01 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Basic characteristics

A total of 156 adult patients diagnosed with TMJ-OA were included in this study, comprising 24 males (15.3%) and 132 females (84.7%). The detailed flow of patient inclusion, treatment, and data availability throughout the study period was systematically recorded (Fig. 4). The age of participants ranged from 18 years and older, with a mean age of 27.05 ± 9.18 years. CBCT condylar morphology

A total of 156 patients were included in the baseline (T0) radiographic analysis, and interobserver reliability for categorical grading of condylar cortical bone changes was assessed using a randomly selected subset of 20 patients. A weighted Cohen’s kappa (κ) of 0.82 was obtained, reflecting strong agreement between raters. Further processing the condylar score into three categories: improved/not change/deterioration, we found that 63.5% of patients showed condylar improvement, 17.7% showed no change, and 18.8% showed deterioration at T3 following nPOS treatment (Table 4).

Discussion

The study is now explicitly characterized as a retrospective case series, and all findings are interpreted as preliminary observational evidence. Comparisons with conventional stabilization splints are presented descriptively rather than inferentially to avoid overstating comparative effectiveness and to maintain appropriate methodological rigor [22]. This study highlights several key advances in occlusal splint therapy for TMJ-OA across device design, target population, and evaluation methodology. First, in terms of device design, the nPOS replaces the conventional anterior resin connector with a palatal free archwire. This modification preserves the biomechanical advantages of posterior occlusal support while substantially improving patient comfort and long-term treatment compliance. Second, with respect to the study population, the present research specifically focuses on adult patients with TMJ-OA, a group that has been relatively underrepresented in previous splint-related studies, which have primarily involved adolescents or patients with predominantly myofascial temporomandibular disorders. Third, and most importantly, the evaluation framework integrates longitudinal clinical assessments conducted at six predefined time points with a grid-based CBCT scoring system for condylar cortical bone. This combined clinical–radiographic approach links symptomatic improvement with objective morphological changes, enabling a multidimensional assessment of therapeutic efficacy that has rarely been applied in prior studies of occlusal splint therapy for TMJ-OA.

In terms of specific clinical parameters, analysis revealed that treatment with the nPOS resulted in a statistically significant increase in MMO, with an average improvement of 3.11 mm compared to baseline after a treatment duration of three to six months. This improvement was sustained over time, with comparable gains observed at the 6-to-12-month follow-up. Limited mouth opening in patients with TMJ osteoarthritis is often associated with painful inflammation and structural lesions. Therefore, any reproducible, pain-free increase in mouth opening may directly translate into functional benefits and improved quality of life, such as facilitating eating, maintaining oral hygiene, and undergoing dental treatment. Therefore, improvement in mouth opening should be regarded as a positive, comprehensive indicator of functional recovery. By contrast, previous studies reported a mean MMO increase of 15.77 mm after three months of treatment with SS in patients with disc displacement [23] reflects the reversible nature of soft tissue restriction, while in our TMJ-OA cohort, more often it is due to pain-induced functional limitations. Furthermore, the nPOS demonstrated a notable time-sensitive advantage over the ARS, which typically requires continuous intervention for up to six months before significant improvements in mouth opening are observed [24]. By providing uniform posterior support and redistributing occlusal loads, the splint likely reduces compressive stress within the temporomandibular joint compartment and attenuates pain-related reflex inhibition of the masticatory muscles. The observation that functional improvement precedes substantial pain relief suggests that mechanical decompression and normalization of joint kinematics may rapidly promote a less guarded and more physiological range of mandibular motion, even while inflammatory processes are still undergoing modulation.

Joint pain in TMJ-OA is mechanistically linked to synovial inflammation, abnormal muscle function, and mechanical overloading. The occlusal splint improves the pain associated with TMJ-OA by reestablishing occlusal mechanical equilibrium, decreasing intra-articular pressure, and optimizing the distribution of stresses [25]. In current study, the nPOS demonstrated statistically significant relief of spontaneous pain in T3 and T4. Notably, our findings at T3 are consistent with the long-term efficacy reported for traditional SS. According to Akbulut et al. [26], 84% of patients experienced complete resolution of TMJ pain after six months of treatment with a 3 mm SS. Interestingly, significant improvements in MMO were observed at T2, preceding the statistically significant alleviation of pain at T3. In contrast, statistically significant reductions in spontaneous pain and joint tenderness were observed at the 6-month follow-up (T3). This temporal dissociation between functional improvement and pain relief is consistent with the pathophysiology of TMJ-OA, in which pain is driven not only by mechanical overload but also by persistent synovial inflammation and biochemical cascades involving cytokines and neuropeptides [27]. By establishing a more favorable biomechanical environment, the nPOS may progressively reduce mechanical stimuli that sustain inflammatory signaling, thereby producing a delayed yet durable analgesic effect. This sequence—where functional improvement precedes meaningful pain reduction—also underscores the importance of patient education, reassurance, and adherence during the early phases of splint therapy.

Joint noises in TMJ-OA are primarily attributed to disc displacement and surface irregularities of the condyle [28]. In the present study, a significant reduction in joint noises observed at T3 suggests that the nPOS may enhance coordination among the condyle, disc, and glenoid fossa [29, 30]. While ARS have been shown to be highly effective in eliminating joint noises [31], a potential drawback lies in their design: the forced condylar repositioning achieved through anterior occlusal coverage may increase the risk of developing an anterior open bite [32]. In contrast, the nPOS, by improving posterior occlusal contact morphology without excessively increasing anterior vertical dimension, thereby minimizing the risk of occlusal changes [11]. Therefore, despite the longer duration required to achieve statistical significance in noise reduction compared to aggressive repositioning strategies, the nPOS offers a biomechanically favorable alternative for TMJ-OA patients, providing both symptom relief and enhanced occlusal stability. With regard to ICL symptoms, although some patients reported subjective relief of symptoms and data demonstrated an objective trend towards improvement, the improvement rate did not reach statistical significance. This is likely attributable to the limited number of patients manifesting this particular symptom within the study cohort, exacerbated by the attrition of subjects during the follow-up period. Consequently, the current data are insufficient to draw definitive statistical conclusions about the efficacy of nPOS for ICL.

Pathological cortical erosion in TMJ-OA is primarily driven by inflammatory mediators and biomechanical imbalance [28]. In this study, 63.5% of patients exhibited condylar cortical improvement at T3, accompanied by a statistically significant reduction in mean CBCT scores. Radiographic signs of condylar cortical bone repair often lag behind improvements in clinical symptoms, reflecting the intrinsically slow nature of bone remodeling and turnover. This temporal delay highlights the value of CBCT as an objective modality for monitoring medium- to long-term therapeutic effects that may not be immediately captured by symptom-based clinical assessments alone. These findings suggest that the nPOS may have re-established biomechanical equilibrium, potentially activating adaptive remodeling mechanisms in the subchondral bone trabeculae—consistent with the theory of mechanically induced bone repair [33]. A critical finding warranting in-depth discussion is that 18.8% (n = 16) of patients exhibited radiographic deterioration at T3. This means that the treatment works in different ways for different people, which is normal for TMJ-OA because it is a long-term condition. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that among adolescent and young adult patients treated with manual reduction combined with occlusal splint stabilization, 21.9% showed no improvement in condylar bone status. In contrast, the proportion of patients experiencing progressive condylar bone resorption in the non-intervention group reached 37.1% [24]. In adults with diminished bone remodeling capacity, the biological threshold for positive adaptation is likely higher. The “non-responder” phenotype in our cohort may be attributed to several factors: (1) Advanced disease stage: in such cases, patients may have already reached a point where their bones cannot be improved through treatment [34]. (2) Uncontrolled parafunction: persistent bruxism could overwhelm the biomechanical unloading provided by the nPOS. (3) Individual biological variance: differences in inflammatory mediator profiles (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) are known to critically influence the catabolic-anabolic balance in TMJ-OA [28]. In contrast, traditional hard SS lacks precise occlusal zoning for joint load control and suffers from insufficient long-term efficacy evidence [32, 34].

While the observed improvements are temporally associated with nPOS therapy, alternative or contributory explanations must be considered. First, the natural history of TMJ-OA is variable; some patients may experience spontaneous symptomatic fluctuation or even periods of remission. However, the improvement in symptoms (pain, MMO) and objective CBCT scores at T3 was significant in most patients. This suggests that natural history is not the only factor. Second, it is undeniable that patients were enrolled at the peak of their pain, and subsequent symptom relief might include a statistical effect of “regression to the mean”. However, the objective imaging indicator of CBCT scores also showed improvement, which suggests that the treatment had a real biological effect. Third, while patients’ reports might be affected by the placebo effect and more attention, the doctor’s examination and objective CBCT imaging show that nPOS has real therapeutic effects. Therefore, the most logical explanation is that the nPOS provides a therapeutic effects stem from the combined effects of nPOS’s biomechanical intervention and the natural progression of the disease in some patients.

Existing studies have demonstrated varying degrees of effectiveness among different types of occlusal splints in alleviating TMJ-OA symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of selecting personalized treatment strategies tailored to the disease stage, clinical subtype, and individual patient characteristics [35]. Within a stepped-care or individualized management framework for TMJ-OA, the nPOS may occupy a distinct niche. For patients presenting with painful loading and early degenerative changes, but without acute disc displacement requiring recapture, the nPOS offers a conservative intermediate option. It provides targeted mechanical intervention more specific than a full-coverage SS, yet is less invasive and morphologically disruptive than an ARS. Its design prioritizes compliance, making it suitable for long-term management aimed at modifying joint loading patterns and supporting natural adaptive processes. Future studies should incorporate multimodal evaluation approaches—such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electromyographic analysis of masticatory muscles, and mandibular motion trajectory tracking—to more accurately characterize structural and functional changes in the TMJ and objectively assess the therapeutic efficacy of the nPOS.

The retrospective and non-controlled design of this study inherently limits the ability to draw definitive causal inferences regarding the therapeutic efficacy of the nPOS. The most prominent limitation is the substantial loss to follow-up during long-term observation, which resulted in outcome data from only 12 patients being available at the 24-month time point (T5). Consequently, the statistical power at this stage is insufficient to support robust conclusions regarding long-term (> 12 months) treatment effectiveness, and the corresponding findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory in nature. In addition, the absence of parallel control groups—such as patients treated with conventional occlusal splints, those receiving no splint therapy, or those managed with alternative conservative interventions—markedly constrains the ability to attribute observed clinical or imaging changes specifically to the nPOS intervention. Despite these limitations, the present findings suggest that the nPOS may represent a promising conservative treatment option for TMD. These results warrant further validation through well-designed prospective, controlled studies, with particular emphasis on long-term outcomes and appropriate statistical modeling.

Conclusion

In this observational study, nPOS therapy was associated with improvements in the management of TMJ-OA symptoms. Significant improvements were observed in MMO, joint noises, joint tenderness, and spontaneous pain.
