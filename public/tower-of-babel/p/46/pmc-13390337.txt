Posterior-to-anterior periventricular white matter hyperintensity area ratio: a supportive MRI feature for differentiating cerebral amyloid angiopathy in lobar intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Yingjie Zhang, Sihui Wang, Xinrong Wu, Yao Zhong, Shengjun Sun, Yi Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnosing cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) in primary lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) remains challenging using routine MRI markers. We investigated a quantitative posterior-dominant pattern of periventricular white matter hyperintensity (PWMH) as a potential supportive imaging feature for CAA within the Boston v2.0 framework.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 229 patients aged ≥ 50 years with primary lobar ICH, classified as CAA (n = 123) or CAA-negative (n = 106) according to the Boston criteria v2.0. On FLAIR, the largest anterior and posterior periventricular WMH areas were measured bilaterally, and the posterior-to-anterior PWMH area ratio (PA-AR) was derived. Multivariable logistic regression models were developed, and diagnostic performance was evaluated using ROC analysis.ResultsPatients with CAA showed a greater posterior PWMH burden and higher PA-AR than CAA-negative controls (median 1.69 vs. 0.79; p < 0.001). Adding PA-AR to the covariate-adjusted model improved discrimination for CAA (AUC 0.804; bootstrap mean AUC 0.813, 95% CI 0.759–0.867). In the predefined diagnostic “gray zone” subgroup, PA-AR remained informative. The AUC was 0.887 in univariable analysis and 0.888 after age adjustment. PA-AR was also higher in patients with posterior lobar CMBs than in those with no CMBs or mixed-location hemorrhage. ConclusionsA posterior-dominant periventricular WMH pattern, quantified by PA-AR, is associated with CAA in lobar ICH. This pattern is also related to posterior-predominant lobar CMB topography. PA-AR may serve as a supportive MRI feature that complements Boston criteria v2.0 markers for identifying CAA within a real-world mixed lobar ICH population, particularly in diagnostically challenging patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04992-5.Keywords: Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy, Cerebral Hemorrhage, White Matter Hyperintensities, Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Background

For elderly populations, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a major cause of primary lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), marked by repeated bleeding episodes [1]. In CAA cases, MRI may reveal multiple cortical microbleeds (CMBs) and cortical superficial siderosis (cSS). Additionally, white matter changes are often observed, including severe perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale (CSO-PVS) and white matter hyperintensities (WMH) [2]. Unlike the peribasal ganglia distribution characteristic of arteriolosclerotic small vessel disease (aSVD), WMH associated with CAA often appear as multiple small, scattered subcortical lesions on MRI. Moreover, research has also shown a partial correlation between the number of these scattered WMH lesions and the presence of whole-cortex amyloid deposition [3, 4]. Consequently, multiple subcortical punctate WMH have been incorporated into the Boston Criteria v2.0 as a non-hemorrhagic marker, improving its diagnostic sensitivity for CAA [5].

Additionally, studies in cognitively healthy older adults show that periventricular white matter hyperintensity (PWMH), which is mainly found in the frontal and parietal lobes, has a significant correlation with PiB-PET-measured amyloid burden [6]. Further analyses using log-transformed semi-automated segmentation show that posterior-predominant PWMH is a feature of CAA [7]. These findings suggest that CAA may contribute to the pathogenesis of PWMH. However, it remains unclear whether this distribution pattern is valid for the clinical diagnosis of CAA. Furthermore, the characteristics of this pattern in patients with lobar ICH require further exploration and confirmation. Moreover, the mechanisms underlying this posterior predominance are still poorly understood. A key unanswered question is whether this PWMH pattern is intrinsically linked to the established posterior preference of CAA-related hemorrhagic lesions, such as CMBs.

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to identify and quantify spatial patterns of PWMH on routine MRI in patients with primary lobar ICH. A key objective was to examine whether a derived quantitative index could serve as an assistive MRI feature for distinguishing CAA within the Boston v2.0 framework. A second objective was to investigate the association between a posterior-dominant PWMH pattern and a posterior topography of hemorrhagic-event markers.

Study design and participants

We conducted a single-center retrospective observational study. Consecutive patients with primary lobar ICH admitted to the Department of Neurology, Beijing Tiantan Hospital (Capital Medical University), between January 1, 2019 and June 30, 2024 were screened. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of patient inclusion. CAA, cerebral amyloid angiopathy; lobar ICH, lobar intracerebral hemorrhage; CNS, central nervous system; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; SWI, susceptibility-weighted imaging; GRE, gradient-recalled echo

Eligible patients were ≥ 50 years old with primary lobar intracerebral hemorrhage. Exclusion criteria were: (1) deep symptomatic hemorrhage (basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, or brainstem); (2) absence of susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) or gradient-recalled echo (GRE) sequences; and (3) alternative causes on CT/MRI, including tumor, head trauma, vascular malformation, hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke, or central nervous system vasculitis. We also excluded patients with recent pre-admission anticoagulant medication use to avoid potential exacerbation of WMH, CMB, cSS, and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) [8].

CAA classification

We classified patients using the updated Boston criteria version 2.0 [5]. Those with neither deep ICH nor deep CMBs on SWI/GRE sequences were classified as having CAA; this included diagnoses of both probable and possible CAA. The remaining patients, who did not meet the Boston v2.0 imaging criteria for CAA, formed the CAA-negative group. This group is heterogeneous and may include patients with mixed small vessel disease (SVD) phenotypes. This spectrum could involve hypertension-related arteriolosclerosis, amyloid co-pathology, or both. Cerebellar bleeding lesions were classified as neither deep nor lobar hemorrhages.

Clinical variables

Baseline clinical variables included age, sex, history of ischemic stroke, history of ICH, and prior cognitive decline. Cardiovascular risk factors included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and smoking status. Stroke severity on admission was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [9, 10].

MRI acquisition and imaging marker assessment

MRI examinations were performed on 3.0 T scanners (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthcare; Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands; GE Discovery 750, General Electric Company). Detailed acquisition parameters are provided in Table S1.

All imaging assessments were performed by an experienced neurologist (Y. Zhang), who had five years of neuroimaging experience and was blinded to all clinical data. Any uncertainties in the assessments were adjudicated by a senior neuroradiologist with over 30 years of experience. Imaging markers were rated according to the STandards for ReportIng Vascular changes on nEuroimaging-version 2 (STRIVE-2) consensus criteria [11]. Assessments were performed using axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and SWI/GRE sequences. SWI served as the primary sequence for evaluating hemorrhagic markers. GRE was used only when SWI was unavailable. The distribution of SWI versus GRE usage between the study groups is reported in the Results section.

Hemorrhagic markers (CMBs, cSS, and SAH)

CMBs and cSS were identified and localized on axial SWI or GRE images. According to STRIVE-2 [11], we defined the lobar regions as the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes, and the deep regions as the basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, and brainstem. All regions were then referenced to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space template [12]. For topographic classification, we defined the anterior lobar regions as the frontal and temporal lobes. Similarly, the posterior lobar regions were defined as the parietal and occipital lobes. The same anterior/posterior definition was applied to both CMBs and cSS.

CMB distribution was categorized into the following six types: (0) no CMBs; (1) Anterior-predominant lobar CMBs, defined as anterior lobar CMBs only, or mixed lobar CMBs with anterior predominance (anterior CMB count ≥ 150% of posterior count); (2) Posterior-predominant lobar CMBs, defined as posterior lobar CMBs only, or mixed lobar CMBs with posterior predominance (posterior CMB count ≥ 150% of anterior count); (3) deep CMBs only; (4) Mixed lobar CMBs without predominance, defined as mixed lobar CMBs involving both anterior and posterior lobes without deep CMBs that did not meet predominance criteria; (5) mixed-location hemorrhages (MLH), defined as concurrent lobar and deep CMBs.

Given the lobar cortical predilection of cSS, cSS was categorized as: (0) no cSS; (1) anterior lobar cSS only; (2) posterior lobar cSS only; (3) mixed cSS involving both anterior and posterior lobes.

New-onset subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) was evaluated on the initial non-contrast CT scan after admission.

PWMH quantification and derived indices

PWMH was quantified independently by two raters (Y. Zhang and S. Wang, neuroradiologist with 5 years of experience) on axial FLAIR images. Both raters were blinded to clinical data and SWI/GRE sequences to prevent assessment bias related to hemorrhagic markers. The quantification of PWMH focused on confluent periventricular hyperintensities that were directly contiguous with the ventricular margin. We specifically excluded subcortical white matter lesions that were separated from the ventricles by normal-appearing white matter.

PWMH measurements were performed using 3D Slicer (version 5.9.0) [13]. Bilateral periventricular regions were assessed at the anterior horns (frontal horns) and posterior horns (occipital horns). For each region, we selected the axial slice with the greatest PWMH burden. Manual planimetric delineation was then performed on these slices to obtain the cross-sectional PWMH area in mm², as illustrated in Fig. 2D. A detailed step-by-step workflow for 3D Slicer is provided in Methods S1 to ensure reproducibility, as requested. This workflow includes procedures for display standardization and the regions of interest (ROI) delineation.

We computed the cross-sectional PWMH areas in four regions: the left anterior horn (AL), right anterior horn (AR), left posterior horn (PL), and right posterior horn (PR). From these, we derived three summary metrics: the total anterior PWMH area (PWMH-TA, AL + AR), the total posterior PWMH area (PWMH-TP, PL + PR), and the total global PWMH area (PWMH-T, AL + AR + PL + PR). Furthermore, we calculated two posterior-to-anterior indices: the area ratio (PA-AR = PWMH-TP / PWMH-TA) and the area difference (PA-AD = PWMH-TP − PWMH-TA). We also calculated the posterior-to-anterior PWMH distance ratio (PA-DR), following the method described by Deasy et al. [14]. This ratio was defined as the maximum posterior PWMH distance to the ventricular boundary divided by the maximum anterior PWMH distance. For patients in whom both PT and AT were zero, PA-AR was assigned a value of 1 to avoid undefined values and to reflect the absence of anterior or posterior predominance.

To account for potential ventricular enlargement that could influence PWMH interpretation, we measured the Evans Index (EI). The EI served as a surrogate marker for ventricular enlargement and brain atrophy. The EI was defined as the ratio of the maximum bifrontal width of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles to the maximum inner skull diameter on the same axial slice. This measurement was typically performed at the level of the foramen of Monro [15]. EI was measured in all participants by Y. Zhang using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer. To assess inter-rater reliability, S. Wang independently repeated the measurements in a randomly selected subset of participants. The reliability was then quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement [16].

Handling of unilateral measurement constraints

Based on the typical bilateral symmetry of PWMH [17], we implemented a substitution protocol for cases with unreliable measurements. When acute hematoma-related edema or chronic ICH-related gliosis prevented reliable measurement on one side, we substituted the contralateral homologous region. An additional analysis restricted to patients with directly measurable bilateral PWMH was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the main findings.

Model development and sensitivity analyses

The Boston criteria v2.0 was used as the reference standard. To avoid incorporation bias in this context, cognitive decline and cSS were not included as predictors in the predictive models. The Base Model included age, hypertension, prior ischemic stroke, and SAH. The Adjusted PA-AR Model additionally incorporated PA-AR, and a parallel Adjusted PA-DR Model incorporated PA-DR within the same clinical framework. Diagnostic performance of PA-DR, PA-AR, PA-AD, the Base Model, the Adjusted PA-DR Model, and the Adjusted PA-AR Model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Model discrimination was quantified using area under the curve (AUC).

Robustness analysis using alternative PA-AR formulations

We constructed three alternative or robust metrics to verify that our primary conclusions were not dependent on the ratio form of PA-AR. This approach also helped mitigate potential instability due to near-zero denominators and extreme values:

(1) Dominance Index (DI): DI = (PT − AT) / (PT + AT), where PT = PWMH-TP and AT = PWMH-TA;

(1) Dominance Index (DI): DI = (PT − AT) / (PT + AT), where PT = PWMH-TP and AT = PWMH-TA;

(2) Log-transformed PA-AR (Log-PA-AR): log [(PT + ε)/ (AT + ε)], where ε = 1 mm²;

(2) Log-transformed PA-AR (Log-PA-AR): log [(PT + ε)/ (AT + ε)], where ε = 1 mm²;

(3) Winsorized PA-AR (Winsor-PA-AR): PA-AR values truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

(3) Winsorized PA-AR (Winsor-PA-AR): PA-AR values truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Each sensitivity model was fitted using binary logistic regression (CAA vs. CAA-negative), and compared with the Base Model and the Adjusted PA-AR Model.

Prespecified subgroup and additional sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of PA-AR in a “diagnostic dilemma (gray zone)”, we performed a prespecified subgroup analysis. This analysis specifically included patients without cSS and with 0–1 lobar CMBs, while excluding those with deep CMBs. Given the limited number of CAA events in this subgroup, discrimination was assessed using a univariable PA-AR model and an age-adjusted PA-AR model.

To assess the impact of contralateral substitution, we repeated analyses after excluding patients who required substitution. To address the potential confounding effect of ventricular enlargement, we constructed an EI-substituted model in which age in the Adjusted PA-AR Model was replaced by EI. A comparison of this model against the Base Model and the Adjusted PA-AR Model is detailed in the Results section.

Statistical methods

There were no missing values in the variables used for the analyses. Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

We assessed the inter-rater reliability of both PWMH and EI measurements using ICC and Bland–Altman analysis with 95% limits of agreement. The ICC was calculated based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (ICC [1, 2]). In line with established guidelines, ICC values greater than 0.75 were considered acceptable [16].

Covariates were prespecified based on clinical plausibility and univariable screening (p < 0.05). The apparent AUC was initially estimated on the full sample. To evaluate model stability, we derived bootstrap mean AUCs together with their 95% confidence intervals using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. We employed decision curve analysis to quantify the net benefit across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities. The Treat-all and Treat-none strategies served as comparators in this analysis. The optimal cutoffs were determined using the Youden index [18]. Finally, we evaluated model fit and performance using the log-likelihood, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) [19], and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [20]. Direct ROC comparisons between PA-AR and PA-DR were performed using the DeLong test.

Model calibration was primarily evaluated using calibration plots, with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test used as a supplementary assessment of goodness-of-fit. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α < 0.05.

All analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.13) in the Spyder environment (v6.0.5), primarily using scikit-learn and statsmodels.

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Patients with CAA were older than CAA-negative controls (median 70 vs. 63 years; p < 0.001). The CAA group had a lower prevalence of hypertension (82.1% vs. 92.5%; p = 0.034) and prior ischemic stroke (8.9% vs. 21.7%; p = 0.012), but a higher rate of prior cognitive decline (14.6% vs. 4.7%; p = 0.023).

CSVD imaging markers

The median time from symptom onset to MRI did not differ significantly between the groups (CAA: 7 days [IQR 5–9] vs. CAA-negative: 6 days [IQR 4–8]; p = 0.390). The proportion of GRE use was similar between groups (CAA: 8/123, 6.5% vs. CAA-negative: 5/106, 4.7%; p = 0.767). Patients with CAA showed a significantly higher rate of cSS (56.9% vs. 17.9%; p < 0.001) and concurrent SAH (33.3% vs. 9.4%; p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between two groups in the presence of CMB (p = 0.937).

PWMH quantification and reproducibility

PWMH planimetric quantification on axial FLAIR showed excellent inter-rater agreement. Of the 916 ventricular regions assessed (from 229 patients), acute ICH-related effects obscured the ipsilateral area in 111 regions (12.12%), necessitating contralateral substitution. This situation affected 69 patients, corresponding to 30.1% of the study population. The proportion was comparable between groups (CAA-negative: 30 [29.3%] vs. CAA: 39 [31.7%]; p = 0.678), with no significant between-group difference. In three of the 229 patients (1.31%), both the anterior (AT) and posterior (PT) PWMH areas were zero, as confirmed by blinded re-evaluation. For these patients, the PA-AR was set to 1 to avoid undefined values and to reflect the absence of anterior or posterior predominance. The inter-rater reliability was good to excellent across all measurements, with ICCs of 0.764–0.896 for PWMH metrics and 0.870 for the EI (Table S2).

Compared with CAA-negative controls, the CAA group showed significantly larger posterior PWMH areas, including PWMH-PL (median 104.40 vs. 48.88 mm²; p < 0.001), PWMH-PR (104.70 vs. 34.44 mm²; p < 0.001), PWMH-TP (214.89 vs. 95.40 mm²; p < 0.001), and PWMH-T (369.93 vs. 190.62 mm²; p < 0.001). Posterior-to-anterior indices (PA-AR, PA-AD, and PA-DR) were also higher in CAA.

Univariable diagnostic performance of PWMH-derived indices

In univariable analyses, PA-AR demonstrated the highest discrimination among the PWMH-derived indices, with an AUC of 0.721 (95% CI 0.648–0.790). Its odds ratio was 1.414 (95% CI 1.17–1.71; p < 0.001). The performance of the other indices was as follows: PA-AD (AUC = 0.709; OR = 1.003) and PA-DR (AUC = 0.661; OR = 1.563). Multivariable model performance and internal validation

Covariates were selected based on clinical plausibility and between-group univariable screening. The Base Model included age, hypertension, prior ischemic stroke, and SAH and yielded an AUC of 0.780 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.789, 95% CI 0.729–0.845). When PA-AR was added (Adjusted PA-AR Model), discrimination increased to an AUC of 0.804 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.813, 95% CI 0.759–0.867) (Table 2). In parallel, an Adjusted PA-DR Model incorporating PA-DR in the same clinical framework yielded an AUC of 0.791 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.802, 95% CI 0.740–0.858). The two adjusted models included five predictors, corresponding to an EPV of approximately 24.6 (123 events/5 predictors).

In the multivariable analysis, PA-AR remained independently associated with CAA (OR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.12–1.65; p = 0.002). Additionally, significant associations were also observed for age (OR = 1.09 per year, 95% CI 1.05–1.13; p < 0.001), SAH (OR = 4.47, 95% CI 1.97–10.04; p < 0.001), and prior ischemic stroke (OR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.16–0.91; p = 0.031). In the parallel Adjusted PA-DR Model, PA-DR also remained independently associated with CAA (OR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.02–1.98; p = 0.038), together with age, SAH, and prior ischemic stroke. Multicollinearity was not observed in the Base Model, Adjusted PA-DR Model, or Adjusted PA-AR Model (all VIFs ~ 1.0; Table S3).

Direct ROC comparison showed that PA-AR had significantly better univariable discrimination than PA-DR (DeLong p = 0.010). By contrast, although the Adjusted PA-AR Model showed a numerically higher AUC than the Adjusted PA-DR Model, the difference between the two adjusted ROC curves was not statistically significant (DeLong p = 0.140).

Supplementary calibration and decision curve analysis further supported the model’s calibration and clinical utility (Figure S1). The Brier score was 0.180, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test yielded a χ² of 7.318 (p = 0.503), indicating good calibration.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether contralateral substitution influenced the primary findings. This analysis excluded all 69 patients who required substitution, leaving 160 patients for model fitting. In this subgroup, the Base Model yielded an AUC of 0.781 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.788, 95% CI 0.714–0.856) and the Adjusted PA-AR Model achieved an AUC of 0.805 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.819, 95% CI 0.752–0.883). The discrimination of the two models remained comparable to the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the association between PA-AR and CAA (Table 3; Fig. 4).

Diagnostic dilemma (“gray zone”) subgroup analysis

A predefined “gray zone” subgroup (no cSS, 0–1 lobar CMBs, and no deep CMBs) included 59 patients (CAA-negative: n = 32; CAA: n = 27). In this clinically challenging subgroup, PA-AR showed good discrimination for CAA. The univariable PA-AR Model achieved an AUC of 0.887 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.885, 95% CI 0.787–0.961), while the Age-adjusted PA-AR Model yielded a nearly identical AUC of 0.888 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.886, 95% CI 0.788–0.961). Sensitivity and specificity were unchanged between the two models (0.593 and 0.875, respectively) (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that PA-AR maintained good and stable diagnostic performance in the gray zone subgroup, with little change after additional adjustment for age.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

Sensitivity analyses using alternative PA-AR formulations yielded similar model performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.804 to 0.818 and bootstrap mean AUCs ranging from 0.804 to 0.818, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Table S4, Figure S2).

CAA patients showed slightly higher EI and maximal frontal horn width than CAA-negative controls (EI: p = 0.007; frontal horn width: p = 0.030). Given the strong collinearity between EI and age, we replaced age with EI in the Adjusted PA-AR Model and constructed an EI-substituted Model as a robustness check. The EI-substituted Model yielded an AUC of 0.776 (bootstrap mean AUC 0.788, 95% CI 0.729–0.849), which was broadly comparable to the Base Model but lower than the Adjusted PA-AR Model (AUC 0.804; bootstrap mean AUC 0.814, 95% CI 0.758–0.868). These findings suggest that the main association of PA-AR with CAA was not solely attributable to ventricular enlargement (Table S5, Figure S4).

PA-AR differed significantly across CMB distribution groups (Kruskal–Wallis H = 28.6, p < 0.0001). In contrast, PA-AR did not differ significantly across cSS distribution groups (Kruskal–Wallis H = 6.134, p = 0.1053; Fig. 6B; Table S6).

Discussion

This study characterized the distribution of PWMH in patients with primary lobar ICH. We evaluated the PA-AR, derived from routine FLAIR imaging, as a potential supportive marker for CAA within the Boston criteria v2.0 framework. Our key observation was a posterior-dominant PWMH pattern in CAA-related lobar ICH. The PWMH areas were significantly larger in the posterior horns compared to the anterior horns. This finding is consistent with previous reports linking posterior-weighted WMH distribution to CAA [7, 14]. Compared with CAA-negative patients, those classified as CAA showed significantly larger PWMH areas in all posterior regions, supporting a posterior predominance of the periventricular WMH burden. Specifically, the median area in the left posterior horn was 104.40 mm² versus 48.88 mm² (p < 0.001); in the right posterior horn, it was 104.70 mm² versus 34.44 mm² (p < 0.001). The total bilateral posterior horn area was also significantly larger in the CAA group (214.89 vs. 95.40 mm²; p < 0.001).

Among PWMH-derived indices, PA-AR showed moderate discriminative ability for CAA (AUC = 0.721; OR = 1.414, 95% CI 1.17–1.71; p < 0.001). The multivariable model, adjusted for age, hypertension, prior ischemic stroke, and subarachnoid hemorrhage, achieved an AUC of 0.804 for discriminating CAA. Moreover, PA-AR remained independently associated with CAA in this model, and the internal validation performance was stable, yielding a bootstrap mean AUC of 0.813 (95% CI 0.759–0.867). In the directly measurable bilateral cohort excluding patients who required contralateral substitution, the Base Model and the Adjusted PA-AR Model yielded AUCs of 0.781 and 0.805, respectively, with comparable bootstrap performance, supporting the robustness of the main findings in patients without mirrored measurements. Collectively, these findings suggest that PA-AR may provide supportive information that complements established Boston v2.0 markers.

Deasy et al. [14] developed a distance-based posterior-to-anterior periventricular WMH metric and reported good discriminative performance in a cohort comparing CAA with hypertensive arteriopathy. Our study, in contrast, had a distinct focus. We evaluated symptomatic primary lobar ICH patients who were classified under the Boston v2.0 criteria as either CAA or CAA-negative. Furthermore, our cohort was composed of Asian (Chinese) individuals, which reflects a more clinically heterogeneous group where mixed small vessel disease phenotypes may coexist. We used an area-based ratio derived from routine FLAIR planimetric measurements to quantify regional PWMH burden. Our multivariable model incorporating PA-AR yielded an AUC of 0.804, with a bootstrap mean AUC of 0.813 (95% CI 0.759–0.867). A potential advantage of this area-based metric is that it may better capture the heterogeneous morphology of PWMH, which can appear as caps or band-like patterns [11]. Although PA-AR showed significantly better univariable discrimination than PA-DR, its advantage in the adjusted clinical model was numerical rather than statistically confirmed. Therefore, PA-AR should be interpreted as a promising area-based alternative or supportive metric, rather than definitive evidence of superiority over the previously published distance-based approach.

A clinically important question is whether PA-AR provides value in diagnostically uncertain cases, where hemorrhagic markers may be insufficient to establish a confident CAA diagnosis. In the predefined “diagnostic dilemma/gray zone” subgroup (no cSS, 0–1 lobar CMBs, and no deep CMBs), PA-AR retained good diagnostic performance. Specifically, the univariable PA-AR model achieved an AUC of 0.887, and the age-adjusted PA-AR model yielded a nearly identical AUC of 0.888. This indicates that PA-AR-quantified posterior PWMH burden may be informative in cases where conventional hemorrhagic markers are indeterminate. Because this subgroup included only 27 CAA cases, we simplified the analysis to reduce the risk of overfitting. Nevertheless, this subgroup analysis remains exploratory and should be confirmed in independent cohorts enriched for diagnostically uncertain cases.

Several biological mechanisms may contribute to posterior predominance of PWMH in CAA. WMH represents a heterogeneous imaging phenotype with region-specific substrates. The underlying mechanisms of WMH differ by location. Deep WMH (DWMH) is primarily linked to arteriolosclerotic small vessel disease and chronic ischemic injury. In contrast, PWMH has been more closely associated with mechanisms such as venous collagenosis and disruptions in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or interstitial fluid dynamics [21–25]. Emerging evidence from experimental and imaging studies indicates that multiple pathways may converge to influence WMH formation. Specifically, glymphatic dysfunction and neuroinflammation are thought to interact with amyloid-β (Aβ) pathology, which exacerbates white matter damage [26]. Histopathological studies have reported inflammatory and structural white matter injury patterns associated with cortical vascular Aβ burden [27]. Imaging work has shown spatial links between amyloid burden and PWMH distribution, as well as associations with lobar hemorrhagic markers [6]. Our cohort revealed that higher PA-AR values were associated with specific CMB distribution patterns. Post hoc comparisons showed significantly higher PA-AR values in the posterior lobar CMB group than in the no-CMB group and the mixed-location hemorrhage group, while the anterior lobar CMB group also differed from the no-CMB group. This spatial concordance between hemorrhagic and non-hemorrhagic markers points to a shared posterior vulnerability in CAA [6, 28]. The underlying mechanism may involve the posterior predilection of sporadic CAA, in which vascular dysfunction causes abnormal interstitial fluid buildup, particularly in posterior periventricular regions [29, 30]. Taken together, these findings support posterior-dominant PWMH as a potentially distinguishing feature within CAA-related lobar ICH. PA-AR did not differ significantly across cSS distribution groups, which may reflect limited power and the relatively coarse categorization of cSS patterns. Larger studies incorporating composite measures of cSS distribution and burden are warranted to clarify the relationship between cSS patterns and PWMH topology.

We performed several robustness analyses to address potential methodological concerns. First, we evaluated alternative formulations of the primary metric, including the Dominance Index, log-transformed PA-AR, and winsorized PA-AR. These alternative formulations yielded model performance (AUC range: 0.804–0.818) that was consistent with our primary analysis. Second, we addressed the potential influence of ventricular enlargement on planimetric measurements. We assessed the Evans Index as a surrogate marker for ventricular enlargement and brain atrophy. Because EI showed strong collinearity with age, we constructed an EI-substituted model in which age was replaced by EI. The resulting performance was broadly comparable to the Base Model, while remaining lower than the Adjusted PA-AR Model, suggesting that the main results were not materially altered after accounting for the potential influence of ventricular enlargement on PWMH interpretation. Together, these analyses support the stability of the primary findings across different assumptions and measurement considerations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective single-centre study, and selection bias may exist, particularly because patients without MRI or without SWI/GRE sequences were excluded. The classification of CAA in this study relied on the Boston criteria v2.0. However, the absence of pathological confirmation or Aβ-PET may introduce potential misclassification, especially within the heterogeneous CAA-negative group. Importantly, the CAA-negative group in our study was not intended to represent a pure hypertensive arteriopathy reference cohort, but rather a heterogeneous non-CAA lobar ICH comparator group reflecting the real-world clinical setting in which the marker may be applied. Therefore, the diagnostic performance of PA-AR should be interpreted as its ability to help identify CAA within a mixed lobar ICH population, rather than as definitive evidence that it specifically distinguishes CAA from pure hypertensive arteriopathy. Future studies may further refine the characterization of the non-CAA comparator group using more detailed HA-related imaging markers. In some patients, hematoma mass effect and perihematomal edema could have influenced the assessment of hemorrhagic markers and the delineation of regional PWMH, although predefined handling rules and sensitivity analyses were used to mitigate these effects. Our study quantified PWMH using a maximal-slice planimetric approach rather than full volumetric segmentation, and automated volumetric mapping may further refine spatial characterization in future studies. Because Boston v2.0 requires age ≥ 50 years, our findings may not generalize to younger patients or to non-ICH presentations of CAA. Finally, although we performed internal validation and calibration assessments, external validation in independent cohorts remains necessary before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

In summary, our study shows that PA-AR captures a posterior-dominant PWMH pattern that is associated with CAA in patients with primary lobar ICH. When combined with routine clinical covariates, PA-AR contributed to diagnostic discrimination and may serve as a supportive MRI feature that complements existing Boston criteria v2.0 markers in differentiating CAA from CAA-negative lobar ICH. These findings should be interpreted in the context of a real-world mixed lobar ICH population. Future studies should validate these findings in independent cohorts and further investigate the relationship between quantitative PWMH mapping and CAA-related pathology, ideally by integrating imaging markers with Aβ-PET or neuropathological assessment.
