Loop versus anchor tenodesis of the long head of the biceps tendon: a prospective randomized controlled pilot study
Authors: Martin Bischofreiter, Christina Kölblinger, Michael Gattringer, Alexander Azesberger, Michael Stephan Gruber, Felix Rittenschober, Harald Kindermann, Reinhold Ortmaier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe long head of the biceps tendon is a frequent source of anterior shoulder pain, often treated with tenodesis. While anchor tenodesis is well established, it carries risks of implant-related complications. This study evaluates an implant-free “loop tenodesis” technique as a potentially safer and cost-effective alternative.MethodsIn this prospective, double-blinded, randomized exploratory study, 48 patients undergoing arthroscopic biceps tenodesis were randomized to either loop (n = 24) or anchor (n = 24) tenodesis. Clinical outcomes were assessed using ASES, Constant, and DASH scores, as well as range of motion, supination strength, satisfaction, aesthetic appearance, and return-to-sport. Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare non-parametric data.ResultsBoth groups showed significant improvements with similar gains in functional scores. Supination strength recovery was greater in the loop group (103% vs. 93%). There were no significant differences in range of motion or complications. Notably, only the anchor group showed cases of Popeye deformity (2 patients). Return to sport was achieved in 95.3% of patients, typically within 3 months. No revision or implant-related issues occurred.ConclusionLoop tenodesis offers equivalent clinical outcomes to anchor-based techniques, with fewer aesthetic concerns and no implant-associated risks, making it a safe, reliable, and cost-effective alternative for biceps tenodesis.

Introduction

The long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) is well known as a source of pain, especially for anterior shoulder pain. Abnormalities of the biceps tendon, such as pulley lesions with instability or subluxations, superior labral anterior to posterior lesions (SLAP) and tenosynovitis can lead to significant pain [1–3].They are often combined with a tear of the rotator cuff. Various techniques have been developed over the last decades to address pathologies of the LHBT. Two of the most frequently employed techniques during arthroscopic shoulder procedures are the release (tenotomy) or attachment (tenodesis) of the LHBT.

With tenotomy, the tendon is cut and released from its attachment. Proponents of tenotomy suggest that this is a technically simple procedure that enables easy rehabilitation and rapid return to activity, with a low complication and reoperation rate. However, it may lead to Popeye deformity and cramping of the biceps muscle [4, 5].

Tenodesis, on the other hand, is recommended to preserve the normal length-tension relationship of the LHBT, which can lead to improved functional outcomes and fewer aesthetic issues. Some studies suggest tenodesis may be particularly beneficial for athletes and younger patients [5–8].

There are multiple surgical options to perform tenodesis of the LHBT. A meta-analysis from Anil et al. showed that different tenodesis procedures of the LHBT (arthroscopic intracuff tenodesis, arthroscopic subpectoral tenodesis, and open subpectoral tenodesis) resulted in better functional outcome scores and fewer Popeye deformities than simple tenotomy [6].

While anchor tenodesis techniques that secure the LHBT directly to the bone using suture anchors, interference screws or bone tunnels have demonstrated superior biomechanical and clinical outcomes compared to soft-tissue fixation methods [9]. However, these procedures are not without their potential complications. Patients may experience persistent pain at the anchor insertion site, and there have been reported cases of fractures and infections associated with the implanted foreign materials used in these procedures [10, 11].

In contrast, a suture-based tenodesis technique does not require implants. This method utilizes a suture construct that forms a loop around the proximal portion of the LHBT, which is then secured by the loop as an “autotenodesis” at the proximal end of the bicipital groove, without necessitating rigid fixation devices. This method, which was first described by Kerschbaum et al. in 2019, has shown promising results in biomechanical settings. However, clinical data evaluating its performance in patients has been limited to date [12]. Given the potential for implant-related complications associated with anchor or other tenodesis techniques, this loop tenodesis approach may confer certain advantages [12, 13].

Although no prospective randomized controlled trials on this topic have been published to date, we did not formulate confirmatory hypotheses. At present, the available evidence is insufficient to reliably estimate an expected between-group difference and thus to perform a valid power calculation for loop versus anchor tenodesis. Therefore, the aim of this prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial is exploratory: to compare clinical outcomes, aesthetic appearance, and complication rates of loop tenodesis and anchor tenodesis for the treatment of symptomatic long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) pathology in patients undergoing arthroscopic shoulder surgery.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a randomized controlled trial design carried out at a single center. Three shoulder surgeons, each possessing over 5 years of experience, performed all surgical procedures. Participants completed questionnaires and underwent clinical evaluation by two trained assessors (an orthopaedic resident at baseline and a medical student at follow-up), unaware of the participant’s group assignment and surgical procedure. The institutional review board approved the study protocol (EC No. 249/2022, Johannes Kepler University Linz), and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number: NCT06815796; date of registration: January 12, 2024; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06815796). The patients were randomized by a computer-generated random number assignment to receive either arthroscopic loop tenodesis or arthroscopic anchor tenodesis of the LHBT. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Study patients

The study enrolled participants with symptomatic LHBT pathologies, such as tendinopathy, subluxation, or partial tears, who were scheduled for primary arthroscopic shoulder procedures. Patients were excluded if they had a fixed or irreducible shoulder dislocation, active or previous joint or systemic infection, prior surgery on the affected shoulder, or any other medical condition that would preclude their participation in the study.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were performed in a beach chair position. The surgical procedures involved the establishment of a standard posterior portal and a standard anterior portal. Subsequently, a 30-degree arthroscope was introduced, and a diagnostic arthroscopic evaluation was conducted to identify and document LHBT pathology and any other abnormality encountered. After confirming LHBT pathology, an additional anterolateral portal was created to access the biceps tendon’s long head and the bicipital groove. The rotator cuff interval was opened carefully with a scalpel about 1.5 cm. A surgical clamp was introduced through the anterolateral portal to grasp the LHBT firmly. Following the tenotomy of the tendon near its origin using a radiofrequency ablation probe, the tendon was pulled extraarticular. After that, the bicipital groove was lightly decorticated with an arthroscopic burr to create a slightly bleeding surface. For the anchor tenodesis, a 2.6 mm double-loaded FiberTak® Anchor was inserted high in the bicipital groove through the anterolateral portal. The sutures were extracted extraarticular, and the biceps tendon was secured with one end of the suture using the modified Krackow technique about 2 cm distal to its origin. The opposing end secured the biceps tendon with a single stitch, which was then pulled to reduce the tendon down to the bone. Finally, the sutures were securely tied, and the remaining suture tails were cut. If the second suture pair was not utilized, it was then removed (Figs. 1, 2).

For the loop tenodesis, approximately 1.5 cm of the proximal end of the tendon was folded and fixed with 2.0 Vicryl sutures (Johnson & Johnson Medical GmbH, Ethicon, Norderstedt, Germany) to create a loop and enlarge the tendon diameter. The tendon was then released, allowing for spontaneous retraction and subsequent autotenodesis within the bicipital groove.

Postoperative rehabilitation and physiotherapy

Following a biceps tenodesis procedure without any concomitant injuries, patients were not required to wear a shoulder arm support. However, restricted weight-bearing in elbow flexion was mandatory for the initial six-week postoperative period. Patients were permitted to mobilize the fingers, hand, wrist, and elbow immediately. In contrast, if the surgical intervention involved treatment of an associated injury, such as repair of the labral complex or rotator cuff, patients were obligated to wear a shoulder arm support without a pillow for four to six weeks postoperatively, adhering to a specific rehabilitation protocol. For most surgical procedures, only passive mobilization within a painless range of motion was allowed during the initial four to six weeks, after which active-assisted mobilization with gradually increasing weight was implemented.

Outcome measurements

The primary clinical outcomes were measured by the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score (ASES), the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score (DASH), and the Constant score. Further outcomes were range of motion, supination strength in 90 degrees of elbow flexion using an IDO isometer (Innovative Design Orthopaedics, Redditch, UK ), satisfaction, the presence of a Popeye deformity, and the distance between the lateral epicondyle of the humerus and the highest point of the biceps muscle belly. Patient satisfaction with the surgical procedure was assessed at the final follow-up using a 0 to 10 visual analog scale, with 0 representing the highest level of satisfaction.

Participants who regularly engaged in sports completed a questionnaire assessing their involvement in sports and recreational activities, including the types of sports, frequency, competitive level, time to return to sports, shoulder pain or apprehension during sports, and satisfaction with the surgical procedure. Both preoperative and postoperative data were collected. Shoulder pain and fear during sports were evaluated using a visual analog scale ranging from 0 to 10, with 0 representing the best outcome. For this study, amateur sports were defined as activities in which the participants participated extensively or exclusively without financial compensation.

Statistical analysis

As this was an exploratory study with a small sample size, no formal type I error control was implemented and no multiplicity adjustment was performed. Consequently, all reported inferential results, including p-values, should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Due to the small sample size and the presence of outliers, strictly non-parametric tests were used. Specifically, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for independent samples, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for dependent samples.To evaluate differences between the two surgical techniques (loop vs. anchor), between-group differences in demographic baseline characteristics were assessed initially. Owing to the small sample size and the presence of outliers, nonparametric methods were applied.

Results

Demographics

A total of 48 patients with LHBT pathology were enrolled in this study, including 24 treated with loop tenodesis and 24 treated with anchor tenodesis. Recruitment commenced in January 2023. Follow-up began in June 2024 and was conducted over a 2-month period. The mean age was 58.8 ± 10.5 years in the loop tenodesis group and 56 ± 8.9 years in the anchor tenodesis group. Most of the 48 patients had the right shoulder affected, with 29 (60.4%) patients presenting with right-sided pathology and 19 (39.6%) with left-sided pathology. The dominant shoulder was the right for 43 patients (89.6%), as determined by self-reporting. The demographic characteristics of the two groups were similar, as shown in Table 1.

Concomitant procedures, operative time and complications

Concomitant procedures were performed in the majority (88.9%) of the patients, as indicated in Table 2. The most common concomitant procedure was subacromial decompression (93.8%). Additionally, rotator cuff repair was necessary in 66.7% of the patients. The supraspinatus tendon was the most frequently affected (75.0%), and some patients exhibited involvement of multiple tendons (18.8%).

Overall operative time was slightly longer for the anchor tenodesis group (57.0 ± 24.6 min) compared to the loop tenodesis group (53.0 ± 23.0 min), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.564).

None of the patients had to undergo revision surgery for any reason, and no major complications were reported in either group until the follow-up.

Clinical outcome scores

All clinical scores, including the ASES, DASH, and the Constant score, showed significant improvements from the preoperative examination to the final follow-up in both the loop- and anchor-tenodesis groups, as shown in Fig. 3.

Pre- and postoperative clinical outcome scores for the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) score, and Constant score according to Loop and Anchor

The median ASES score improved significantly in both groups, increasing from 57 to 87,5 in the loop tenodesis group (Z = -3,985, p < 0,001) and from 52 to 82 in the anchor tenodesis group (Z = -3,179, p < 0,001). A similar pattern was observed for the DASH score, which improved from 34,2 to 11,8 in the loop tenodesis group (Z = -3,820, p < 0,001) and from 30.0 to 13.8 in the anchor tenodesis group (Z = -3,630, p = 0,001). The median Constant score also improved significantly in both groups, rising from 56 to 81 in the loop tenodesis group (Z = -2,894, p < 0,001) and from 48 to 74,5 in the anchor tenodesis group (Z = -4,198, p < 0,001). No significant between-group differences were observed in clinical outcome scores at the final follow-up assessment.

Range of motion and strength

Range of motion (ROM) in the shoulder and the elbow was assessed preoperatively and at the final follow-up.

The ROM in the shoulder was recorded during forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation with the arm adducted. Both surgical techniques showed statistically significant improvements in the forward flexion, abduction, and external rotation from the preoperative examination to the final follow-up.

A total of 28 patients (60,9%; loop: n = 15, 65,2%; anchor: n = 13, 56,5%. p = 0.763) had forward flexion > 121° preoperatively, and 19 patients (41,3%; loop: n = 9, 39,1%; anchor: n = 10, 43,5%, p = 1.000) had abduction > 121°. Postoperatively, this increased to 40 patients (83,3%; loop: n = 18, 75,0%; anchor: n = 22, 91,7%, p = 0,245) with forward flexion > 121° and 32 patients (66,7%; loop: n = 15, 62,5%; anchor: n = 17, 70,8%, p = 0,759) with abduction > 121°.

In addition, 39 patients (81,3%; loop: n = 19, 79,2%; anchor: n = 20, 83.3%, p = 1,000) were able postoperatively to raise the hand above the head with the elbow bent backward, compared with only 18 patients preoperatively (37,5%; loop: n = 10, 41,6%; anchor: n = 8, 33,3%, p = 1000).

Internal rotation, defined as the ability to reach the 12th thoracic vertebra (T12) or higher, was present in 19 patients preoperatively (39.6%; loop: n = 11, 45,8%; anchor: n = 8, 33,3%, p = 549) and in 26 patients postoperatively (54,2%; loop: n = 13, 54,2%; anchor: n = 13, 54,2%, p = 1000).

The median preoperative flexion/extension range of motion was 150,0° in both the loop tenodesis and anchor tenodesis groups. At the final follow-up examination, this decreased to 120,0° in both groups. The reduction in range of motion was primarily attributable to a loss of elbow flexion, whereas elbow extension remained unchanged.

The median preoperative pronation/supination range of motion was 180° in both groups and remained unchanged at the final follow-up examination in both the loop and anchor tenodesis groups.

Furthermore, the loop tenodesis group exhibited a more substantial recovery of supination strength, reaching 103% of the unaffected side, compared to the anchor tenodesis group, which achieved 93%. The improvement of the supination strength in kg is shown in Table 3.

Pop-eye deformity and biceps muscle belly

Only in the anchor tenodesis group did two patients develop a visually noticeable “Popeye” deformity. Even though the distance between the lateral epicondyle of the humerus and the highest point of the biceps muscle belly showed no significant difference from the preoperative to the postoperative follow-up (Loop: preoperative 14.1 cm ± 2.2 vs. postoperative 13.5 cm ± 1.4, p = 0.187; Anchor: preoperative 14.7 cm ± 3.7 vs. postoperative 13.7 cm ± 2.0, p = 0.133).

Sports participation

Of the 48 participants, 43 (89.6%) were regularly involved in sports before the surgical intervention, though 4 (9.3%) could not engage in their respective sporting activities immediately preceding the procedure. Among this group of 43 patients who regularly participated in sports, 38 (88.4%) were classified as amateur athletes, while only 5 (11.6%) were professional athletes. 41 of these patients (95.3%) could return to sports participation after the surgery. Additionally, two patients who had not engaged in any sports prior to the procedure began participating in athletic activities (fitness training, hiking) after the surgical intervention. All amateur athletes could return to their prior level of sports participation. Only one (20.0%) of the professional athletes returned to the preoperative sport level, while three (60.0%) of them had to change to an amateur level, and one (20.0%) did not return to any sporting activity.

Most patients who regularly engaged in sports could resume their athletic activities within 3 months post-operatively. Specifically, 28 individuals (58.3%) returned to sports participation within 1–3 months, while an additional 10 patients (20.8%) did so within 4–6 months. Only a small number, comprising four individuals (8.3%), required more than half a year, and only one patient (2.1%) required more than a year to re-engage in their pre-operative sports involvement. Discussion

In our study, we could show that both the loop and anchor-based biceps tenodesis techniques significantly improved clinical outcome scores and restored range of motion and strength in patients with symptomatic biceps tendon pathology. All patients who participated in sports activities preoperatively could return to sports (100%). Moreover, most patients successfully returned to their previous level of sporting activities following the procedures (level of sport).

The study found no significant differences between the loop and anchor tenodesis techniques regarding clinical outcomes, time to return to sports, and complication rates. The loop tenodesis technique was originally established by Kerschbaum et al. in a biomechanical study, which demonstrated its superior resistance to distalization compared to simple tenotomy. Following these findings, Riedl et al.provided the first clinical data in a retrospective series, showing that the technique is safe and yields high patient satisfaction with a low rate of ‘Popeye’ deformity. While these preliminary results were encouraging, the authors emphasized the need for prospective trials to further validate the procedure.14.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare the implant-free loop tenodesis technique with the anchor-based biceps tenodesis procedure. Furthermore, the authors report the first in vivo clinical outcomes of the loop tenodesis method. A previous biomechanical cadaver study has described the loop tenodesis technique as achieving higher ultimate load and stiffness, with less distalization, compared to a simple tenotomy of the LHBT. 12 Although Kerschbaum, who first introduced the loop tenodesis technique, recognized the limited clinical data available on this innovative approach [13].

The loop tenodesis technique has been described as having several advantages, including that it is relatively easy to perform, is cheap, has a short surgical duration, carries a low risk of intraoperative complications, does not require an anchor or screw, has a low risk of infection, and results in a stable fixation of the tendon.

Only two patients exhibited a visually noticeable “Popeye” deformity in our study population, occurring exclusively in the anchor tenodesis group. In 3 recent meta-analyses/systematic reviews, the prevalence of the aesthetic “Popeye” deformity was from 6.8% to 9.0% after biceps tenodesis and from 23.0% to 24.7% after tenotomy of the LHBT [7, 15, 16]. With a percentage of only 4.2% (anchor) and 0.0% (loop), our series shows a lower ratio of “Popeye” deformity. Both patients who experienced the “Popeye” deformity were men, which remains in line with a randomized clinical trial with 114 patients, where males had a significantly higher rate of Popeye deformities than females [17].

The biceps muscle belly position showed no significant difference between the two groups, indicating that both techniques could maintain the anatomic position of the biceps tendon/muscle unit. Even though two patients were visually noticed to have a Popeye deformity, this did not lead to any clinical impairment in our study’s range of motion, strength, or functional outcomes.

The clinical outcome scores significantly improved in both the loop and anchor tenodesis groups. With a mean postoperative Constant score of 75.4 ± 15.6 in the loop group and 75.1 ± 12.9 in the anchor group, the results were comparable to those reported in other studies that performed a tenodesis high in the bicipital groove (72.8–76.1) [18–20]. The mean ASES score for the loop tenodesis group was 86.8 ± 10.3, while the anchor tenodesis group had a mean score of 79.7 ± 15.9. Although the loop tenodesis group showed slightly better results, the difference was not statistically significant. A systematic review of three studies involving 143 patients using only techniques with suture anchors reported a similar mean ASES score of 84.31 ± 7.65 after biceps tenodesis.21 In two other studies with suprapectoral tenodesis, a mean DASH score of 11.2–11.4 was found, respectively, which aligns well with the results of our current analysis (Loop: 14.7 ± 11.2; Anchor: 14.6 ± 12.2) [22, 23].

In our cohort, 83% (40 out of 48 patients) participated regularly in sports before surgery, among these, 37 (93%) patients could return to sports participation after the surgery. Compared to the studies in a 2022 systematic review by Sandler et al., our results are quite promising. The authors reported a combined return-to-sports rate of 63% to 85% after tenodesis of the LHBT [24]. Most of our patients could return to their pre-injury sports activities within 3–6 months (58.3%) and 20.8% within 4–6 months, with no significant differences observed between the loop and anchor tenodesis groups. With improved shoulder pain during and after sports and fear during sports activities, our findings indicate that both techniques reliably enable patients to return to their desired sports participation. Moreover, our patients reported a high level of satisfaction with the post-operative result (mean VAS satisfaction score: Loop: 2.8 ± 3.0, Anchor: 1.9 ± 2.4; p = 0.258, mean: 2.3 ± 2.8).

Humeral fracture is a rare but serious complication that can occur with biceps tenodesis procedures. A recent systematic review with 74,394 patients showed a mean fracture rate of 1.0% − 1.6% (in 669 patients) and a higher risk with open techniques and by using interference screws rather than suture anchors [10]. In another study, none of the patients showed any sign of osteolytic bone around the anchor or a fracture of the humeral bone using a 2.7-mm knotless polyether ether ketone (PEEK) anchor (Microraptor Knotless; Smith+Nephew) [25]. This is in line with the findings of our current study, where no fractures were reported in the anchor tenodesis groups, where a similar small 2.6 mm suture anchor was used. We assume that a small anchor can reduce the risk of bone damage while performing tenodesis. Furthermore, Koch and Burks reported that using an interference screw can lead to damage to the LHBT because of compression against the cortical bone.11 Loop tendon repair with no hardware implantation can avoid these risks and thus reduce the potential for associated surgical complications.

Bicipital groove pain and muscle cramping are well-documented complications following surgical interventions on the LHBT. Previous meta-analyses have indicated that muscle cramping is more prevalent in patients undergoing tenotomy. In contrast, bicipital groove discomfort is more common in those who have undergone LHBT tenodesis, particularly with intracuff procedures [6, 7, 26]

Notably, none of the patients in the current study cohort reported experiencing bicipital groove pain or muscle cramping, although the small sample size should be considered.

Limitations

The major limitations of this study include the heterogeneous patient cohort with variable severity of the pathology of the LHBT and the rotator cuff, as well as the relatively small sample size and short-term follow-up period. The diverse patient population with varying degrees of biceps and rotator cuff pathology may have influenced the outcomes, and the limited sample size and short-term follow-up duration restrict the ability to draw definitive conclusions about the long-term efficacy of the procedures examined.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrates that the implant-free loop tenodesis achieved clinical and functional outcomes comparable to those observed with traditional suture anchor tenodesis for the treatment of long head of the biceps tendon pathologies. The new loop tenodesis is a safe and reliable technique that obviates the need for implantable devices and is, therefore, not only cost-effective but also eliminates the potential for implant-related complications. Furthermore, this implant-free technique may offer advantages by serving as an intermediate approach between straightforward tenotomy and more complex tenodesis procedures.
