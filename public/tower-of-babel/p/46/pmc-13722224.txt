Work-related musculoskeletal injuries and burnout among family physicians: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Waleed Albishi, Fahad Alajlan, Abdulelah Alrasheed, Abdulaziz Alhokair, Abdulaziz Aljohani, Dan Albishi, Abdulrahman Alaseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Burnout is a significant concern among family physicians, affecting physician well-being and the quality of patient care. This study assessed the prevalence of burnout and its association with work-related musculoskeletal injuries (WRMSIs).Methods:A total of 102 family physicians were surveyed. Burnout was assessed using the Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT), and musculoskeletal pain was evaluated with the WRMSI questionnaire. Associations among burnout, WRMSI, and physician characteristics were analyzed.Results:Among participants, 43.2% were between the ages of 31 and 40 years; 51% were men; and 69.6% worked in primary healthcare centers. Burnout was present in 73.5% of respondents. Mean BAT scores showed higher levels of exhaustion (3.31), mental distance (2.82), cognitive impairment (2.68), and emotional impairment (2.47). There was a significant positive correlation between BAT and WRMSI scores (r = 0.324; P < 0.001). Burnout was more common among younger physicians (P = 0.013), those with less experience (P = 0.003), and those working longer shifts (P = 0.016). The most common WRMSI site was the lower back (mean, 4.46), followed by the shoulder (mean, 4.23) and the neck (mean, 3.75). The total mean score on the WRMSI questionnaire was 26.7 (standard deviation, 16.9).Conclusion:Burnout is prevalent among family physicians and closely associated with WRMSI. Younger physicians and those working extended shifts are at the most significant risk. Targeted policies and programs to manage workload, improve ergonomics, provide mentoring, and support wellness are needed to reduce burnout and WRMSI.

Introduction

The medical field is known for its high demands, long hours, and significant psychological pressure. Medical workers, including physicians, have among the highest rates of occupational injuries. While physician well-being has often focused on mental health outcomes such as burnout, work-related musculoskeletal injuries (WRMSI) also negatively affect physicians’ well-being and/or the quality of care they provide to their patients.[123] There is a growing body of evidence suggesting that physicians who are injured at work also suffer verbal violence in the workplace, which adds additional psychological stress and, consequently, raises the likelihood of those physicians experiencing burnout.[45] Thus, physical injuries and professional stress, including psychological burnout, are not distinct issues but rather closely related.[67] Evidence suggests that work-related stress is a significant factor accounting for 60% to 80% of workplace accidents.[8] Additionally, physicians under high work stress may become discouraged and less productive.[9]

Occupational injury is defined as an injury in the workplace or during working hours.[4] Medical staff are vulnerable to hazards and injuries due to the nature of the healthcare environment. A study conducted in the United States found that the incidence of occupational injury among medical staff was five times the national average for all workers.[10] Another study found that two-thirds of orthopedic physicians reported having musculoskeletal occupational injuries, with over 27% of them needing time off work as a direct result of those injuries.[11] Consequently, the quality of medical care patients receive may be significantly affected by the physical and psychological harm experienced by healthcare providers who sustain an occupational injury.[121314] However, most recent research studies evaluating occupational injuries focus on persons working in non-medical fields, such as construction workers and firefighters, with little attention given to occupational safety among medical staff.[41516]

The term “burnout” originated in the late 1960s to characterize the mental and emotional distress experienced by healthcare professionals, which reflects the widespread stress among those working in healthcare institutions.[17] The risk of burnout increases in the presence of several factors, including heavy workloads, unpleasant work environments, job-related stress, and a lack of organizational support.[18192021] A report by the Canadian Medical Association revealed that burnout is far higher among family physicians than other practicing physicians, according to a national survey on physician health.[222324] Mathews et al. highlight the need for system-wide measures, including interprofessional team-based care, alternative remuneration models, structured locum programs, and short-term insurance coverage as means of ensuring physician well-being and maintaining primary care during public health emergencies.[2024] Burnout has severe consequences for individual physicians and organizational outcomes. It is linked to higher rates of medical errors, lower patient satisfaction, increased turnover, and a decline in job satisfaction among physicians. For these reasons, occupational burnout can be identified as an occupational factor that affects physician productivity.[25]

The relationship between the two domains of occupational injury and burnout is an essential, yet little-studied area. Physical discomfort and chronic pain from occupational accidents can directly contribute to stress and fatigue, which, in turn, can lead to burnout. Burnout, on the other hand, can cause mental exhaustion and cognitive overload, which might worsen or increase vulnerability to occupational accidents by causing poor body mechanics, a greater pain threshold, and a lower focus on ergonomics. This reciprocal link points to how psychological discomfort and physical pain mutually reinforce each other.[4] To further underscore its relevance, examining this relationship is beneficial for both practitioners and patients.

For physicians, recognizing the co-occurrence of burnout and occupational injuries can inform preventive strategies, workplace ergonomics, and wellness programs that enhance professional well-being and reduce attrition. For patients, improved physician health translates into better clinical performance, more effective decision-making, and safer, higher-quality care. Thus, addressing these interconnected issues supports both workforce sustainability and optimal patient outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of occupational injuries and burnout among family physicians, including their characteristics, to provide evidence for strategy development to reduce both burdens and improve the quality of care in hospitals and primary care centers.

Methods

Study design and setting

This survey-based cross-sectional, multicenter study included consultants and senior registrars practicing as family physicians across regions in Saudi Arabia; family medicine residents and fellows were excluded.

Population and sample size

The study targeted all actively practicing, board-certified family physicians in Saudi Arabia during the study period (April 2023 to April 2025). Eligible participants included physicians of any gender, years of experience, or practice setting. Physicians who were not board-certified in family medicine or who declined participation were excluded. Participants were recruited using a non-probability, convenience sampling approach via an online survey. The sampling frame consisted of board-certified family physicians identified through professional societies (including the Saudi Society of Family Medicine and Saudi Commission for Health Specialties), institutional mailing lists (hospitals and primary health centers), and verified physician social media groups commonly used by healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia. Invitations containing the survey link were disseminated to the eligible physicians contacted through email invitations, physician-only WhatsApp, Telegram, and Facebook groups, and direct messaging in LinkedIn and X (Twitter).

A total of 102 physicians completed the survey. However, the sample was disproportionately drawn from the Central Region (72.5%), reflecting differential access to recruitment channels and response patterns. As such, the sample may not be fully representative of all regions in Saudi Arabia, and the generalizability of the findings should be interpreted with caution. The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies, assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an estimated prevalence of 10% based on prior literature.[6] This yielded a required minimum sample size of 114 participants. Although the achieved sample size (n = 102) fell short of this target, all completed responses were included in the analysis. This shortfall may have reduced the statistical power of the study and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Instruments

The electronically distributed questionnaire had three parts. The first part concerned sociodemographic traits, including age, gender, job title, field of practice, years in practice, the number of clinic hours worked each week, the number of emergency care shifts, the number of hours per shift, and vacation days per year. The second part was made up of the Standardized Nordic Questionnaire.[26] This 29-item questionnaire addresses the anatomical areas most commonly affected by workplace musculoskeletal pain and has been widely used across disciplines. The third part comprised the Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT-33), a standardized, self-administered questionnaire designed to measure burnout in occupational settings.[27] It is composed of 33 items rated on a Likert scale, and it evaluates four core dimensions of burnout: exhaustion, mental distance, emotional impairment, and cognitive impairment.

Additionally, it assesses two secondary symptoms: psychological and psychosomatic complaints. Each dimension is scored by calculating the average for relevant item responses, with higher scores indicating greater burnout. The BAT-33 is suitable for research, clinical, and workplace health assessments, providing valuable insights for intervention or further evaluation. The scores are evaluated as means from the core dimensions and secondary symptoms, with each item rated on a 5-point scale. Burnout levels are classified based on scores as low (1.00–1.60), average (1.61–2.40), high (2.41–3.29), and very high (3.30–5.00). Individuals are considered to exhibit no burnout if all core dimensions have low or average scores (cutoff point: 2.39); at risk if any scores are moderate; and exhibiting severe burnout if any are high, particularly when secondary symptoms are also elevated. High scores in core dimensions, notably exhaustion and mental distance, may require further clinical evaluation.[27]

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27 (Armonk, New York, IBM Corporation). The data were presented in both numerical and percentage forms for all categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were provided for all continuous variables. The association between WRMSI and BAT scores, in relation to family physicians’ sociodemographic characteristics, was assessed using an independent-samples t-test. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on the plots, both WRMSI and BAT scores showed a normal distribution. Therefore, a parametric test was applied. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to determine the correlation between WRMSI and BAT scores. Statistical significance was set at the P < 0.05 level.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The study population included 102 family physicians; of those, 43.2% were aged 31 to 40 years. Male physicians constituted 51%. The majority (69.6%) were practicing in primary healthcare centers, and 41.2% were senior registrars. The majority (72.5%) were practicing in the Central Region of Saudi Arabia. Physicians with over 10 years of practice experience constituted 25.5%. More than half (55.9%) worked fewer than 8 hours per shift, including those in urgent care, while 69.6% reported having 30 days or fewer of vacation per year. Additionally, 40.2% indicated having a research component in their practice [Table 1].

Prevalence and mean burnout and WRMSI scores among family physicians

The mean scores for the exhaustion, mental distance, cognitive impairment, emotional impairment, and secondary symptoms domains were 3.31, 2.82, 2.68, 2.47, and 2.62, respectively. In each of those domains, physician scores indicated very high levels among 29.4%, 20.6%, 21.6%, 21.6%, and 10.8%, respectively, as shown in Table 2. Among these scores, the mean total BAT score was 28.2 (standard deviation [SD], 0.68), with 21.6% considered a very high level, as shown in Figure 1. Based on the total BAT score, the overall prevalence of burnout in this study was 73.5%, as shown in Figure 2. Regarding WRMSI, the most common site was the lower back (mean score, 4.46), followed by the shoulder (mean, 4.23), and neck (mean, 3.75), while the upper extremity was the least common site (2.18). The total mean score on the WRMSI questionnaire was 26.7 (SD, 16.9) [Table 2].

Correlation between burnout and WRMSI

There was a significant positive correlation between WRMSI and BAT scores (r = 0.324; P < 0.001), suggesting that higher WRMSI scores are associated with higher BAT scores [Figure 3]. Burnout scores were significantly higher among younger physicians (t = 2.537, P = 0.013), registrars/senior registrars (t = 3.627, P < 0.001), those with fewer years in practice (t = 3.077, P = 0.003), and those working longer shifts (t = −2.457, P = 0.016). No significant differences were observed in burnout scores by gender, type of practice, region of practice, number of vacation days per year, or having a research component in practice (P > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were found between WRMSI and any of the physicians’ demographic variables, including age, gender, type of practice, professional rank, region of practice, years in practice, number of hours per shift, number of vacation days per year, and having a research component in practice (P > 0.05 for all) [Table 3].

Discussion

The majority of participants in this study worked in primary healthcare facilities, which suggests heavy workloads, long shifts, a wide range of clinical responsibilities, and frequent physical tasks—all of which could lead to both psychological and physical strain.[2829] Among the respondents, 73.5% met the burnout threshold based on their overall BAT score, indicating extremely high levels of burnout among family physicians. The presence of emotional and mental strain was highlighted by their high mean exhaustion score and significantly high percentages across several burnout domains.

WRMSI scores were particularly noteworthy, with a mean total score of 26.7, suggesting that musculoskeletal strain is prevalent in this population. The clinically significant positive correlation between WRMSI and burnout (r = 0.324, P < 0.001) indicates that psychological weariness and physical injury may reinforce one another. This is in line with Shin et al., who showed that physicians under more physical stress may also be under more psychological stress, which can lead to a vicious cycle that can impair performance and well-being.[30] Similarly, our findings align with a prior study, which showed that burnout was considerably more likely to occur in younger physicians and those with fewer years of practice. Additionally, longer shifts were associated with higher burnout levels, highlighting the impact of work hours on physician mental health.[3132] However, non-significant correlation between WRMSI with any demographic factors in our study contrasts with a prior study, which indicated that the severity of WRMSD was substantially linked with work experience, age, and Body Mass Index (BMI), but not with gender among office workers.[33]

Moreover, prior literature has also shown that heavy workloads, administrative responsibilities, emotional strain, and increasing patient complexity have been linked to high burnout rates among family physicians, typically 50% to 70%, which aligns with our study.[34] Similarly, a survey of 31 healthcare professionals found that the average burnout level was 57.4%, with scores ranging from 16% to 86%.[35] This is further supported by the findings of a systematic review of burnout estimates, which demonstrated that 67.0% (122 out of 182) of studies that included data on total burnout reported prevalence estimates ranging from 10% to 80.5%.[17] In our study, the prevalence of burnout was 73.5%, which is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the high prevalence of burnout and musculoskeletal issues among primary care physicians globally.[353637]

Numerous studies have also shown a significant correlation between burnout and age, with lower levels of burnout as one gets older.[3839] This has been attributed to role uncertainty, less experience, fewer coping mechanisms, and more exposure to frontline clinical activities.[40] Our findings are consistent with this, indicating that burnout is considerably higher among younger physicians and those who have just started their careers. Nevertheless, another systematic review of the factors contributing to burnout identified a substantial correlation between aging and both a higher sense of personal accomplishment and an increased risk of depersonalization, which contrasts with our findings and thus suggests that these findings may not hold everywhere.[41]

Similar to our study, Baker et al. found that extended sitting, repetitive motions, uncomfortable postures during examinations, and computer-based documentation are among the major occupational complaints primary care and emergency physicians worldwide have regularly reported in terms of WRMSI.[42] This explains the higher mean WRMSI score in our study. Notably, the lack of correlation between WRMSI and demographic variables is consistent with several earlier studies that found that musculoskeletal injuries are common across all age and gender groups, highlighting that practically all practitioners, regardless of role or seniority, are affected by the physical demands of clinical practice.[3943] However, one study found gender disparities in musculoskeletal complaints.[38] Women were more likely to experience hip and lower back pain, concern about the course of their illness, poorer mental health, poorer sleep, and a lower overall quality of life. In contrast, men had poorer functional status, a higher workload, more work-related injuries, and worse impairment.[38] The substantial relationship between WRMSI and burnout found in the present study has also been reported globally, with studies showing that physical strain, fatigue, and persistent pain can worsen emotional exhaustion.[44]

The high incidence of burnout and WRMSI suggests that institutional measures are desperately needed to promote the physical and mental health of family physicians. Structured workload management, including balancing clinical responsibilities across physicians, optimizing shift duration, and ensuring sufficient rest time, should be a top priority for healthcare administrators.[45] Regular musculoskeletal screening, access to physiotherapy, and ergonomic workstation upgrades could all help lower WRMSI.[46] Emotional exhaustion may also be reduced by implementing measures that prevent burnout, such as resilience training, protected time for administrative tasks, mentorship for junior physicians, and private mental health support.[46] Physician safety and labor productivity may be improved at the policy level by incorporating occupational health standards into family medicine practice guidelines.

Limitations

We employed a non-probability convenience sampling approach and online survey recruitment approach. This inherently tends to introduce selection bias, with a narrow regional distribution attributable to the overrepresentation of physicians from the Central Region, which renders our findings not fully generalizable to all family physicians across Saudi Arabia. Besides, the achieved sample size (102) was slightly below the estimated sample requirement (114); the implication of this is a potential reduction in the statistical power of the findings. Although we looked at important demographic and occupational parameters, we were unable to evaluate every factor that could affect burnout or musculoskeletal injuries, such as ergonomic exposure, task distribution, administrative burden, or psychosocial stressors at work. Furthermore, using self-reported measures raises the risk of recollection or response bias, and the classification of musculoskeletal symptoms might inaccurately reflect their severity or chronicity. Finally, the cross-sectional design further restricts the capacity to determine causal or temporal correlations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that burnout is common among family physicians. Burnout was closely associated with WRMSI. The combination of mental exhaustion and physical strain shows the double challenge physicians face in demanding clinical settings. Younger physicians and those working long shifts were more vulnerable. These findings highlight the need for support and workload management tailored to different career stages. To ensure the stability of the healthcare workforce and maintain safe, high-quality patient care, healthcare organizations, and policymakers should focus on improving mentorship and wellness programs and making routine screening for burnout and WRMSI a standard part of practice.
