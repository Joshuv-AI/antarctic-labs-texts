Prospective Clinical Evaluation of a Novel Repositionable Plastic Stent for Endosonography‐Guided Hepaticogastrostomy With or Without Antegrade Stenting (With Videos)
Authors: Shuntaro Mukai, Atsushi Sofuni, Takayoshi Tsuchiya, Reina Tanaka, Ryosuke Tonozuka, Yukitoshi Matsunami, Kazumasa Nagai, Hiroyuki Kojima, Hirohito Minami, Noriyuki Hirakawa, Kyoko Asano, Kento Shionoya, Takao Itoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and study aimsA repositionable plastic stent (PS), developed to overcome the limitations of conventional pusher‐type PS in endosonography‐guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS‐HGS), minimizes the gap between the inner sheath and stent to improve insertability. This prospective single‐center study evaluated its feasibility, safety, and clinical effectiveness in malignant and benign biliary diseases.Patients and MethodsBetween August 2022 and August 2024, 100 consecutive patients undergoing EUS‐HGS using the repositionable PS were enrolled. Mechanical tract dilation was performed, and antegrade metal stenting was added when feasible.ResultsTrainees performed 76% of the procedures. The median bile duct diameter was 4.3 mm. Technical and functional success rates were 99% and 93.9%, respectively, without need for cautery dilation. Initial PS advancement failed in two benign cases with rigid bile ducts; the stent system was pulled back for additional dilation, enabling successful placement. Adverse events occurred in 10% of cases (biliary peritonitis, 6; cholangitis, 2; biloma from early stent bending, 1; bleeding, 1), all manageable, with intervention required in two cases. No stent migration occurred during the first month after insertion. Recurrent biliary obstruction developed in 24 cases, all with successful reintervention. Median time to recurrence of biliary obstruction was significantly longer for EUS‐HGS combined with antegrade stenting than for EUS‐HGS alone (389 vs. 168 days, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThis repositionable PS enables safe, effective EUS‐HGS using mechanical dilation alone and corrective pullback for difficult cases. Its controlled deployment and reliable reintervention support its usefulness in both malignant and benign biliary diseases.

Introduction

Endosonography‐guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS‐HGS) has been widely reported as a useful alternative for obstructive jaundice in patients with unresectable malignant biliary obstruction when conventional transpapillary biliary drainage by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is difficult due to duodenal strictures or surgically altered anatomy [1, 2, 3, 4]. Furthermore, EUS‐guided antegrade intervention, which involves performing antegrade treatment via a route created by EUS‐HGS, has also been shown to be useful in benign cases [5, 6, 7, 8]. EUS‐HGS is effective for preoperative biliary drainage and may have expanded indications in biliary diseases [9]. However, concerns remain regarding serious adverse events such as bile leakage and stent migration or dislodgement [10]. Therefore, the development of dedicated stents that enable safer EUS‐HGS is needed.

A plastic stent (PS) specifically designed for EUS‐HGS or EUS‐HGS combined with antegrade stenting (EUS‐HGAS) across malignant distal biliary obstruction has been developed, with reported usefulness and safety [11, 12]. Conventional dedicated PS devices used a pusher‐type system that did not allow repositioning once advanced. To overcome this limitation, a repositionable PS delivery system permitting pullback in difficult cases was developed. Although an initial gap between the inner sheath and stent impaired insertion, subsequent refinements improved usability. The current system now offers insertion performance comparable to conventional pusher‐type PS. In this study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of this novel repositionable PS in patients with malignant or benign biliary disease.

Methods

Patients

This single‐center prospective study was performed at Tokyo Medical University and included patients in whom EUS‐HGS was attempted for malignant biliary obstruction or benign biliary disease between August 2022 and August 2024. Patients younger than 20 years, those who underwent EUS‐HGS with an initial plan for placement of a metal stent (MS), those in whom puncture was deemed to be difficult because of insufficient dilation of the intrahepatic bile duct on EUS evaluation, and those who underwent EUS‐HGS during a live endoscopic demonstration were excluded. The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. This study was approved by our institutional review board (Tokyo Medical University, T2022‐0059), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000048423). Data were collected in accordance with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Novel Repositionable PS for Endosonography‐Guided Hepaticogastrostomy

This repositionable PS delivery system (TYPE‐IT, Gadelius Medical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) consists of a single‐pigtail PS with a 7‐Fr diameter, an inner sheath, and a pusher catheter (Figure 2). The pusher catheter and PS are connected by a thin string. Removing the inner sheath from the PS releases the string, leaving only the PS in place. The tip of the inner sheath is tapered, and the gap between the inner sheath and the PS is minimized to improve the ability of the stent to penetrate into the bile duct. The two flaps on each of the distal and proximal sides and the pigtail at the proximal end prevent migration or dislodgement of the PS. Relatively large apertures below the flaps and four small holes between 2 and 4 cm from the tip improve the drainage effect. The stent has an effective length of 15 cm, which allows it to be used in a wide variety of cases, whether benign or malignant. The lumen of the PS is widened to improve drainage, whereas a balance between stiffness and flexibility in the stent material improves ease of insertion into the bile duct.

Endoscopic Procedures

EUS‐HGS was performed by either an expert (T.I., with experience of more than 100 EUS‐HGS procedures) or trainees (with experience of fewer than 50 procedures). EUS‐HGS was performed using a conventional curved linear echoendoscope (GF‐UCT 260; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan, or EG‐740UT; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) as previously described [13]. Briefly, after puncture of the bile duct with a 19‐gauge or 22‐gauge needle under EUS guidance, an insulated stiff guidewire (0.025‐in. VisiGlide for a 19‐gauge needle or 0.018‐in. Fielder for a 22‐gauge needle) was advanced. Tract dilation was primarily performed using a 7‐Fr ultra‐tapered bougie dilator or a 3‐mm tapered‐tip balloon dilator. If dilation was difficult due to a rigid bile duct wall, a drill dilator or a 4‐mm balloon dilator was used, with a cautery dilator reserved as a last option. When a 0.018‐in. guidewire was used, it was exchanged for a 0.025‐in. guidewire after catheter advancement. Following tract dilation, a repositionable plastic stent was placed at the HGS site. In patients with malignant distal biliary obstruction, if the guidewire could be advanced into the duodenum, additional antegrade stenting using an uncovered metal stent was performed [12] (Figure 3).

Study Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcome was technical success, defined as successful placement of the PS between the intrahepatic bile duct and the stomach in an appropriate position. The secondary outcomes were the functional clinical success rate, procedure‐related adverse events, and long‐term clinical outcomes, including the rate of recurrent biliary obstruction (RBO), time to RBO, and stent‐related adverse events. Functional clinical success was defined as a reduction in serum total bilirubin by 50% or a decrease to < 2 mg/dL and sufficient improvement of liver dysfunction within 2 weeks in cases with malignant biliary obstruction, and as improvement of cholangitis and establishment of an access route for subsequent antegrade treatment in benign cases. The severity of the procedure‐related and stent‐related adverse events was graded in accordance with the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy lexicon. RBO was evaluated according to the 2024 Tokyo criteria [14, 15].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or as the median [interquartile range (IQR)] and were compared between groups using the Student's t‐test or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test. Time to RBO was compared using the Kaplan–Meier product‐limit method. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log–rank test, and patients who underwent planned additional antegrade intervention for benign diseases before RBO or died without RBO were censored. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 108 patients underwent EUS‐HGS using the repositionable PS during the study period. After eight exclusions, 100 of these patients were included in the study. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The patients had a mean age of 67.2 years and consisted of 54 men and 46 women. The indications for EUS‐HGS were as follows: biliary drainage for malignant biliary strictures (n = 72), creation of an access route for antegrade therapy in benign biliary disease (n = 26), and control of recurrent cholangitis (n = 2). Anatomy was normal in 50 cases and surgically altered in 50. At the time of EUS‐HGS, 23 patients had cholangitis and 60 had obstructive jaundice (total bilirubin > 2 mg/dL).

It occurred in six cases; imaging showed no localized bile collection or biloma, and all six patients improved promptly with conservative antibiotic therapy. In one case, early stent kinking on the gastric side caused drainage failure, followed by bile leakage and biloma formation between the stomach and liver; this was managed by stent repositioning and EUS‐guided drainage. In another case, intraperitoneal bleeding due to gastric wall vessel injury required coil embolization.

Comparison of Short‐Term Clinical Outcomes Between Malignant and Benign Cases

The short‐term clinical outcomes of EUS‐HGS are compared between malignant and benign cases in Table 3. In benign cases, intrahepatic bile duct dilation was performed significantly less often, and a significantly higher number of cases required 22‐gauge needle puncture. Furthermore, there were many benign cases with a rigid bile duct wall caused by recurrent cholangitis, and a significantly higher number of cases required a drill dilator or 4‐mm balloon dilator as a tract dilation device. However, there was no significant difference in the final clinical outcomes. These results suggest that procedures using this stent can be performed safely and effectively in both benign cases with high procedural difficulty and malignant cases.

Long‐Term Clinical Outcomes

The long‐term outcomes are shown in Table 4. In 24 cases, RBO occurred because of cholangitis or recurrence of jaundice. In all cases, the guidewire could be inserted into the bile duct from the side of the placed PS, and reintervention was performed. In one case, cholangitis developed because of stent occlusion within 4 weeks after the procedure. Although the tract was not sufficiently mature, it was possible to access the bile duct using a straight‐type guidewire, and cholangitis was managed by additional placement of a nasobiliary drainage tube. Reintervention was required in 52 cases and was successful, including in those requiring scheduled additional procedures other than for RBO. Kaplan–Meier curves for stent patency are shown in Figure 5. Stent patency was maintained for significantly longer after EUS‐HGAS than after EUS‐HGS (median time to RBO, 168 vs. 389 days, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The findings of this prospective study confirm the efficacy and safety of EUS‐HGS using a novel repositionable PS. Although trainees performed 76% of the procedures, the overall technical success rate was high at 99%, suggesting that the technique may be reproducible in a high‐volume center with substantial experience using this repositionable PS. The adverse event rate of 10% is comparable to the 15.5% reported in a recent meta‐analysis of EUS‐guided biliary drainage (EUS‐BD) procedures [10]. The drainage effect in patients with obstructive jaundice due to malignant biliary strictures was favorable, and longer stent patency was achieved when antegrade stenting was added whenever feasible. Furthermore, reintervention was easily performed in cases of RBO and when additional antegrade treatment was required for benign disease. The ability to pull back the stent allowed safe management of difficult cases, improving overall procedural safety. This repositionable PS may therefore be considered a useful option for EUS‐HGS.

Several recent reports have highlighted the efficacy of EUS‐guided choledochoduodenostomy (EUS‐CDS) using a lumen‐apposing MS for malignant biliary obstruction [16, 17]. This technique is simple, reliable, and associated with a low risk of bile leakage. A meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing EUS‐CDS and conventional ERCP demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes [18]. The absence of post‐ERCP pancreatitis has prompted discussion of EUS‐CDS as a primary drainage approach. However, when the common bile duct is not sufficiently dilated, the risk of procedural failure increases. A retrospective multicenter study identified a bile duct diameter of ≤ 15 mm as a risk factor, with a 7% failure rate and serious adverse events in half of these cases [19]. Additionally, retrograde cholangitis remains a concern in patients with duodenal strictures. In cases requiring double stenting, the risk of biliary dysfunction was significantly higher with EUS‐CDS than with EUS‐HGS [20]. Moreover, in patients with surgically altered anatomy, EUS‐CDS is not feasible and EUS‐HGS is often the only option. Therefore, proficiency in both techniques is essential.

In many institutions, a MS is placed in the HGS route during EUS‐HGS to reduce bile leakage. Although MS provides better drainage than a PS, there are concerns regarding serious adverse events such as migration or dislodgement [21]. Techniques to prevent stent migration and devices with antimigration features have been developed, but complete prevention remains difficult [22, 23, 24]. There has been a report in which, despite use of an MS with an antimigration function, migration on the gastrointestinal side was prevented, whereas the hepatic side migrated and required emergency surgery [25]. Furthermore, when dilation of the intrahepatic bile duct is insufficient, there is a risk of focal cholangitis or liver abscess due to obstruction of bile duct branches. RBO caused by hyperplasia over time is also a concern. A partially covered MS, in which the hepatic side or half of the stent is uncovered, may help address these issues; however, problems remain regarding removability after long‐term placement [2, 26]. Because stent removal is particularly important in benign cases, careful stent selection and placement are required, making the procedure technically demanding for less experienced operators. In contrast, PS offers several advantages, including a lower risk of branch duct obstruction, ease of positioning and placement, and removability after long‐term placement. In addition, reintervention is generally easier, making PS especially beneficial in benign biliary disease. These practical considerations provide a rationale for PS, but the present single‐arm study cannot establish superior patency, safety, or reintervention outcomes compared with MS.

The main disadvantage of a PS is the higher risk of bile leakage immediately after placement compared with a MS. Early dedicated PS systems used a pusher‐type stent, and tract dilation was performed with a 4‐mm balloon or cautery dilator, resulting in slight overdilation and an increased risk of delayed bile leakage. However, with current practice using a 7‐Fr bougie dilator or a 3‐mm balloon dilator, bile leakage is theoretically minimal after successful placement of a 7‐Fr PS [27, 28]. The repositionable feature also allows the stent to be pulled back and additional tract dilation to be performed when the bile duct wall is rigid. In our study, stent placement was achieved in 79% of cases using a 7‐Fr bougie or 3‐mm balloon dilator, and the biliary peritonitis rate of 7% was comparable to that reported for MS. In one case, a biloma requiring EUS‐guided drainage developed due to poor drainage caused by early stent kinking within the stomach. This issue has been rare in our experience but should be kept in mind.

EUS‐HGAS has been reported to be useful because placement of an MS across a distal bile duct stricture, together with a stent in the HGS route, creates two drainage pathways [29, 30]. This approach is expected to prolong time to RBO and reduce the risk of biliary peritonitis. A large retrospective study with propensity score matching demonstrated significantly longer time to RBO with EUS‐HGAS compared with EUS‐HGS alone [31]. Although that study included many cases with MS placement in the HGS route, our results similarly showed significantly prolonged RBO‐free survival with additional antegrade stenting compared with PS placement alone. Although acceptable stent patency can be achieved with EUS‐HGS using PS, the need for regular stent exchange due to the risk of occlusion‐related cholangitis remains a limitation. This drawback may be mitigated by additional antegrade metal stenting at the stricture site. Although concerns exist regarding adverse events such as pancreatitis and cholecystitis, the risk can be minimized by placing an uncovered MS across the papilla only when the pancreatic duct is already obstructed by tumor. In our series, no cases of pancreatitis or cholecystitis occurred among the 35 patients who underwent EUS‐HGAS.

In recent years, several studies have evaluated the efficacy of antegrade therapy via the EUS‐BD route in benign cases [5, 6, 7, 8, 32, 33]. In such cases, stents placed in the HGS route must be removable to allow additional antegrade treatment and accommodate long‐term management; therefore, a PS or fully covered MS is generally selected. In our series, bile duct dilatation was often insufficient in benign cases, increasing procedural difficulty and sometimes necessitating puncture of the central bile duct. In these situations, use of a fully covered MS raises concerns about branch duct obstruction and hyperplasia due to overdilation or bile duct spasm. Even under these conditions, the repositionable PS can be placed safely and offers advantages in benign disease. Peroral cholangioscopy‐guided antegrade intervention via the HGS route has also been reported to be effective [6, 34]. Although insertion of a cholangioscope typically requires further dilation after placement of a 7‐Fr PS, recent advances in smaller diameter cholangioscopes have facilitated this approach. Nevertheless, with ongoing development of fully covered MS devices with antimigration features, further evaluation is needed to determine the optimal stent choice for benign cases. The repositionable PS enables safe and reliable procedures with minimal bile leakage and is applicable to both benign and malignant conditions. We have previously reported its effectiveness and safety for acute cholangitis and preoperative drainage [9, 35]. Because the stent can be pulled back and repositioned, it is particularly suitable for training novice operators. In addition, PS is less expensive than MS, contributing to reduced medical costs.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with extensive experience using this PS, and even trainees were familiar with the device, introducing potential technical bias. Second, the PS is currently available only in limited regions, and some tract dilation devices used in this study are not widely accessible, limiting generalizability. Third, this study had no metal stent comparator; therefore, no conclusion can be drawn regarding whether PS for EUS‐HGS or EUS‐HGAS provides clinically meaningful advantages over MS for EUS‐HGS in terms of patency, safety, or reintervention. Fourth, the comparison of EUS‐HGS alone with EUS‐HGAS was an exploratory treatment‐as‐received analysis and was subject to substantial selection bias because antegrade stenting was attempted only when guidewire passage across the stricture was feasible. Fifth, the high proportion of procedures performed by trainees supports feasibility within an experienced center but does not by itself establish reproducibility in less experienced settings. Randomized or appropriately controlled multicenter studies comparing PS and MS, as well as prospectively defined evaluations of antegrade stenting and operator experience, are needed.

In conclusion, the MS is currently the mainstream stent of choice for EUS‐HGS. However, when this novel repositionable PS becomes more widely available, it is expected to become a useful option.
