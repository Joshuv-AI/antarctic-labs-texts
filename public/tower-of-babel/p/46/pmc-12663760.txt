Domain-like patterning of dendritic asymmetry and orientation defined by gap junctions in the mouse retina
Authors: Gergely Szarka, Anett Futácsi, Tamás Kovács-Öller, Boglárka Balogh, Boldizsár Czéh, Béla Völgyi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryRetinal topology, along with dendritic arbor symmetry and orientation, are key morphological features that are closely linked to the visual functions of RGCs. tOFF-α RGCs are implicated in the detection of approaching motion, an essential visual cue for triggering innate escape responses. Here we demonstrate that dorsally and ventrally oriented tOFF-α dendritic arbors are preferentially found in the temporal and ventral-peripheral retinal regions, respectively. This spatial organization likely enhances sensitivity to objects approaching from above or behind. Notably, tOFF-α RGCs that are electrically coupled via GJs exhibit similar arbor asymmetry and orientation, forming distinct morphological clusters that are spatially segregated from neighboring arrays. These clusters suggest the existence of localized functional domains within the tOFF-α RGC population, potentially supporting a refined mechanism for direction-specific approach detection.Subject areas: neuroscience

Introduction

Retinal Ganglion Cells (RGCs) and their upstream networks detect visual features, such as color, contrast, movement, and others, in a subtype-specific manner.1,2 The traditional view suggests that RGCs are evenly spread across the retina, forming regular mosaics within each subtype to ensure all visual features are equally represented across the visual field.3,4,5 However, recent research challenges the idea of uniform RGC distribution, showing the existence of region-specific retinal maps and related anisotropic functional properties.6,7,8,9 One type of regional specialization stems from uneven spatial inputs, such as the different ways color is processed in dorsal versus ventral retinal areas. For example, the ventral retina in mice has a higher density of blue cones, allowing better detection of objects silhouetted against the sky in the upper visual field. This asymmetry travels through the retinal circuitry and is transmitted to central visual centers by color-selective RGCs, helping to distinguish objects against the blue sky in the mouse’s upper visual field.3,10,11,12 Another form of retinal anisotropy involves asymmetries in dendritic arbor shapes and the manner in which inputs are sampled. For instance, JAM-B cells and anterior motion-sensitive direction-selective RGCs (DSGCs) have asymmetric dendritic trees, oriented along the dorsal-to-ventral axis or pointing temporally, aligned with their roles in detecting upward and forward motion, respectively.13,14 Additionally, both ON-OFF and ON DSGCs are arranged to match the optic flow produced by translational and rotational self-motion, a structural feature supported by their dendritic tree orientations.15,16 Overall, these findings highlight the importance of both topographic distribution and dendritic architecture in shaping visual function.

Among RGC subtypes, transient OFF alpha (tOFF-α) cells are particularly well-studied due to their critical role in detecting approaching objects, a key visual feature for initiating escape behavior.17,18 These cells form a regular mosaic across the retina, which has traditionally been interpreted as evidence for uniform feature extraction.19,20 However, tOFF-α cells also display notable topographic variation, including a peak in cell density within the mid-peripheral ventro-temporal retina7,19 and a gradient from transient to sustained light responses along the ventral-to-dorsal axis.21,22 Additionally, while some tOFF-α cells exhibit radially symmetric dendritic arbors, the majority display asymmetric structures.23,24,25,26 Functionally, tOFF-α cells establish both homogeneous RGC-RGC and heterogeneous RGC-amacrine cell gap junction (GJ) connections,23,24,27,28,29 which have been proposed to mitigate potential signal anisotropies caused by morphological asymmetry. However, the relationship between dendritic arbor symmetry and retinal topography in tOFF-α cells remains poorly understood. Furthermore, it is unclear whether lateral GJ coupling serves to average out functional disparities arising from variations in dendritic orientation.

In this study, we observed modest preferences for upward (ventral-to-dorsal) and downward (dorsal-to-ventral) dendritic arbor orientation in tOFF-α RGCs located in the temporal and dorsal peripheral retinal regions, respectively. Although the functional significance of these biases remains to be determined, these observations suggest that the orientation of dendritic arbor may play a role in the retinal ganglion cell’s response to light. Surprisingly, tracer-coupled tOFF-α cells consistently shared similar dendritic asymmetry and orientation, forming distinct morphological clusters that differed from adjacent tOFF-α populations. This indicates that rather than compensating for dendritic disparities, GJ-mediated coupling appears to promote the clustering of similarly oriented cells, suggesting the formation of localized functional domains within the tOFF-α population. While functional domain organization has been well documented in central visual structures such as the primary visual cortex (V1) and the superior colliculus (SC),20,30,31 our findings represent evidence of RGC clustering and domain formation in the retina, the most peripheral component of the visual system.

Results

Quantification of RGC dendritic arbor symmetry and orientation

It has been shown previously that tOFF-α cells display a variety in their dendritic arbor structures, as some cells display a somewhat asymmetric tree, whereas other arbors are symmetric.23,28 The goal of this work is to describe the extent of this morphological variation and to reveal any organizing schemes that determine it. To study this, we obtained quantitative measures for dendritic arbor asymmetries and orientations for individual tOFF-α RGCs (note that orientation values are only relevant for asymmetric arbors). The selective labeling, isolation of individual RGCs, and acquisition of quantitative measures (asymmetry and orientation) were a multi-step process. To isolate the cells, we reconstructed tOFF-α RGCs using the FIJI Simple Neurite Tracer plugin (Figures 1A, 1D, and 2) in z stacks of specimens labeled for the selected tOFF-α cell(s). This approach enabled the selective visualization and analysis of individual targeted RGC dendritic arbors, while allowing exclusion of unrelated or partially labeled neurons from the analysis as needed. Following reconstruction, imaged stacks were projected along the z axis to generate a two-dimensional representation of each tOFF-α dendritic arbor. First, the geometric center of the soma was identified, after which the dendritic field boundary was defined by drawing a circle centered on the soma with a radius equal to the distance from the soma center to the most distal dendritic tip (Figures 1B and 1E). Then, we digitally removed the somas to obtain the center of mass (COM) of the dendritic field using the Measure tool in Fiji (note that the removal of the soma was necessary because the large soma area skewed the COM values). Finally, by connecting the geometrical center with the COM, we obtained a vector for each examined cell; the vector direction provided information for the arbor orientation while the vector length quantified the extent of dendritic asymmetry (Figures 1C and 1F). The orientation was quantified by calculating the angle of the vector line in relation to the horizontal temporal-nasal axis, with the four cardinal directions providing the prominent landmarks as follows: temporal: 0°, dorsal: 90°, nasal: 180°, ventral: 270° (see compass in upper right corner of Figures 1C and 1F). By utilizing this method, we were able to quantify both the orientation and the asymmetry for each examined tOFF-α RGC in our sample (Figures 1A–1C). To determine whether the observed asymmetry was significant, we generated a cell-specific null distribution simulating symmetric dendritic arbors. For each cell, a set of 50 points (representing dendritic branches) was randomly distributed within a circle of the same radius as the observed arbor. The COM was computed for each simulated arbor, and the radius normalized the distance from the soma to the COM to generate a simulated asymmetry metric. This procedure was repeated 10,000 times per cell to produce a null distribution of expected asymmetry under symmetric branching. The 95th percentile of the simulated asymmetry distribution was used as the threshold for significance. Observed asymmetry values exceeding this threshold were considered significantly asymmetric. Z scores were also calculated to quantify the magnitude of deviation from the null distribution. This approach enabled an objective, per-cell determination of dendritic asymmetry, taking into account differences in dendritic field size.

Compatibility of tOFF-α labeling methods

To selectively study tOFF-α RGCs, we either utilized a Cre-dependent TdTomato (TdT) expressing virus construct (AAV-Chrimson-TdTomato) injected intravitreally to a parvalbumin-Cre (Pv-Cre) mouse line or the SMI32 (non-phosphorylated neurofilament-H) antibody staining. It is important to note that, although the SMI32 and Pv+ RGC populations exhibit only partial overlap, both subsets encompass tOFF-α RGCs. Since neither labeling method allows for the selective staining of tOFF-α cells, we utilized either anti-HCN4 or anti-ChAT immunohistochemistry to identify the tOFF-α cells (Table 1). Only strong SMI32+ cells that co-stratified with the HCN4+ type 3a bipolar cell axon terminals32 or with the OFF ChAT lamina were used for reconstruction (Figure 3B). According to these criteria, dendrites of the examined tOFF-α cells stratified right underneath the OFF CHAT-band in stratum 8 according to the 10 strata in this classification scheme.33 In our previous studies, we demonstrated that cells displaying both HCN4+ co-stratification and high SMI32 expression exhibited tOFF-α-specific light responses and were subsequently identified as tOFF-α cells through tracer injections.24,34 Therefore, both methods (Pv-TdT labels and SMI32 IHC) were adequate to visualize tOFF-α cell somata and dendritic arbors. On the other hand, the two methods were complementary, as Pv-TdT labels resulted in sparser staining in the inner plexiform layer (IPL), thus allowing tOFF-α dendritic processes to be isolated for tracing more efficiently and precisely. In contrast, a-SMI32 staining provided labels for the entire retinal tissue, enabling topographical mapping. Therefore, it was necessary to compare the compatibility of the two labeling methods in our sample. To this end, we first utilized both Pv-TdT and SMI-32 staining in the same retinal specimen (N = 3 retinas, n = 27 cells; Figures 3A–3C). We found that all PV-TdT labeled tOFF-α somata displayed SMI-32 staining, as well attesting to the colocalization of NF-H and Pv in these RGCs. Following the graphical reconstruction of the Pv-TdT-stained RGCs, the dendritic arbor morphologies were reminiscent of those described for tOFF-α RGCs previously.23,26,27,28,35 The utilized qualitative morphological cues included a large and round (ovoid) cell body, stout primary dendrites, and a large dendritic arbor that stratified narrowly in the OFF sublamina. In addition, the a-HCN4 serum counter-label in our specimen showed that dendrites of the Pv-TdT/SMI-32 stained RGC dendrites co-stratified with type 3a bipolar cell axon terminals, further confirming the identity of our cells (Figures 3A–3C).

To examine if the two labels were compatible, we analyzed data obtained from dually stained (both Pv-TdT and SMI-32) tOFF-α RGCs (N = 3 retinas, n = 27 cells). We calculated asymmetry and orientation values for both staining and compared their results (Figures 3D–3F). We found that both asymmetry and orientation values were very similar, thus attesting (p > 0.05; orientation: Watson’s U2 test; asymmetry: Wilcoxon signed rank test) that the two staining methods were comparable, and they can be utilized in parallel in the same study.

Asymmetry and orientation of tOFF-α RGC dendritic arbors are largely independent of retinal location

Subsequently, central and peripheral retinal regions were sampled from all four quadrants of the SMI32-stained specimens to assess potential topographic specificity in dendritic arbor asymmetry and/or orientation of reconstructed tOFF-α cells. In our sample of tOFF-α cells (N = 5 retinas, n = 249 cells), we observed substantial variability in dendritic arbor symmetry ranging from highly symmetrical (4% = essentially perfect symmetry) to markedly asymmetrical (65% = extreme asymmetry) morphologies. The distribution of dendritic asymmetry values was unimodal, with a prominent peak between 20% and 35%. Furthermore, comparison across the eight retinal regions revealed that all areas contained a mixture of both symmetric and asymmetric tOFF-α RGCs. Indeed, the mean dendritic arbor asymmetry values across all examined regions consistently fell within the 20%–35% range (Figures 4A and 4B), and no significant topographic differences in tOFF-α cell dendritic asymmetry were detected. The only discernible trend was a somewhat higher proportion of symmetrical cells in the dorsal-peripheral region and a greater prevalence of asymmetric cells in the ventral-peripheral region. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p > 0.5). Thus, the observed variability in dendritic arbor asymmetry is unlikely to result from regional data pooling and is more likely attributable to other underlying factor(s).

Examined tOFF-α RGCs varied considerably for the orientation of their dendritic arbors as well; the measured values ranged between 1 and 358° (Figure 4D). Note that only RGC arbors (20.8% of all cells) with significant asymmetry values (∼15%; see methods for details) were included in this analysis. However, the frequency histogram displayed a biphasic distribution with two distinct peaks, indicating preferential arbor orientations toward either the dorso-temporal or ventro-temporal axis. Comparison of RGC dendritic orientation values across the eight retinal regions revealed significant differences between quadrants (Figure 4E; p < 0.05; ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗p < 0.05; Watson-Williams Test), whereas no significant differences were observed between central and peripheral regions (p > 0.05; Watson’s U2 test). Although a broad range of dendritic orientations was observed across all quadrants, specific regional biases emerged: (1) RGCs in the dorsal quadrant tended to exhibit dorso-nasal orientation; (2) those in the ventral quadrant favored ventral orientation; (3) nasal quadrant RGCs showed a naso-temporal alignment spanning the visual field and (4) RGCs in the temporal quadrant predominantly oriented dorsally (Figures 4C–4E). While the data do not indicate strong topographic specializations in dendritic arbor asymmetry or orientation, a modest degree of regional selectivity was observed, particularly in the peripheral retina. Specifically, tOFF-α RGC arbors in the temporal quadrant exhibited a preference for dorsal orientation, while those in the ventral-peripheral quadrant similarly favored ventrally oriented arbors (Figures 4C and 4E).

Single-cell neurobiotin-injected GJ-coupled arrays

The minimal topography-related orientation preference observed in tOFF-α RGC dendritic arbors suggests that arbor asymmetry and orientation may not play a significant role in visual signaling. Notably, GJs between neighboring tOFF-α cells and wide-field amacrine cells have been shown to equalize response kinetics.23,24,26,27,28,35 The GJ coupling of neighboring tOFF-α cells is supported by the significant dendritic overlap observed in our calculations (coverage factor: 1.26 ± 0.09; n = 8 arrays). This coupling may also function to mitigate signaling anisotropies arising from dendritic orientation and asymmetry within the coupled network. In that scenario, GJ-coupled RGC arrays should contain RGC neighbors with very different orientation and asymmetry values. To test this, we extended our observations by utilizing single-cell neurobiotin (NB) injections (N = 6 retinas, n = 10 arrays; n = 39 traced tOFF RGCs) (Figures 5A–5D; note that sampling of injected cells was performed randomly with no intention to cover all retinal areas). Following the reconstruction of both the injected and the NB-coupled tOFF-α cells, we quantified dendritic asymmetry and orientation for each cell. The pooled data revealed a broad distribution of both asymmetry (Figure 5E) and orientation values, consistent with our previous findings based on a larger dataset labeled with SMI-32. However, when the data were disaggregated and analyzed at the level of individual coupled arrays, the variability in both measures was substantially reduced (Figures 5E and 5F). Within each coupled array, tOFF-α RGCs exhibited highly similar asymmetry and orientation values, while the parameters varied considerably across different arrays. When comparing intra-array data to inter-array data, the differences were significant for both asymmetry (p < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA) and orientation (p < 0.001, Watson-Williams Test) (Figures 5E and 5F). These findings do not support our initial hypothesis that electrical coupling serves to equalize dendritic orientation and asymmetry to reduce signal anisotropy. Instead, the results suggest that GJ-coupled tOFF-α RGCs share similar dendritic properties that retain and may further enhance signaling anisotropy, potentially contributing to a specific visual processing function (see discussion). It is also noteworthy that some arrays included lightly coupled RGC somata whose dendritic arbors were not discernible, precluding their inclusion in the analysis.

Transient OFF alpha domains

Based on the above finding, we hypothesized the existence of an organizing principle wherein electrically coupled cells exhibit shared morphological and functional properties, while cells across coupled arrays differ more substantially. Such GJ-coupling-based shared properties thus suggest that these cells form discrete functional domains within the retina. To test this hypothesis on a broader scale, we examined the existence of such domains using two datasets: one derived from SMI-32-labeled tOFF-α RGCs (N = 5 retinas, n = 249 cells) and another from NB-injected, tracer-coupled tOFF-α RGC arrays (N = 6 retinas, n = 10 arrays). In both populations, dendritic asymmetry values spanned a wide range, reflecting the fact that cells were sampled from diverse retinal regions. Notably, the distributions of asymmetry values were comparable between the two datasets, suggesting that both populations contained a similar diversity of dendritic morphologies. To compare variability within and across arrays, we calculated the average of standard deviations in asymmetry values for each population. For the NB-coupled arrays, standard deviations were computed individually for each array and then averaged. To ensure a meaningful comparison, subsets of 6–9 SMI-32-labeled cells (matching the number typically observed in NB-coupled arrays) were selected from localized retinal regions (matching retinal areas of NB-coupled arrays). Selection was further constrained to ensure that the dendritic arbors of these cells collectively spanned a retinal area (∼500 × 500 μm) similar to that of the corresponding NB-coupled arrays. The results revealed a clear difference in variability: the average standard deviation of dendritic asymmetry was significantly lower within NB-coupled arrays compared to the SMI-32 datasets (Figure 6A). A similar trend was observed for dendritic orientation, where orientation values varied considerably more across the SMI-32-labeled population than within NB-coupled arrays (n = 10 arrays; asymmetry: ∗∗∗p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U-test; orientation: ∗p < 0.05, Watson’s U2 test) (Figure 6B).

To further examine the organization of tOFF-α RGCs into functional subpopulations, we analyzed reconstructed cells from both Pv-TdT and SMI-32-labeled samples in which more than seven tOFF-α RGCs were fully reconstructed within the same retinal region. This sampling strategy enabled the comparison of dendritic asymmetry and orientation values among RGCs that likely belonged to two or more distinct coupled arrays (Figures 6C and 6D). Within these local ensembles, we observed that neighboring RGCs often formed subgroups with similar dendritic asymmetry and orientation, suggesting the presence of iso-symmetric and iso-oriented subpopulations (see example in Figure 6C, Pv-TdT-labeled sample). When orientation values were plotted against asymmetry in a two-dimensional space, the data points formed clearly distinguishable clusters (Figure 6D), further supporting this observation. These findings confirm that the emergence of iso-symmetric and iso-oriented subpopulations is not exclusive to the NB-injected samples but instead represents a general feature of tOFF-α RGC organization, independent of labeling method or experimental approach.

Discussion

Dendritic asymmetry and orientation of RGCs in the mouse retina

The population of tOFF-α RGCs has been shown to form a heterogeneous population in terms of their dendritic symmetry. While some tOFF-α cells exhibit radially symmetric dendritic arbors, others display pronounced asymmetry.25,28 Despite this variation, previous studies have not explored the potential functional consequences of dendritic arbor asymmetry or the related parameter of arbor orientation, bearing in mind that only asymmetric arbors can be assigned a meaningful orientation. This study addresses this gap by examining, whether dendritic symmetry and orientation in tOFF-α cells correlate with retinal topography or reflect other underlying organizational principles. Importantly, dendritic asymmetry is not unique to tOFF-α RGCs; several other RGC subtypes in the mouse retina also exhibit this feature. For example, JAM-B cells possess highly asymmetric arbors, elongated and consistently aligned along the dorsal-ventral axis.13,23,37 This morphological polarity supports their functional specialization in detecting upward motion.13 Similarly, a subset of ON-OFF direction-selective ganglion cells (DSGCs) exhibits dendritic asymmetry oriented toward the temporal pole, enabling them to preferentially respond to anterior (forward) motion.14 These examples underscore the broader principle that dendritic asymmetry in the retina is both common and often closely tied to visual function.

However, even in these highly specialized populations, morphological variability exists. For instance, JAM-B cells in the far peripheral dorsal and ventral retina have been reported to exhibit less pronounced asymmetry or even near-symmetrical arbors.13 Thus, a degree of variability is a general feature across RGC types. Unlike JAM-B and anterior-motion-sensitive DSGCs, which are relatively homogeneous in their asymmetry and arbor orientation, the tOFF-α population examined in this study displayed considerable diversity in both parameters. In fact, only a small subset of tOFF-α cells exhibited dendritic arbor asymmetry comparable to that of JAM-B or anterior-tuned DSGCs. Moreover, arbor orientations within the tOFF-α population were broadly distributed without a clear directional bias, indicating that orientation-based functional specialization at the population level is unlikely.

Furthermore, when dendritic asymmetry and orientation were analyzed in relation to retinal topography, including quadrant location and eccentricity, no strong correlations were observed. The only exceptions were subtle trends: a ventral-to-dorsal arbor orientation bias in the temporal retina and a dorsal-to-ventral bias in the ventral peripheral retina. These localized patterns may reflect functional adaptations. For instance, ventral-to-dorsal dendritic orientation in the temporal retina could support tuning for downward motion in the binocular visual field. In contrast, dorsal-to-ventral orientation in the ventral periphery might facilitate detection of overhead object movement. Previous studies have established that tOFF-α cells are tuned to detect approaching motion and actively suppress responses to lateral motion.17,18 While approaching motion can occur from any direction, it is plausible that the visual system emphasizes directions associated with greater ecological threats, such as overhead and downward trajectories, via subtle morphological specializations. Thus, the observed orientation biases may represent anatomical substrates that support selective tuning for behaviorally salient motion cues.

Potential visual function of tOFF-α RGC dendritic asymmetry and orientation preference

Interestingly, we found that tOFF-α RGCs within the same GJ-coupled array exhibited significantly less variability in both dendritic arbor asymmetry and orientation compared to randomly sampled cells, even when those samples were drawn from the same retinal region. This finding was robust across arrays and suggests that dendritic orientation, previously considered functionally ambiguous in this cell type, indeed plays a role in tOFF-α cell function. Unlike the JAM-B and anterior motion-sensitive ON-OFF direction-selective (DS) cells, whose orientation-dependent functions span their entire populations, the orientation-related tuning in tOFF-α cells appears to be confined to localized domains defined by GJ coupling. This form of functional clustering or domain organization is well-documented in other areas of the visual system. For example, neurons in the primary visual cortex form ocular dominance and iso-orientation columns,38,39,40 and similar clustering of functionally aligned neurons has been observed in subcortical structures such as the SC where functional clustering of matching orientation tuned neurons was observed.20,30,41 However, to our knowledge, this study presents the first evidence of morphological clustering among RGCs in the mammalian retina. Specifically, we demonstrate that tOFF-α RGCs with similar dendritic asymmetry and orientation are not randomly distributed across the retina but instead form localized “iso-asymmetry” and “iso-orientation” domains. This observation was unexpected, as our initial hypothesis posited that GJ coupling among tOFF-α cells with diverse dendritic orientations would serve to normalize or average out orientation-dependent signal disparities. Contrary to this assumption, our data reveal the opposite: electrical coupling appears to reinforce, rather than diminish, local signal anisotropy. We propose that this morphological organization provides the anatomical substrate for the formation of functional tOFF-α RGC domains, wherein local signal disparity, arising from aligned dendritic asymmetry and orientation, is not suppressed, but rather amplified, possibly to serve a specialized role in visual processing within discrete retinal microcircuits. The SC, a direct retinorecipient visual brain center, contains diverse functional cell types that are organized into spatially clustered patterns.42 However, it remains unclear how relatively unbiased visual signals from specific RGC types are transformed within the SC into specialized and clustered representations. Our observation of tOFF-α RGC domain formation may contribute to this transformation, potentially facilitating the emergence of functional specificity. Elucidating this process could provide critical insights into how the SC integrates parallel streams of retinal input to construct functional maps that underlie visually guided behaviors.

Cells within each domain were directly connected via GJs, and this electrical coupling likely enhances functions related to dendritic arbor asymmetry and orientation. These findings suggest that, in addition to operating at the level of individual cells and the entire population, tOFF-α RGCs also function as local domains, spatially constrained groups of electrically coupled cells with shared morphological properties. This domain-level organization provides further support for the idea that tOFF-α RGCs, and potentially other GJ-coupled RGC subtypes, encode multiple aspects of the visual scene and transmit multiplexed visual information to downstream brain areas.43,44,45 Within this multiplexing framework, visual information may be encoded across three distinct organizational levels, including (1) single-cell encoding, representing precise, cell-specific visual features such as object position; (2) local domain encoding, where groups of coupled RGCs contribute information such as motion direction or orientation; and (3) global population encoding, involving large RGC assemblies that span broader retinal regions and extract high-level visual statistics. While the exact nature of the multiplexed features remains to be fully elucidated, prior studies have shown that tOFF-α RGCs are particularly tuned to detect approaching objects,17,18,24 thus the multiple levels of coding might be related to this visual aspect. It is plausible, therefore, that different components of an approaching stimulus are distributed across these hierarchical levels: for instance, object momentary location could be encoded by individual cells, motion detection or speed by large-scale RGC ensembles, and motion direction by domain-level organization within GJ-coupled networks. This domain-based encoding of motion direction may be particularly critical in cases where the trajectory of an approaching object is not strictly perpendicular to the retinal surface and instead contains both approaching and lateral motion components. As shown previously, lateral motion triggers rapid inhibition of tOFF-α RGCs,17 a mechanism thought to suppress responses to irrelevant stimuli such as non-threatening objects sweeping across the visual field. However, when a predator approaches from an oblique angle, such as during a diving attack, the lateral component could, in principle, suppress the detection of the actual threat via this same inhibitory mechanism. In such cases, a reinforcing mechanism, such as the amplification of orientation-specific signals through GJ coupling, may counteract the inhibition at the single-cell level and preserve a behaviorally relevant signal. Considering the animal’s visual axis and the extent of its monocular and binocular fields, the ventral and temporal GJ-coupled tOFF-α RGC domains appear to be optimized for detecting predators approaching from above and behind. Although frequent head pitch and roll movements can substantially alter visual conditions, it remains plausible that mice derive functional advantage from this morphological arrangement. While this model remains speculative, it raises the possibility that domain-level organization in the retina supports robust detection of ecologically important visual events under complex motion conditions. Future studies will be required to experimentally test this hypothesis and define the computational advantages conferred by GJ-mediated domain formation in the retina.

Limitations of the study

This study is based on morphological reconstructions and tracer-coupled arrays, without direct physiological recordings to confirm domain-level functional consequences. Sampling was limited to specific retinal regions and may not capture full variability across the retina. Finally, the proposed role of tOFF-α domains in approach detection remains speculative and requires in vivo behavioral validation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Adult male or female (P30-90) Pv-Cre (Parvalbumin-Cre, Strain #:017320, The Jackson Laboratory; n = 14) mice were used in this study. After overnight dark adaptation, animals were put under deep anesthesia using Forane (4%, 0.2 mL/L) inhalation and terminated through cervical dislocation. Dissection and experimentation were carried out in mammalian Ringer’s solution (125 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgCl2, 25 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM glucose, pH 7.449) under dim red illumination. The eyes and the retina were removed and hemisected anterior to the ora-serrata. All animals were treated in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Maintenance, animal housing, and experimental protocols were all carried out in accordance with the local Animal Welfare Committee guidelines and regulations (University of Pécs, BA/73/00504-5/2021; BA02/2000-27/2024) and ARRIVE guidelines. All efforts were made to minimize pain and discomfort, and all procedures were done by obeying the 3R law.

Method details

Tracer and dye injections

Intracellular dye injections were performed with an Axopatch 200B patch clamp (PC) amplifier (Axon Instruments Inc., Union City, CA, USA) and ICS-filled PC pipettes (20–30 MΩ borosilicate glass pipettes, 1.5/0.84 mm ID/OD, WPI) in loose patch configuration (voltage clamp mode). Signals were digitized with a Digidata 1440A ADC (Axon Instruments, Inc.) and acquired with WinWCP software (John Dempster, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, UK). Electrodes were pulled with a P-87 micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA, USA). The ICS contained 125 mM potassium gluconate, 8 mM NaCl, 0.6 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM Mg-ATP, and 0.4 mM Na-GTP at pH 7.3 (KOH adjusted) and was supplemented with a combination of 0.5% A568-hydrazide and either 4% NB to fill up target cells via electroporation [NB: +65 mV pulses at 1 Hz (V = −50 mV; R = 90 MΩ).

Viral injections

The mice were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (90 mg/body weight). Subsequently, the mice were placed on a heating pad, maintaining a steady 33°C body temperature. The eye was carefully elevated from its socket, and a small puncture was made just under the ora serata with a 27-G needle. Utilizing a borosilicate pipette (1–2 MΩ borosilicate glass pipettes, 1.5/0.84 mm ID/OD, WPI), 0.5 μL of pAAV-Syn-FLEX-rc[ChrimsonR-tdTomato] (AAV5) (100 μL at titer ≥5×1012 vg/mL, Addgene) virus was injected into both eyes of Pv-Cre mice (3–4 weeks incubation period).

Post hoc histochemistry

To visualize NB-filled cells, cells were electroporated for a minimum of 15 min, and the sample was left incubating in oxygenized Ringer’s Solution for at least 30 min. In addition to tracer visualization, we also performed other immunohistochemistry staining (Table 1). In all cases the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15–25 min, washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and blocked with CTA (5% Chemiblocker, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.05% sodium azide in PBS) overnight, then incubated in the primary antibodies diluted in CTA for 2 days, and following a through washing (4× in PBS), the secondary antibodies were added, if necessary along with Streptavidin Cy5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1,500× dilution) for the Neurobiotin visualization (Table 1) in CTA overnight. Washed retina samples were placed on slides mounted in VectaShield (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) and cover-slipped for microscopy.

Confocal microscopy and image processing

Retinal samples were scanned with a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope with 20× (Z = 1 μm; Zeiss W Plan-Apochromat 20/1.0×) and 63× objectives (Z = 0.5 μm; Zeiss Plan Apochromat 63/1.4) at high resolution and normalized laser intensity. Minor manipulations of brightness and contrast of images were performed in FIJI – ImageJ, NIH, and Adobe Photoshop CC (Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

Cell reconstructions

For all samples, retinal topography was determined using anti S-opsin labeling.50 Subsequently for whole retinal labels, such as the SMI-32 staining or Pv-Cre x AAV-Chrimson-TdTomato expression, central (0.5 mm from the optic disk) and peripheral (1 mm from the optic disk) areas (500 μm × 500 μm) were selected in the four cardinal topographic quadrants (Ventral, Dorsal, Nasal, Temporal) account for a total of 8 sample regions from each retina. High-resolution (2970 pixel by 2970 pixel) confocal Z-stacks were taken from the bottom of the ganglion cell layer to the top of the inner plexiform layer. (Figures 2A and 2B). Confocal z-stacks were then imported into FIJI. The transient OFF alpha cells were identified by the co-stratification of the RGC arbor with either the HCN4 (Hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated potassium channel 4) (Figures 2C–2E) or with the OFF ChAT (Choline Acetyltransferase) lamina, previously shown to have tOFF-α specific light responses (such as brisk transient full-field response and selectivity for approach stimuli over bar stimuli).24,34 Utilizing the Simple Neurite Tracer51 plugin, all paths of each cell were traced. First, the soma was traced in concentric circles from the first to last virtual section in which it was present, then, originating from the soma path, further paths were traced to the upcoming intersection until the whole arbor was selected. Having selected all the paths belonging to each cell, the fill-out feature was performed, and the fill was exported as a grayscale image. The grayscale images were used for further analyses, and using the FIJI three-dimensional (3D) viewer plugin, 3D models were generated for visualization.

Asymmetry and orientation feature analyses

After the cell reconstruction, the Z-stacks for each given cell were collapsed using the standard deviation Z-project feature in FIJI. The center points of the somas were marked, and then the somas were removed artificially without influencing the arbor metrics. Using the x, y coordinates of the center point of the soma as the midpoint and the distance to the furthest point on the dendritic arbor as radius, a circle was drawn with the “draw.circle” command in FIJI macro editor. This circle was then added as a region of interest (roi), and the center of mass (CoM) was determined, using the measure command. Calculating the distance between the geometrical center (defined as the arithmetic center of the soma) and the center of mass, then dividing that by the radius of the roi circle and multiplying it by 100, we were able to get an asymmetry ratio given as a percentage. Where the number approaches zero if the cell is entirely symmetrical, and it increases toward 100% if the cells are more asymmetrical (see equation below).

The angle of the vector connecting the geometrical center and the center of mass relative to the horizontal Temporal-Nasal axis was calculated. The four cardinal directions providing the prominent landmarks were as follows: Temporal: 0°, Dorsal: 90°, Nasal: 180°, Ventral:270° for the left eye (for the right eye, the Temporal and Nasal angles were switched around). All cells were given an asymmetry and an orientation metric.

Circular mean calculation, statistics, and visualization

The circular nature of the orientation angles means that calculating the arithmetic mean or standard deviation will give a skewed result, because 0° equals 360°. Therefore, we calculated the circular metrics using the pycircstat Python package (https://github.com/circstat/pycircstat). Visualization of circular boxplots was done by using the R code published in Butarazzi et al. 2018.52 Morphological domains were determined using the Python toolbox Scikit-learn48 to perform the K-means clustering algorithm, where the appropriate cluster number was determined using the elbow method.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Origin2018 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) or the pycircstat Python package (https://github.com/circstat/pycircstat) in case of circular data. Statistical significance was assumed under p = 0.05, and statistical significance was reported as ∗ p < 0.05 and ∗∗∗ p < 0.001. Statistical results are shown both in the Results and the respective Figure legends. The relevant definitions of N and n-values are stated in the corresponding Result paragraph.
