Differential functionality of fluoropyrimidine nucleosides for safe cancer therapy
Authors: Tim Holzinger, Julia Frei, Natalia Teresa Jarzebska, Hans-Dietmar Beer, Thomas M. Kündig, Steve Pascolo, Severin Läuchli, Mark Mellett
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Chemotherapies are standard care for most cancer types. Pyrimidine analogs including 5-fluorouracil, cytosine arabinoside, 5-azacytidine, and gemcitabine are effective drugs that are utilized as part of a number of anticancer regimens. However, their lack of cell-specificity results in severe side effects. Therefore, there is a capacity to improve the efficacy of such therapies, while decreasing unwanted side effects. Here, we report that while 5-fluorocytosine is not chemotherapeutic in itself, incorporated into a ribonucleoside and more importantly into an RNA oligonucleotide, it induces cytotoxic effects on cancer cells in vitro. Interestingly, these effects are rescued by both uridine and thymidine. Similarly, in-vitro 2′-deoxy-5-fluorocytidine inhibits the growth of tumor cells but has the advantage of being less toxic to human primary cells compared with 5-fluorocytidine, suggesting that the deoxyribonucleoside could exhibit less side-effects in vivo. Thus, this work indicates that the potency of 5-fluorocytidine and 2′-deoxy-5-fluorocytidine should be further explored. In particular, oligonucleotides incorporating 5-fluorocytosine could be novel chemotherapeutic drugs that could be formulated in cancer-specific particles for safe and efficacious cancer treatments.

Introduction

Although immunotherapies have emerged as potent and safe tools against numerous malignancies, chemotherapies remain the standardized treatment of care for most cancers. Pyrimidine analogs such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cytosine arabinoside, 5-azacytidine, or gemcitabine are effective drugs that are the basis of a number of anticancer regimens, including breast cancer and colorectal cancer (CRC). Specifically, 5-FU remains the first line of treatment in CRC management. Despite the efficaciousness of such chemotherapies, their lack of cell specificity induces side effects and extended treatment courses that can lead to drug resistance [1]. Therefore, there is scope to improve the current fluoropyrimidine arsenal (Supplementary Figure 1A–F, Supplemental digital content 1, http://links.lww.com/ACD/A559) and increase their specificity.

Fluoropyrimidines were first noted for their antitumour effects when it was discovered that rat hepatomas acquired uracil more rapidly than normal tissue, indicating that uracil metabolism could be a potential therapeutic target [2,3]. Since then, fluoropyrimidine 5-FU has been used for the treatment of a number of cancers, including breast and head and neck cancers but has especially made an impact in CRC management [4].

Indeed, 5-FU is the backbone of most therapeutic regimens for patients with CRC, for whom sole surgical excision is insufficient. This may be for adjuvant, neoadjuvant, sole chemotherapy, or combined therapeutic approaches, in double or triple chemotherapeutic regimens with agents like oxaliplatin or irinotecan.

Patients with inoperable metastatic disease with wild-type RAS (KRAS, or less frequently with NRAS and HRAS) tumors, as seen in as much as half of all CRC cases, may be treated with an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitor as cetuximab, while still benefiting and thus receiving fluoropyrimidine-containing chemotherapy [3,5].

Contrarily, cancers with RAS and BRAF V600E mutations do not respond well to EGFR inhibitors, since their mutated genes cause the EGFR signaling pathway to be permanently active, hence it is ineffective to inhibit targets upstream of this mutation. In these cases, a combination of bevacizumab and fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapeutic regimens are used [6,7].

CRC is the third biggest cause of cancer mortality. 5-FU is usually administered continuously over 24–48 h of intravenous infusion after administration of leucovorin/folinic acid for an optimal risk-to-benefit ratio. Capecitabine is a relatively new oral prodrug of 5-FU, which showed noninferiority in clinical trials and can be used as a second-line therapy for colorectal, gastric, and esophageal cancers. The efficient oral delivery of capecitabine is dampened by it being less effective when used with the very widely utilized class of proton pump inhibitors [8,9]. In dermatology, 5-FU topical creams may be used to treat actinic keratosis, certain types of basal cell carcinomas, or verruca vulgaris also known as skin warts [10].

5-FU is a uracil analog with a fluorine atom at the C-5 position. Most antimetabolite drugs either inhibit essential biochemical processes or are incorporated into nucleic acids. 5-FU does both, making it a potent toxic molecule to tumor cells [4]. While more than 80% of 5-FU is metabolized into nonactive compounds by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase, the rest of the intravenously administered 5-FU enters the cell the same way uracil does, where it is converted into several active metabolites: 2′-deoxy-5-fluorouridine monophosphate (FdUMP), 2′-deoxy-5-fluorouridine triphosphate (FdUTP), and 5-fluorouridine triphosphate (FUTP) [4].

These active metabolites determine the three 5-FU-induced mechanisms of action. First, extensive misincorporation of 5-FU into RNA subsequent to its transformation into FUTP leads to disruption of physiological RNA function and processing. 5-FU exerts toxic effects not only through incorporation in mRNA, but also in tRNA, rRNA, and snRNA [11–14]. The second mechanism of action is the metabolization of 5-FU into FdUTP and its incorporation into DNA and termination of replication. The last and what is believed to be the most important pathway is the inhibition of thymidylate synthase, an essential enzyme in replenishing the thymidylate pools of the cell, by FdUMP [4]. This leads to a lack of dTMP and an accumulation of dUMP and its phosphorylated metabolites. This deficiency of dTMP can be evaded by resistant tumor cells by an overexpression of thymidine kinase to salvage thymidylate from thymidine [15], contrarily, a downregulation of thymidine kinase leads to less transformation of FdUrd to the active metabolite FdUMP, shifting toxicity away from thymidylate synthase inhibition. The accumulation of dUMP leads to increased amounts of dUTP. This can be alleviated by overexpression of the pyrophosphatase dUTPase, resulting in an increased accumulation of dUMP but the reduction of dUTP levels and thus less cell damage by DNA misincorporation [16]. Another, more obvious mechanism of resistance is the upregulation of thymidylate synthase expression.

5-FU therapy is associated with mild or severe cardiotoxicity, and can lead to life-threatening conditions in less than 1% of 5-FU-treated individuals and thus to discontinuation of treatment [17]. Other significant side effects include neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and diarrhea, which can especially be a big problem for oral drug delivery of the 5-FU precursor, capecitabine [18]. In addition, as mentioned above, there is an issue concerning drug resistance to long-term treatment with 5-FU [1,19]. It is the goal of the present study to investigate whether there is scope for improvement of fluoropyrimidines for cancer therapies.

Analogous to 5-FU, 5-FC is a cytosine analog, whose hydrogen atom on the C-5 position is replaced by a fluorine atom. 5-FC was first synthesized in 1957 and has no antimetabolic properties in humans [20]. Thus, it cannot be used as an anticancer drug. However, when metabolized to 5-FU by cytosine deaminase, which is expressed in susceptible fungal cells, it can be further metabolized to FUTP, FdUMP, and FdUTP, and exert the cytotoxic properties of 5-FU. Due to the absence of cytosine deaminase in humans, 5-FC has been a stable part of systemic antimycotic medicines. It is usually used in combination with amphotericin B for the treatment of severe cryptococcosis [21], an opportunistic infection by Cryptococcus species, severe Candida infections, or aspergillosis, which are mostly observed in immunocompromised people. Last, it is also used for chromoblastomycosis, one of the most prevalent mycoses in tropical and subtropical areas [20,22].

Here, we re-evaluated the anticancer potential of 5-FU and 5-FC as free bases and their respective nucleosides. As such, we tested 5-FC, FCyd, FdCyd, 5-FU, 5-fluorouridine (FUrd), FdUrd, and an RNA oligonucleotide containing 5-FC in different human tumor and primary cell lines. From these studies, we reinforce the concept of using 5-FU and 5-FC as free nucleosides and propose a novel approach to their inclusion into oligonucleotide sequences for the development of safer chemotherapies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The HT29 cells were a kind gift from Silvia Lang and Michael Scharl, Clinic for Gastroenterology and Hepatology, University Hospital Zurich. The SW480 cells were a kind gift from Martin Pruschy, Laboratory for Applied Radiology, Department of Radiation Oncology, University Hospital Zurich. HT29, HEK293, and HeLa were all maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in combination with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest, Nuaillé, France) and 0.2% Normocin (Invivogen, San Diego, California, USA), while for SW480 Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. Keratinocytes were maintained in a keratinocyte serum-free medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with epithelial growth factor and bovine pituitary extract (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and seeded for experiments after three passages. Fibroblasts were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified eagle serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% FBS and anti/anti (containing 100 U/ml of penicillin, 100 µg/ml of streptomycin and 250 ng/ml amphotericin B). Human primary keratinocytes were acquired from three different healthy donors. Human primary fibroblasts were acquired from one healthy donor. The day before the experiment the cells were split half-half into two flasks, to ensure an exponential growth curve, as well as a large enough quantity of cells at the day of the experiment. This exponential growth correlates with best cell health and metabolism, therefore providing optimal experimental conditions for the following days. All cell lines and primary cells were cultured at 37 °C at 5% CO2.

Drugs

5-FC, FCyd, FdCyd, 5-FU, FUrd, and FdUrd (Table 1) were all acquired from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). All drugs were prepared as a stock solution in Dulbecco’s PBS (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), aliquoted at −20 °C, and were only thawed once. The 5-FC oligo RNA dilution was freshly prepared for each experiment using the same stock solution. The synthetic 5-FC oligoribonucleotide (Dharmacon; Horizon Discovery Group Company, Lafayette, Colorado, USA) contained one fluorinated cytosine base (sequence: R185FC: 5′-A.G.U.G.U.U.A.U.U.C(5F).U.U.G.U.A.U.G.G-3′). The oligo was resuspended in RNase-free water at 1 mg/ml and stored at −20 °C. The RNA stock solution was diluted in an appropriate medium and utilized in experiments analogous to other drugs.

Cell viability assays

First, 100 µl of the appropriate medium with the drug at double the targeted concentration was added to all the treated wells. To achieve this, serial dilutions were made. The starting concentration of the drug for the serial dilution was mixed in a separate container to achieve optimal dispersion in all columns. The final drug concentrations used in the experiments were 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.0625, 0.0313, 0.0156, and 0.0078 µg/ml of 5-FC, FCyd, FdCyd, 5-FU, FUrd, and FdUrd. The nontreated control consisted of wells of appropriate medium only. To conclude, 100 µL of HEK293, HeLa, HT29, SW480, human primary keratinocytes, or human primary fibroblast cells resuspended (100 000 cells/ml) in the appropriate medium was added to sample wells. HEK293, HeLa, HT29, and SW480 cell lines and human primary keratinocytes were incubated for 3 days at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Human primary fibroblasts were incubated with appropriate drugs for 7 days, at 37 °C, 5% CO2. These two steps assured a yield of 200 µl of solution with adequate concentrations in every well. After the incubation period, the medium was removed and replaced with 200 µl of PBS-Cell-Counting-Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution at 2.5% volume of CCK-8 concentration. Cells were subsequently incubated for 6 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for optimal reduction of CCK-8 (proportional to cells per well). Subsequently, absorbance was measured in each well by the Promega GloMax Explorer (Promega Corp., Madison, Wisconsin, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm, as indicated by the manufacturer’s protocol. Drug concentrations are indicated in µg/ml since this alleviates possible biases towards larger molecules or oligonucleotides, which possess a much higher molar weight than free 5-FC or 5-FU and would give the wrong impression of potency when using molar units for concentrations.

Uridine and thymidine rescue experiments

First, 50 µl of RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, and 0.2% Normocin medium with thymidine or uridine at four-fold the targeted concentration was added to the relevant wells. The concentrations of uridine were 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.0625, 0.0313, and 0.0156 mmol/L and for thymidine 80, 40, 20, 10, 5, 2.5, and 1.25 µmol/L (Table 2). Serial dilutions were used to achieve these uridine and thymidine concentrations before adding any other substrates. In complementary experiments, only the highest concentration of thymidine and uridine from the rescue titration experiment was used. Following this, 50 µl of RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, and 0.2% Normocin medium with drugs at quadruple the required concentration was added to all the treated wells. 5-FCyd, 5FUrd, FdCyd, and FdUrd were used in this experiment; they were dosed at a concentration of 0.2 µmol/L. In complementary rescue experiments, the same drugs were dosed in HEK293 and HeLa cells as indicated in the legend. The starting concentration of uridine and thymidine for the serial dilution and the drug medium solution was mixed in a separate container to achieve optimal dispersion in all columns. To conclude, 100 µl of cell suspension (100 000 cells/ml) in RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, 0.2% Normocin medium was added to all sample wells. These three steps assured a yield of 200 µl of solution with adequate concentrations in every well. In this experiment we chose to use molar concentrations, since the molar mass of the four nucleosides tested is nearly equal, alleviating biases against small molecules and facilitating comparisons between drugs and rescuing agents. Thymidine and uridine were used at concentrations much higher than physiological levels to ensure rescuing effects of the highest concentrations of rescuing nucleosides used [23,24].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism, Version 9.1.0 (216) (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). Data was analyzed by two-way analysis of variance and the appropriate ad-hoc test as indicated: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Fluoropyrimidine ribonucleosides and deoxyribonucleosides induce dose-dependent killing of human tumor cells

HEK293 cells were incubated with increasing concentrations of fluoropyrimidine nucleosides FCyd, FdCyd, and free 5-FC for 72 h (Fig. 1a). While, as expected, free 5-FC had no effect on human cell viability, FCyd, and FdCyd induced a dose-dependent cytotoxic response, which was significant at even low concentrations (0.0078 µg/ml). In addition, FUrd and FdUrd induced significantly more cell death compared with free 5-FU (Fig. 1b).

Percentage cell viability in comparison to nontreated control after 3 days of incubation at 37 °C with indicated concentrations of drugs. Cells were treated with respective drug concentrations (by serial dilution) on day 0. Data indicated by mean and 95% confidence interval. The experiment was performed in triplicates. The data presented is one representative of three independent experiments, which were analyzed by a two-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. The nucleoside drugs are compared with their respective control and the level of significance is indicated in the respective colors.

Interestingly, the chemotherapeutic capacities of FCyd and FUrd were observed to be significantly higher than FdCyd and FdUrd in HeLa cells (Fig. 1c and d). FCyd inhibited tumor cell growth at low concentrations (0.0156 µg/ml) when compared with 5-FC (Fig. 1c), while FUrd displayed significantly superior toxicity compared with 5-FU in all but the two lowest concentrations (0.0156 and 0.0078 µg/ml) (Fig. 1d). Notably, FdUrd was significantly less efficient than 5-FU at high doses (Fig. 1d).

In the HT29 cell line, FdCyd inhibited cell growth at all concentrations tested and FCyd also showed a dose-dependent response (Fig. 1e). FdUrd was significantly more efficacious across all concentrations compared with 5-FU, while FUrd was significantly better than 5-FU in all but the lowest concentration (0.0078 µg/ml) (Fig. 1f).

The cell growth of SW480 cells was significantly more inhibited by all nucleosides than by the base analogs, except at the lowest concentration of FCyd. SW480 cells showed more susceptibility to higher drug concentrations of the ribonucleosides than their deoxidized counterparts for both pyrimidine nucleoside pairs (Fig. 1g and h). FCyd inhibited cell growth more than FdCyd at the highest four drug concentrations but was less potent at lower concentrations (0.0078–0.0625 μg/ml). The uracil derivatives all showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity in SW480 cells and FUrd was significantly more potent than 5-FU at all concentrations tested.

Interestingly, HT29 cells showed a similar susceptibility to FdCyd and FCyd. The same was observed for HEK293 cells and SW480 were less sensitive to deoxynucleosides, while HeLa cells showed only little cytotoxicity in response to FdCyd. This might suggest a difference in the pathway affected by fluoropyrimidines in HeLa cells or a difference in the expression of enzymes between the cell lines. In all cell lines, as expected, 5-FC does not impair tumor cell growth in contrast to 5-FU (Fig. 1a and b).

Thymidine and uridine show differential rescue effects in drug-treated HEK293 and HeLa cells

To determine whether functionality in HEK293 cells is due to effects on RNA or DNA we incubated HEK293 cells with 0.2 µmol/L FCyd or FdCyd and increasing concentrations of uridine or thymidine (Fig. 2a and b). We observed a significant rescue effect of all tested uridine concentrations used in combination with FCyd compared with FCyd alone, while FdCyd toxicity was not rescued at any concentration of uridine (Fig. 2a). Surprisingly, we saw a significant rescuing effect of thymidine on FCyd- and FdCyd-associated toxicity at all tested thymidine concentrations compared with the respective fluoropyrimidine alone (Fig. 2b). In the case of FUrd we showed a significant rescuing effect by all uridine concentrations compared with FUrd alone, while the cytotoxic effect of FdUrd was not rescued by any uridine concentration (Fig. 2c).

For FUrd there was no significant rescuing effect by thymidine, while in contrast to this, a significant rescuing effect of thymidine was shown at all concentrations in combination with FdUrd compared with FdUrd alone (Fig. 2d).

To determine whether HeLa cells display a divergent response to rescue with uridine and thymidine than HEK293 cells, both cell lines were treated with one concentration of thymidine (80 µmol/L) and uridine (1 mmol/L) with FCyd, FdCyd, FUrd, or FdUrd. In agreement with the previous rescue titration experiment, uridine and thymidine controls were used to show nontoxic properties and only one fluoropyrimidine concentration per cell line and agent was used. From previous cell viability assays these drug concentrations were chosen to represent the IC80.

In HeLa cells, FCyd functionality was significantly reduced by uridine but not thymidine. More interestingly and unexpectedly the same effect of uridine was found for the FdCyd-induced cytotoxicity (Supplementary Figure 2A, Supplemental digital content 1, http://links.lww.com/ACD/A559). The FUrd effect was rescued by uridine while it was enhanced by the addition of thymidine. Unexpectedly, FdUrd-induced anticancer activity was also significantly inhibited by the addition of uridine and not thymidine (Supplementary Figure 2B, Supplemental digital content 1, http://links.lww.com/ACD/A559).

In HEK293 cells (Supplementary Figure 2C and D, Supplemental digital content 1, http://links.lww.com/ACD/A559) the cytotoxicity induced by ribonucleosides FCyd and FUrd were reversed upon addition of uridine, while anticancer effects induced by the deoxyribonucleosides FdCyd and FdUrd were both rescued by thymidine, as previously observed (Fig. 2a–d). Again, FCyd-associated cytotoxicity was also rescued by thymidine (Supplementary Figure 2C, Supplemental digital content 1, http://links.lww.com/ACD/A559) as shown in the titration assay (Fig. 2b).

The HeLa cells reacted unexpectedly to the addition of thymidine and uridine. Notably, FdUrd and FdCyd were rescued by uridine and not thymidine as one would expect for DNA-targeted fluoropyrimidines, while FCyd and FUrd were rescued by uridine as anticipated. In addition, in HEK293 cells FCyd-induced toxicity was rescued by thymidine.

Ribonucleoside fluoropyrimidines show more toxicity on human primary cells of the skin than their deoxyribonucleoside counterparts

Next, it was of interest to evaluate the toxicity of fluoropyrimidines in human primary cells. Keratinocytes and fibroblasts were subjected to increasing concentrations of our fluoropyrimidines; 5-FC, FCyd, FdCyd, 5-FU, FUrd, and FdUrd, as above for 72 h (for keratinocytes) or 7 days (for fibroblasts) (Fig. 3a–d). We observed a significantly stronger cytotoxic effect of the ribonucleosides FCyd and FUrd than their deoxidized counterparts.

In human primary keratinocytes, significantly higher toxicity was shown by FCyd compared with FdCyd across all concentrations (Fig. 3a). FUrd was shown to be significantly more toxic compared with FdUrd at all concentrations, while 5-FU was more toxic than FdUrd at the highest concentration of 1 µg/ml (Fig. 3b).

In human primary fibroblasts, a similar pattern was observed. FCyd showed a higher cytotoxic effect compared with FdCyd across all but two concentrations, while FdCyd was toxic only at the three highest concentrations (Fig. 3c). While FUrd showed significantly higher toxicity at all concentrations when compared with FdUrd, 5-FU only exerted higher toxicity than FdUrd at the two highest concentrations (Fig. 3d). The ribonucleosides, FCyd and FUrd showed a similar trend to their cytotoxic pattern in SW480 cells.

Interestingly, FCyd and FUrd were distinctly more cytotoxic to human primary keratinocytes and fibroblasts compared with FdUrd and FdCyd. Therefore, the use of FCyd and FUrd therapeutically would require a more targeted, tumor-specific drug delivery system.

5-Fluorocytosine oligoribonucleotide shows dose-dependent cell killing in HEK293 and HeLa cells

Based on our previous results incorporating 5-FU in an RNA oligonucleotide for targeted chemotherapy [25], we tested the potential of 5-FC-containing RNA. HEK293 and HeLa cells were treated for 72 h with 5-FC, FCyd, and 5-FC-containing RNA and cell viability was assessed. In HEK293 cells, we observed a significantly greater cytotoxic effect of FCyd compared with 5-FC at all drug concentrations. The 5-FC-containing RNA had a significant toxic effect on HEK293 cells at the 0.5 and 1 µg/ml concentrations (Fig. 4a).

In HeLa cells, FCyd showed significantly higher cytotoxicity compared with 5-FC in all but the lowest concentration, while the 5-FC oligo RNA showed a significantly more toxic effect compared with 5-FC at the three highest concentrations (Fig. 4b). A similarly toxic effect was achieved by incorporating FCyd into an oligo RNA as compared with free FCyd when correcting for the mass of the active compound. These data demonstrated that RNA oligos incorporating 5-FC could be a potential anticancer therapeutic when packaged in tumor-targeted nanoparticle formulations or in the form of tumor-specific aptamers.

Discussion

The fact that FCyd and FUrd exert high cytotoxicity in all four cell lines compared with FdCyd and FdUrd, which were highly efficacious only in two out of four cell lines, makes them promising candidates for cancer treatment and potentially in resistant tumors. However, most currently researched chemicals focus on the FdUMP-mediated inhibition of thymidylate synthase, for example, NUC-3383 or the fluoropyrimidine polymers FdUMP [10] (F10), or CF10, which is an F10 molecule with the addition of arabinosyl cytidine at the 3′ terminus and polyethylene glycol at the 5′ terminus [23,26–29]. This is especially interesting since a major challenge in today’s clinical chemotherapeutic treatment with fluoropyrimidines is the development of chemoresistance. However, looking at off-target effects using human primary keratinocytes and fibroblasts we found that the RNA nucleosides were more toxic than their deoxidized counterparts or 5-FU. This toxic effect on keratinocytes and fibroblasts reinstates that 5-FU can cause side effects affecting the skin with hand-foot syndrome being the most relevant and severe side-effect, ranging from mild to severe and discontinuation of treatment.

The cell lines SW480 and HT29 represent important human colon cancer subpopulations. SW480 cells are known to express a mutant p53 and have a KRAS G12V mutation, whereas HT29 also harbor a p53 mutation in addition to a BRAF V600E mutation. While the mutational status of the TP53 gene is not therapeutically decisive in CRC, mutations in KRAS and BRAF are two conditions, which prevent the use of EGFR inhibitors, leading to the greater importance of chemotherapeutics in these cases [30]. Further, these two cell lines are microsatellite stable, which is important to keep in mind because microsatellite instability in stage IV nonresectable metastatic disease is an indication for first-line pembrolizumab monotherapy.

In vitro, uridine rescued FUrd- and FCyd-induced cytotoxicity in HEK293 and HeLa cells, while interestingly, FCyd, in contrast to FUrd, was also rescued by thymidine in HEK293 cells. Although the two ribonucleosides induced nearly equal toxicity across all cell lines and primary cells tested, they showed a different trend from one another in SW480 cells and keratinocytes. This leads us to conclude that the two ribonucleosides are not metabolized in the exact same way. This may be due to differences in deaminase expression, or FCyd is not only directly metabolized into its uracil counterpart but may have other active metabolites.

In addition, we tested whether the cytotoxic effect of FdUrd, which is thought to be due to a strong inhibition of thymidylate synthase, could be reversed by co-administration of thymidine to replenish the thymidine pools since thymidylate synthase converts dUMP to dTMP. This was confirmed in HEK293 cells, which exhibited rescue by thymidine co-treatment in combination with FdCyd and FdUrd. The opposite was shown in HeLa cells, which exhibited less but still a significant rescue by uridine but not by thymidine. HeLa cells are relatively resistant to the deoxyribonucleosides FdCyd and FdUrd. This necessitated a distinctly larger amount of drug, compared with FCyd and FUrd to reach an estimated 20% cell viability desired in the rescue experiments.

This resistance may be due to (1) overexpression of thymidylate synthase, which requires an increased amount of FdUMP to inhibit the abundant enzyme in the cell; (2) overexpression of dUTPase, which metabolizes dUTP to dUMP and therefore leads to a reduction of the misincorporation of wild-type or fluorinated uracil into DNA, while also leading to an increased accumulation of dUMP; (3) downregulation of thymidine kinase, converting FdUrd to FdUMP, leading to a decreased FdUMP production and therefore less inhibition of thymidylate synthase, while upregulation of the same enzyme (thymidine kinase) would also lead to an increased replenishment of the thymidylate pools via the thymidine salvage pathway. These mechanisms would lead to a shift of toxicity towards other pathways, for example, FUTP accumulation, resulting in RNA misincorporation, which would explain the rescuing effect of uridine in HeLa cells.

Most of all, we discovered that RNA incorporating 5-FC in place of cytosine residues can be equally chemotherapeutic and inhibit the survival of tumor cells in vitro comparable to 5-FU-immunochemotherapeutic RNA (5-FU-icRNA) [25]. 5-FC is not cytotoxic in itself but can be metabolized to 5-FU and its active metabolites. Indeed, as confirmed in HEK293, HeLa, and HT29 cells, commercially available 5-FC has no effect on tumor cells. As 5-FC-icRNA acts as a chemotherapeutic drug, whereas free 5-FC cannot, this leads us to assume that the 5-FC oligonucleotides are broken down into ribonucleosides or ribonucleotides and not down to the base level. Targeting tumor cells specifically with 5-FC-icRNA using nanoparticle formulations could be a strategy to dampen down side effects associated with free fluoropyrimidines. Indeed, the benefit of increased cytotoxicity of the ribonucleosides is canceled out by an increase in side effects, but this could be resolved by a targeted delivery system. If directly targeted to the tumor tissue and thus highly concentrated locally, side effects would be offset, making ribonucleosides a superior choice of drug compared with other fluoropyrimidines. RNA nanoparticles incorporating 5-FC could be a novel chemotherapeutic agent that can be exploited clinically in sole and combination treatments for cancer.

To achieve targeted delivery, one could consider a protamine delivery system. The incorporation of mRNA into protamine protects RNA from degradation and facilitates uptake into tumor cells [31–33]. In this RNA-protamine delivery system the active compounds FCyd and FUrd, inside of an RNA oligonucleotide, could be delivered to the tumor. Alternatively, 5-FU and 5-FC containing oligonucleotides having sequences corresponding to tumor-specific aptamers could be used. Theoretically, the labile-RNA approach has superior safety benefits compared with stable DNA fluoropyrimidine polymers such as F10 or CF10. However, as we show here, deoxyribonucleoside fluoropyrimidines may be less toxic to nontumor cells than ribonucleoside fluoropyrimidines. Thus, further developments and comparisons of RNA or DNA oligonucleotides containing 5-FU or 5-FC must be conducted. We envision that such chemotherapeutic oligonucleotides could revolutionize chemotherapies, building on the available and well-characterized fluoropyrimidine drugs for more targeted, safe, and efficacious chemotherapies.
