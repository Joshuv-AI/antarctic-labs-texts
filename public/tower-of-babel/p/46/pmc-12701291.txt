Thiocarbamoylimidates as Precursors of New 5‐Amino‐1,2,4‐Triazole and Oxadiazole Derivatives In Silico Prediction of Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion Parameters
Authors: Oumaima Gatri, Thierry Roisnel, Marie Cordier, Vincent Dorcet, Mohamed Lotfi Efrit, Cédric Fischmeister
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A series of 23 heterocyclic derivatives incorporating 1,2,4‐triazole and oxadiazole motifs are synthesized via cyclization of thiocarbamoylimidates with hydrazine salts. Reaction parameters including solvent, base, and temperature are systematically optimized and very good results are obtained in the environmentally friendly reaction media composed of water and ethanol, under mild conditions of temperature (40 °C). The reaction is very versatile and allows for the introduction of a wide variety of functional groups. All the new compounds are submitted to in silico evaluation of their drug potential using the SWISS‐ADME tool. Several compounds of both families did not violate any drug likeness rules, making them potential candidates for further evaluation of their biological activity and structural optimization.

Introduction

Heterocyclic compounds play an important role in modern organic chemistry, owing to their widespread occurrence in natural products,[ 1 , 2 ] pharmaceuticals,[ 3 ] agrochemicals,[ 4 , 5 – 6 ] and advanced functional materials.[ 7 , 8 – 9 ] Their structure gives these molecules great structural diversity and a wide range of physicochemical and biological properties.[ 10 ] Heterocyclic molecules are central to many essential natural molecules such as the nitrogenous bases of DNA and RNA,[ 11 ] chlorophyl,[ 12 ] and vitamin B12.[ 13 ] In medicinal chemistry, more than half of approved drugs contain a heterocyclic core due to their ability to specifically interact with biological targets. For example, structures such as imidazoles, triazoles, and pyridines are commonly found in antifungal, anticancer, and antihypertensive agents.[ 14 , 15 – 16 ] Moreover, heterocyclic compounds play a fundamental role in the development of advanced materials, particularly in the fields of organic electronics, chemical sensors, and functional dyes.[ 17 ] Given their wide‐ranging utility, the development of new synthetic methods that are efficient, selective, and more environmentally friendly remains a priority in modern organic chemistry.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 – 22 ] Triazoles and oxadiazoles mark two classes of such heterocyclic compounds that are widely distributed in nature. Their electron‐rich structures facilitate binding to various biological and enzymatic targets, thereby conferring a broad spectrum of biological activities, such as: antibacterial, antifungal, antitumor, anti‐inflammatory, antitubercular, hypoglycemic, antidepressant, anticonvulsant, analgesic, antiviral, anticancer, antimalarial, and antioxidant activities.[ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 – 31 ] Some drugs incorporating the 1,2,4‐triazole motif have been commercialized for several years (Figure 1 ).

Some examples of APIs based on 1,2,4‐triazole.[ 57 , 58 ]

Owing to this wide range of biological applications, numerous 1,2,4‐triazole compounds have been synthesized with various substitution patterns, of which the 1,3,5‐trisubstitution pattern was extensively studied (Figure 2 ).[ 32 , 33 – 34 ] Numerous molecular structures have been developed for the synthesis of those compounds featuring essentially aromatic and heterocyclic substituents. Very often, functionalized aryl substituents are used to introduce a variety of functional groups (halides, alkoxydes, amines, nitro, nitrile, esters….).

Some examples of 1,3,5‐functionalized 1,2,4‐triazole.[ 59 , 60 , 61 – 62 ]

Beside this structural diversity brought by the triazole–carbon linkage, the functionalization by amino group directly linked to the triazole heterocycle have been rarely studied. It is expected that an additional nitrogen heteroatom could extend the portfolio of accessible structures bringing new and original properties. To date, only few examples have been reported (Figure 3 ).

Some examples of amino‐substituted 1,2,4‐triazole.[ 59 , 63 ]

For several years, our research group has directed its efforts toward the synthesis of heterocyclic compounds from functionalized imidates.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 – 41 ] Recently, we have been interested in the synthesis of triazole‐containing compounds and we have shown that the route involving N‐((sulfonyl)‐carbamoyl))‐imidates and hydrazine derivatives in methanol could lead efficiently to 5‐N‐substituted 1,2,4‐triazoles (Scheme 1 ).[ 42 ]

Synthesis of triazole sulfonamides from N‐((sulfonyl)‐carbamoyl)‐imidates.

Herein we present a new route for the synthesis of arylamino‐substituted triazole derivatives involving thiocarbamoylimidate intermediates and easy to handle hydrazine salts Scheme 2 . Our objective is to develop this synthetic methodology and to optimize the reaction conditions, paying attention to the principles of sustainable chemistry.[ 43 , 44 – 45 ] In particular, we have been looking for environmentally friendly reaction media as most of the triazole synthesis are performed in undesired solvents such as dichloroethane (DCE), dimethyl formamide (DMF), methanol, and other hazardous solvents.[ 46 , 47 ]

Results and Discussion

We initiated our study with the synthesis of triazole 3a from the 3,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)thiocarbamoylimidate 1a and phenylhydrazine hydrochloride salt. 3a was selected as it contains fluorinated substituent that are ubiquitous in medicinal chemistry[ 48 , 49 – 50 ] and because it is readily accessible in high yields from commercially available isothiocyanate and easily prepared methyl‐3‐methylbenzimidate (Scheme 3 ).

Synthesis of triazole 3a (top) and reaction mechanism (bottom).

In order to optimize the reaction conditions for the synthesis of 1,2,4‐triazoles 3a, a systematic study was conducted by varying the solvent, base, temperature, and amount of base necessary to release phenyl hydrazine into the reaction medium (Table 1 ). First attempts were conducted in toluene using either organic base (Et3N) or inorganic bases (see ESI). Surprisingly, despite their poor solubility in toluene, inorganic bases promoted the reaction, in particular Cs2CO3 which allowed full conversion of 1a but modest yield of 49% yield at 80 °C due to the formation of unidentified side‐products (Table 1, entry 3). Further experiments were conducted with cesium carbonate in various solvents including diethyl carbonate (DEC, Table 1, entry 4) and DMSO (Table 1, entry 5) in which 61% yield was reached.

Finally, we turned our attention to greener solvents such as water and ethanol. If a modest yield of 22% was obtained in water (Table 1, entry 6) due to the poor solubility of 1a, a much higher yield or 74% was reached in ethanol (Table 1, entry 7), in which 1a was fully soluble. In light of these results, we considered carrying out the reaction in a mixture of the two solvents. For this purpose, 2a and Cs2CO3 were first mixed at room temperature in water to generate free phenylhydrazine to which 1a dissolved in EtOH was added dropwise. This procedure proved cleaner than the previous one in toluene and although the conversion was not quantitative (90%), 3a was obtained with an isolated yield of 82% (Table 1, entry 8). The new compound 3a was fully characterized by NMR analysis and HRMS (see ESI) and a molecular structure was determined by X‐ray diffractometry (see ESI). As shown in Figure 4 , the molecular structure unambiguously confirmed that the reaction proceeded in a regioselective manner delivering the 1,3,5‐trisubstituted triazole 3a resulting from the initial nucleophilic addition of the primary amine of hydrazine (‐NH2, red pathaway in Scheme 3) and not the secondary amine (NHR2, blue pathway in Scheme 3). This molecular structure also revealed interesting features such as the sp2 nature of the nitrogen atom N1 bridging the triazole ring with the bis‐trifluoromethylbenzene ring (Table 2 , entry 4). This hybridization allows conjugation between those two cycles which are almost coplanar as evidenced by selected torsion angles (Table 2, entries 5 and 6) and dihedral angle between these two planar cycles (Table 2, entry 8). Of note the phenyl ring at N2 deviated from the triazole plane as a result of steric repulsion with the adjacent N‐phenyl ring (Table 2, entry 8).

With the optimal reaction conditions established, we next explored the substrate scope of the cycloaddition between 3,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)thiocarbamoylimidate 1a and a variety of substituted hydrazine salts 2 in a H2O/EtOH medium (Figure 5 ). We initially focused on aryl‐substituted hydrazine derivatives. We introduced para‐substituted phenyl groups bearing electron‐donating (OMe, Me) and electron‐withdrawing (NO2) substituents, which smoothly delivered the desired triazoles 3b‐3d in high yields ranging from 77% to 87%. The reaction was further extended to hydrazine salts containing aliphatic and functionalized side chains such as ethyl esters (CO2Et) and a primary alcohol, affording compounds 3e‐3g in good to high yields ranging from 68% to 89% Notably, the introduction of a sulfoxide‐containing side chain afforded compound 3h in up to 97% yield, demonstrating the excellent tolerance of the reaction toward polar functional groups of the hydrazine salts. Overall, the reaction exhibited broad substrate scope and delivered the desired 1,2,4‐triazoles in good to excellent yields across all tested substrates.

Scope of 3,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)thiocarbamoylimidate 1a precursors in the cycloaddition reactions with substituted hydrazine salts.

To further demonstrate the versatility of this cycloaddition protocol, we extended the scope to phenylthiocarbamoylimidates 4a‐d, bearing various substituents at the imidic carbon (R2). These were reacted with a representative set of hydrazine salts 2, under the previously optimized conditions (Cs2CO3, H2O/EtOH, 40 °C). The reaction smoothly delivered a series of new 1,2,4‐triazole derivatives 5a‐i, incorporating diversity at both the N1 and N3 positions of the triazole core (Figure 6 ). Functional groups including ethyl esters, alkyl chains, methoxy, chloro, and nitro substituents were well tolerated, affording the corresponding products in moderate to excellent yields. Notably, the presence of ester or electron‐withdrawing substituents did not hinder the cyclization efficiency. Here again, an X‐ray structure could be obtained confirming the selectivity of the cycloaddition (Figure 6, 5e, 50% ellipsoid probability, H atoms omitted for clarity). This result highlights the broad functional group compatibility of the reaction and its applicability for rapid diversification of triazole scaffolds with potential interest in medicinal chemistry.

Scope of phenylthiocarbamoyimidate 4a‐d in the cycloaddition reactions with different substituted hydrazine salts.

Encouraged by the efficiency of the reaction under mild conditions, we extended this methodology to the synthesis of 1,3,4‐oxadiazole derivatives. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the synthesis of oxadiazoles starting from thiocarbamoylimidates under such conditions. Specifically, a series of N‐aryl and N‐alkyl O‐methylthiocarbamates 4a–d were treated with hydroxylamine salts in the presence of Cs2CO3 in an H2O/EtOH medium at 40 °C for 6 h, affording the corresponding oxadiazoles 6a–d in excellent yields ranging from 88% to 96% (Figure 7 ). In addition, the same protocol was successfully applied to 3,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)thiocarbamoylimidate 1a, reacting with hydroxylamine hydrochloride under identical aqueous–ethanolic conditions, delivering the corresponding 1,2,4‐oxadiazole 7 in excellent yield. The selectivity of the reaction was here too confirmed by the X‐Ray molecular structure of 6b (Figure 7, 50% ellipsoid probability, H atoms omitted for clarity).

Scope of 3,5‐bis(trifluoromethyl)thiocarbamoylimidate 1a and phenylthiocarbamoylimidate 4a‐d in the cycloaddition reactions with hydroxylamine salts.

In Silico ADME Evaluation of Synthesized Triazole Derivatives and Oxadiazole Derivatives

In order to assess the physicochemical properties and drug‐likeness of the newly synthesized triazole and oxadiazole derivatives, an in silico ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) study was performed using the Swiss‐ADME platform.[ 51 ] The physicochemical properties, lipophilicity, water solubility, and predicted pharmacokinetic profiles were calculated for both series. In particular, compliance with the Lipinski rules has been closely scrutinized.[ 52 ] This rule of five allows predicting suitable drug candidates based on five physicochemical properties: 1) MW < 500 g.mol−1; 2) lipophilicity monitored by then octanol/water partition coefficient log PO/W which should ideally be lower than 5; 3) the number of hydrogen bond donor HBD < 5; 4) the number of hydrogen bond acceptor HBA < 10; and 5) the number of rotatable bonds RB < 10. These rules have later been completed with the topological polar surface area tPSA < 140 Å2.[ 53 ]

The 3a–3h derivatives fulfilled some of the important requirements with molecular weights ranging from 430 to 540 g.mol−1, polar surface area (tPSA) varying between 42.74 and 88.56 Å2, hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) ranging from 8–10, HBD < 5, and RB < 5 (see ESI for full details). However, in all cases this family of compounds displayed high lipophilicity and low water solubility suggesting reduced oral bioavailability and limited absorption hence making them less favorable as viable oral drug candidates. This excessive lipophilicity and low water solubility are mainly due to the two ‐CF3 substituents and the presence of phenyl‐substituents. These issues should be easy to adjust thanks to the versatility of the synthesis method and one step into this direction was already achieved with compound 3f (Figure 8 ).

Bioavailability radar for compounds 3a (left) and 3h (right).

In contrast, the 5a–5i series demonstrated more favorable ADME profiles. These compounds have lower molecular weights (288–357 g.mol−1), moderate lipophilicity (LogP range 1.86–4.62), and satisfactory water solubility (LogS range −3.71 to −6.08). Their polar surface area (tPSA) values range from 42.74 to 95.23 Å2, with HBA counts between 2 and 4, and HBD counts consistently at 1 across the series. The number of RB varied from 4–7, indicating balanced molecular flexibility and favorable physicochemical characteristics. Importantly, eight out of nine compounds in this series adhered to Lipinski, Ghose,[ 54 ] Veber,[ 52 ] Egan,[ 55 ] and Muegge[ 56 ] guidelines, suggesting strong potential for further biological testing. As observed with compounds 3a‐3h, an excess of phenyl substituents was detrimental to bioavailability due to excessive insaturation of the molecule. Here too, a simple adjustment to the molecular structure has mitigated this problem (Figure 9 ).

Bioavailability radar for compounds 5h (left) and 5b (right).

Similarly, the 6a–6d series comprised low‐molecular‐weight derivatives (MW: 203–251 g mol−1) with favorable lipophilicity (LogP range 2.58–3.41), aqueous solubility (LogS range −3.45 to −4.41), and polar surface area (tPSA) values clustered around 50.95 Å2. All compounds in this set had low HBA/HBD counts (3 HBA, 1 HBD), limited rotational flexibility (3–4 RB), making them potential candidates with favorable oral bioavailability profiles. As already observed, some compounds displayed a too high unsaturation (6a, 6b, 6d) that could be mitigated replacing a phenyl substituent by an iso‐propyl (6c, see SI)

To further investigate the physicochemical properties of the three series of compounds, their human intestinal absorption (HIA) and blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration were investigated using the “BOILED–Egg” model also accessible from the Swiss‐ADME Server. This model provides a simple and quick prediction of gastrointestinal absorption and probability of brain absorption. The model plots WlogP versus tPSA and locate the compounds in two zones of interest corresponding to gastrointestinal absorption (white zone) and brain access (yellow zone).

According to the BOILED–Egg model, the analysis of the three series of molecular structures highlights significant differences. As anticipated from results presented, the series of compounds 3a‐h does not feature favorable predictions due to their high lipophilicity and only 3g and 3f appear in the graph while all the other compounds are out of range (Figure 10 , left).

BOILED‐egg model of products 3a‐h and 5a‐h.

In the series 5a‐5i, the majority of the molecules (eight out of nine) fall within the yellow zone, indicating a general propensity for BBB penetration while being identified as non‐PGP substrates (PGP‐, not effluxed by P‐glycoprotein) (Figure 10, right). In contrast, compound 5i, positioned in the white zone, appears unable to penetrate the CNS effectively and is classified as a PGP substrate (PGP=, effluxed by P‐glycoprotein). This significant shift is likely due to the presence of the NO2 substituent, which increases the polarity of the molecule and reduces its ability for passive diffusion across the BBB.

The BOILED‐Egg model further confirmed favorable oral bioavailability profiles for compounds 6a, 6b, 6c, and 6d. All these compounds are positioned within the yellow region, indicating high probability for gastrointestinal absorption and BBB permeability (Figure 11 ).

BOILED‐egg model of products 6a‐d.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed an efficient and selective method for the synthesis of functionalized 1,2,4‐triazoles and oxadiazoles using thiocarbamoylimidates as versatile precursors. The methodology proved general and tolerant of various functional groups. It should be noted that this transformation is performed under mild temperature conditions (40 °C) in a reasonable amount of time (6 h) and in environmentally friendly reaction medium (H2O/EtOH). The in silico swiss‐ADME screening of physicochemical parameters clearly differentiates the three series, highlighting the favorable pharmacokinetic profiles of the triazoles 5 and oxadiazoles 6 as promising candidates for further in vitro and in vivo biological studies. Their balanced physicochemical properties, combined with favorable BOILED–Egg predictions, support their potential as central nervous system–active scaffolds and justify their prioritization for subsequent medicinal chemistry optimization. The series of compounds 3 was found less interesting essentially due to high lipophilicity. However, having demonstrated the broad scope of the synthesis method, the lipophilicity of these compounds should be easily adjustable to meet the requirements of Lipinski's rules, and results in this direction have already been achieved. Importantly, these findings not only deepen our understanding of the structure–property relationships within this class of heterocycles but also contribute to the broader field of medicinal chemistry by providing a strong foundation for the design of more potent and selective derivatives. The next steps will focus on synthesizing targeted analogs and conducting comprehensive biological evaluations to fully assess their therapeutic potential and mechanism of action.
