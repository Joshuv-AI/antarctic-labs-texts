Reattempt of tyrosine kinase inhibitor discontinuation after maintenance therapy with ponatinib in patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia in the chronic phase: Result of the Japan Adult Leukemia Study Group RE‐STOP219 study
Authors: Noriyoshi Iriyama, Naoto Takahashi, Katsumichi Fujimaki, Takaaki Ono, Masuho Saburi, Masahide Yamamoto, Fumihiko Kimura, Eisei Kondo, Nobuyuki Aotsuka, Hiroki Yokoyama, Seiichiro Katagiri, Yosuke Minami, Maho Ishikawa, Yachiyo Kuwatsuka, Masatomo Miura, Akiko M Saito, Toshiki I Saito, Itaru Matsumura, Yasushi Miyazaki, Emiko Sakaida, Yoshinobu Maeda, Takahiro Yamauchi, Hitoshi Kiyoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTreatment‐free remission (TFR) is now a key goal for chronic‐phase chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML‐CP) after achievement of a prolonged deep molecular response. About half of these patients can remain in remission without treatment, whereas those who relapse must resume lifelong therapy. This study investigated whether patients who previously failed tyrosine kinase inhibitor discontinuation could achieve TFR after receiving ponatinib as maintenance therapy. Patients who had relapsed after prior tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) discontinuation and subsequently regained molecular response 4.5 (MR4.5; defined as a BCR‐ABL1 transcript level ≤0.0032% on the International Scale [IS]) were enrolled, switched to ponatinib (15 mg/day) for 12 months and those who maintained MR4.5 discontinued TKI therapy. The primary end‐point was the proportion of patients with TFR at 12 months. Patients losing major molecular response (MMR) resumed ponatinib or their prior TKI. Of 50 enrolled patients, 49 received ponatinib and 41 proceeded to TKI discontinuation. At 12 months, the proportion of patients with TFR was 36.6% (90% confidence interval: 24.1–50.6). Univariate analysis identified favourable factors for TFR: first discontinuation of imatinib, longer initial TKI therapy and longer first TFR duration. In conclusion, ponatinib maintenance followed by a second attempt at TKI discontinuation may facilitate successful TFR in selected CML patients. However, the risk of adverse events highlights the need for careful patient selection.Keywords: chronic myeloid leukaemia, ponatinib, second discontinuation attempt, treatment‐free remission, tyrosine kinase inhibitor

INTRODUCTION

The introduction of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) imatinib revolutionized the treatment of chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), dramatically improving prognosis and demonstrating long‐lasting efficacy, particularly in the chronic phase (CP). 1 Second‐generation TKIs (2G‐TKIs) have further improved clinical outcomes in patients who are resistant to imatinib. 2 As a result, the life expectancy of patients with CML‐CP is now nearly equivalent to that of the general population. 3 , 4 However, lifelong TKI therapy is typically required, raising concerns about high medical costs and long‐term adverse events, including potentially fatal complications such as arterial occlusive events (AOEs). 5 , 6

Among patients with CML‐CP who achieve and sustain a deep molecular response (DMR), approximately 39%–65% are able to maintain DMR or major molecular response (MMR), thereby achieving what is known as treatment‐free remission (TFR). This paradigm shift has redefined the therapeutic goal in CML from ensuring survival to achieving TFR, with increasing emphasis on not only achieving TFR but also improving quality of life through supportive care or dose reduction. Several imatinib discontinuation trials conducted worldwide have consistently demonstrated the feasibility of TFR. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 As a result, TKI discontinuation under strict clinical criteria is now being incorporated into routine clinical practice. In Japan, the Japan Adult Leukemia Study Group (JALSG) conducted the STIM213 trial, an imatinib discontinuation study, in which 64.6% of patients maintained MMR 3 years after stopping imatinib therapy. 12 Additional discontinuation trials involving 2G‐TKIs have been conducted, yielding results that demonstrate a comparable rate of successful TFR to that observed with imatinib discontinuation. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Successful TKI discontinuation strategies are expected to offer multiple benefits, including reduced drug exposure, lower healthcare costs, avoidance of TKI‐related adverse events and improved quality of life. 18 , 19 Clinical trials evaluating a second attempt at TFR have primarily been conducted in patients who relapsed after discontinuing imatinib. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 However, patients who relapse following 2G‐TKI therapy have few remaining options due to the lack of established strategies.

Ponatinib, a third‐generation TKI, has demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against various BCR::ABL1 mutations, including the T315I mutation, which confers resistance to other TKIs. 24 Its efficacy has been validated in clinical trials, and in Japan, ponatinib has been approved and reimbursed for use in patients with resistant or intolerant CML‐CP, making it a viable option in clinical practice. 25 , 26 Accordingly, JALSG has initiated a clinical trial named CML RE‐STOP219 Study, targeting patients who previously failed TKI discontinuation, including those treated with 2G‐TKIs. In this study, patients receive ponatinib maintenance therapy followed by a second attempt at TKI discontinuation. The results of this trial are expected to demonstrate the potential of TFR after ponatinib maintenance, offering new hope to patients with CML who wish to live free from lifelong TKI therapy.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients

The JALSG CML RE‐STOP219 study was designed to evaluate the feasibility of achieving TFR in patients with CML‐CP who had previously failed TKI discontinuation. For a detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria, refer to Table S1. Importantly, patients must have attempted TKI discontinuation either as part of a formal clinical trial, in which the criteria for discontinuation and retreatment depended on the study design, or under strict real‐world conditions, defined as having received TKI therapy for at least 3 years and maintained molecular response 4.5 (MR4.5; defined as a BCR‐ABL1 transcript level ≤0.0032% on the International Scale [IS]) for a minimum of 2 years. After relapse, patients became eligible for enrolment once they had re‐achieved MR4.5 with retreatment using a TKI other than ponatinib. The registration period for the trial spanned from February 2020 to March 2023, and the cut‐off date for evaluating the primary end‐point was set for March 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Clinical Research Act of Japan (Japan Registry of Clinical Trials identifier: jRCTs041190117).

Treatment and monitoring

Following enrolment, patients were switched from their current TKI to ponatinib at a dose of 15 mg/day within 90 days. BCR::ABL1 transcript levels, measured using the International Scale (IS), were monitored every 3 months during the 12‐month ponatinib maintenance phase (formally 52–58 weeks). Patients who maintained MR4.5 during this period discontinued ponatinib and entered the TFR phase, followed by a 24‐month observation period. During the TFR phase, BCR::ABL1 IS levels were monitored monthly for the first 6 months, every 2 months for the next 6 months and quarterly thereafter. TKI therapy was resumed upon loss of MMR. In such cases, ponatinib or the previously used TKI was restarted within 30 days and were monitored until MMR re‐achievement or until 6 months following TKI reinitiation. Molecular monitoring was conducted according to the European LeukemiaNet recommendations. 27 At the third month of maintenance therapy, trough plasma concentrations of ponatinib were measured by high‐performance liquid chromatography, as previously described. 28 The design of the CML RE‐STOP219 study and additional information regarding molecular monitoring and adverse events are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The primary end‐point of the study was the TFR rate at 12 months after ponatinib discontinuation in the full analysis set (FAS). The per protocol set (PPS) further excluded patients with eligibility violations or significant protocol deviations from the FAS. The safety analysis set included all patients who received at least one dose of study medication. Primary efficacy analyses were conducted on the FAS, with supportive analyses on the PPS. Safety analyses were performed on the safety analysis set. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value <0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Detailed information regarding study end‐points, sample size calculation and statistical analysis is provided in Table S1.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 50 patients were enrolled in this study, and none were deemed ineligible after enrolment. Of the 50 patients enrolled, one withdrew consent prior to initiating treatment. The remaining 49 received ponatinib maintenance therapy. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table S2. During the maintenance phase, eight patients discontinued ponatinib and subsequently withdrew from the study (due to adverse events [n = 6], physician decision [n = 1] or withdrawal by subject [n = 1]). No patients experienced loss of MR4.5 during ponatinib maintenance therapy. Ultimately, 41 patients underwent a second TKI discontinuation.

Characteristics of patients who proceeded to the TFR phase are shown in Table S2. Among those 41 patients, 73.2% were male, with a median age of 46 years (range 11–72) at initial diagnosis. The vast majority were of Asian ethnicity (92.7%). Risk classification at initial diagnosis revealed that 48.5% of patients were categorized as low risk, 33.3% intermediate risk, and 18.2% high risk by Sokal score. Only one patient (2.4%) had prior exposure to interferon‐α therapy. The first discontinued TKIs were dasatinib (39.0%), nilotinib (36.6%), imatinib (14.6%) and bosutinib (9.8%). The molecular response depth at the time of first TKI discontinuation was MR4.5 in 97.6% of cases. TKI re‐initiation was triggered by loss of MMR in 82.9% of cases, loss of MR4.0 in 14.6% of cases and loss of MR4.5 in 2.4% of cases. The median treatment duration prior to first TKI discontinuation was 54 months (range 19–200), with a TFR duration of 3 months (range 2–54). Notably, this cohort included patients who discontinued TKI therapy after fewer than 3 years, as they were enrolled in clinical trials that did not require a minimum treatment duration (n = 2; 19 and 21 months). Following TKI re‐initiation, the median treatment duration from TKI restart to study enrolment was 17 months (range 3–90). MR4.5 was re‐achieved with a median of 3 months (range 1–16) and the median time from MR4.5 re‐achievement to study enrolment was 14 months (range 0–80). Patients were registered at a median age of 54 years (range 27–75).

Outcomes

In the FAS, the proportion of patients with TFR at 12 months, defined as the primary end‐point of this study, was 36.6% (90% CI: 24.1–50.6). The Kaplan–Meier curve illustrating the MMR maintenance rate is presented in Figure 1A. In patients who resumed TKI therapy, the reason for reinitiation was loss of MMR in all cases. The DMR maintenance rate, defined as the depth of MR4.5, was 17.1% at 12 months (Figure 1B). Three cases of TKI withdrawal syndrome were reported, all of which were grade 1. All relapsed patients regained MMR by the time of the 3‐month assessment, regardless of the type of TKI used (Figure 1C,D). No cases of disease progression or death occurred during the observation period.

Factors analysed for their association with TFR included sex, age at diagnosis, Sokal risk, type of TKI used at first discontinuation, depth of molecular response at TKI reinitiation, time until first TKI discontinuation, TFR duration, treatment duration after TKI reinitiation, time to MR4.5 from TKI restart, duration of MR4.5 maintenance, age at registration and plasma concentration of ponatinib. Continuous variables were stratified based on their approximate median values. Univariate analysis identified several favourable factors associated with TFR: (1) First TKI discontinuation with imatinib; (2) longer treatment duration prior to first TKI discontinuation; (3) longer TFR duration during the first TKI discontinuation (Table 1 and Figure S1A–L).

A PPS analysis was conducted on 33 patients, excluding eight with protocol deviations. Within the PPS, the proportion of patients with TFR at 12 months was 30.3% (90% CI: 17.5–46.0). The Kaplan–Meier curve illustrating the MMR maintenance rate is presented in Figure S1M. Reasons for exclusion from PPS analysis included: (1) Missing BCR::ABL1 IS data at one or more time points during maintenance therapy (n = 3); (2) insufficient duration of ponatinib maintenance therapy (n = 3; short by 2, 15, and 15 days); (3) Excessive duration of ponatinib maintenance therapy (n = 1; exceeded by 15 days); and (4) continuation of ponatinib despite a severe adverse event (n = 1; lipase increase). In the last case, ponatinib was continued, and follow‐up testing confirmed normalization of lipase levels.

Adverse events

Adverse events were evaluated in 49 patients who received ponatinib maintenance therapy (Table S3). The most frequently observed adverse event was hypertension, occurring in 26 (53.1%) of patients, including six cases (12.2%) of grade 3 or higher. However, no patients discontinued ponatinib due to hypertension. Other common adverse events (≥10%) included elevated alanine aminotransferase, elevated lipase, myalgia, headache and fatigue. During ponatinib maintenance therapy, blood pressure (BP), HbA1c, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) and triglyceride (TG) levels were routinely monitored (Table S4). A significant increase in both BP and TG levels was observed. Adverse events that led to ponatinib discontinuation included elevated lipase (n = 3), acute coronary syndrome (n = 1; risk factors: older age [67 years], current smoking, hypertension and dyslipidaemia), acute heart failure (n = 1; risk factors: older age [64 years] and a history of smoking) and liver dysfunction (n = 1). The patient who developed acute coronary syndrome required cardiac intervention, while the patient with acute heart failure improved with conservative treatment. One patient discontinued the study by the physician's decision due to a suspected allergic reaction to ponatinib. No serious adverse events were reported among patients who resumed ponatinib treatment after discontinuation (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the role of ponatinib maintenance therapy in facilitating a second TFR attempt in patients with CML who previously failed TKI discontinuation. Among the 49 patients who received ponatinib maintenance therapy, 41 proceeded to the TFR phase and 15 patients maintained MMR at 12 months. In the FAS analysis, the 90% confidence interval for the TFR rate at 12 months had a lower bound above the predefined threshold of 20%, indicating that the intervention statistically met the minimum clinical criterion. Notably, imatinib discontinuation, a longer treatment duration before the first discontinuation and a longer TFR duration were identified as prognostic factors for the success of a second discontinuation attempt. Although the duration of DMR before TKI discontinuation is known to influence TFR outcomes, 29 this factor did not significantly affect the success of second discontinuation in our study. This discrepancy suggests that the therapeutic effect of ponatinib may play a greater role in achieving TFR than prior TKI exposure. However, it should be noted that the follow‐up period in this study was limited to 2 years after TKI discontinuation. Longer observation is warranted because, when MMR loss is used as the criterion for molecular recurrence, 17% of recurrences have been reported to occur after 24 months. 30 Moreover, follow‐up of these patients is currently underway as part of the TKI discontinuation observational study (the J‐SKI study) conducted by the Japanese Society of Hematology. 31

Ponatinib therapy is associated with a risk of AOEs, which are known to increase depending on both the dose and the duration of treatment. 5 , 6 , 32 , 33 To mitigate this risk, we used a low dose of 15 mg and limited maintenance therapy to 1 year, along with strict eligibility criteria. Despite these precautions, adverse events were not fully avoidable, and six patients discontinued ponatinib due to severe toxicity. In addition, six patients developed grade ≥3 hypertension, although these events did not lead to treatment discontinuation. When considering those who received ponatinib maintenance therapy as the denominator, 15 of 49 (30.6%) patients ultimately achieved TFR. Given the benefit–risk balance observed in this study, applying this treatment strategy to routine clinical practice may not be appropriate, particularly for patients with cardiovascular risk factors. However, because the number of cases is limited, it is premature to draw firm conclusions about the usefulness of ponatinib for re‐achieving TFR based on this study alone. Since several clinical trials with similar designs have been conducted internationally (NCT04160546 and NCT04043676), we anticipate that ongoing studies will provide greater clarity regarding the benefit–risk balance in the future.

Currently, some studies regarding second attempts at TKI discontinuation have been reported (Table S5). The RE‐STIM study investigated 70 patients who had previously failed to achieve TFR and underwent a second attempt at TKI discontinuation. In this study, 48% of patients maintained TFR at 12 months. 20 The TRAD study focused on patients who had failed TFR after imatinib discontinuation and subsequently received dasatinib for 2 years before a second attempt. Despite long durations of MR4.5 maintenance, the TFR rate was notably low, with only 3 of 35 patients achieving TFR. 21 The DAstop2 study also evaluated patients who had failed a first attempt at TKI discontinuation and subsequently received dasatinib maintenance therapy for 2 years, including at least 1 year of MR4.0 maintenance, followed by a second attempt at TKI discontinuation. Among 61 patients who underwent a second discontinuation, the TFR rate 1 year after dasatinib discontinuation was excellent at 57%, which was markedly higher than that reported in the TRAD study. 22 In the French Nilo post‐STIM study, patients who relapsed after imatinib discontinuation were treated with nilotinib for 2 years, including at least 1 year of sustained MR4.5, followed by a second attempt at TKI discontinuation. A total of 31 patients were enrolled, 22 transitioned to the TFR phase and the TFR rate at 24 months was 59.1%. 23 Thus, the outcomes of previous second TKI discontinuation studies have varied substantially. These differences in treatment outcomes are presumed to be influenced by variations in patient characteristics at the time of first TKI discontinuation. Indeed, the study designs were markedly heterogeneous. When comparing these designs, the TRAD study enrolled patients who had discontinued TKI after maintaining a deeper and longer DMR before the first discontinuation (MR4.5 ≥2 years) and subsequently relapsed. In contrast, other studies included patients who met the EURO‐SKI criteria, which involve shorter and less stringent molecular requirements (MR4.0 ≥1 year). Furthermore, the TRAD study defined molecular relapse after the second discontinuation as two consecutive losses of MR4.0 as well as loss of MMR, and this difference in relapse criteria is presumed to be associated with the lower TFR rate observed in that study. When comparing these reports with our own findings, ponatinib did not appear to dramatically improve the TFR rate. However, it is important to note that the previous clinical trials included most patients who had failed imatinib discontinuation, and no evidence was available regarding patients who were treated with 2G‐TKIs. In the current study, 85.4% of patients underwent their first TKI discontinuation following treatment with 2G‐TKIs. Although the DMR duration prior to the first TKI discontinuation was not formally recorded in this study population, based on the criteria for routine clinical practice or enrolment in the JALSG studies (N‐STOP216 [identifier: UMIN000024984] and D‐STOP216 [identifier: UMIN000024985]), 34 , 35 at least 27 patients (66%) had sustained MR4.5 for more than 2 years. Despite the implementation of stringent criteria for the first TKI discontinuation, a subset of patients who experienced relapse were still able to achieve TFR.

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical backgrounds of the enrolled patients, particularly the depth of DMR at the time of initial TKI discontinuation, were influenced by the design of previous clinical trials and the sensitivity of the molecular monitoring methods available. This variability makes it challenging to apply uniform criteria across the cohort. Additionally, definitions of relapse following the first TKI discontinuation were inconsistent; some patients were evaluated based on the loss of DMR rather than MMR. Furthermore, because this study focused on the rare scenario of a second TKI discontinuation, the sample size was limited, which imposed statistical constraints on performing multivariate analyses to identify factors associated with successful TFR.

In conclusion, this study suggests that a second TKI discontinuation following ponatinib maintenance therapy may offer the potential for successful TFR in selected patients. However, the occurrence of serious adverse events, such as cardiovascular complications, highlights the importance of a careful benefit–risk assessment and patient selection before applying this approach in clinical practice.
