Spin-controlled enantioselective near-infrared photocatalysis with chiral MgO/Co3O4 nanoparticles
Authors: Hui Zhangsun, Rui Hu, Liguang Xu, Chuanlai Xu, Hua Kuang, Changlong Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerein, we report D-/L-penicillamine-mediated magnesium oxide-doped cobalt oxide nanoparticles that exhibit strong mirror-image circular dichroism signals ( ~ 102 mdeg at ~800 nm) and function as effective near-infrared-driven photocatalysts for the enantioselective hydroxylation of tyrosine to dihydroxyphenylalanine. The D-nanoparticles demonstrated pronounced enantioselectivity, consuming 92.48% of L-tyrosine versus only 53.85% of D-tyrosine. Magnesium incorporation proves essential, increasing catalytic activity by 22.78 percentage points relative to undoped cobalt oxide nanoparticles. Mechanistically, Mg incorporation modulates the electronic structure to induce surface oxygen vacancies, which act as electron bridges to facilitate oxygen activation, significantly lowering the reaction barrier. Molecular dynamics simulations further unveil an inverse affinity-activity relationship, where heterochiral pairs enable efficient catalytic turnover through weaker, transient single hydrogen bonds, whereas high-affinity homochiral pairs are sequestered in non-productive states via rigid dual hydrogen bonds. This work establishes a distinct paradigm for rational catalyst design, balancing binding affinity with catalytic mobility.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Chirality, a ubiquitous form of intrinsic asymmetry, not only fundamentally dictates the structure and function of innumerable biological and chemical systems1–3, but its synthetic control via asymmetric catalysis to produce enantiopure compounds is also of paramount importance in pharmaceuticals and advanced materials4–7. To this end, photocatalysis has emerged as a potent green-enabling technology that offers mechanistic opportunities for asymmetric synthesis8. By leveraging solar energy, it unlocks distinct reaction pathways—such as those involving photoexcitation and single-electron transfer—offering effective strategies for achieving stereocontrol9–11. Consequently, the convergence of chirality with photocatalysis to construct highly efficient enantioselective catalytic systems constitutes a highly attractive yet formidable research frontier12. However, conventional photocatalysts predominantly rely on high-energy ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) excitation, squandering the vast near-infrared (NIR, λ ~ 780–2500 nm) region—the largest constituent (>50%) of the solar spectrum13,14. Therefore, to enhance spectral efficiency, the development of novel photocatalytic systems that can extend asymmetric catalysis into the NIR region represents a critical and unresolved challenge.

To address this challenge, chiral inorganic nanomaterials have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional stability, tunable electronic structures, and intense chiroptical responses15,16. Unlike organic ligands, these materials exhibit higher electronic polarizability and synergistic effects, enabling stereoselectivity through structural chirality, surface states, and, critically, electron spin polarization17,18. This phenomenon underpins the chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect, where asymmetric spin-orbit coupling acts as an intrinsic spin filter, generating strong spin polarization during electron transfer19. The CISS-induced polarization is particularly potent for photocatalysis, as it can overcome spin-forbidden reactions like triplet O₂ activation20. To date, research has been largely concentrated on chiral plasmonic nanosystems (e.g., Au, Ag), which utilize polarization-induced asymmetric “hotspots” to control reaction pathways21,22. Nevertheless, these plasmonic systems face significant limitations, including high cost, scarcity, and poor long-term stability23,24.

In this context, earth-abundant transition metal-based nanomaterials emerge as ideal alternatives to noble metals25, distinguished by their high redox activity and chemical stability26,27. In particular, Co3O4 is renowned for its mixed Co²⁺/Co³⁺ valence states and intrinsic capacity to mediate O₂ activation, making it a suitable platform to study the interplay between CISS and oxygen-involved photocatalysis28. Furthermore, it is well-established that fabricating dual-site or bimetallic catalysts is a powerful strategy to optimize apparent quantum efficiency29,30. These hetero-interfaces act as selective contacts to extract photogenerated charge carriers, effectively preventing electron-hole recombination and promoting higher conversion31,32. Introducing structural modulators like MgO, for instance, is an efficacious strategy to tune the electronic structure via defect engineering and induce interfacial oxygen vacancies (OVs)33, which serve as key active sites for enhancing surface reactions and accelerating O2 activation34–36. Therefore, engineering bimetallic systems that synergistically couple defect-enhanced charge separation from dual-atom sites with quantum spin-selective activity remains a formidable challenge37.

In this work, we report the synthesis of D-/L-penicillamine (D-/L-Pen)-mediated MgO-doped Co3O4 nanoparticles (D-/L-MgO/Co3O4 NPs, named D-/L-NPs), which leverage a strong NIR chiroptical response (g-factor ≈ 2.9 × 10−3) to achieve the NIR-driven (808 nm, 5 W) enantioselective hydroxylation of tyrosine to dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA). The system exhibits distinct enantioselectivity, in which the D-NPs catalyst preferentially catalyzes L-tyrosine (L-Tyr), while the L-NPs catalyst favors D-tyrosine (D-Tyr). Remarkably, Mg-driven defect engineering induces OVs, significantly lowering the activation energy barrier. The CISS characterization confirms that the NPs function as effective spin filters, generating spin-polarized intermediates that govern the enantioselective catalytic pathway, while molecular dynamics (MD) simulations elucidate the molecular-level origins of this selective mechanism.

Results

Preparation, characterization, and nir chiroptical properties of chiral NPs

Hierarchical, flower-like chiral MgO/Co3O4 nanospheres were fabricated through a one-pot hydrothermal self-assembly process (Fig. 1a). The synthesis utilized Co2+ and Mg2+ precursors in the presence of Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, surfactant) and hydrazine hydrate (reducing agent). The enantiomer of D-/L-Pen served as the sole symmetry-breaking agent to successfully endow the resulting inorganic nanoparticles with chiral nanostructures, yielding the D-MgO/Co3O4 NPs and L-MgO/Co3O4 NPs. The physicochemical properties of the as-synthesized NPs were systematically investigated. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed a uniform, three-dimensional (3D) hierarchical flower-like nanospheres (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Statistical analysis of the particle size distribution revealed that the average diameter of the D-NPs was approximately 382.4 ± 0.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images further elucidated the flower-like or urchin-like spherical architecture of the nanomaterials (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1d). To definitively visualize the internal architecture, 3D TEM tomography was performed (Supplementary Movie 1). Furthermore, the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping and EDS spectrum confirmed the homogeneous distribution and spatial overlap of Co, Mg, O, S, N, and C elements within the D-NPs (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). This finding unequivocally indicates the successful incorporation of Co and Mg into a homogeneous composite oxide.

The optical chirality of the NPs was confirmed by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. The D-NPs and L-NPs exhibited strong, mirror-symmetric signals, defined by a broad cotton effect centered in the NIR region. Specifically, the D-NPs displayed a pronounced negative CD minimum (~ −98 mdeg at ~800 nm), mirrored by a positive maximum (~102 mdeg) for the L-NPs, with the corresponding g-factor value reaching 2.9 × 10−3, collectively confirming their strong chiroptical activity (Fig. 1d). These results provide compelling evidence that the molecular chirality of penicillamine was effectively transferred to the inorganic MgO/Co3O4 nanocrystals38. In stark contrast, the DL-MgO/Co3O4 NPs (DL-NPs), synthesized with racemic DL-Pen, displayed a negligible chiroptical response. Meanwhile, the corresponding SEM and TEM images revealed a distinct nanoflower-like morphology of DL-NPs, closely resembling that of D-NPs and L-NPs (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of D-, L-, and DL-NPs reveal a broad and intense absorption band centered at ~800 nm, confirming their strong photoresponse in the NIR region (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

Furthermore, this chiral transfer mechanism was substantiated by vibrational spectroscopy. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy showed the conspicuous absence of the characteristic S-H stretching vibration (~2526 cm⁻¹) in all synthesized NPs, a peak that was clearly present in the spectrum of free Pen (Fig. 1e)39. This finding was corroborated by Raman spectroscopy, which also showed a lack of the S-H vibrational mode (~2476 cm⁻¹) for the chiral NPs (Supplementary Fig. 3b)40. The concomitant disappearance of the S-H signal in both FTIR and Raman spectra unequivocally indicates a strong and stable chelation interaction, forming a metal-thiolate bond between the thiol group and surface metal atoms41. This confirms the robust chemical linkage between ligands and the nanoparticle surface, an interaction crucial for the efficient imprinting of chirality onto the inorganic lattice42. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermogravimetric (DTG) analysis of the D-NPs composite revealed a markedly shifted decomposition peak from ~260 °C (D-Pen) to ~400 °C, confirming robust covalent anchoring of the ligand43. Quantification of the distinct decomposition stage (220–600 °C) yields a D-penicillamine content of approximately 33.45% in the D-NPs (Supplementary Fig. 4). To estimate the surface ligand density, the number of penicillamine molecules anchored per nanoparticle was quantified42. A high surface ligand density of approximately 4.501 × 105 molecules per D-NP was determined by quantifying desorbed penicillamine via LC-MS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To further elucidate the crystalline phase and surface chemistry of the nanomaterials, a comprehensive analysis was performed. The nearly identical X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of D-NPs and L-NPs display broad diffraction peaks, all indexable to the Co3O4 phase (JCPDS No. 42-1467)44, which suggests their nanocrystalline nature (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The absence of any distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to MgO indicates that the Mg species are highly dispersed within the Co3O4 nanocrystalline matrix, rather than forming a separate, bulk crystalline phase45. Furthermore, the chemical composition and elemental valence states were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The high-resolution Co 2p spectrum displays the characteristic spin-orbit doublets of Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2. The Co 2p3/2 envelope was resolved into two main peaks attributed to Co3+ (781.4 eV) and Co2+ (784.0 eV), accompanied by a prominent satellite (Sat.) peak at 787.4 eV. A corresponding set of peaks was observed in the Co 2p1/2 region for Co3+ (797.2 eV), Co2+ (798.3 eV), and a satellite feature at 803.5 eV (Fig. 1f)46,47. The coexistence of both Co2+ and Co3+ is a distinctive feature of the Co3O4 crystalline phase, providing strong evidence for its successful formation. Concurrently, the Mg 1s spectrum displayed a sharp singlet peak centered at around 1304.2 eV, attributed to Mg²⁺ within the oxide lattice (Fig. 1g). The high-resolution spectra for S 2p, N 1s, and C 1s further confirmed the presence of these elements (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d). Collectively, the XPS results unequivocally confirm the chemical identity of the nanomaterials as a MgO/Co3O4 composite oxide.

To probe the role of MgO, we prepared control catalysts of D-Co3O4 NPs without MgO doping and characterized their structural, surface, and optical properties. SEM and TEM reveal that D-Co3O4 adopts a spherical morphology but lacks the hierarchical, flower-like architecture observed for the MgO/Co3O4 composite (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). The XRD pattern confirms the Co3O4 phase, while XPS analysis verifies the Co3O4 composition and the presence of the anchored penicillamine ligand via metal-thiolate bonding (Supplementary Fig. 7c–e). Although the bare D-Co3O4 NPs exhibit optical activity, their CD signal is relatively weak and is blue-shifted relative to the MgO/Co3O4 NPs; concomitantly, the UV–Vis absorption maximum shifts toward ~700 nm (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, we also optimized the concentration of the Mg precursor (0–40 mg/mL) during the synthesis process. SEM analysis confirmed that while low Mg concentrations sustained the desired hierarchical nanoflower morphology, excessive concentrations compromised structural integrity, leading to agglomeration (Supplementary Fig. 9). This structural evolution directly governed the chiroptical activity (Supplementary Fig. 10). An optimal concentration of 4 mg/mL yielded the most well-defined nanoflower morphology and maximized the chiroptical signals, achieving a CD intensity of −94.3 mdeg and a g-factor of −2.61 × 10−3. Thus, Mg incorporation is fundamentally crucial, acting not only as a compositional modulator but also as a structural directing agent essential for achieving optimal chiral integrity.

Mismatched enantioselectivity via NIR photo-activation

The enantioselective catalytic performance of the chiral NPs was evaluated in the photocatalytic oxidation of L-Tyr and D-Tyr. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a, the D-NPs and L-NPs were designed to selectively recognize and convert tyrosine enantiomers into DOPA under NIR irradiation (808 nm, 5 W). The reaction progress was monitored by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy, with the consumption rate of Tyr evaluated by tracking the intensity decay of its characteristic emission peak at ~300 nm. As shown in Figs. 2b–e, both D-NPs and L-NPs exhibited significant and distinct enantioselectivity. Specifically, the D-NPs catalyst, when paired with its matched substrate D-Tyr, resulted in a moderate consumption after 12 h (Fig. 2b). In sharp contrast, the same catalyst showed a pronounced preference for the mismatched L-Tyr substrate, achieving a rapid and pronounced signal consumption (Fig. 2c). Notably, this preferential conversion was accompanied by a concomitant redshift of the emission peak from ~300 to 314 nm, matching the standard DOPA product and indicating selective hydroxylation (Supplementary Fig. 11)48. Meanwhile, this mismatched preference was precisely mirrored by the L-NPs catalyst. It demonstrated high activity toward the D-Tyr substrate, resulting in a substantial PL decay (Fig. 2d), while remaining significantly less active toward its matched L-Tyr substrate (Fig. 2e). In addition, the undoped D-/L-Co3O4 NPs were assessed as a control. The D-/L-Co3O4 NPs retained their intrinsic enantiopreferences; specifically, D-Co3O4 NPs preferentially catalyzed L-Tyr, whereas L-Co3O4 NPs exhibited the characteristic mismatched preference for D-Tyr (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 12). Crucially, MgO incorporation functioned as a potent performance promoter, providing a 22.78 percentage point enhancement in L-Tyr consumption relative to the undoped D-Co3O4 NPs. Specifically, the D-NPs consumed 92.48% of L-Tyr versus 53.85% of D-Tyr, while L-Tyr exhibited a mirrored selectivity (80.07% for D-Tyr vs. 48.30% for L-Tyr), further confirming the mismatched enantioselectivity (Fig. 2h). To conclusively validate the catalytic outcome and verify the molecular identity of the hydroxylated species, non-targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass (LC-MS/MS) was employed. The DOPA (m/z = 198.0834) was definitively identified in positive ion mode, confirming that the conversion proceeds through highly selective tyrosine hydroxylation (Fig. 2i). The process achieved a 47.87% L-DOPA yield with an enantiomeric excess (ee) of 98.77% (Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, control experiments under NIR irradiation without catalysts showed negligible transformation, underscoring the indispensable role of the chiral MgO/Co3O4 nanoparticles in driving this asymmetric process (Supplementary Fig. 14).

To delineate the molecular determinants of enantioselectivity, we first evaluated the role of the chiral ligand. Although D-cysteine (D-Cys) and D-glutathione (D-GSH) modified NPs retained the hierarchical nanoflower morphology (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b), they exhibited distinct chiroptical signatures and failed to impart significant enantioselectivity toward Tyr (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d and 16a–e). This highlights that the specific molecular scaffold of penicillamine is indispensable for creating the effective chiral pockets required for stereoselective catalysis. The necessity of symmetry breaking was further evidenced by the achiral MgO/Co3O4 NPs (achiral-NPs), which showed negligible selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 17), confirming that the restoration of spatial symmetry leads to non-spin-polarized carrier transport and subsequent non-selective oxygen activation. Furthermore, the structural integrity of the catalyst also proved critical; D-NPs synthesized without PVP (D-NPs-PVPfree) underwent disordered aggregation and lost their chiroptical signals, resulting in a dramatic loss of enantioselectivity (Supplementary Fig. 18). Subsequently, we verified the specificity of NIR excitation. Under broadband Xe lamp irradiation, the characteristic PL redshift associated with DOPA was absent (Supplementary Fig. 19), suggesting that non-selective broadband illumination triggers non-specific substrate degradation rather than the desired site-specific hydroxylation. Collectively, these results confirm that the observed asymmetric conversion is a synergistic process mediated by the specific chiral architecture and NIR-triggered spin-selective pathways.

Mechanism of enantioselective photocatalysis

Furthermore, to elucidate the mechanism underlying the enantioselectivity, we investigated the chiral recognition (adsorption) and then the photocatalytic pathway. First, the chiral interaction between the catalysts and substrates in the absence of irradiation was probed using CD spectroscopy. As expected, L-Tyr and D-Tyr display perfect mirror-image CD signals (Fig. 3a). The corresponding UV-Vis absorption spectra are shown in Supplementary Fig. S20. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 3b, CD spectra of the supernatants after incubating rac-Tyr with D-NPs or L-NPs demonstrated distinct enantioselective adsorption behaviors49. Specifically, D-NPs preferentially adsorbed D-Tyr, resulting in an excess of L-Tyr remaining in solution, whereas L-NPs showed the opposite trend22. Interestingly, this observed adsorption preference was found to be the opposite of the catalytic preference, suggesting a complex interplay between chiral recognition and catalytic conversion within this system.

To establish the thermodynamic and electronic foundation of the photocatalytic process, we first characterized the band structure of the D-NPs. UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectra and Mott-Schottky plots reveal a narrow bandgap of 1.48 eV and a flat-band potential of 2.36 V, confirming that the catalyst possesses sufficient overpotential for NIR-driven tyrosine hydroxylation (Supplementary Fig. 21). Real-time surface dynamics monitored via in situ diffuse reflectance infrared fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) under 808 nm irradiation showed a progressive intensification of peaks corresponding to –OH and C–O vibrations, providing direct spectroscopic fingerprints of the hydroxylated intermediate formation at the catalyst interface (Supplementary Fig. 22). The enhanced catalytic performance is fundamentally rooted in the accelerated charge carrier dynamics induced by Mg-doping. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient photocurrent measurements reveal that D-NPs possess a markedly reduced Nyquist semi-circle and an enhanced photocurrent response (~28 μA cm−2), significantly exceeding that of the undoped D-Co3O4 NPs (~17 μA cm−2) (Supplementary Fig. 23). These results collectively demonstrate that the D-NPs system exhibits accelerated interfacial charge transfer kinetics and effectively suppressed recombination of photo-generated electron-hole pairs50. Such superior carrier dynamics are intrinsically rooted in Mg-induced defect engineering. Specifically, the intense electron spin resonance (ESR) signal at g = 2.003 of D-NPs, coupled with the substantial enrichment of defective oxygen species observed in the XPS O 1s spectra (531 eV), unambiguously confirms the stabilization of high-density surface oxygen vacancies (OVs) (Fig. 3c, d). Functioning as efficient electron-trapping sites, these OVs facilitate the chemisorption and single-electron reduction of O2, thereby triggering a more robust reactive oxygen species (ROS) cascade to drive the enantioselective hydroxylation.

Next, the nature of the driving ROS was further elucidated using spin-trapping ESR spectroscopy51. Under 808 nm irradiation, the rapid decay of the TEMPO triplet signal in D-NPs, compared to the high residual intensity in D-Co3O4 NPs, unequivocally proves that Mg incorporation maximizes photogenerated hole (h⁺) liberation (Fig. 3e)52,53. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 24, this elevated h⁺ population subsequently drives the production of ·OH, ·O2⁻, and ¹O2. Consistent with the h⁺ generation data, the D-Co3O4 NPs exhibited the lowest signal intensities for all three ROS species, thereby underscoring the pivotal role of Mg-induced defects, such as oxygen vacancies, in promoting surface oxygen activation. Systematic radical scavenging assays confirmed this mechanism: the dramatic suppression of activity by EDTA−2Na (h⁺ scavenger) and IPA (·OH scavenger) identifies h⁺ and ·OH as the primary oxidative species (Fig. 3g). Collectively, these results reveal a synergistic pathway where Mg-induced OVs act as electronic bridges to synchronize charge separation and oxygen activation, lowering the activation energy barrier for the enantioselective conversion of L-Tyr to DOPA.

Concurrently, to mechanistically rationalize the pivotal role of Mg doping in enhancing this synergistic ROS generation, we propose a defect-mediated oxygen activation pathway, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3h. Specifically, the incorporation of Mg modulates the surface electronic structure, inducing the formation and stabilization of surface OVs that function as the primary reductive centers and electron-trapping sites33,54. Under NIR irradiation, their preferential capture of photogenerated electrons both facilitates the chemisorption and single-electron reduction of adsorbed O2 to ·O2⁻, and actively promotes charge separation by liberating a greater population of highly oxidative h⁺55. This primary cascade subsequently drives the formation of other radicals: the liberated h⁺ oxidizes H2O to form ·OH, while ¹O2 is generated via the interaction between ·O2⁻ and h⁺. Consequently, the OVs-centered electronic environment acts as an efficient electron-bridge network that synergistically accelerates the ROS cascade, lowers the activation energy barrier, and suppresses electron-hole recombination to collectively enhance the catalytic activity56,57.

Substrate scope generality and long-term structural robustness

Next, we sought to investigate the generality of the enantioselectivity by extending the substrate scope to other aromatic amino acids. Initially, the substrate scope was expanded to include the chiral enantiomers of phenylalanine (Phe), for which control experiments lacking the catalyst showed negligible substrate degradation (Supplementary Fig. 25a, b). Consistent with the tyrosine results, the D-NPs preferentially consumed L-Phe (87.09%) over D-Phe (64.01%), while L-NPs favored D-Phe (75.63%) over L-Phe (53.70%) (Supplementary Fig. 25c–g). Critically, LC-MS/MS analysis of the reaction mixture (D-NPs with L-Phe) definitively identified tyrosine (m/z 182.10) as a major product after 10 h, confirming the catalytic process involves the same aromatic hydroxylation pathway (Supplementary Fig. 26). Furthermore, this mismatched generality was confirmed with tryptophan (Trp). Again, D-NPs preferentially consumed L-Trp (77.15%) over D-Trp (64.94%), while L-NPs favored D-Trp (72.94%) over L-Trp (62.84%), with corresponding control experiments showing minimal conversion (Supplementary Fig. 27). The oxidative nature of this conversion was validated by the LC-MS/MS identification of hydroxylated tryptophan derivatives (Supplementary Fig. 28). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the mismatched enantioselectivity is a general characteristic of this catalytic system for aromatic amino acids, although the overall conversion efficiencies were lower than that observed for tyrosine. Beyond high activity and selectivity, realizing practical utility in photocatalysis necessitates long-term material stability. Accordingly, post-reaction characterization by SEM and TEM confirmed the high structural stability of the D-NPs, showing the hierarchical nanoflower morphology was well-preserved after prolonged photocatalytic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 29). Furthermore, the consistent CD signal intensity over 12 h of continuous NIR irradiation unequivocally demonstrates the high photochemical stability and overall structural integrity of the D-NPs (Supplementary Fig. 30). Complementary high-resolution XPS spectra of Co 2p and Mg 1s (Supplementary Fig. 31) reveal no significant variations in binding energies or valence states after five cycles. These results confirm the absence of transition metal leaching or surface reconstruction during the photocatalytic process. This superior, demonstrated stability is a critical prerequisite for achieving high cycling performance and ensuring the long-term practical utility of the chiral photocatalyst.

Spin-selective charge transport driven by the CISS effect

To probe the non-classical physical mechanism governing the mismatched enantioselectivity, we therefore conducted a series of characterizations to probe for the CISS effect58. Firstly, magnetic conductive atomic force microscopy (mc-AFM) was employed to measure spin-polarized electron transport through the chiral (D- and L-NPs) and racemic (DL-) NPs (Fig. 4a). These experiments were conducted under both upward (Hup) and downward (Hdown) pre-magnetization conditions of the tip. For each sample, I–V curves were measured 50 times to obtain a statistically robust average, with the raw data represented by the dashed traces (Supplementary Figs. 32–34). Notably, the measured current is expected to be enhanced when the tip’s magnetic field direction aligns with the material’s electron spin direction, whereas it is suppressed when the directions are anti-aligned. As shown in Fig. 4b, c, the D-NPs exhibited significantly enhanced current under upward tip magnetization, while the L-NPs demonstrated the exact, mirror-image behavior, preferentially conducting under downward magnetization. These results indicate that the chiral NPs function as efficient spin filters, selectively permitting the passage of electrons with a specific spin orientation59,60. By comparison, the DL-NPs exhibited no such asymmetry, unequivocally confirming that the observed spin-filtering effect originates from the intrinsic material chirality (Fig. 4d). Our analysis revealed a high degree of spin polarization for the chiral MgO/Co3O4 NPs, reaching approximately 83.51% (Supplementary Fig. 35). This substantial P value provides compelling, quantitative evidence of a strong CISS effect, confirming the robust spin-modulating capability of the chiral NPs. Furthermore, magnetic circular dichroism (MCD) spectra were measured under both parallel (N-S) and anti-parallel (S-N) magnetic fields (Fig. 4e). The chiral D-NPs and L-NPs displayed symmetric, mirror-image signals of comparable intensity but opposite sign. Importantly, the MCD response was independent of the direction of the applied magnetic field (N-S or S-N), while the DL-NPs showed no MCD signal, confirming that the spin selectivity arises from the intrinsic chiral structure of the material rather than being induced by the external magnetic field62. Moreover, the D-NPs sample demonstrated non-magnetic behavior, with no saturation hysteresis, thereby definitively excluding ferromagnetic ordering as the origin of the spin selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 36). This CISS effect mechanism suggests that, under the influence of a chiral potential, charge carriers, initially possessing random spin orientations, become selectively modulated, ultimately leading to the generation of spin-polarized carriers (Fig. 4f). It is reasonable to propose that the spin-polarized current, as directly evidenced by mc-AFM, influences the spin state of the charge carriers. This, in turn, leads to the generation of spin-polarized radical intermediates on the catalyst surface, governing the enantioselective outcome of the reaction63,64.

Molecular dynamics insights into the mismatched enantioselectivity

Subsequently, to probe the molecular origin of the mismatched stereoselectivity, we performed extensive MD simulations to probe the microscopic interactions between L-Tyr molecules and the D-Pen or L-Pen modified NP surfaces65. Representative snapshots of the simulations immediately visualize the two distinct interaction modes that form the basis of our hypothesis. The mismatched pair exhibits natural steric hindrance, precluding strong multi-point binding and instead favoring the formation of only a weaker, transient single hydrogen bond. We hypothesize that this weak interaction affords the L-Tyr greater conformational freedom, allowing its benzene ring to access the adjacent catalytic site, thereby defining a productive binding state (Fig. 5a). Conversely, when the molecular chirality matches, the L-Tyr molecule can form stable dual hydrogen bonds with the anchored L-Pen ligand due to a high degree of spatial and chiral complementarity (a “lock-and-key” fit), resulting in strong binding affinity (Fig. 5b). This high-affinity interaction tends to sequester the L-Tyr, locking it in a conformation where its amino and carboxyl groups are tightly bound to the Pen ligand. Crucially, as the catalytic hydroxylation must occur on the benzene ring, this conformation sterically hinders the substrate’s benzene ring from accessing the adjacent catalytic active site on the MgO/Co3O4 surface, thereby inhibiting the reaction. These final simulation snapshots provide a striking visual illustration of this density difference. The mismatched D-NPs surface exhibits only sparse, transient interactions, consistent with the productive binding mechanism, whereas the matched L-NPs surface is densely populated by sequestered L-Tyr substrates, characteristic of the non-productive state (Supplementary Fig. 37).

To quantitatively evaluate this binding hypothesis, the simulation trajectories were analyzed in detail. First of all, the time evolution of hydrogen bond formation provides direct evidence for the difference in binding stability. As shown in Fig. 5c, the matched system rapidly forms and maintains a substantially higher number of hydrogen bonds compared to the mismatched system. This suggests that the lock-and-key fit is indeed stabilized by multi-point H-bonds. In addition, this binding difference is further reflected in the local structural ordering. The radial distribution function (RDF) for the L-Pen/L-Tyr system showed a significantly higher and more defined peak, indicating a stronger local structural ordering compared to the D-Pen/L-Tyr system (Fig. 5d). This strong local ordering further translates to a higher substrate accumulation at the catalyst interface. The number density profile (Fig. 5e) reveals a significantly greater L-Tyr enrichment at the matched L-NPs surface, suggesting that this high-affinity matched binding effectively sequesters a dense layer of substrates in the non-productive conformation. Finally, the thermodynamic basis for this binding preference was quantified. As shown in Fig. 5f, the significantly more negative coulomb and van der Waals (VDW) energies for the matched system corroborate that the non-productive lock-and-key configuration is its thermodynamically preferred binding mode. In summary, the MD simulations provide mechanistic insights into the origin of the mismatched selectivity. The high-affinity, thermodynamically favorable binding of matched pairs is non-productive as it sequesters the substrate and inhibits catalysis. Conversely, the weaker, transient binding of mismatched pairs facilitates the necessary conformational freedom for a productive interaction with the catalytic site. This inverse relationship between binding affinity and catalytic activity offers a plausible explanation for the experimental result where D-NPs show superior efficacy for L-Tyr, and L-NPs for D-Tyr.

Discussion

In summary, this work establishes chiral MgO/Co3O4 NPs as an efficient platform for NIR-driven enantioselective hydroxylation. We unveil a previously unrecognized mechanistic interplay involving bimetallic defect engineering, spin-selective charge transport, and interfacial binding thermodynamics that governs the catalytic outcome. As a tailored bimetallic construct, the Mg-Co sites provide complementary electronic environments not readily achieved in monometallic oxides, facilitating a cooperative modulation of oxygen vacancy formation and carrier dynamics. The deliberate incorporation of Mg proved indispensable; besides modulating the morphology, Mg introduces electronically accessible surface OVs, which substantially accelerate O₂ activation and expand the ROS cascade, resulting in markedly enhanced catalytic efficiency relative to monometallic Co3O4. Mechanistically, the catalysts operate through cooperative spin control and dynamic chiral binding that demonstrate an engineered balance among electronic structure tuning, defect-mediated activation pathways, and transient chiral recognition. It should be noted that even though the classical MD simulations provided valuable atomistic insights into the interfacial interactions, they rely on an idealized structural model. The developed principle offers a generalizable design guideline for developing next-generation stereoselective photocatalysts.

Methods

Materials

All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 99%), magnesium acetate tetrahydrate (C4H6MgO4•4H2O, >99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and trichloromethane (AR, ≥99%) and ethanol (≥99.5%) were procured from Aladdin Co. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, M.W. = 58,000, K30) and D-Cysteine (D-Cys) was obtained from Macklin Inc. D-/L-penicillamine (D-/L-Pen), D-/L-phenylalanine (D-/L-Phe), D-/L-tryptophan (D-/L-Trp) and D-Glutathione (D-GSH) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrazine hydrate aqueous solution (AR, ≥85%) and hydrochloric acid were acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultra-pure water (≥18.25 MΩ, Milli-Q, Millipore).

Characterization methods and instruments

The morphology and structure of the nanomaterials were characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU-8220) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Rigaku UIV diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were recorded on a Thermo K-Alpha spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a monochromatized Al Kα source (hν = 1486.68 eV). Chiroptical and optical properties were investigated using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (CHIRASCAN, Applied Photophysics) and UV-vis absorption spectroscopy (Shimadzu-UV-3600 plus). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded on an Edinburgh FLS980 photoluminescence spectrometer equipped with an Xe lamp. System control and data acquisition were facilitated using F980 software. All experiments were conducted at room temperature. Solid samples were dispersed in KBr to obtain Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra, which were then measured on a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer. Raman spectra were captured with a Raman spectrophotometer (Horiba Jobin Yvon S.A.S, France). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA/DTG) was performed on a METTLER TGA instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Reactive species were measured using electron spin resonance (ESR) signals recorded on a Bruker EMXnano spectrometer. Ligand quantification and reaction product identification were performed using an AB SCIEX QTRAP 5500 system (LC-MS).

Synthesis of flower-like chiral MgO/Co3O4 nanospheres

The D-/L-penicillamine-mediated MgO-doped Co3O4 nanospheres (D-/L-MgO/Co3O4 NPs) were synthesized following a modified hydrothermal method based on previous literature reports. First, 0.8 mL of Co(NO3)2·6H2O solution (54.5 mg/mL) was homogeneously mixed with 11 mL of DMSO under magnetic stirring. Next, 1.4 mL of C4H6MgO4·4H2O (4 mg/mL) and 0.8 mL of PVP solution (40 mg/mL) were sequentially added to the mixture. The solution was then sonicated to form a homogeneous precursor solution. Subsequently, 1.4 mL of a D-/L-Pen solution (30 mg/mL) was injected into the mixture as a chiral ligand, causing the color of the system to change from transparent to black. After adding 0.2 mL of hydrazine hydrate solution and continuing magnetic stirring to ensure a complete reaction, the final mixture was transferred to a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was then heated at 160 °C for 12 h. Following the reaction, the product was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with deionized water, and finally redispersed in deionized water for storage. To obtain a powdered sample, the precipitate was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. For a comparative study, chiral Co3O4 NPs were prepared by omitting the C4H6MgO4·4H2O addition while keeping all other experimental conditions identical.

Quantification of surface ligands on chiral nanoparticles

A modified approximate calculation method was employed to quantify the surface density of Pen on the chiral MgO/Co3O4 NPs43. First, 1 mL of the chiral NPs solution was centrifuged, and the resulting precipitate was collected. The collected NPs were then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h until a constant mass was obtained. The final mass of the dried NPs powder was accurately measured, denoted as m1. Assuming the NPs are ideal spheres, the mass of a single NP (m2) was calculated using the formula m2=43πR3ρ, where ρ is the material density, and R is the NP radius. The total number of NPs (NNPs) in the sample was then estimated as the ratio of the total mass to the single NP mass: NNPs=m1m2. Next, the dried MgO/Co3O4 NPs were treated with a small volume of HCl and subjected to sonication to ensure complete desorption of Pen molecules from the NPs surface. The resulting supernatant was collected and brought to a final volume of 1 mL. The mass of the desorbed Pen (mPen) in the supernatant was then quantified using LC-MS. The total number of Pen molecules (NPen) on the NPs was calculated using the formula Npen=mpenMwNA, where Mw is the molecular weight of Pen and NA is Avogadro’s constant. Finally, the number of Pen molecules per single NP was determined by calculating the ratio of the total number of Pen molecules to the total number of NPs: NpenNNPs.

Enantioselective photocatalytic hydroxylation of tyrosine

The enantioselective photocatalytic performance of the chiral MgO/Co3O4 NPs was evaluated against D-/L-Tyr substrates (0.4 mg/mL). In a typical photocatalytic experiment, a specific amount of the chiral catalyst was dispersed in 10 mL of the substrate solution. The resulting suspension was continuously stirred and irradiated using an 808 nm laser (5 W) at room temperature. At designated time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h), aliquots of the suspension were collected. The collected samples were immediately centrifuged and filtered to completely remove the catalyst particles. The clear supernatant was then subjected to PL spectroscopy and MS analysis to determine substrate consumption and product formation.

Magnetic conductive-probe atomic force microscopy (mc-AFM)

Spin-dependent transport measurements were conducted using an SPM-9700HT atomic force microscope system. Samples were prepared by drop-casting dispersions of D-NPs, L-NPs, or racemic DL-NPs onto clean, conductive fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates. A CoCr-coated conductive probe was operated in contact mode. Prior to measurements, the probe was pre-magnetized for at least 1 h using a permanent magnet to establish a magnetization direction either upward (Hup) or downward (Hdown) relative to the FTO substrate. During analysis, a bias voltage was applied to the FTO substrate, and the resulting current-generated by electrons tunneling from the substrate, through the nanoparticle layer, to the magnetized tip was recorded to characterize the spin-dependent transport. Current-voltage (I-V) curves were acquired over a voltage range of −9 V to 9 V (frequency 0.5 Hz). To ensure statistical reliability and mitigate potential sample damage, I-V curves were recorded from at least 50 distinct positions for each sample and subsequently averaged59.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation methodology

Atomistic molecular dynamics simulations were performed in the GROMACS66 (version 2022.6) simulation package using the CHARMM-interface force field for the nanoparticle and the CHARMM 36 force field67 for other molecules. The water molecules were modeled using the TIP3P water model. The nanoparticle with a diameter of around 4 nm was built through the unit cell of Co3O4 with random replacement of Co with Mg atoms. 43 D-/L-Pen molecules were randomly decorated on the surface via thiol linkages to the surface. The protonation of the carboxylate groups and amine groups used the COO− and NH3+ states to represent neutral conditions. After energy minimization of the decorated nanoparticle, 43 L-Tyr molecules were randomly inserted into a box of around 8 nm with the nanoparticle at the center. The entire construct was solvated with over 12,000 water molecules. Electroneutrality was achieved by introducing appropriate counterions through random replacement of solvent molecules. To stabilize the solvent density, a 2 ns equilibration phase was executed under solute position restraints following extensive energy minimization. Subsequently, a 300 ns unrestrained production trajectory was generated with a 1 fs integration step. System temperature was maintained at 298 K via a Nosé-Hoover thermostat. Short-range interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm, while long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a 0.1 nm grid spacing68. The LINCS algorithm69 constrained all bonds involving hydrogen. Finally, hydrogen bonds were identified geometrically, requiring a donor-acceptor distance below 0.35 nm and an angle not exceeding 30°. This computational approach cannot fully capture the dynamic surface complexities, transient defect states, or the exact thermodynamic evolution of the material under actual macroscopic catalytic conditions.
