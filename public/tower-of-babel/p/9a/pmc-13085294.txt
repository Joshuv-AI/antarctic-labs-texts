Temporal dynamics and functional maturation of the infant gut microbiota during the first year of life
Authors: Fei Sun, Jingda Lu, Jingliang Qiu, Yue Jiao, Jiaxin Zhuang, Binyan Meng, Lai-Yu Kwok, Zhi Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe infant gut microbiota undergoes structured, time-dependent succession during the first year of life, yet high-resolution longitudinal characterization remains limited. Here, full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using PacBio long-read technology on 68 fecal samples from 12 exclusively breastfed infants in Hohhot, China, across six time points from birth to one year (7, 30, 60, 100, 200, and 360 days).ResultsIn this study, 68 fecal samples from 12 infants were analyzed. A total of 16 bacterial phyla, 192 genera and 251 species were annotated, with the dominant phyla being Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria; the dominant genera including Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus and Escherichia-Shigella; and the dominant species comprising Streptococcus_salivarius, Bifidobacterium_longum and Bifidobacterium_pseudocatenulatum. Alpha diversity generally increased by days 360, while community composition shifted from early Bifidobacterium dominance toward greater taxonomic and functional complexity. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) revealed that host and environmental factors collectively explained 33.92% of community variation (P = 0.001). Six temporal colonization patterns were observed through MaAsLin2 analyses., including mid-term explosive and sustained increasing trajectories. Functional profiling using Tax4Fun2 revealed time-specific enrichment of metabolic pathways, including fatty acid biosynthesis, xenobiotic degradation, and autophagy regulation at day 360. The findings of this longitudinal study be interpreted considering its modest sample size (n = 68). Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate and generalize these observations.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that infant gut microbiota assembly follows a non-random, host-guided trajectory, with dietary transitions and microbial interactions driving structural and functional maturation, providing a high-resolution framework for understanding early-life microbiome development and its implications for infant health. However, due to the limited sample size, the relevant research results have certain limitations. In the future, it is necessary to expand the sample scale and conduct more convincing studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04930-y.Keywords: Alpha Diversity, Breastfeeding, Bifidobacterium, Long-Read Sequencing, Microbial Succession, PacBio, Tax4Fun2

Introduction

The human gut harbors tens of thousands of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotes, that collectively constitute a complex ecosystem known as the gut microbiota [1]. This microbial community plays essential roles in host physiology, including nutrient digestion and absorption, immune system maturation, and metabolism regulation [2]. Disruptions in the early-life gut microbiota have been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes in infancy and beyond, such as allergies and inflammatory diseases (e.g., asthma), metabolic disorders (e.g., type 1 diabetes), and altered neurocognitive development [3–5]. Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbial colonization follows a highly dynamic, age-dependent trajectory throughout the human lifespan [6–10]. In particular, the first year of life represents a critical developmental window during which the gut microbiota undergoes rapid succession, transitioning from a low-diversity, unstable state toward a more complex and resilient community [11]. Although the traditional paradigm posited that microbial colonization begins only at birth, shaped by delivery mode, feeding practices, and environmental exposures [12], recent studies have reported microbial signals in amniotic fluid, placenta, and fetal tissues [13–15]. However, the existence of a prenatal microbiome remains contentious due to concerns regarding contamination and methodological artifacts [16, 17]. Studies have shown that maternal microbes can be detected in infant meconium (the first stool passed) [18], and the composition of this microbiota is influenced by factors including delivery mode and parity [19], with three distinct enterotypes identified, dominated by Escherichia, Enterococcus and a mixed genus consortium, respectively [20], Shortly after birth, the infant's gut microbiota subsequently undergoes ecological succession and structural changes under strong selective pressure. Regardless of its origin, the postnatal period is unequivocally recognized as the primary phase for extensive microbial engraftment and community assembly.

Current research on the infant gut microbiota largely relies on short-read amplicon sequencing or metagenomic sequencing. While the former offers advantages in cost and throughput, its limited read length results in insufficient taxonomic resolution, making accurate species-level identification challenging. The latter can capture functional gene information but is constrained by high costs and analytical complexity, this limitation results in insufficient characterization of temporal dynamics, thereby restricting its widespread application in longitudinal, large-scale cohorts [11, 21]. Recent large-scale infant cohort studies using these technologies have begun to outline the broad patterns and key influencing factors of microbiota development. However, these studies remain limited by the aforementioned technical bottlenecks, falling short in resolving high-resolution taxonomic dynamics, precisely characterizing temporal trajectories, and reliably inferring functional potential, thereby impeding a deeper understanding of the core regulatory mechanisms underlying early microbiota development [22–24]. In contrast, third-generation sequencing technologies, such as single molecule, real-time sequencing, enable full-length 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (spanning the V1 to V9 regions), thereby significantly improving species-level classification accuracy and enabling more reliable detection of low-abundance and novel taxa [25–27]. This enhanced resolution is especially valuable for characterizing the rapidly evolving infant gut microbiome, where subtle shifts in composition may have outsized functional consequences [28].

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that the infant gut microbiota undergoes structured, time-dependent changes in diversity, composition, and predicted function during the first year of life, driven by both deterministic successional processes and host-specific factors. To test this hypothesis, longitudinal stool samples were collected from 12 exclusively breastfed infants in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China, across six time points from birth to one year of age. Full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using the PacBio Revio platform with Kinnex library preparation, enabling high-resolution profiling of microbial communities [29]. Integrated analyses were conducted to (1) identify dominant taxa and their dynamic interactions, (2) characterize temporal trends in alpha and beta diversity, (3) delineate successional patterns through MaAsLin2 analyses-based trend modeling, and (4) predict functional potential. By combining long-read sequencing with rigorous longitudinal sampling and ecological modeling, this study provides a detailed, systems-level view of gut microbiota development in early infancy, offering foundational insights into how microbial colonization influences immune maturation, metabolic programming, and long-term disease susceptibility.

Methods

Participant recruitment

All 12 infants enrolled in this study were full-term, with gestational ages of 38–41 weeks, and their mothers remained free of any illnesses throughout pregnancy and lactation in Table 2. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the mothers were in pregnancy at the time of enrolment.; (2) residence in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China; and (3) exclusive breastfeeding. Exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of gestational or perinatal complications or adverse birth outcomes (eg, Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)); (2) inability of family members to perform required at-home sample handling procedures; (3) presence of severe illness in the infant during the lactation period (eg, infant congenital heart disease (CHD)); and (4) diagnosis of any other serious medical condition likely to affect gut microbial composition (eg. diseases requiring long-term use of antibiotics or immunosuppressants).

Sample collection and storage

A total of 68 fecal samples were collected from each infant at six specific time points: postnatal day 7, 30, 60, 100, 200, and 360 (± 3 days). Following enrollment, illustrated instructions and sterile sampling kits were provided to parents or legal guardians. Fecal samples were collected by family members into 50 mL enzyme-free, sterile centrifuge tubes (without dedicated preservation buffer). Upon completion of sample collection, family members immediately notified researchers, and the samples were transported to the laboratory with ice packs within one hour, then immediately placed directly into an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80 °C for long-term storage with no cold storage transition step. Researchers were promptly informed by family members after each sample collection to ensure the timeliness of sample transportation and processing. Potential biases associated with parental self-sampling (including inconsistent operational standardization and variations in the duration of sample exposure to room temperature) were considered in this study, and were thoroughly analyzed and discussed in the study limitations section.

Genomic DNA extraction and quality assessment

Fecal samples stored at −80 °C were retrieved and thawed on ice. Following thawing, samples were homogenized thoroughly using a vortex mixer. Metagenomic DNA was extracted using the SPINeasy DNA Spin Kit for Feces (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity and purity of the extracted DNA was assessed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (120 V, 30 min). DNA concentration and quality were measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Samples meeting quality criteria (the concentration of genomic DNA ≥ 40 ng/μL, the A260/280 ratio ranging from 1.8 to 2.0) were diluted with sterile nuclease-free water to a final concentration of 1 ng/μL for subsequent analysis.

PCR Amplification and product purification

The diluted genomic DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene target region, ddH2O was used as the negative controls, employing barcode-tagged primers (F: AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG; R: RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT) [30]. Amplification conditions are shown in Table 1. Amplification products were verified by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Subsequently, PCR amplicons were purified using a magnetic bead-based purification kit, AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA), to remove primer dimers and non-specific amplification products.

Kinnex library preparation

Barcoded PCR products from individual samples were pooled in equimolar amounts and used to prepare libraries with PacBio’s Kinnex long-read sequencing technology, using reagents provided by Pacific Biosciences (Menlo Park, CA, USA). The workflow began with an end repair step, in which PCR products were treated with Kinnex end repair enzyme at 20 °C for 30 min to generate blunt-ended DNA fragments. This was followed by adapter ligation, where Kinnex-specific sequencing adapters (20 μM) and T4 DNA ligase were added, and the reaction was incubated at 20 °C for 15 min. Next, 16S rRNA gene fragments were concatenated into long linear DNA arrays through a linear array assembly step, performed using Kinnex assembly enzyme at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the library was purified via size selection using SMRTbell® magnetic beads at 0.45 × and 0.8 × bead-to-sample ratios to enrich for fragments in the 500 to 2000 bp range, thereby improving overall library quality and suitability for long-read sequencing.

Library quality evaluation and sequencing

The purified library was quantified using a Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), and fragment size distribution was analyzed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) to confirm that the primary peak fell within the expected range (1.5 to 3 kb). Libraries meeting quality thresholds were sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform (PacBio Biotechnology Research, Menlo Park, CA, USA) using single molecule, real-time sequencing to generate high-fidelity long read data.

Data quality control and bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed based on sample-specific barcode and primer sequences. High-quality clean tags were extracted from demultiplexed HiFi reads using SeqKit (v2.9.0) [31]. Quality control and denoising were performed using the DADA2 (vR3.5) plugin [32] within the QIIME 2 (2024.10) platform [33] with a 99% sequence similarity threshold (matching the taxonomic resolution at the species level) [34, 35], to infer amplicon sequence variants and construct a feature table. SeqKit filtering retained reads ≥ 1400 nt with mean Q-score ≥ 38.80; DADA2 denoising included chimera removal (–chimera-method consensus) and excluded singletons (features with < 2 observations). Representative amplicon sequence variants were taxonomically classified by alignment against the SILVA 138.2 (2024.7) reference database (http://www.arb-silva.de/). Functional potential of the microbial communities was predicted using Tax4Fun2 with its built-in Ref99NR reference database (a non-redundant species-level reference sequence library clustered at a 99% sequence similarity), where the minimum similarity threshold was set to 97%. [36].

Temporal trends in gut microbial composition

MaAsLin2 analysis employed a mixed-effects generalized linear model for microbiota abundance data (TSS normalization, log transformation, invalid feature filtering), with infant individual as the random effect and days (7d as baseline) as the fixed effect. Wald test was used to assess the association significance between microbial features and relative time, with FDR controlled via the BH (Benjamini-Hochberg) method for multiple test correction [37].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.3). Alpha diversity indices, including the Shannon index, observed species, Chao1 index, and Simpson index of diversity, were calculated using the vegan (2.7–2) package to evaluate within-sample microbial diversity. The Kruskal–Wallis test (KW test for short) was performed to assess the overall differences in the distribution of alpha diversity indices across multiple sample groups, Dunn's Kruskal–Wallis multiple comparison test with the significance threshold set at adjusted P < 0.05. Based on the UniFrac distance metric, beta diversity was assessed via principal coordinate analysis and non-metric multidimensional scaling to characterize inter-sample microbial community structure. PERMANOVA analysis was based on the UniFrac distance matrix, with 999 permutations by default, implemented via the adonis2 function. Microbial composition was analyzed using the LorMe package [38]. Additionally, correlation coefficients among dominant bacterial taxa were computed using the SparCC algorithm (with 500 iterations, Dirichlet normalization, and log-transformation) to examine their associations [39]. Data visualization was implemented with the ggplot2 (4.0.1) package. Tax4Fun2 functional pathway enrichment analysis [36], the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used to correct for false discovery rate (FDR), with the significance threshold set at adjusted P < 0.05 to control false positive results. All statistical tests were two, sided unless otherwise indicated. Benjamini, Hochberg FDR correction was done separately for each group of tests (e.g., Wald tests for MaAsLin2 temporal trends), with adjusted P < 0. 05 considered significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Twelve newborns infants were initially enrolled in this study, with longitudinal fecal samples collected over a one-year period (total n = 68, each time point 7 days (n = 11), 30 days (n = 12), 60 days (n = 12), 100 days (n = 11), 200 days (n = 11), and 360 days (n = 11)), One participant, infant N, was discontinued from the study at two months of age due to insufficient breast milk supply, which precluded continuation of exclusive breastfeeding. Another participant, infant K, had missing data from the first time point because insufficient sample quantity prevented sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 1). Despite this withdrawal, demographic and baseline characteristics were reported for all 12 originally enrolled participants (Table 2). Samples collected from infant N were also included in the analysis. The data processing and analysis workflow is shown in the Supplementary Fig. 2.

Sequencing data quality and coverage

Fecal samples (n = 68) collected from the 12 infants across six time points were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The PacBio sequencing generated approximately 1400–1600 nt full-length reads covering all variable regions, operational taxonomic unit sequences with ≤ 1% incorrect bases. Quality control results confirmed data reliability: for all samples, the mean Q score was 38.94. Following quality control, an average of 22,409 high-quality reads per sample was obtained. The highest read count was observed in sample Y_100 (28,412 reads), while sample C_30 yielded the lowest (19,670 reads). The average read length was 1,466 nt, reflecting consistent sequencing performance (Fig. 1A). There were variations in the read output among individual samples, which may be attributable to inconsistencies in the sample collection process.

To assess sequencing sufficiency and data quality, we performed rarefaction curve analysis. Rarefaction curves for all samples plateau at approximately 10,000 reads (Fig. 1B-D), indicating that sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of microbial taxa present. This saturation confirms that the generated data volume was adequate for robust downstream microbiome analysis.

Taxonomic composition and ecological interactions in the infant gut microbiota

Taxonomic profiling across developmental stages

A total of 68 fecal samples, with at least three time points per infant, were included in the taxonomic analysis. After denoising with DADA2, 801,575 valid full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences were taxonomically annotated based on the SILVA 138.2 database, yielding annotation rates of 88.94% (genus level) and 51.71% (species level). To reduce data noise and improve the biological interpretability of results, we excluded two types of data: (i) low-abundance taxa with a relative abundance < 0.01% in each sample; and (ii) taxa identified as non-bacterial after taxonomic annotation. After data filtering, ASV counts varied across samples, with a minimum of 15,549. To eliminate sequencing depth bias in subsequent microbial diversity and composition analyses, all samples were rarefied to this standardized depth.

At the phylum level, a total of 16 bacterial phyla were successfully annotated, the five most abundant bacterial phyla were visualized in a stacked bar plot (Fig. 2A). Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, and Proteobacteria were consistently dominant across all samples. Verrucomicrobia was detected only in infants A, R, and K, suggesting limited inter-individual variation among core phyla but potential niche-specific colonization.

Taxonomic composition of the infant gut microbiota. Stacked bar plots depict the relative abundance microbial taxa across all infant samples at the (A) phylum, (B) genus, and (C) species levels. The letters above the bars indicate infant identifier and infant age (days)

At the genus level, a total of 192 bacterial genera were successfully annotated, the top 10 most abundant taxa were represented in a stacked bar plot (Fig. 2B). Dominant genera included Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides. Temporal fluctuations in their relative abundances reflected both inter-individual differences and age-dependent developmental trajectories.

At the species level, a total of 251 bacterial species were successfully annotated, a stacked bar plot of the top 15 most abundant species was generated (Fig. 2C). Key species included Streptococcus salivarius, Bifidobacterium longum, and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum. Pronounced inter-individual variability was evident in the temporal trajectories of these species. Aimed to systematically analyze the distribution patterns of shared and unique bacterial species in the infant gut microbiota at six key time points (days 7, 30, 60, 100, 200, and 360), the study constructing a species–time point presence-absence matrix based on all annotated species and visualizing the data using an UpSet plot (Supplementary Fig. 3). The analysis identified 67 core species present across all six time points, primarily including Bifidobacterium longum, Streptococcus salivarius, and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, among others. Meanwhile, a distinct temporal succession pattern of unique species was observed at each time point: 5 unique species on day 7 (e.g., Lactococcus garvieae); 6 on day 30 (e.g., Leptospira licerasiae); only 6 on day 60 (e.g., Klebsiella pneumoniae); a significant increase to 8 on day 100 (e.g., Lactobacillus panis); 3 on day 200 (e.g., Dialister succinatiphilus); and a peak of 27 unique species on day 360 (e.g., Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis).

These results indicate that the infant gut microbiota exhibits clear temporal specificity during development, with the number of unique species showing nonlinear fluctuations and peaking at day 360. This dynamic pattern suggests that the colonization of the infant gut microbiota is not a simple linear accumulation but rather an ecological process driven by multiple factors such as immune system maturation and environmental exposure. This continuous trajectory of competition, selection, and adaptation ultimately drives the microbial community toward a more complex and stable adult-like structure.

Ecological relationships of dominant microbiota taxa

To further investigate the correlations among dominant species, this study performed a correlation analysis on dominant species using the SparCC algorithm. From the results of the statistical histogram (Fig. 3A), the correlation coefficient |ρ| among dominant species was ≤ 0.2, indicating a generally weak correlation between species overall; a correlation with |ρ|≥ 0.3 was defined as a moderate or higher correlation between species. Specific interspecific correlation characteristics were observed from the correlation heatmap (Fig. 3B): Bifidobacterium longum was moderately positively correlated with Bifidobacterium bifidum (ρ = 0.54), Streptococcus salivarius was strongly positively correlated with Streptococcus vestibularis (ρ = 0.66), and Bifidobacterium bifidum was positively correlated with Bifidobacterium breve (ρ = 0.32); Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum was moderately negatively correlated with Enterococcus faecalis (ρ = −0.42), and Enterococcus faecalis was negatively correlated with Ruminococcus gnavus (ρ = −0.32). The above results indicated that positive correlations were prevalent among conspecific genera, while negative correlations were mostly observed between heterologous genera. This might be attributed to the synergetic colonization characteristic of species within the same genus, which enables them to jointly maintain the homeostasis of the intestinal microbiota. Further analysis combined with the genus-level correlation heatmap revealed that Bifidobacterium was positively correlated with Lacticaseibacillus, Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, but negatively correlated with Veillonella, Klebsiella and Enterococcus. It is thus tentatively hypothesized that these beneficial bacteria may tend to form synergetic interactions and symbiosis in the infant gut, rather than a simple interspecific competitive relationship.

Determinants of variation in infant gut microbiota composition

Given the observed temporal shifts in gut microbiota diversity, potential contributing factors were examined using participant metadata summarized in Table 2. To identify the key factors shaping the gut microbiota structure, this study employed Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance PERMANOVA (via the adonis2 function) was applied to assess the influence of nine variables (individual identity, ethnicity, delivery mode, and infant age (days), etc.) on overall microbial community structure (Table 2).

The full PERMANOVA model, incorporating all nine factors, was statistically significant (P = 0.001) and accounted for approximately 33.92% of the total variation in microbiota composition, indicating that these variables collectively exert a substantial influence on community assembly (Table 3). Among the nine variables assessed only two factors exerted statistically significant effects on the overall gut microbial community structure: individual identity and infant age (days). Specifically, individual identity accounted for 30.50% of the total variation in microbial community structure (P = 0.001), serving as the dominant driving factor, whereas infant age (days) explained 3.41% of the variation (P = 0.001), reflecting a significant yet modest effect. The remaining seven variables did not exhibit statistically significant associations with microbial community structure (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 4). Subsequently, α-diversity analysis was conducted in this study with the Shannon index as the core metric. For comparisons among multiple groups in this study, we used one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA); for comparisons involving two independent samples, we used the t-test. The results showed that infant age had a statistically significant effect on the α-diversity of the microbial community (P < 0.05). Additionally, parity exerted effect (P = 0.08), with the gut microbial α-diversity of second-born infants being higher than that of first-born infants (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Temporal shifts in alpha and beta diversity of the infant gut microbiota

Longitudinal assessment of species composition revealed continuous remodeling of the infant gut microbiota during the first year of life. To characterize changes in microbial diversity over time, alpha diversity metrics, including the Shannon index, observed species, Chao1 index, and Simpson index of diversity, were evaluated across all 68 samples. The Shannon and Simpson indices increased without statistical significance across time points (Fig. 5A), which reflect species evenness. This suggests that the relative abundance distribution of different species in the infant gut microbiota gradually became more balanced—an important indicator of the maturation of the microbial community structure—although the changes between time points did not reach statistical significance. On the other hand, the Chao1 index and observed species index, which characterize species richness, exhibited characteristic fluctuations: both peaked at around 100 days of age while maintaining an overall upward trend. This phenomenon may be attributed to the increasingly complex external environment that infants are exposed to around 100 days of age, leading to a significant increase in the sources of bacterial species entering the gut. At the same time, bacterial colonization in the gut undergoes natural selection and competition, and some species fail to establish stable residency. As a result, species richness did not continue to rise sharply after reaching its peak but instead transitioned into a more gradual upward trend.

After clarifying the temporal changes in alpha diversity, we further performed principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on the UniFrac distance to analyze the overall differences in microbial composition among samples of different ages. No distinct clustering of samples according to collection time point was evident in either ordination plot (Fig. 5B), the indicates that the temporal succession of the infant gut microbiota is characterized by gradual and subtle ensemble-level changes. Further pairwise comparisons of the Shannon index across sampling days were conducted to identify specific intervals of significant diversification (Fig. 5C). Statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were detected between samples collected at day 360 and those obtained at both days 7 and 30, indicating that microbial diversity at the end of the first year was substantially higher than in the early neonatal period, which reflects that the microbiota undergoes continuous ecological succession and becomes progressively enriched with infant growth.

Temporal dynamics of the infant gut microbiota

To characterize the temporal dynamic trends in gut microbial composition at the species level, this study first determined the optimal number of clusters (K) using the elbow method (Supplementary Fig. 5 A), then grouped microbes into clusters with similar temporal variation patterns via hierarchical clustering, and further these derived temporal patterns statistically through MaAsLin2 association analysis.

The six trends were categorized into three distinct temporal patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5B): (i) Early colonization (panels 1 and 4), characterized by peak abundances at 30 days and 7 days post-birth respectively followed by a gradual decline; (ii) Mid-term colonization (panels 2 and 3), showing abundance maxima emerging at 60 days and 100 days post-birth with a subsequent drop to low levels; (iii) Late-term colonization (panels 5 and 6), exhibiting a sharp rise in abundance at 360 days and 200 days post-birth separately after a prolonged period of low abundance.

The early colonization stage (Fig. 6, panels A and D), as part of the gradual progression of intestinal colonization, the infant gut microbiota was dominated by infant-type core probiotics (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus) with low taxonomic diversity (3–5 core genera), including Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium breve, Lactobacillus rhamnosus Enterococcus faecalis and other related strains. The functions of some of these genera have been previously confirmed to be closely associated with the host’s environmental adaptability and immune regulation. [40–42]. The middle colonization stage (Fig. 6, panels B and C), with the continuous progression of colonization, nutrient-metabolizing commensal genera such as Ruminococcus (e.g.,Ruminococcus lactaris, Ruminococcus bicirculans), Streptococcu (e.g., Streptococcus infantis, Streptococcus salivarius), and Bacteroides (e.g., Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides ovatus)were enriched, the gut microbiota increased to more than 10 genera, particularly the genus Ruminococcus, which is commonly associated with short-chain fatty acid production [43]. The late-term colonization stage (Fig. 6, panels E and F), the microbiota is represented by genera such as Pseudomonas (e.g., Pseudomonas lundensis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and Weissella (e.g., Weissella confuse, Weissella salipiscis), which are predominantly environmental-origin genera. the infant gut microbiota further rose to more than 15 genera. This phenomenon may be attributed to complementary food introduction, which in turn drives the intestinal microenvironment to shift from a closed state dominated by the mother-infant system to an open state. Notably, Bifidobacterium underwent a distinct subtype transition, shifting from infant-type strains (Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum) to universal-type strains (Bifidobacterium animalis, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Bifidobacterium faecale), thus adapting to the nutritional transition from breast milk to complementary foods.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the infant gut microbiota undergoes highly structured, time-dependent shifts, with distinct microbial consortia emerging at specific postnatal stages. These temporal patterns provide a framework for investigating the interplay between microbial colonization and host functional development during early life.

Temporal dynamics of predicted microbial functions in the infant gut

To infer the functional potential of the developing gut microbiota, microbial community composition data were used to predict functional profiles via Tax4Fun2. Functional annotations were assigned to the predicted microbial functions based on KEGG categories. Functional annotations were organized into three hierarchical levels: level 1 (broad functional categories), level 2 (functional modules), and level 3 (specific metabolic pathways). Heatmap visualizations of relative functional abundances across all three levels (Supplementary Fig. 6–8) revealed weak global temporal patterns at the holistic level, detailed information on Level 3 pathways is provided in Supplementary Table 1, suggesting that while the relative abundances of individual functions varied over time, these fluctuations did not follow a clear developmental trajectory. This pattern implies a degree of functional redundancy or stability in the infant gut microbiome during the first year of life.

To identify time-dependent functional shifts, longitudinal analysis was performed on differentially abundant features at each hierarchical level, including the top 10 significantly varying pathways at level 3 (Fig. 7A). At level 1, six functional categories were annotated: Cellular Processes, Environmental Information Processing, Genetic Information Processing, Human Diseases, Metabolism, and Organismal Systems (Supplementary Fig. 6). Among these, only the Human Diseases category under KEGG functional annotation exhibited statistically significant variation over time (P < 0.05). Its abundance followed a dynamic trajectory, characterized by an initial slow increase, a subsequent decline, a mid-infancy rebound, and a final decrease, suggesting that metabolic pathways associated with disease-associated functional modules are not static.

At level 2, 47 functional modules were identified across the major level 1 categories. Four modules showed significant temporal differences (P < 0.05): those associated with Cancers, Cellular Growth and Death, Circulatory System, and Xenobiotics Degradation and Metabolism of Xenobiotics. The module linked to Cellular Growth and Death displayed a progressive increase in relative abundance, whereas the other three modules declined over time. This divergence indicates functional specialization and diversification of the microbiota as the host matures. At level 3, hundreds of metabolic pathways were annotated, of which 43 were significantly differentially abundant (P < 0.05). These were predominantly involved in core metabolic processes, such as Fatty Acid Metabolism and Biosynthesis, Degradation of Aromatic Compounds, and Drug Metabolism, Cytochrome P450. Cluster heatmap analysis of these differential pathways revealed time-specific functional signatures (Fig. 7B). For instance, the “Regulation of Autophagy in Animals” pathway was significantly enriched only at day 360, suggesting that late infancy may represent a critical window for the emergence of host-interfacing microbial functions related to cellular homeostasis and immune regulation.

In summary, functional prediction analysis revealed that functional pathways of the infant gut microbiota are continuously enriched with growth and development. Within the first year of life, the external environment infants are exposed to grows increasingly complex; additionally, complementary foods introduction shifts their dietary structure from exclusive breastfeeding to a diversified diet, elevating intestinal digestive and metabolic demands and ultimately driving the diversified development of gut microbiota functional pathways [44]. However, this study only included healthy infants, and analysis of disease-associated functional modules lacks a corresponding control group; thus, explicit inference of host disease relevance from disease-associated functions is unfeasible.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study, full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using the PacBio Revio platform on 68 fecal samples collected from 12 exclusively breastfed infants across six time points during their first year of life. Together, these integrated analytical approaches combining MaAsLin2 association analysis, targeted temporal module modeling, and Tax4Fun2 based temporal functional prediction represent a complementary analytical framework to the methodologies employed in large-scale metagenomic and 16S rRNA gene sequencing infant cohorts. Unlike previous large metagenomic cohorts that prioritize clinical phenotype correlations and mother-infant transmission but lack in-depth systematic temporal analysis [45, 46], and large 16S amplicon cohorts that focus on identifying drivers of microbial variation yet have notable deficiencies in functional characterization [47, 48]. This study approach enabled high-resolution characterization of the dynamic succession of the infant gut microbiota revealing structured temporal shifts in diversity, composition, ecological interactions, and predicted functional potential.

Alpha diversity was observed to increase gradually with age, with statistically significant differences detected between early (days 7 and 30) and late (day 360) time points, confirming that the first year represents a period of active microbial maturation. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Actinomycetota, and Proteobacteria predominated, a pattern consistent with prior reports of infant gut microbial architecture [49]. At the genus level, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Lactobacillus emerged as dominant taxa, aligning with findings by a previous study. These genera are recognized as keystone early colonizers, with their trajectories serving as biomarkers of healthy microbial development [3]. The decline in Bifidobacterium abundance at approximately day 100 may be due to the increasingly complex living environment and consequent environmental changes, which required the gut microbiota to adapt. Notably, Bifidobacterium exhibited a distinct subtype shift from infant-type strains (Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum) to universal-type strains (Bifidobacterium animalis, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Bifidobacterium faecale), thus adapting to the nutritional transition from breast milk to complementary foods [50, 51]. Although individual dietary records were not available to confirm this association. This dietary shift introduces complex substrates and increases microbial and functional complexity, which was reflected in our Tax4Fun2 predictions showing enrichment of pathways related to xenobiotic metabolism and aromatic compound degradation.

Notably, Lactobacillus reached its abundance peak at day 100, suggesting a role as an early colonizer. Its initial rise may reflect successful engraftment in the neonatal gut, followed by competitive exclusion during mid-infancy. In contrast, Staphylococcus, commonly transmitted via breast milk, declined steadily over time, consistent with its reduced fitness under increasingly anaerobic and competitive conditions of a maturing gut. This decline likely facilitates niche opening for obligate anaerobes, marking a transition toward a more stable, adult-like microbiota. At the species level, Streptococcus salivarius and Bifidobacterium longum were among the most abundant, with the former potentially of maternal origin, underscoring vertical transmission as a key colonization route. Despite inter-individual variation, dominant species followed convergent temporal trajectories, implying that host developmental processes, particularly dietary transitions and immune maturation, act as overarching drivers of microbial succession.

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance analysis confirmed that microbial community structure was significantly influenced by a combination of factors, including nine factors such as postnatal age, individual identity, parity, ethnicity, and delivery mode (P = 0.001), collectively explaining 33.92% of total variance, However, results of the single-factor test indicated that most study factors did not reach a significant level. Age factor emerged as the strongest contributors, while individual and ethnic showed a trend toward influence, possibly mediated by culturally shaped maternal diet or caregiving practices [52], though differences between ethnic groups did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size. Parity and delivery mode exerted a detectable effect primarily between 30 and 200 days, the gut microbial diversity of second-born infants was higher than that of first-born infants, which might be attributed to parity modulating the effect of maternal gestational weight gain on the infant gut microbiome [53]. consistent with literature indicating that birth mode shapes early colonization but its impact wanes by 6 to 12 months as environmental and dietary factors dominate [54, 55]. These findings reinforce the concept that while early-life exposures set the initial trajectory, postnatal factors progressively override initial conditions during microbiota maturation.

Functional predictions via Tax4Fun2 revealed significant temporal shifts in metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in fatty acid metabolism, aromatic compound degradation, and cytochrome P450-mediated drug metabolism. As solid foods are introduced, the functional demands on the microbiota shift toward complex carbohydrate breakdown and immune-modulatory metabolite production, driving the observed structural and functional reorganization of the gut microbiota [56, 57]. This supports a model in which the developing microbiota actively contributes to host nutrient metabolism and immune maturation, though causal roles remain to be confirmed through integrated multi-omics or targeted intervention studies.

Strengths of this study include the use of PacBio Revio long-read sequencing with Kinnex library preparation, which enabled full-length 16S rRNA profiling and improved taxonomic resolution, particularly at the species level, compared to conventional short-read approaches. The longitudinal design with dense sampling across the first year allowed for robust temporal modeling (e.g., MaAsLin2 analysis) and detection of non-linear successional patterns. Additionally, the exclusive focus on breastfed infants from a single geographic region (Hohhot, Inner Mongolia) reduced confounding from feeding heterogeneity and environmental variability.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. (1) The sample size (n = 12) was modest, inconsistent operational standards for sample collection, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses (e.g., by ethnicity or delivery mode) and increasing susceptibility to individual outliers. (2) Fecal samples remain an effective carrier for investigating the dynamic changes of the human gut microbiota [58, 59], yet they have certain limitations: fecal samples contain a category of satellite microbiota, which only exists in feces, does not adhere to the intestinal mucosa, and is not a component of the indigenous gut microbiota, thus potentially interfering with the interpretation of the authentic characteristics of the indigenous gut microecology. (3) Functional predictions were derived from 16S rRNA gene data using Tax4Fun2; though useful for exploratory analyses, this approach does not directly quantify microbial functional activity or gene content and cannot replace metagenomic/metabolomic validation. Additionally, observational data alone cannot establish causal relationships between microbial taxa and host outcomes.

Collectively, these findings underscore that infant gut microbiota development is a highly dynamic, multi-factorial process shaped by host physiology, environmental exposures, and microbial ecology. The microbiota does not merely respond passively to host changes but is strongly associated with nutrient processing, immune modulation, and ecosystem stabilization, highlighting its central role in early-life health programming.

Conclusion

The infant gut microbiota undergoes a structured and dynamic transformation during the first year of life, evolving from a Bifidobacterium-dominated, low-diversity community toward a more complex and functionally diverse ecosystem. This transition is most pronounced around the time of solid food introduction, which acts as a key ecological perturbation driving microbial and metabolic reorganization. Bifidobacterium serves as a critical regulator, suppressing disease-associated functions and fostering cooperative interactions that support community stability. While multiple factors, including postnatal age, individual host characteristics, parity, ethnicity, delivery mode, and other factors. influence microbial trajectories, their effects are integrated within a developmental framework governed by host maturation and dietary progression. These results emphasize that the first year represents a sensitive window during which the gut microbiome is both highly plastic and profoundly influential on host metabolic and immune development. Strategies aimed at supporting a healthy, resilient microbiota during this period, such as sustained breastfeeding and judicious introduction of complementary foods, may have lasting implications for lifelong health.
