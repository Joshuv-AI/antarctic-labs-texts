Randomized, controlled trial comparing the efficacy of intercostal nerve block in thoracoscopic surgery by timing: study protocol for a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Yasuharu Sekine, Hideo Ichimura, Sho Ueda, Shinsuke Kitazawa, Naohiro Kobayashi, Yuya Murata, Kumiko Yamada, Shinichi Inomata, Kazushi Maruo, Yukio Sato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEven minimally invasive thoracic surgeries carry a postoperative cost in pain. While regional anesthesia may alleviate this anticipated pain, especially intercostal nerve blocks (ICNB), the timing of block administration for optimal effect (at the beginning of surgery vs. before wound closure) remains controversial. Here, we plan (1) to test the hypothesis that performing ICNB at the beginning of surgery provides superior postoperative analgesic effects compared to before wound closure and (2) to observe perioperative outcomes to determine whether the timing affects acute and chronic postoperative pain, or intraoperative patient management.MethodsThis single-blinded, single-center, randomized, controlled trial will be conducted at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, University of Tsukuba Hospital from Sep 29, 2024, to June 30, 2026. Patients 16–79 years old within the regular population of patients undergoing lung wedge resection via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) without a utility window approach and who are ASA-PS classes 1–3 will be enrolled to receive ICNB either pre-operatively or before wound closure. Groups will be single-blinded (patient only) at random, and the primary outcome will be assessment by Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores during movement, measured 4 h after returning to the ward. Secondary outcomes will be (1) any additional analgesic use within the 4-h return window, (2) intraoperative measurements (including amounts of remifentanil used, time from surgery completion to extubation, occurrence of shivering or nausea after extubation, use of antiemetics, and maximum blood pressure/heart rate), (3) acute pain during hospitalization (using routinely administered NRS), (4) postoperative analgesic use, and (5) quality of life (QOL) scores on EQ-5D and SF-36 questionnaires at preoperative and 3-month postoperative timepoints. Chronic pain at 3 months post-surgery will also be determined by NRS.DiscussionA complete evaluation of ICNB efficacy as a preemptive analgesic approach will contribute to the development of improved perioperative pain management techniques. A reduction in the duration of surgery-related symptoms and an improvement in postoperative QOL will be the result.Trial registrationJapan Registry of Clinical Trials: identifier jRCT1031240358. Introduction

Background and rationale (6a)

Thoracic surgery is a procedure associated with significant postoperative pain. This pain can be categorized into acute and chronic phases, both of which remain clinically relevant challenges, particularly in procedures involving the lateral approach. Various surgical studies and innovations have been explored to alleviate postoperative pain [1, 2], such as using different types of thoracotomy and suture placements for the intercostal spaces [3]. More recently, minimizing incision size has been achieved through advancements in video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) techniques [4] and these studies reported efficacy in reducing postoperative pain.

Recent guidelines in thoracic surgery, based on Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) recommendations, advocate for multimodal pain management strategies incorporating regional anesthesia techniques, such as epidural analgesia, paravertebral blocks, and intercostal nerve blocks [5]. Epidural analgesia is considered the gold standard; however, it has several limitations, including potential complications (e.g., hypotension, urinary retention, postoperative ileus, hematoma, and abscess formation), as well as contraindications in patients taking anticoagulants. Furthermore, the success rate of epidural catheter placement varies significantly depending on the anesthesiologist’s experience. The failure rate of epidural catheter placement has been reported to range from 6.8 to 20% [6, 7].

Conversely, intercostal nerve block (ICNB) offers certain advantages over epidural analgesia, including a lower risk of procedure-related complications and the absence of rebound pain often observed following epidural catheter removal [8]. Moreover, reports suggest that the benefits of epidural analgesia may be limited, as pain associated with surgical procedures has decreased with the widespread adoption of VATS techniques [9]. In this context, the appropriate combination of commonly used surgical approaches and perioperative regional anesthesia techniques should be re-evaluated at each institution in light of advancements in surgical technology and the trend toward less invasive approaches.

Furthermore, the guidelines mention pre-emptive analgesia. In thoracic surgery, a few previous studies have reported the effectiveness of pre-emptive epidural analgesia [10, 11], but none have yet demonstrated the effectiveness of pre-emptive analgesia using ICNB.

According to our in-house protocol, we employ ICNB before wound closure in video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), particularly in procedures that can be completed using only surgical ports (such as surgeries for spontaneous pneumothorax). Nonetheless, there is room for improvement, including addressing cases where surgeons inadvertently forget to perform ICNB during wound closure. Administering ICNB at the start of surgery could prevent these issues, potentially enhancing its preemptive analgesic effects while avoiding omission.

Currently, we lack data on the degree of pain, the extent of chronic pain, or the quality of life (QOL) reported by patients under our in-house protocol. Therefore, we designed this exploratory, randomized trial to compare the effects of different timings for ICNB administration. While comparative studies with other regional anesthesia techniques exist, no studies have specifically investigated the aspect of optimal timing for ICNB.

Objectives (7)

This study will test the hypothesis that performing intercostal nerve block (ICNB) at the beginning of surgery provides superior postoperative analgesic effects compared to before wound closure. The study targets patients undergoing VATS using only surgical ports at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, University of Tsukuba Hospital.

Trial design (8)

This is a single-center, single-blinded, randomized clinical trial designed to test the efficacy of ICNB intervention in lung surgery. This trial has been registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials: identifier jRCT1031240358. The protocol structure is based on the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) checklist.

Intervention description (11a)

This study uses 0.375% Ropivacaine (7.5 mg/mL of Ropivacaine was diluted twofold with an equal volume of normal saline to prepare 0.375% Ropivacaine.). As a local anesthetic, it is expected to provide perioperative pain relief. Potential complications include bleeding due to intercostal vessel puncture and pneumothorax due to lung puncture during the procedure.

Administration of the investigational drug

Beginning of Surgery Group: ICNB will be performed at the start of surgery. Ropivacaine (0.375%) will be injected into the intercostal muscles near each port site, under thoracoscopic visualization. Total volume: 15–20 mL (56.25–75 mg of Ropivacaine), with a maximum of 30 mL (112.5 mg of Ropivacaine) per surgery if additional ports are used.

Explanation for the choice of the comparators (6b)

We have an in-house protocol to perform intercostal nerve blocks before wound closure. By comparing this approach with a group receiving the block at the beginning of surgery, the effect of intercostal nerve blocks to provide similar pre-emptive analgesic effects can be contrasted against those reported for other regional anesthetic techniques.

Prohibited care

Due to the nature of the study, other regional anesthesia techniques, including epidural analgesia, are not permitted. Intraoperative anesthesia management and postoperative pain control will be conducted as described above. There is no prohibited care defined. However, to ensure that study participants do not suffer more pain than they would typically experience in clinical practice, discretion by the attending surgeon is allowed.

Provisions for post-trial care (30)

If an adverse event occurs, necessary treatment and care will continue to be provided to participants within the scope of standard health insurance, even after the completion of the trial.

Participant timeline (13)

For patients referred to our department for surgery, such as those with spontaneous pneumothorax, the attending surgeon will confirm the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients scheduled for surgical treatment will receive an explanation of the trial and, after providing consent to participate, they will be asked to complete a preoperative questionnaire. For participants deemed not to require a follow-up visit at three months (postoperatively) by the attending surgeon, the questionnaire will be mailed and they will be asked to return their responses. Sample size (14)

According to a previous study [11], the mean difference in pain scores during movement at 4 h postoperatively was 1.9, with a pooled standard deviation of 1.6. Assuming a conservative standardized effect size (mean difference/pooled SD) of 1, the minimum number of cases required to achieve a statistical power of > 0.8 in the primary analysis was estimated to be 17 per group. Considering potential dropouts, the sample size was set at 20 cases per group. Given that approximately 50 eligible cases are expected in the fiscal year 2023, it was determined that the study could be completed within one year.

Recruitment (15)

The recruitment pool will be those patients scheduled for surgery at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, University of Tsukuba that meet all inclusion criteria but none of the exclusion criteria.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation (16a)

Participants will be registered and randomly assigned to one of the two groups using an electronic data capture (EDC) system (ACReSS). Stratification will be performed based on preoperative pain scores (NRS = 0 vs. NRS > 0).

Concealment mechanism (16b)

After providing informed consent, participants will be registered in the trial. Preoperative pain scores (NRS) will be collected and used as stratification factors. Group allocation will then be performed automatically through an EDC system (ACReSS), ensuring that the assignment cannot be predicted in advance. Participants will not be informed of their allocation to maintain single-blinding.

Implementation (16c)

The allocation will be automatically generated by the ACReSS system. The allocation information will be shared with the attending physicians, operating surgeons, and anesthesiologists to ensure adherence to the trial protocol, but participants will remain blinded to their allocation. Primary outcomes will be based on patient-reported measures, while all other outcomes will consist of objective observational data with no subjective assessment by the attending physicians.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded (17a)

In this study, only patients will be blinded (single-blind design), since physicians must be unblinded to conduct the surgeries and postoperative care safely.

Procedure for unblinding if needed (17b)

Not applicable. Intercostal nerve block was the only procedure set as a single-blinded procedure. As previously described in the sections on “permitted care” and “prohibited care,” all other procedures are identical in both groups.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes (18a)

The primary endpoint will be collected on paper-based case report forms, and the entries will be completed by the nursing staff responsible for the patients. To ensure consistency in assessment, the study investigators provided training sessions to the ward nursing staff on the evaluation methods. Quality of life (QOL) data will be collected on paper forms completed by the patients themselves. Upon collection, the attending physician will check for any missing responses and remind the patients if necessary. All other secondary outcomes will be obtained from data recorded in the electronic medical record system or the electronic anesthesia recording system. Data entry into the ACReSS system will be performed by a designated data manager. As the dataset consists of structured fields, no independent verification process or double data entry will be implemented.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up (18b)

Follow-up visits are a routine part of post-operative management and will be conducted as scheduled by surgeons. Participant retention is expected to remain high, as there is no additional reporting burden placed on participants other than the taking of the NRS scores and the EQ-5D and SF-36 questionnaires. Prior to obtaining consent, patients were informed about the postoperative QOL survey to be conducted three months postoperatively.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee (5d)

This trial is a single-center, clinical trial protocol aligned with our clinical practice, comparing the effects of an approved local analgesic drug based on the timing of its administration. We plan to conduct this trial without establishing a coordinating center or trial steering committee; this approach has been approved by the Clinical Research Board (CRB).

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure (21a)

For the same reasons as mentioned above, a monitoring committee will not be established. Instead, monitoring will be conducted within the research group by personnel designated by the principal investigator, using a mutual monitoring system. Monitoring will be performed by research members other than those responsible for obtaining participant explanations and consent. This monitoring structure has been approved by the CRB as it better aligns with clinical practice.

Adverse event reporting and harms (22)

Since this trial evaluates the effects of differences in the timing of administration of an approved medication, serious adverse events from the change in procedure timing (i.e., preoperative vs. before wound closure) are not anticipated. Potential adverse events associated with the intercostal nerve block procedure itself include puncture of the intercostal blood vessels or the lung. If such events occur, they will be documented in the surgical records and appropriately managed during the surgery. In case other adverse events occur, prompt and appropriate care will be provided to the participants. Depending on the severity of the event, it will be reported to the hospital director and the CRB within the specified timeframe. The report will include an assessment of the causal relationship between the adverse event and the trial.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct (23)

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the effects of the timing of administration of an approved drug, with both the control group and the experimental group reflecting our clinical practice. Therefore, no audits are planned for this study.

Discussion

We positioned this study as exploratory due to the lack of detailed data on our current in-house protocol. In this trial, we aim to evaluate whether intercostal nerve block (ICNB) in VATS procedures demonstrates efficacy as the primary endpoint, based on the hypothesis that it has a preemptive analgesic effect. This will be assessed by measuring the NRS pain score at 4 h postoperatively. In pain management trials, it is crucial to collect data and disclose the analysis results within well-controlled environments at each institution. Through this process, a standardized approach to pain management can be developed as collective knowledge.

The required sample size for this study was calculated based on prior research with a similar protocol. However, since the reference study utilized epidural analgesia [11], we anticipate two possibilities: a negative possibility that the analgesic effect of ICNB may be weaker than epidural analgesia or a positive possibility that rebound pain, commonly observed with epidural analgesia, may not occur [8].

Performing ICNB at the beginning of surgery offers a practical advantage by preventing unintentional omissions. During protocol development, the anesthesiologist co-investigator suggested additional potential benefits of ICNB at the start of surgery, including reduced intraoperative remifentanil usage, shorter anesthesia recovery times, and decreased postoperative nausea and vomiting. These considerations led us to include these factors as secondary endpoints in the study.

Regarding chronic pain, even minimally invasive procedures (such as VATS) have been reported to result in a higher frequency of chronic pain compared to laparoscopic cholecystectomy [12–15], likely due to neuropathic pain associated with intercostal nerve injury. This study also aims to address the clinical question of whether the timing of ICNB influences the frequency of chronic pain. Additionally, we plan to explore predictive factors for the development of chronic pain after VATS, including preoperative patient-reported outcome (PRO) scores, such as EQ-5D and SF-36. Identifying preoperative predictive factors for chronic pain could lead to improved postoperative care for high-risk patients.

As for stratification, the preoperative NRS pain score was selected as the sorting criterion. Although pain research has reported diverse factors influencing hyper- and hypoalgesia [16–19], our prospective PRO survey in lung cancer surgery patients revealed that a significant proportion of patients experience preoperative pain [20, 21], much of which is chronic pain unrelated to lung cancer. Furthermore, we observed that postoperative pain score trajectories differed depending on the presence or absence of preoperative chronic pain (submitted to another journal). This finding aligns with observations from other studies [22, 23]. Based on our previous research, we stratified patients into groups with a preoperative NRS of 0 and those with a non-zero NRS.

The International Association for the Study of Pain released a revised definition of pain that emphasizes it as an always personal experience, influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors to varying degrees [24]. We hope that the findings from this study will serve as benchmark data to further improve pain management in VATS procedures.
