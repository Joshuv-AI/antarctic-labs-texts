Plasma phenylalanine is associated with hospital mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome: a prospective metabolic profiling cohort study
Authors: Li-Chung Chiu, Chi-Jen Lo, Chun-Ming Fan, Hsiang-Yu Tang, Tien-Ming Chan, How-Wen Ko, Scott Chih-Hsi Kuo, Ping-Chih Hsu, Chung-Shu Lee, Yu-Jr Lin, Kuo-Chin Kao, Li-Pang Chuang, Han-Chung Hu, Mei-Ling Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhenylalanine accumulation is associated with inflammation, immune system activation, and oxidative stress—all of which are involved in the pathophysiology of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). This study evaluated the correlation between longitudinal changes in plasma phenylalanine levels and clinical outcomes in patients with ARDS.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study conducted in Taiwan focused on plasma amino acid profiling in ARDS patients between February 2017 and June 2018, and again between November 2021 and October 2023. Serial changes in plasma amino acid levels and clinical variables measured at days 1, 3, and 7 after ARDS onset were compared with clinical outcomes.ResultsThis study included 214 ICU patients, including 180 patients with ARDS and 34 patients without. Among ARDS patients, the in-hospital mortality rate was 45%. Plasma phenylalanine levels were significantly higher among ARDS patients than among ICU controls at all timepoints (days 1, 3, and 7). Plasma phenylalanine levels and phenylalanine/tyrosine ratios were significantly higher among non-survivors than among ARDS survivors throughout the observation period (all p < 0.05). ARDS patients with high plasma phenylalanine levels (> 125.3 µM; 47 patients; 26.1%) presented a significantly elevated risk of organ failure (i.e., higher APACHE II and SOFA scores) as well as 28-, 60-, 90-day, and all-cause hospital mortality, compared to those with low plasma phenylalanine levels (≤ 125.3 µM; 133 patients; 73.9%) (all p < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that plasma phenylalanine levels and phenylalanine/tyrosine ratios at day 1 were independently associated with hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.009, [95% CI 1.001–1.017], p = 0.037 and adjusted OR 2.851, [95% CI 1.045–7.780], p = 0.041, respectively). A plasma phenylalanine level of > 125.3 µM had the highest predictive value for in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 4.825, [95% CI 1.324–17.583], p = 0.017).ConclusionsPlasma phenylalanine at the onset of ARDS is independently associated with hospital mortality. These findings indicate that phenylalanine metabolism may be disrupted early in the course of ARDS. They also indicate that plasma phenylalanine levels could serve as an early prognostic metabolic biomarker and potential therapeutic target.

Background

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) results from direct or indirect lung injury, with a high incidence of multiorgan failure and mortality. Its complex pathophysiology is characterized by acute hypoxic respiratory failure and oxidative stress [1, 2]. Currently, there is a lack of reliable biomarkers to predict disease progression, guide clinical decision-making, or personalize pharmacotherapy treatment.

Metabolomics provides an objective overview of an individual’s biological endotype and metabolic fingerprint. It is a powerful tool for elucidating complex cellular mechanisms and identifying potential biomarkers for diagnosis, prognostication, and therapeutic decision-making [3, 4]. Amino acid metabolism contributes to protein synthesis, energy production, gene regulation, mitochondrial homeostasis, and overall cellular function [5].

Phenylalanine is an amino acid that is essential to human health; however, its accumulation is associated with inflammation and immune system activation. Excess phenylalanine accumulation can lead to the formation of toxic metabolites—such as phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate and phenylacetate—which can impair the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), promote the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and disrupt antioxidant defense systems [6–9].

Phenylalanine is metabolized by phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) into tyrosine. A deficiency in PAH can lead to phenylalanine accumulation and an increased phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio. This ratio has been used as an indicator of PAH enzymatic activity and has been associated with immune activation, inflammation, and oxidative stress [10–12]. Therefore, the accumulation of phenylalanine, along with an elevated phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio, may be linked to oxidative stress and dysregulated inflammatory responses during ARDS.

An imbalance between endogenous ROS production and antioxidant defenses can cause oxidative stress and subsequent cellular damage [7, 13, 14]. In ARDS, dysregulated inflammatory cascades and immune responses lead to excessive ROS generation and oxidative stress, which can compromise endothelial and epithelial barriers and increase alveolar–capillary permeability [13, 15]. Assessing individual metabolic phenotypes may yield valuable prognostic markers, while targeting metabolic derangements represents a promising therapeutic avenue to advance the precision management of ARDS [16, 17].

Previous studies found that plasma phenylalanine levels were correlated with the severity and mortality of ARDS; however, they involved relatively small sample sizes, lacked ICU control patients, and did not measure longitudinal changes in clinical and metabolic profiles [18, 19]. The impact of plasma phenylalanine on the clinical outcomes of ARDS patients remains unclear. This study investigated the potential association between early phase phenylalanine metabolic derangement and clinical outcomes in patients with ARDS.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study conducted metabolic profiling of amino acids between February 2017 and June 2018 and again between November 2021 to October 2023 in a tertiary care referral center—Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMH), Linkou branch, Taiwan. All patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) were screened. ARDS was defined in accordance with the Berlin criteria [20]. ICU control patients not meeting the Berlin criteria for ARDS were also included. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) age < 20 years; (2) mortality within 7 days after ARDS onset or after ICU admission; and (3) inability to obtain informed consent from patients or their legal representative. At the time of signing the informed consent form, patients or their legal representatives were informed that blood samples would be collected following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. The feeding rate during the remaining hours was increased to compensate for the calorie deficit and to meet the daily caloric requirement. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of CGMH for Human Research (CGMH IRB No. 201407524B0, 201801052A3, 201801497B0, 202100595A3, 202201833A3, and 202300897A3).

Data collection

Demographic data, underlying comorbidities, and ARDS etiologies were recorded for all participants. Data were collected at days 1, 3, and 7 after ARDS onset (ARDS patients) or at days 1, 3, and 7 after ICU admission (control subjects). Collected variables included clinical and laboratory variables, plasma amino acid concentrations (metabolic profiling), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores. Additional data included the dates of hospital and ICU admissions, the presence of shock (defined as a state of tissue hypoperfusion and distributive, cardiogenic, hypovolemic, and obstructive shock were included), use of inotropic agents or renal replacement therapy, the onset of ARDS, mechanical ventilator initiation and liberation, ICU and hospital discharge dates, and time of death.

Sample collection and processing

For both ARDS patients and ICU control patients, plasma samples were obtained after fasting overnight for at least 8 h at days 1, 3, and 7 after ARDS onset (ARDS patients) or at days 1, 3, and 7 after ICU admission (control subjects). Whole blood (10 mL) was drawn under sterile conditions using EDTA-coated tubes. Within 2 h after collection, the samples underwent centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the plasma was transferred into a 15 mL polypropylene tube. The resulting plasma was transferred to 15 mL polypropylene tubes and centrifuged again under the same conditions to remove residual cell debris. The final plasma samples were aliquoted in an Eppendorf tube and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Analysis of amino acids in plasma

Amino acid levels were measured using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography. The methods used for sample collection, preparation, and metabolic profiling analysis are detailed in Supplementary File 1.

Outcome measurements

This study assessed the 28-day, 60-day, 90-day, and all-cause hospital mortality. Hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause during the index hospital stay. Patients who remained alive for 90 days following hospital discharge were classified as survivors. Additional recorded outcomes included the incidence of shock and acute kidney injury, the use of inotropic agents and renal replacement therapy, the duration of mechanical ventilation, and the lengths of ICU and hospital stays.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, or as median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using a Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test for equal proportions or Fisher’s exact test.

Correlations between two continuous variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic curves and the Youden index were used to identify optimal cutoff values for dichotomizing continuous variables. Univariable analysis was first performed to identify the risk factors associated with hospital mortality. Variables with statistical significance were then entered into multivariable logistic regression models using stepwise selection. Results are reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Cumulative probabilities of hospital survival in each group as a function of time were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY).

APACHE II and SOFA scores were significantly higher among non-survivors compared to survivors (both p < 0.05). The mean SOFA scores of non-survivors presented a stepwise increasing trend from day 1 to day 7, whereas survivors exhibited a stepwise decrease over the same period. Inflammatory and metabolic indicators—including white blood cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and lactate levels—were significantly elevated in non-survivors (all p < 0.05).

At day 1 after ARDS onset, the mean plasma phenylalanine level was significantly higher in non-survivors than in survivors (150 ± 117.6 vs. 109.2 ± 68.2 µM, p = 0.007). From day 1 to day 7, plasma phenylalanine levels presented a stepwise increase in non-survivors and a stepwise decrease in survivors, with all between-group differences reaching the level of significance. Similarly, phenylalanine/tyrosine ratios were significantly higher in non-survivors across all three timepoints (days 1, 3, and 7; all p < 0.05). Vasopressor use was significantly higher in non-survivors. Factors associated with hospital mortality

After adjusting for significant confounding variables, multivariable logistic regression models revealed a number of factors that were independently positively associated with hospital mortality. These included lower body mass index, higher neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio at day 1, higher plasma phenylalanine value at day 1, and higher plasma phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio at day 1.

ARDS patients with higher plasma phenylalanine levels or higher plasma phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio at day 1 had a significantly higher risk of hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.009, [95% CI 1.001–1.017], p = 0.037 and adjusted OR 2.851, [95% CI 1.045–7.780], p = 0.041, respectively). Among the studied variables, plasma phenylalanine levels at day 1 > 125.3 µM had the highest predictive value and was independently associated with hospital mortality (adjusted OR 4.825, [95% CI 1.324–17.583], p = 0.017) (Table 3). The 90-day survival rate was significantly higher in the low plasma phenylalanine group (≤ 125.3 µM) compared with the high plasma phenylalanine group (> 125.3 µM) (62.4% vs. 34%, p = 0.001, log-rank test) (Fig. 3). There were no significant differences between two groups in terms of age, gender, or body mass index. APACHE II scores at day 1 and SOFA scores at day 1, day 3, and day 7 were significantly higher in the high plasma phenylalanine group (all p < 0.05). The high plasma phenylalanine group presented lower hemoglobin values, lower platelet counts, and higher creatinine and lactate levels, with all differences reaching the level of significance (all p < 0.05). At all timepoints, plasma phenylalanine/tyrosine ratios were significantly higher in the high plasma phenylalanine group, compared to the low plasma phenylalanine group (all p < 0.05).

The 28-, 60-, 90-day, and all-cause hospital mortality rates were significantly higher in the high plasma phenylalanine group, compared to the low plasma phenylalanine group (all p < 0.001) (Table 5). The percentages of patients experiencing shock and those requiring inotropic support were significantly higher in the high plasma phenylalanine group (both p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of new-onset acute kidney injury, renal replacement therapy, the duration of mechanical ventilation, length of ICU stay, or length of hospital stay. Discussion

The key finding of this study is that early phase plasma phenylalanine levels and phenylamine-to-tyrosine ratio are correlated with hospital mortality, suggesting their potential utility as biomarkers for predicting disease progression and clinical outcomes in ARDS patients.

Phenylalanine is an essential amino acid derived from various dietary sources and from muscle catabolism during fasting. It is metabolized into tyrosine through the action of PAH. Mutations in the gene encoding PAH lead to PAH deficiency, which causes the autosomal recessive metabolic disorder phenylketonuria. Phenylalanine accumulation and a corresponding increase in the phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio is due primarily to PAH deficiency, which may be related to impaired hydroxylation and transamination induced by a reduction in the activity of the cofactors, such as tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH, reduced form of NAD+), oxygen or Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) [11, 21, 22] (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Schematic diagram of phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism

Schematic diagram of phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism

The phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio is regarded as a surrogate for PAH or BH4 bioavailability, and has been associated with immune activation and inflammation [10–12]. Neopterin, a molecule released by monocytes and macrophages in response to interferon-γ stimulation, has been identified as a sensitive biomarker for the activation of T helper 1 type cellular immunity [12, 23]. One previous study reported positive correlations among phenylalanine concentration, phenylalanine/tyrosine ratio, and neopterin concentration in patients with sepsis [12]. These findings further suggest that the accumulation of phenylalanine is associated with inflammation, immune system activation, and oxidative stress.

Experimental and cellular models have revealed that hyperphenylalaninemia can induce redox status impairment, induce mitochondrial dysfunction, elevate oxidative stress, and trigger inflammatory responses [6]. Elevated phenylalanine levels have been shown to suppress the activity of the intracellular endogenous antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase, a key intracellular antioxidant enzyme. Hyperphenylalaninemia also inhibits excretion of the lipid-soluble antioxidant coenzyme Q10, a lipid-soluble antioxidant and essential electron carrier between complexes I/II and III of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) during oxidative phosphorylation.

Collectively, these findings indicate that hyperphenylalaninemia may impair the activity of the mitochondrial ETC, contributing to the excess generation of ROS and oxidative stress [6–9]. Given that excessive ROS generation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction are central to the pathogenesis of acute lung injury [13, 15], it is plausible that hyperphenylalaninemia contributes to the development and progression of ARDS by exacerbating oxidative damage.

Previous studies have reported correlations between plasma phenylalanine and both ARDS severity and mortality; however, those findings are based on a small number of ARDS patients (37 and 42 subjects, respectively). Moreover, those studies used healthy patients as controls instead of ICU patients without ARDS and did not include serial measurements of plasma metabolic profiles during the early phase of the syndrome [18, 19].

In research on acute severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, severe perturbations in the metabolic pathways of amino acids have been associated with elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines [22, 24, 25]. Those studies reported that the plasma phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio was significantly higher among SARS-CoV-2 patients, compared to healthy controls. They also reported that plasma phenylalanine was independently positively correlated with disease severity and critical outcomes.

Our findings based on longitudinal assessments of clinical and metabolic variables during early phase ARDS revealed that plasma phenylalanine values were significantly higher in ARDS patients than in ICU control patients. Moreover, non-survivors of ARDS exhibited significantly elevated plasma phenylalanine levels and phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratios, relative to survivors. These findings indicate that phenylalanine tyrosine metabolism may be disrupted during acute inflammation—such as the early phase of ARDS and coronavirus disease 2019—and could potentially serve as a prognostic biomarker.

Oxidative stress activates multiple intracellular signaling pathways that can lead to mitochondrial damage, impaired mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) activity, and lipid peroxidation—disrupting membrane integrity and fluidity [7, 8, 14, 26]. These processes collectively contribute to cell death (e.g., apoptosis), tissue injury, and ultimately organ dysfunction. ARDS is characterized by a cascade of exaggerated, dysregulated inflammatory responses as well as immune reaction activation and oxidative stress, which may contribute to multiorgan failure—the leading cause of death among ARDS patients [1, 2, 13, 15].

A recent experimental study based on human and murine models demonstrated that the metabolic signature indicative of mitochondrial β-oxidation dysfunction precedes sepsis-induced organ failure and histological evidence of cellular apoptosis, suggesting that they could serve as an early biomarker of organ dysfunction [27]. Phenylalanine overproduction may disrupt immune regulation, exacerbate inflammation, and promote oxidative stress and microvascular endothelial damage, leading to organ injury [8, 19, 22, 28]. This indicates that phenylalanine accumulation during the early phase of ARDS could amplify the deleterious effects of oxidative stress, overwhelming dysregulated inflammatory and immune responses, eventually leading to multi-organ damage and/or death. Thus, phenylalanine levels would be regarded as an early indicator of impending organ dysfunction.

This study was subject to various limitations, which should be considered in interpreting our findings. First, this prospective cohort study was conducted at single tertiary-care referral center in Taiwan and lacked external validation, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, as in previous studies [10–12], we identified phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio as an indicator of PAH; however, we did not examine the activity of PAH or its cofactors, such as BH4, NADH, neopterin, and Vitamin V6 status, to verify their causal roles in ARDS pathophysiology. Third, our analysis did not extend to the activity of antioxidant enzymes—such as superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase—nor did it assess oxidative stress as a potential biomarker. Fourth, while endogenous muscle catabolism is a known contributor to hyperphenylalaninemia and occurs rapidly during critical illness [29], we did not measure markers of muscle breakdown, such as creatine kinase, myoglobin, or skeletal muscle metabolites, to determine their influence on phenylalanine levels. One previous study using multivariable analysis reported that muscular breakdown (indicated by creatine kinase) was not significantly associated with elevated phenylalanine levels, but still concluded that abnormal phenylalanine metabolism plays a crucial role in stress hyperphenylalaninemia [28].

Finally, as an observational study, our objective was to evaluate the correlations between plasma phenylalanine levels and clinical outcomes in ARDS. Thus, causality cannot be inferred. Further research is warranted to identify the precise molecular mechanism by which phenylalanine tyrosine metabolism contributes to the pathogenesis of ARDS.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrated that plasma phenylalanine concentration at ARDS onset is independently associated with the hospital mortality in ARDS patients. Further investigations are needed to confirm the causal role of phenylalanine metabolism in ARDS pathogenesis and to facilitate the development of early prognostic markers, as well as preventive or therapeutic strategies targeting phenylalanine metabolism to mitigate disease progression, morbidity, and mortality in ARDS.
