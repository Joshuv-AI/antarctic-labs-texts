Association of C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index and its modified indices with stroke risk in individuals at cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Dan Yang, Yan Deng, Zihan Su, Min Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke pathogenesis in Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) is driven by a complex interplay of insulin resistance (IR), systemic inflammation, and adiposity. This study investigated whether integrating adiposity-related measures with the inflammation-IR axis could refine stroke risk prediction in individuals at CKM stage 0–3.MethodsA total of 3,348 participants (aged ≥ 45) at CKM stages 0–3 were included from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Baseline levels, cumulative exposures, and longitudinal changes were assessed for the original C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) and seven adiposity-modified CTI indices. Associations with stroke risk were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, and potential non-linear relationships were examined using restricted cubic splines. Predictive performance at 3 and 5 years was assessed using fixed-horizon receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Discrimination was quantified by the area under the curve (AUC) and compared using DeLong’s test, while incremental predictive value was further evaluated using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index. Mediation analysis was used to evaluate the potential role of estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV) in these associations. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to test result robustness, and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression was performed to determine the relative contribution of components within CTI-related indices.ResultsDuring follow-up, 263 new cases of stroke were observed (7.9%). In fully adjusted models, participants in the highest tertile of all CTI-related indices had significantly increased stroke risk, with hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.92 (95%CI 1.35–2.73) for original CTI to 2.52 (95%CI 1.76–3.61) for CTI-WHtR. K-means clustering of longitudinal changes identified three trajectory groups; compared with the stable group, both markedly increasing and slowly increasing trajectories of CTI and its modified indices were significantly associated with higher stroke risk. In fixed-horizon ROC analyses, the 3-year AUCs for all indices exceeded 0.70. Among baseline indices, CTI-BRI, CTI-BMI, CTI-WC, and CTI-WHtR showed significantly higher AUCs than the original CTI, whereas only CTI-BMI demonstrated a small but significant improvement in IDI over CTI (IDI = 0.0021, 95% CI 0.0004–0.0038; P = 0.012). Among cumulative indices, cumCTI-BMI and cumCTI-WHtR showed significantly higher 3-year AUCs than cumCTI. CTIs-stroke associations were stronger in advanced CKM stages (2–3). Furthermore, component analysis identified CVAI (29.0% in 2012; 29.3% in 2015) as the primary driver of predictive power. ePWV partially mediated the associations between CTI-related indices and incident stroke, with mediation proportions of 12.44%–21.20%.ConclusionsCTI and adiposity-modified CTI indices were independently associated with incident stroke among adults at CKM stages 0–3, especially in advanced CKM stages. Incorporating adiposity-related information provided modest and inconsistent improvement in short-term prediction, with CVAI contributing most prominently and ePWV suggesting a potential arterial stiffness pathway. These findings support the relevance of combined inflammatory, IR, and adiposity burden in stroke risk assessment, but the clinical use of complex composite indices should be balanced against screening feasibility.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03282-1.

Introduction

Globally, stroke remains a leading cause of death and chronic disability, with China bearing the highest burden [1–3]. According to 2020 nationwide data from the Chinese population aged 40 years or older, the prevalence of stroke reached 2.6%, with an incidence and mortality rate of 505.2 and 343.4 per 100,000 person-years, respectively [4]. This escalating burden primarily attributed to an aging population and the increasing prevalence of key risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [5]. To better capture the complex pathophysiological processes contributing to stroke in this context, the American Heart Association (AHA) has proposed the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome in October, 2023 [6]. Evidence from numerous epidemiological studies indicates that as the CKM syndrome progresses from stage 0 to stage 3, the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) increases incrementally [7, 8], where stroke represents a particularly severe and frequent manifestation of CVD in this context [9]. In light of this, and given the AHA’s emphasis on early intervention during the preclinical stages (0–3), research focused on predicting new-onset stroke within this early CKM population is imperative [8].

The pathogenesis of stroke is critically driven by insulin resistance (IR) and chronic inflammation [6, 7, 10, 11]. IR predisposes to stroke by driving atherosclerosis via inflammatory pathways, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [12, 13]. Concurrently, chronic inflammation acts as a potent, independent risk factor by promoting atherogenesis, compromising vascular function, and facilitating thrombosis, thereby directly increasing the incidence of ischemic stroke [14, 15]. Considering this mechanistic interaction, the CRP-TyG index (C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index, CTI) was developed as a combined biomarker, integrating C-reactive protein (CRP, a non-specific marker of systemic inflammation) with the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG, a recognized surrogate of IR). Although Ruan et al. initially proposed the CTI for cancer survival prediction [16], it has garnered increasing attention in metabolic research, with applications extending to diabetes, CVD, and stroke [17–21]. In addition, a growing body of evidence suggests that indicators combining various obesity-related measures such as body mass index (BMI), body roundness index (BRI), waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), a body shape index (ABSI), weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), and Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), enhances the prediction of adverse health outcomes, including CVD [22–24], stroke [25–27], periodontitis [28], anxiety [29], and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [30]. However, this integrative approach has been predominantly confined to the TyG index [22–30]. It remains unknown whether the corresponding CTI-based composite indices (CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, CTI-WC, CTI-WHtR, CTI-ABSI, CTI-WWI, and CTI-CVAI) can improve stroke prediction, particularly in individuals at early CKM stages.

To address this knowledge gap, this study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), aiming to prospectively evaluate and compare the associations between these seven CTI-derived indices and incident stroke among adults in CKM stages 0–3. Clarifying their prognostic value could enhance early risk stratification and inform targeted preventive strategies for this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study population

The methodology of the CHARLS study has been documented previously [31]. In brief, CHARLS is a nationally representative prospective cohort that employed a multistage probability sampling strategy to recruit individuals aged 45 and older from 450 villages across 28 province in China [31]. Its original national baseline survey (Wave 1) was conducted from June 2011 to March 2012 and enrolled 17,708 participants. Follow-up surveys were subsequently collected in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5), and standardized fasting blood samples were collected in both Wave 1 and Wave 3.

The present study aimed to simultaneously investigate the associations of three dimensions of exposure (baseline levels, cumulative exposures, and longitudinal changes) of CTI and its modified indices with subsequent stroke incidence. Consistent with the approach adopted by Xiaoyan Wang et al. [32], we defined the window covering Wave 1 (2011–2012) to Wave 3 (2015) as the exposure baseline period of the current study, and ascertained stroke outcome events during the follow-up period after Wave 3 through Wave 4 and Wave 5. Eligible participants were required to be aged 45 or above with complete data on CRP, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), BMI, and WC at both Wave 1 and Wave 3. Individuals were excluded if they had a history of stroke or heart disease prior to the 2015 survey (Wave 3), were lost to follow-up, or lacked documented stroke outcomes during the follow-up period. Consequently, a final analytical cohort of 3,348 participants was included. A detailed flowchart of participant selection is provided in Supplemental Figure S1.

Determination of incident stroke

Participants were considered to have stroke if they answered “yes” to the following questions from a health status questionnaire: “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with a stroke?” The primary outcome was incident stroke after the exposure baseline period. Among participants without stroke before or at Wave 3, incident stroke was defined as the first self-reported physician-diagnosed stroke during subsequent follow-up surveys, namely Wave 4 or Wave 5. The onset time was estimated as the interval between the last stroke-free interview and the first interview at which stroke was reported [21, 32]. Participants without incident stroke were censored at their last available follow-up interview.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and clinical expertise due to their established associations with the exposures or outcomes [20, 21, 32–34]. These covariates comprised sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, clinical measures, and laboratory data. The sociodemographic factors included age (continuous, using the birth date and the date of baseline assessment), sex (male/ female), area of residence (city/ rural), marital status (married/ others), and educational attainment (no formal education [< 1 year], primary school [1–6 years], or above primary school [> 6 years]). The lifestyle factors covered current smoking and drinking status (yes/ no). Clinical measures encompassed systolic and diastolic blood pressure (continuous) and diabetes status (yes/ no), defined by elevated FPG (≥ 125 mg/dL), Glycated Hemoglobin (HBA1C ≥ 6.5%), self-reported diagnosis, or relevant medication use. Laboratory data included total cholesterol (TC, continuous), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, continuous), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, continuous). Definitions of CKM syndrome stages 0–3

The definition and staging of CKM syndrome were based on the framework provided in the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement [6]. The stages were operationalized as follows: Stage 0, no identifiable CKM syndrome risk factors; Stage 1, presence of abdominal obesity and/or prediabetes; Stage 2, presence of specific metabolic conditions (type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2DM], hypertension, and hypertriglyceridemia) or chronic kidney disease (CKD); Stage 3, presence of early cardiovascular disease in individuals with concomitant obesity, metabolic conditions, or CKD.

The detailed diagnostic procedures for the constituent (e.g., overweight, abdominal obesity, hypertension, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, CKD) and the Framingham risk score for CVD were adopted from the methodology described by Xiaoyan Wang et al. [32]. As participants classified as CKM stage 4 were excluded during the Wave 1 and 3 surveys, the present analysis was restricted to CKM stages 0–3.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality before comparative analyses. Normally distributed variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and compared using the one-way ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as the median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages, with between-group comparisons performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate based on expected frequencies.

To characterize different exposure patterns of CTI and its modified indices, three complementary grouping strategies were applied. First, baseline values at Wave 1 in 2012 were categorized into tertiles to evaluate the association between initial CTI levels and subsequent stroke risk. Second, cumulative exposures from Waves 1 to 3, spanning 2012 to 2015, were categorized into tertiles to capture the effect of longer-term exposure burden. Third, K-means clustering was used to classify participants according to longitudinal trajectories of CTI and its modified indices across Waves 1–3. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method as shown in Supplemental Figure S2. Cluster-specific trajectories and baseline characteristics are presented in Supplemental Figure S3 and Supplemental Table S1. In subsequent analyses, participants in the lowest tertile and those in the stable trajectory class (Class 3) were used as the reference groups, respectively.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the associations of the CTI and its modified indices with incident stroke in middle-aged and older adults at CKM stages 0–3. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using three models. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, area of residence, marital status, educational attainment, smoking status, and drinking status. Model 3 was further adjusted for diabetes, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), TC, LDL-C, and eGFR. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Specifically, to account for multiple comparisons in Model 3, we applied a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Collinearity diagnostics based on Model 3 were performed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the results are presented in Supplemental Table S2. Furthermore, we assessed the nature of these associations by testing for linear trends and potential nonlinearity. Linear trends were evaluated by modeling the median value of each exposure tertile as a continuous variable. Potential nonlinear associations were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models under the fully adjusted Model 3 framework. The splines were defined by four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles, with the 5th percentile as the reference.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the modifying influence of covariates on the associations of interest. The fully adjusted Model 3 specification was reapplied within strata defined by a range of demographic and clinical factors, namely age, sex, residence, marital status, education, smoking, drinking, and histories of hypertension, diabetes, and CKD. Specifically, in each subgroup analysis, the covariate set was adjusted as appropriate by excluding the corresponding stratification variable from the model. The significance of these interactions was determined by incorporating interaction terms into the models and applying likelihood ratio tests. Additionally, the cohort was dichotomized based on CKM staging into early (stages 0–1) and advanced (stages 2–3) groups. This clinical grouping allowed for a comparison of the effects of CTI and its modified indices on stroke risk across distinct phases of cardiometabolic health.

To evaluate the clinical predictive performance of the different CTI variants for incident stroke, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed at two clinically relevant fixed time points (3-year and 5-year) based on predicted risk scores derived from Cox regression models under the Model 3, and the corresponding area under the curve (AUCs) were quantified. To determine whether the modified CTIs offer superior predictive performance compared with the original CTI, pairwise comparisons of AUCs were performed using DeLong’s test, and incremental predictive performance was further assessed using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index. The relative predictive weights of the components (CRP, FPG, TG, and obesity-related anthropometric measures) were evaluated using weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model. A total of 1,000 bootstrap resamples were used to obtain stable estimates. The WQS model generated normalized weights ranging from 0 to 1, with larger weights indicating greater relative contribution of each component to stroke prediction within the composite index [32].

Given the established association between arterial stiffness and CVD [36–38], we postulated that estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV) mediates the CTIs-stroke association. This hypothesis was examined through mediation analysis within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework [39], which enabled the decomposition of the total effect of CTIs on stroke into its direct and indirect components. Finally, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the findings. These included: 1) propensity score matching using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching a caliper of 0.2 to reduce measured confounding, with covariate balance assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD) and defined as acceptable when SMD < 0.10; 2) inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on Model 3 covariates, followed by weighted Cox proportional hazards regression in the resulting cohort; 3) estimation of the E-value according to the method proposed by Vander Weele and Ding [40] to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding on the observed associations; and 4) z-score standardization of the original and modified CTI indices, followed by re-estimation under the fully adjusted model to examine whether the results were robust to differences in index scaling.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.3. The major R packages used included “survival”, “cluster”, “factoextra”, “splines”, “cowplot”, “timeROC”, “riskRegression”, “nricens”, “pROC”, “forestploter”, “lavaan”, “MatchIt”, “WeightIt”, “survey”, “cobalt”, and “ggplot2”, among others. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

The final analytical cohort comprised 3,348 participants (mean age 58.0 years; 54.9% female). Supplemental Table S3 summarizes the baseline distributions of the CTI and its modified indices, including their cumulative tertile ranges. Notably, when the cohort was stratified by CTI tertiles, clear gradients in baseline characteristics emerged (Supplemental Table S4). Compared to the lowest tertile, participants in higher CTI tertiles had a greater proportion of females, urban residents, and non-drinkers; a higher burden of hypertension, and diabetes; and a more adverse cardiometabolic profile, characterized by significantly elevated SBP, DBP, weight, WC, TG, FPG, CRP, and HB1AC levels, alongside lower HDL-C and eGFR levels (Supplemental Table S4). Consistent with this pattern, a similar gradient of metabolic risk factors was observed across CKM stages (Supplemental Table S5).

Associations of CTI and its modified indices with stroke risk among individuals at CKM stages 0–3

During the follow-up, the cumulative incidence of stroke in the cohort was 7.9% (263 events among 3,348 participants). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses showed substantial heterogeneity in both the direction and strength of the associations between multiple CTIs and incident stroke (Table 1, and Supplemental Tables S6-S7). After full adjustment for potential confounders, participants in the highest tertile of baseline CTI, CTI-BRI, CTI-BMI, CTI-WC, CTI-WHtR, CTI-ABSI, CTI-WWI, and CTI-CVAI showed a significantly higher risk of incident stroke during follow-up compared with those in the lowest tertiles (P < 0.001; Table 1). Specifically, the increased risks were as follows: 92% for CTI (HR: 1.92; 95% CI 1.35–2.73, P < 0.001), 130% for CTI-BRI (HR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.61–3.30, P < 0.001), 116% for CTI-BMI (HR: 2.16; 95% CI 1.53–3.06, P < 0.001), 126% for CTI-WC (HR: 2.26; 95% CI 1.60–3.21, P < 0.001), 152% for CTI-WHtR (HR: 2.52; 95% CI 1.76–3.61, P < 0.001), 107% for CTI-ABSI (HR: 2.07; 95% CI 1.46–2.94, P < 0.001), 107% for CTI-WWI (HR: 2.07; 95% CI 1.47–2.93, P < 0.001), and 109% for CTI-CVAI (HR: 2.09; 95% CI 1.47–2.97, P < 0.001). Consistent with the baseline findings, cumulative exposure to CTI and its modified indices showed robust positive associations with incident stroke risk. In the fully adjusted Cox models, both the second and third tertiles of cumulative CTI-related indices were significantly associated with higher stroke risk compared with the lowest tertile, with the highest tertile yielding HRs ranging from 2.06 for cumCTI (95% CI 1.44–2.94) to 2.47 for cumCTI-CVAI (95% CI 1.74–3.52) (Supplemental Table S6). Moreover, in the longitudinal trajectory analyses (Supplemental Table S7), Class 3, characterized by low baseline CTI-related levels that remained relatively stable over time, was used as the reference group. In the fully adjusted models, participants in Class 1, who had relatively high baseline CTI-related levels followed by a gradual increase, showed consistently higher risks of incident stroke than those in Class 3 across all CTI-related trajectory indicators, with HRs ranging from 2.01 for CTI (95% CI 1.38–2.95, P < 0.001) and CTI-BRI (95% CI 1.40–2.88, P < 0.001) to 2.74 for CTI-WWI (95% CI 1.85–4.05, P < 0.001). Participants in Class 2, characterized by intermediate baseline CTI-related levels followed by a marked increase over time, also had significantly elevated risks of incident stroke compared with Class 3, with fully adjusted HRs ranging from 1.39 for CTI-BRI (95% CI 1.02–1.88, P < 0.001) to 2.11 for CTI-BMI (95% CI 1.53–2.90, P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S7). These findings suggest that individuals with higher CTI-related levels, whether persistently high with gradual increases or increasing markedly from intermediate levels, had a substantially increased risk of incident stroke compared with those maintaining low and stable CTI-related levels. After FDR correction, the associations for the highest tertile groups remained statistically significant across all CTI-related indicators, and the elevated stroke risks observed for both trajectory patterns were largely retained (Table 1, and Supplemental Tables S6-S7). Overall, these findings support the robustness of the observed associations after accounting for multiple comparisons, although a few associations in intermediate-risk categories were attenuated and Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the associations between CTIs and stroke risk were generally consistent in direction and magnitude across most predefined strata. In CKM stage-stratified analyses, the associations were generally weak or non-significant among participants with early CKM stages 0–1 (Supplemental Tables S8-S10), suggesting limited predictive value of the original CTI in this lower-risk subgroup. However, several modified CTI indices, particularly CTI-BRI, CTI-BMI, CTI-WHtR, and some cumulative or trajectory-based modified indices, still showed significant associations with stroke risk (Supplemental Tables S8-S10). In contrast, among participants with advanced CKM stages 2–3, the associations between CTI-related indices and stroke were stronger and more consistent across baseline, cumulative, and trajectory-based analyses (Supplemental Tables S11-S13). Stratification by age, sex, area of residence, marital status, educational level, smoking and drinking status, and history of hypertension, diabetes, or CKD revealed distinct interaction patterns for baseline and cumulative CTIs (Fig. 2, and Supplemental Figures S4-S10). Overall, most interaction tests were not statistically significant, indicating broadly consistent associations across subgroups. For baseline CTI-related indices, only CTI-BMI exhibited a significant interaction with hypertension history (P for interaction = 0.021, Fig. 2). Among participants without hypertension, both Tertile 2 and Tertile 3 of baseline CTI-BMI were associated with increased risks of incident stroke compared with Tertile 1, with HRs of 2.43 (95% CI 1.48–3.99, P < 0.001) and 2.24 (95% CI 1.21–4.16, P = 0.011), respectively (Fig. 2). Among participants with hypertension, the association was significant for Tertile 3, with an HR of 1.91 (95% CI 1.24–2.95, P = 0.003), whereas Tertile 2 was not significantly associated with stroke risk (HR: 1.13, 95% CI 0.71–1.80, P = 0.620) (Fig. 2). For cumulative CTIs, two significant interactions were observed: cumCTI-BMI interacted with marital status (P for interaction = 0.024, Fig. 2), showing a stronger association in individuals among married participants; and cumCTI-WWI interacted with educational level (P for interaction = 0.047, Supplemental Figure S9), demonstrating a more pronounced effect in those with educational attainment.Fig. 2Subgroup analysis of the association between cumulative and baseline CTI-BMI and stroke risk in individuals with CKM stages 0-3 in model 3. Sensitivity analyses employing both PSM and IPTW yielded results consistent with the primary Cox regression analysis, reinforcing the robustness of our findings against potential biases and confounding (Supplemental Tables S14-S16). The quality of matching and weighting was supported by SMDs, with 87.5%–100.0% of covariates achieving SMDs less than 0.1 after PSM and 93.8%–100.0% after IPTW, indicating well-balanced comparison groups. To further quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for all models. All E-values exceeded 1, suggesting that a considerable unmeasured confounder would be needed to explain away the observed associations. For instance, for the Tertile 3 group of baseline CTI, the observed HR was 1.92 with an E-value of 3.25 in the original analysis; the corresponding estimates remained similar after PSM (HR: 1.91, 95% CI 1.29–2.84; E-value: 3.23) and IPTW (HR: 2.17, 95% CI 1.44–3.27; E-value: 3.76) (Supplemental Table S14). This indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both baseline CTI and incident stroke by at least 3.25-fold each, above and beyond the measured covariates, to fully nullify the observed association in the original analysis. Similarly, the associations for cumulative CTI-related indices and CTI-related trajectory classes were largely preserved after PSM and IPTW, with E-values generally indicating moderate-to-strong robustness to unmeasured confounding (Supplemental Tables S15-S16).

In addition, when the original and modified CTI indices were standardized as z-scores, their associations with incident stroke remained robust and generally consistent (Table 2 and Supplemental Table S17). In the fully adjusted model, each 1-SD increase in baseline CTI-related indices was associated with a 33%–41% higher risk of stroke, with HRs ranging from 1.33 (95%CI 1.18–1.51, P < 0.001) for CTI-BRI to 1.41 (1.24–1.61, P < 0.001) for CTI-WC and to 1.41 (1.23–1.61, P < 0.001) for CTI-WHtR (Table 2). Similarly, each 1-SD increase in cumulative CTI-related indices was associated with a 35%–41% higher risk of stroke (Supplemental Table S17). All associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction under the fully adjusted model. Comparative predictive performance of CTI and its modified indices

We evaluated the performance and interrelationships of various CTIs in predicting stroke risk using ROC curve analysis, Delong’s test, and IDI analysis (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Figures S11-S17). In fixed-horizon ROC analyses, all CTI-related indices showed acceptable discriminatory performance for incident stroke at 3 years, with AUCs exceeding 0.70 (Fig. 3A). For baseline indices, the original CTI had a 3-year AUC of 0.703 (95% CI 0.637–0.783, Fig. 3A). Discussion

This study represents the first effort to systematically modify the CTI and compare the predictive value of these novel indices for stroke in adults with CKM stages 0–3. We found that all modified CTIs (including baseline, cumulative, even the changes) were significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke, even after adjustment for demographic, clinical, and metabolic confounders. These associations were robust across sensitivity and subgroup analyses and were not materially affected by differences in index scaling after z-score standardization. Notably, fixed-horizon ROC analyses indicated that CTI and its modified indices had acceptable short-term discriminatory performance, particularly at the 3-year horizon; however, the incremental improvement provided by adiposity modification was modest and inconsistent, especially when assessed by IDI and at the 5-year horizon. A linear dose–response pattern was observed for all baseline and cumulative CTIs, indicating graded increases in stroke risk without an apparent threshold effect. Mediation analyses further revealed that ePWV, a marker of arterial stiffness, partially mediated the associations between CTIs and stroke, supporting the potential role of vascular stiffness in the pathway linking metabolic dysregulation to stroke. Finally, WQS regression identified CVAI as the most influential component in the obesity-related stroke risk model in both 2012 and 2015, highlighting its sustained relevance over time. Together, these findings suggest that CTI-related indices may serve as potential biomarkers for stratifying stroke risk in individuals with CKM stages 0–3, while their incremental clinical utility requires cautious interpretation.

The CTI was initially proposed as a novel biomarker that integrates both IR and systemic inflammation [16], and has subsequently been associated with multiple metabolic and vascular outcomes [21, 41–45]. The biological plausibility of its association with stroke is supported by the reciprocal interaction between IR and inflammation. IR promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and atherogenesis, whereas chronic inflammation accelerates vascular injury, plaque instability, and thrombosis [46–49]. These processes may be further amplified by coexisting cardiometabolic conditions, making CTI a potentially integrative marker of multifaceted vascular injury rather than a reflection of a single metabolic abnormality [50–53]. In line with the AHA CKM framework, which emphasizes early identification and intervention before overt cardiovascular disease develops [6], our findings support the relevance of inflammation–IR burden for stroke risk assessment within CKM stages 0–3.

Although previous studies have reported associations between CTI and stroke in selected high-risk populations, such as those with hypertension or dysglycemia [18, 21, 41, 54], evidence across the broader CKM spectrum has remained limited. Moreover, most prior studies relied on single-timepoint CTI measurements [55], which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of metabolic risk. By evaluating baseline level, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal change simultaneously, our study provides a more comprehensive assessment of CTI-related burden over time. This approach is consistent with evidence from related indices, such as TyG, suggesting that temporal changes may better reflect cardiometabolic disease progression than isolated measurements [56].

The rationale for constructing adiposity-modified CTI indices deserves careful interpretation. It is well known that the TyG index is a core component of CTI and has been widely modified by incorporating anthropometric or adiposity-related measures, including BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, ABSI, WWI, and CVAI, to improve risk stratification for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and other metabolic outcomes [22–30]. Because CTI extends TyG by incorporating CRP, it is biologically plausible that adding adiposity information may further capture the combined burden of IR, inflammation, and obesity-related metabolic dysfunction. Importantly, the associations remained generally consistent after z-score standardization, suggesting that the observed relationships were not simply driven by differences in numerical scale among component variables. In addition, the linear dose–response patterns observed in RCS analyses indicate that stroke risk increased progressively with higher CTI-related burden, without evidence of a clear threshold. These findings underscore the potential value of early and sustained management of inflammation-IR-adiposity burden in CKM populations.

Despite the significant associations observed across CTI-derived indices, their predictive gain over the original CTI was limited. Our results should not be interpreted as demonstrating strong clinical superiority of modified CTI indices as stand-alone predictive tools. Rather, these indices appear to be associative risk markers that may provide modest short-term discrimination in selected contexts. This distinction is important because statistical significance does not necessarily translate into meaningful clinical utility. One possible explanation is that the original CTI already captures a substantial portion of the inflammation–IR signal relevant to stroke, leaving limited additional predictive information to be gained by simply incorporating adiposity-related measures. Future research should evaluate whether these indices improve prediction beyond established clinical risk scores and whether they meaningfully reclassify individuals into actionable risk categories.

Given that CTI and its modified parameters are all composite indices, it is essential to clarify the relative contribution of each component. Among the adiposity-related components examined, CVAI consistently showed the highest relative weight among adiposity-related indicators in both 2012 and 2015. This finding aligns with existing literature documenting CVAI as a well-validated measure of visceral adiposity in Chinese populations [26, 27, 57] and a reliable predictor of cardiometabolic risk [57–59]. Compared with single anthropometric measures, CVAI integrates age, sex, BMI, WC, TG, and HDL-C, thereby capturing both body fat distribution and lipid metabolic status [57]. Since the original CTI mainly reflects inflammation, glycemia, and triglyceride-related IR, the incorporation of CVAI may add complementary information related to visceral adiposity and lipid metabolism. Nevertheless, although CTI-CVAI may provide a broader pathophysiological profile, its clinical application should be balanced against the added complexity of calculating composite indices.

Having established the association between CTI indices and stroke risk, we further explored potential mediating mechanisms. In this study, ePWV was assessed at Wave 3, after baseline CTI measurement and before stroke ascertainment during Waves 4–5, providing a clearer temporal sequence than a purely cross-sectional mediation design. While previous investigations have confirmed the mediating role of ePWV in the relationship between AIP and stroke [32], our study extends this evidence by showing that ePWV also partially mediates the association between CTI-related indices and stroke risk. Although the mediation effects were modest, they persisted after comprehensive covariate adjustment, supporting arterial stiffness as a potential pathway linking cardiometabolic dysfunction to cerebrovascular pathology. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as ePWV was estimated from age and blood pressure rather than directly measured by tonometry or vascular imaging.

While this study provides valuable insights into CTIs-based stroke prediction, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, stroke outcomes were identified through self-reported diagnoses rather than adjudicated medical records, neuroimaging, hospitalization data, or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. Therefore, misclassification is possible, including the potential inclusion of participants with undiagnosed or underreported prevalent mild stroke or transient ischemic attack at baseline. Such misclassification may have influenced the observed associations, particularly short-term associations. However, this method has been widely used and validated in multiple large-scale epidemiological studies [60, 61], including previous research based on the CHARLS database [60]. In addition, the CHARLS questionnaire did not allow reliable differentiation among ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and transient ischemic attack, so our findings should be interpreted as applying to overall self-reported stroke rather than specific stroke subtypes. Second, although we have adjusted for a comprehensive set of potential confounders in our analysis, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual confounding (such as atrial fibrillation, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulant use, statin therapy, antihypertensive treatment intensity, physical activity, and dietary factors) due to unmeasured factors. Nevertheless, our sensitivity analyses using E-values demonstrated that the observed associations would remain robust even in the presence of potential unmeasured confounders. Third, our study defined CKM stages solely based on baseline assessments without accounting for potential stage transitions during follow-up. However, this approach aligns with our primary objective of evaluating the predictive capacity of CTI and its modified indices, for which we specially conducted trajectory analyses. Fourth, our study population was drawn from the CHARLS database, which primarily consists of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. This specific demographic composition may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Future studies should validate our results in large multinational cohorts encompassing diverse age groups and ethnic backgrounds. Fifth, as an observational study, our research cannot establish causal relationships between CTI measures and stroke risk. Future intervention studies are needed to determine whether reducing levels of CTI and its modified indices can effectively prevent stroke occurrence. Despite these limitations, this study provides a more complete understanding of the complex relationship between cardiometabolic risk factors and stroke, offering valuable insights for both research and clinical practice.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes that each of the seven modified CTI indices independently predicts new-onset stroke in adults with CKM stages 0–3, particularly in those with advanced CKM stages, and these associations were not materially affected by differences in index scaling after z-score standardization. Incorporating adiposity-related information into CTI provided modest improvement in short-term prediction, with BMI- and WHtR-modified indices showing relatively better 3-year performance. Component analysis further identified CVAI as the most prominent contributor, while mediation analysis suggested ePWV as a potential arterial stiffness-related pathway linking CTI-related burden to stroke risk. Overall, these findings support the relevance of combined inflammatory, IR, and adiposity burden in stroke risk assessment, but the clinical application of complex composite indices should be balanced against their incremental predictive value and screening feasibility.
