β-galactosidase-targeted senolytic prodrug ameliorates preclinical models of post-traumatic osteoarthritis
Authors: Qi Li, Tong Wu, Yifei Fan, Jin Cheng, Junyan Wang, Boyang Xu, Mengze Sun, Xi Gong, Hongkui Deng, Yingfang Ao, Xiaoqing Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCellular senescence is involved in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA). This study aimed to identify prodrug SSK1 as a senolytic strategy for alleviating OA.MethodsAn oxidative stress-induced cellular senescence model was established to evaluate cell viability, replication, and genotoxicity after SSK1 treatment. Human OA chondrocytes and explants were collected to evaluate the therapeutic effect of prodrug SSK1 in vitro. In vivo evaluation was performed in young and aged male murine models. SSK1 (intra-articular injection every 3 days) was administrated 2 weeks after anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) surgery. Animals were sacrificed 8 weeks after surgery. OA phenotype was analysed by micro-computerised tomography (μCT), histology and pain-related behaviour tests.FindingsSSK1 showed precise, efficient, and broad-spectrum elimination of senescent chondrocytes. When co-cultured with human osteoarthritic chondrocytes and cartilage explants, the senolytic SSK1 prevented the generation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors, enhanced production of extracellular matrix (ECM) molecules, and promoted a regenerative chondral environment. Intra-articular administration of SSK1 showed improved pain response, enhanced retention of ECM, and remodelled subchondral bone homeostasis in both young and aged ACLT-induced OA murine model.InterpretationSSK1 is an effective candidate for senolytics in alleviating OA. The anti-ageing therapeutic effect of SSK1 lies in restoring a regenerative phenotype by improving the proliferation microenvironment, and reducing the accumulation of apoptotic signals in the joint microenvironment.FundingFunded by the Regional Innovation Joint Fund of the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Integrated Project) (U23A6009), the National Natural Science Foundation of China (82202687, 82172420, and 82072486), the Beijing Municipal Natural Science Foundation (7222213), and the Capital's Funds for Health Improvement and Research (2022-4-20511), and Beijing Tongren Hospital Seed Funds (2021-YJJ-ZZL-008).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Cellular senescence plays an important role in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA). Elimination of senescent chondrocytes by senolytic small molecule compounds show therapeutic effects in OA mice. However, results from a recent phase II clinical trial in the treatment of patients with painful knee OA were not optimistic. Hence, the development of new senolytics with different mechanisms for OA anti-ageing therapy is appealing. SSK1, a prodrug that consists of gemcitabine modified with an acetyl galactose moiety, could target senescence-associated β-galactosidase and eliminate senescent fibroblasts. SSK1 improves physical function and lifespan in aged mice and demonstrates good anti-inflammatory effect in non-human primates. The therapeutic action of SSK1 in OA disease deserves comprehensive investigation.

Added value of this study

Prodrug SSK1 has precise, efficient, and broad-spectrum elimination of senescent chondrocytes. SSK1 reduces the senescence-associated secretory phenotype in human OA chondrocytes and explants. Intra-articular injection of SSK1 significantly alleviates OA development and progression in an anterior cruciate ligament transection-induced animal model. The anti-ageing therapeutic effect of SSK1 lies in restoring a regenerative phenotype by improving the proliferation microenvironment and reducing the accumulation of apoptotic signals in the joint microenvironment.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study suggests that SSK1 may have value as a clinical treatment strategy for patients with OA. Prodrug-based senotherapy represents a significant conceptual advance to manage OA and other ageing diseases.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disorder in humans, affecting an estimated 250 million people worldwide.1 With an increasing ageing population, over 9.6% of men and 18.0% of women will develop symptomatic evidence of OA by age 60.2 As a major public health challenge, the disorder is increasingly influencing the personal quality of life of patients, while also placing a substantial financial burden in most countries.3 Clinically, common strategies for OA treatment, including lifestyle management, physical therapy, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, and hyaluronic acid, only palliate the symptoms, instead of decelerating or reversing the pathological process of OA. Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore new therapeutics that can alleviate or reverse OA progression.4

As a degenerative joint disorder, OA has an aetiology of ageing, trauma, obesity, strain, abnormalities, and deformities.5 Among them, the pathogenic effect of cellular senescence has emerged as one of the main hallmarks of OA.6 Cellular senescence is a stress-induced, durable cell-cycle arrest of previously replication-competent cells.7 Accompanied by increased expression of cell cycle inhibitors p16 and p21, senescent cells are characterised by the production of pro-inflammatory factors, namely the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).8 These SASP components accumulated in the chondral microenvironment not only directly promote matrix catabolism in cartilage but also trigger a chronic inflammatory response, further aggravating OA disease progression.9 Therefore, targeting senescence is emerging as an attractive therapeutic strategy to delay or reverse OA development.10 For instance, eliminating senescent cells using senolytic UBX0101, could upregulate the synthesis of cartilage-related extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and provide a regenerative microenvironment in post-traumatic OA mice.6 Similarly, administration of quercetin and fenofibrate, previously reported senolytics, also showed reduced proteoglycan loss in OA rats or interleukin 1β (IL-1β)-treated human cartilage explants, respectively.11,12 Additionally, targeting cellular senescence by stabilising heterochromatin also effectively attenuates OA development in a post-traumatic mouse model.13 These studies demonstrated that, at least at the preclinical level, removing of senescent chondrocytes resident in articular cartilage or preventing cellular senescence provides an attractive paradigm to treat OA disease. However, a Phase II clinical trial of UBX0101 in the treatment of patients with painful knee OA was revealed as not optimistic,14 indicating that preventive senolysis has some issues that should be addressed from a clinical perspective. In fact, the translation of current senolytic compounds to human diseases is hampered by their limited specificity in senescent cells, narrow therapeutic windows, and potential toxicity in healthy cells.15 Accordingly, developing new senolytics that solve clinical translational application-related issues in OA anti-ageing therapy is urgently required.

Senescent cells often exhibit accumulated lysosomal β-galactosidase expression, exploited as senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal).16 Recently, we developed a prodrug, SSK1, which consists of gemcitabine modified with an acetyl galactose moiety and a nontoxic aromatic spacer.17 The compound can selectively eliminate senescent cells broadly, halt chronic and systemic inflammation, and improve physical function and lifespan in aged mice.17 Furthermore, SSK1 demonstrated a good anti-inflammatory effect in non-human primates infected with SARS-CoV-2.18 Based on these results, we hypothesised that SSK1 could be an encouraging senolytic candidate for OA treatment. However, the role of SSK1 in senescent chondrocytes and cartilage-related ECM synthesis regulation during the development and progression of OA remains unknown.

In this study, we identified the selective clearance capacity of SSK1 for replication, oxidative stress, and toxic drug-induced senescent chondrocytes from mice, rats, and humans. Considering its translational potential, we investigated the regulatory effectiveness of SSK1 in chondrocytes derived from patient with OA in vitro and cartilage explants ex vivo. Furthermore, we used an anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) surgical in vivo model, which resembles the clinical progression of post-traumatic OA, to test the therapeutic strategy of SSK1 in attenuating cartilage degeneration and remodelling of the subchondral bone in young and naturally aged murine.

Methods

Ethics

Human specimens were collected from patients at Peking University Third Hospital with the approval of the Human Ethics Committee (Nos. 2013003 and M2020311). Full written informed consent was provided by all participants before operative procedure. Animal study was approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (No. LA2020353) and in compliance with animal use guidelines (ARRIVE).

Cell isolation and culture

The primary chondrocytes from mice, rats, and humans were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (LG-DMEM; HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, HyClone). The MSCs from rats, and humans were cultured in minimum essential medium alpha (MEM-α; HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Primary normal fibroblasts (NFs) from skin were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (HG-DMEM; HyClone) with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

For mouse and rat primary chondrocyte isolation, the cartilage fragments were obtained from femoral condyles. Human primary normal chondrocytes or OA chondrocytes were isolated from intact area or damaged areas (Supplementary Figure S1) in femoral condyles of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) patients, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The inclusion criteria include: (1) Male and female patients age 55–75 years; (2) diagnosed with OA and is planned for TKA. The exclusion criteria include: (1) Presence of rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, gout or infection; (2) Prior cartilage, meniscus, or ligament-related surgery at the same knee; (3) Corticosteroid or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs usage in the 3 months prior to enrolment; (4) Presence of uncontrolled systemic disease; (5) Presence of psychological illness in the 12 months prior to enrolment; (6) Pregnant women. Chondrocytes were isolated as we previously described.19 Briefly, the cartilage fragments were minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin (Invitrogen) for 30 min, and then with 0.2% type II collagenase (Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 4–6 h with continuous shaking. Chondrocytes were resuspended in LG-DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S.

For MSCs isolation, inguinal adipose tissues from rat or infrapatellar fat pad from patients underwent knee arthroscopy were harvested according to the previous description.20 Briefly, adipose tissue was finely minced and digested in MEM-α containing 0.1% type I collagenase (Invitrogen) for 2 h at 37 °C with continuous shaking. After centrifugation, the lipid layer was removed, and the cells were resuspended and cultured in MEM-α supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Human primary normal skin fibroblasts were kindly provided by Prof. Zelian Qin and isolated as previously described.21

Cellular senescence induction

To avoid replication-induced senescent cells, low-passage proliferative chondrocytes (<2 passages), MSCs (<4 passages), and fibroblasts (<4 passages).

For replication-induced senescence, cells were passaged until they lost the ability of proliferation and became senescent. Chondrocytes were used as senescent cells after 8 passages. MSCs and human fibroblasts were senescent after approximately 10 passages.

For peroxide hydrogen (H2O2)-induced senescence, cells were treated with 200 μM H2O2 for 4 h and cultured in fresh medium for 2 d before further analysis.

For etoposide-induced senescence, cells were incubated in a medium with 2 μM etoposide (E1383, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 12–18 h. After 3 days of treatment, cells were subcultured at a 1:3 ratio for 3 d in a fresh medium.

Cell viability assay

To evaluate the viability of SSK1-treated cells, the PrestoBlue HS Cell Viability Kit (P50201, Invitrogen, USA) was used. Non-senescent and senescent cells including chondrocytes, MSCs, and fibroblasts, as well as human OA chondrocytes were seeded on 96-well plates and treated with indicated concentration of SSK1. After 3 d of incubation with the small molecules, the cell culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed once with PBS. Then, cells were incubated with the cell culture medium containing 10% PrestoBlue reagent at 37 °C for 30 min. The fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission of 590 nm was detected using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The background signal was determined using the negative control of the same culture medium with 10% PrestoBlue reagent alone without cells.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was collected with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and isolated using a Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Then, RNA was converted to cDNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with DNase I (Invitrogen). qRT-PCR was performed with the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology, China) using the ABI QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The fold changes of target genes were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method, and β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene for normalisation. Primer information is detailed in the Supplementary Table S2.

Immunofluorescence

After the indicated treatment, cells were rinsed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15min at room temperature. Then, 10% goat serum was added for 1 h to block nonspecific binding sites. Next, cells were incubated against anti-COL II antibody (ab34712, abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:200), anti-cleaved-caspase3 antibody (ab2302, Abcam; 1:200), anti-p16 antibody (ab54210, Abcam; 1:200), anti-p21 antibody (ab109520, Abcam; 1:200), or anti-γ-H2AX antibody (C2035S, Beyotime; 1:200) overnight at 4 °C. Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488; ab150077, Abcam; 1:500) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647; ab150115, Abcam; 1:500) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min, followed by PBS rinse. Cells were observed under a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany). Key resource information is detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Culture of human OA cartilage explants

Human OA cartilage explants were harvested from damaged femoral condyles of total knee arthroplasty patients as described before. Briefly, the cartilage explants were cut into pieces of approximately 1 mm3 in volume and dispended into LG-DMEM containing 10% FBS supplemented with DMSO (vehicle) or SSK1 (0.5 or 5 μM), which was changed every 3 d. After 1 or 2 weeks of incubation, cartilage explants were collected for histological assays.

SA-β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining of cultured cells and frozen sections

The SA-β-gal activity was detected using an SA-β-gal staining kit (C0602, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, cells or cartilage tissue were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed in SA-β-gal staining fix solution for 15 min at room temperature. After being incubated with Triton X-100 for 1 h and washed 3 times with PBS, the prepared samples were incubated with SA-β-gal staining solution for 16–20 h at 37 °C. Samples were then washed with PBS and observed under a bright field microscope.

Animals

Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats and C57BL/6 (C57) mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co, Ltd. and housed at the Laboratory Animal Centre of Peking University Health Science Centre. Males were used in this study to reduce the effects of oestrogen on skeletal system, especially in aged animal models. All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free facility conditions at 24–26 °C with humidity ranging between 30 and 60% with 12 h light/dark cycles switching at 6 p.m. 3-month-old young SD rats (n = 28) were at three or less per cage. Aged male mice (n = 20) were 20-month-old and caged individually. Animals had free access to diet and activities. Each experimental group of adult rats contained seven animals (n = 7), determined from a priori power estimates using pilot variability in osteoarthritis outcomes in the ACLT model. In the aged groups, five animals (n = 5) were included, which was considered sufficient to detect biologically meaningful differences while also adhering to feasibility and the 3Rs principle. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using computer-generated random numbers. Allocation concealment was maintained by assigning animal ID codes prior to treatment. Investigators performing surgery were aware of group allocation. However, micro-CT scanning, histological staining, and data analysis were conducted by independent researchers blinded to group identity.

The primary outcome was tibial subchondral bone mineral density (medial and lateral compartments), while secondary outcomes included trabecular parameters, cartilage integrity assessed by Safranin O staining, and senescent chondrocyte markers. ACLT surgery was performed under anaesthesia with 2% isoflurane inhalation. Sham-operated animals underwent arthrotomy without ligament transection. SSK1 was administered by intra-articular injection into the knee joint at the indicated concentrations, starting 2 weeks after surgery, with the injection frequency of twice a week. Micro-CT scanning was conducted at 8 weeks post-operation, and histological and molecular analyses were performed at sacrifice. Animals were monitored daily for general health, food and water intake, and potential signs of pain or distress. Postoperative analgesia was provided with buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneously). Humane endpoints were defined as >20% body weight loss or the occurrence of severe distress requiring euthanasia. No animals reached these endpoints or were euthanised during the course of the study.

Induction of the OA model and intra-articular (IA) injection of SSK1

A total of 32 male SD rats aged 3 months and 20 male C57 mice aged 20 months were randomly subjected to anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) or sham surgery. Briefly, animals were anesthetised by isoflurane inhalation, and hindlimbs were shaved and prepared for aseptic surgery. For the ACLT operation, a medial skin and capsular incision were made to expose the ACL. After transection with micro-scissors, the joints were irrigated with saline, and the capsule and skin incision were closed with sutures. For the sham surgery, the same procedures were performed with no ligament transection. The animals were returned to their cages without joint immobilisation. After 2 weeks post-ACTL, the SD rats and C57 aged mice were given an intra-articular injection (100 μL per rat or 20 μL per mouse) of vehicle (saline) or SSK1 (0.02 or 0.1 mM, dissolved in saline) every 3 days (twice a week). Animals were euthanised by the inhalation of carbon dioxide 8 weeks after sham or ACLT operation.

Hot plate test

The hot plate test was applied to analyse the thermal hyperalgesia as previously described.22,23 The sham, vehicle, or SSK1 (0.02 or 0.1 mM)-treated ACLT rats and mice were placed on the hot plate instrument (Ugo Basile, Italy) at 55 °C. The response time was recorded when the hindlimb appeared shaking, jumping, or licking. To avoid scalds, rats or mice were taken out if the hindlimb response time exceeded 30 s. Each animal was measured three times. The experiment was performed in a blinded manner.

Micro-computed tomography (μCT)

Intact knee joints were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h, then immersed in 70% alcohol for μCT scanning. μCT analysis was applied for the detection of osteophyte development and subchondral bone remodelling using an Inveon high-resolution μCT scanner (Siemens, Germany) at a voltage of 70 kV, current of 80 μA and pixel size of 9 μm. For bone mineral density (BMD) measurements, the region of interest (ROI) was drawn separately in the medial and lateral compartments of the tibial subchondral bone. For histomorphometric parameters, including trabecular bone pattern factor (Tb.Pf.) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV), the ROI was further confined to a cubic volume within the medial compartment of the tibial subchondral bone. The cubic ROI was determined using three-dimensional orientation based on sagittal, coronal, and transverse views, with length, width, and height kept constant (corresponding to approximately 30 slices for mouse and 40 slices for rat in depth). This standardised approach minimized variation due to manual placement. All quantitative parameters were calculated using Inveon Research Workplace software.

Histology and immunochemistry

Human cartilage explants or animal knee joints were fixed as mentioned above. Subsequently, the whole joints were demineralised in a decalcifying solution (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 1–2 d.24 After dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned to 5-μm slices, and stained with safranin O-fast green. Histological evaluation for human cartilage explants was performed without decalcification. Safranin O-fast green and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were conducted for animal knee joints. Immunohistological staining was performed using antibodies against p16 (ab54210, Abcam; 1:200), MMP13 (ab39012, Abcam; 1:200), COL II (ab34712, Abcam; 1:200), COL I (ab109520, Abcam; 1:200), Ki-67 (ab15580, Abcam; 1:200) or IgG control (Cat# 30000-0-AP, Proteintech; Abcam; 1:200). Immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against cleaved-caspase3 (ab2302, Abcam; 1:100). The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system was conducted by two blinded observers for histological assessment.25,26 In addition, H&E staining for heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney was performed to evaluate the toxicity of SSK1 in animals.

TUNEL assay

After the indicated treatment, human OA chondrocytes and rat chondrocytes were rinsed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5min, and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10min at room temperature. The cells and 4.0-μm paraffin sections of SD rats knee joints were used for nick-end labelling of nuclear DNA fragmentation with a TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL system, Promega) according to the supplier's instructions. Samples were analysed under a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) for the chondrocyte transcriptome

After 3 days of incubation with SSK1 (5 μM) or vehicle control, total RNA was extracted from the human OA chondrocytes (n = 5) using TRIzol reagent. cDNA libraries were constructed for each pooled RNA sample using VAHTSTM Total RNA-seq. The library was checked with Qubit and real-time PCR for quantification and bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. HISAT2 (2.2.1) was used to build the index of the reference genome, and HISAT2 to align paired-end clean reads to the reference genome. FeatureCounts (2.0.6) was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. And then FPKM of each gene was calculated based on the length of the gene and reads count mapped to this gene. For DESeq2 with biological replicates: Differential expression analysis for two groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.42.0). The resulting P-value was adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg's methods to control the error discovery rate. The threshold of significant differential expression: P-adj ≤0.05 and |log2(fold changes)| ≥ 1. Pathway analysis was used to identify the pathways that were significantly affected by the differentially expressed genes, according to the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). We used cluster Profiler R package to test the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in KEGG pathways. The raw RNA-seq data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number PRJNA1331506 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1331506).

Statistics

For statistical analyses, the differences were calculated by unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests (two groups), one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA (homogeneity of variance, three or more groups), or nonparametric test (uneven variance or non-normal distribution) by using GraphPad Prism 8. All results are expressed as the mean ± SEM, and ‘n’ indicates the number of experiment replicates or the number of mice, rats, or human samples. For the in vivo experiments, the sample size was predetermined by a power calculation. The animals were grouped randomly and blindly by researchers. For the comparisons across multiple biomarkers, FDR P-values were used. P values are as follows: ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Results

Cellular senescence in OA and the selective targeting behaviour of SSK1 for senescent chondrocytes

Human cartilage samples were collected from the patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty. The region with an intact articular cartilage surface was used as the normal human cartilage tissue, whereas the damaged cartilage region was adopted as the OA tissue (Supplementary Figure S1). Histological sections after safranin O staining indicated that normal cartilage tissues from the intact area displayed a smooth surface and evenly distributed proteoglycans, whereas OA cartilages from the damaged region showed an irregular surface accompanied by a weakly retained extracellular matrix (Fig. 1a). The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI)27 score was significantly higher in human OA tissues than that in normal cartilage tissues (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Figure S2), further demonstrating the definition of tissues from the corresponding knee cartilage regions. The relevance of cellular senescence in clinical OA was evaluated in cartilage tissue. The expression level of p16 was markedly increased as evidenced by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, normal human and OA chondrocytes were isolated from the intact and damaged areas, respectively. We found the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells in human OA chondrocytes by 3-fold compared with that in normal human chondrocytes (Fig. 1c). Next, we sought to determine whether the prodrug SSK1 could specifically target senescent chondrocytes. Senescent human chondrocytes induced by replication, oxidative stress (H2O2 treatment), or genotoxic stress (etoposide treatment) were selectively killed by SSK1, whereas normal human chondrocytes remained almost unaffected (Fig. 1d). Specifically, SSK1 initiate to eliminate senescent cells at a concentration of 0.05 μM, and as the concentration increases to 5 μM, its clearance efficiency for senescent cells remains stable at 40%–52%, depending on the pathogenic factors of senescence. To verify whether there was any difference in the efficacy of SSK1 between the species, we treated senescent rats and mouse chondrocytes with SSK1. SSK1 selectively diminished the viability of senescent cells in both species, leaving non-senescent counterparts largely unaffected (Fig. 1e and f). In rat chondrocytes, SSK1 produced a clear concentration-dependent decline in cell viability across all senescence models, including H2O2, etoposide, and replication-induced senescence, starting at 0.5 μM. At 5 μM, cell viability was reduced by nearly or more than 50%, reflecting consistent sensitivity among the different induction methods (Fig. 1e). In mouse chondrocytes, senescent cells induced by H2O2 and etoposide also showed pronounced susceptibility to SSK1, with etoposide-induced cells exhibiting notable sensitivity even at concentrations as low as 0.05–0.5 μM (Fig. 1f). Collectively, our data illustrated that SSK1 has a selective, moderate, and broad-spectrum scavenging ability in human, rat, and mouse senescent chondrocytes in a senescence-dependent manner. Notably, aged stem/stromal cells or fibroblasts residing around the stem cell niche can generate an inflammatory microenvironment that induces a degenerative phenotype.28,29 We also investigated the cell viability of senescent mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and fibroblasts in different species after SSK1 treatment (Supplementary Figure S3). This tendency was consistent with that observed in senescent chondrocytes, suggesting a broad selective clearance of senescence independent of the cell origin.

SSK1 eliminates senescent human OA chondrocytes and modulates ECM generation ex vivo

To evaluate the senotherapeutic activity of SSK1 in OA chondrocytes, human OA chondrocytes were isolated and used to further investigate the effects of SSK on senescence, inflammation, and cartilage degradation. According to the results of gradient screening experiments, the optimal concentration and time for SSK1 to effectively clear OA chondrocytes were 0.5 μM and 3 days, respectively, and the efficiency was significantly improved when prolonging the intervention time (Supplementary Figure S4). Similar to previous results (Fig. 1e), normal chondrocytes were not markedly affected under 5 μM SSK treatment. Therefore, we chose 0.5 and 5 μM for 3 days as the preferred conditions for in vitro studies in the follow-up study. In detail, cell viability assay showed that 0.5–5 μM SSK1 treatment for 72 h stably killed 50% human OA chondrocytes (Fig. 2a). A comparative analysis of ABT-263 and fenofibrate as control senolytic agents were performed, and the clearance of OA chondrocytes by SSK1 was slightly higher than the others (Supplementary Figure S5). The senescence phenotype was rescued in OA chondrocytes after SSK1 treatment, as evidenced by the decreased percentages of SA-β-gal-positive cells from 43.5% to 5.9% (Fig. 2b). To further understand the effects of SSK1, total RNA was extracted and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to determine the mRNA expression levels in human OA chondrocytes. Discussion

The pathological mechanism of OA is complex. Current clinical trials for disease-modifying drugs targeting several aetiologies have small improvements and limited benefits in patients with OA.37,38 Growing evidence suggests that chondrosenescence has an underpinned role in the physiopathology of OA.39 Notably, accumulated senescent chondrocytes aggravate chronic inflammatory responses and result in anabolic disturbance, leading to cartilage degeneration.40 Here, we uncovered the role of cellular senescence in human OA cartilage and cartilages of different OA models. Our results provide evidence that targeting senescence by local IA administration of the prodrug SSK1, a senolytic agent, can mitigate articular cartilage degradation, reverse subchondral bone remodelling, and prevent OA development.

Notably, this study comprehensively evaluates the selectively targeting effect of senolytic agents in mouse, rat, and human senescent chondrocytes as well as MSCs induced by a variety of different senescent causes. The prodrug SSK1 showed a potent efficacy in killing non-dividing senescent cells. Furthermore, senolytic ABT-263 and ABT-199 exhibit significant toxicity to normal chondrocytes,41,42 whereas SSK1 presented no notable toxicity at concentrations ranging from 0.005 to 5 μM. Our results showed that the efficiency of SSK1 in clearing human OA chondrocytes was stable at approximately 50% with the lowest concentration of 0.5 μM, which is more efficient than currently reported senolytics including ABT-263,41 UBX0101,6 fenofibrate,12 ABT-737, and A-1331852. To evaluate the relative efficacy of SSK1, we compared it with ABT-263 and fenofibrate in human OA chondrocytes under identical conditions (0.5 and 5 μM, 72 h). SSK1 showed slightly higher clearance of senescent cells, though not statistically significant. MSCs, as the predominant stem cell type in the joint microenvironment, provide an alternative reservoir for chondrocyte renewal. MSCs are not immune to the effects of the pro-inflammatory factors that accompany OA onset and progression. Conversely, senescent MSCs have a significantly reduced ability to differentiate into chondrocytes and reduced the secretion of protective factors. Our data indicated the good efficiency of SSK1 in manipulating the viability of senescent MSCs induced by replicative, toxicant, or oxidative stressed conditions.

In summary, compared with commonly used senolytic compounds, the eliminative effect of SSK1 on senescent chondrocytes is safer, milder, and more efficient. This is due to the mechanistic advantage of prodrugs that require two steps to exert their biological function, that is, the release of the bioactive senolytic with the assistance of accumulated SA-β-gal in senescent cells, followed by cell death initiations or SASP modulation. Similarly, other galacto-modified prodrugs (as new-type senolytics) also exhibited increased senolytic specificity and reduced toxicity outcomes that can be preferentially activated by SA-β-gal activity.43,44 This evidence suggests that prodrug as senotherapy using SA-β-gal may provide a solution to progress to clinical translation. Furthermore, SSK1-treated human OA chondrocytes and cartilage explants were tested. Renewed chondrogenic ECM production and cascaded SASP factor expression in OA chondrocytes occurred as early as 3 days after SSK1 treatment, emphasising the mobilisation of chondroprotective effects and the role of chondrosenescence in OA progression. Our findings are consistent with the reported role of senotherapy in preventing OA progression and promoting anabolic signalling in OA chondrocytes.45 Similar results were observed in SSK1-treated human OA cartilage explants. Fibrates, a PPARα agonist used for dyslipidaemias in humans, was recently identified as a senotherapeutic molecule.12 When co-cultured with human ageing cartilage, fibrates markedly reduced proteoglycan loss and protected against cartilage degradation in response to IL-1β treatment.12 The percentage of senescent cells in cartilage explants was significantly reduced after ABT-263 or fibrates treatment,39 and the underlying mechanism was related to apoptosis activation and SASP inhibition.46 These data are consistent with SSK1 treatment inducing the selective elimination of senescent OA chondrocytes within cartilage explants and favourably modulating SASP and metabolic responses involved in OA pathogenesis.

This in vitro and ex vivo evidence suggests that the potential use of SSK1 to delay OA progression warrants further in vivo exploration. Our data firmly support the therapeutic potential of SSK1 in both young and aged animals who underwent ACLT surgery-induced OA phenotype. However, current evidence suggests that senolytic therapy, including treatment with ABT-263, UBX0101, dasatinib plus quercetin (D + Q), and fibrates displays conditional benefits in delaying early OA in animal models.47,48 In addition, the combination of different senolytics, as well as systemic and IA administration, affects tissue healing capacity in the aged animals.6 Moreover, oral administration of D + Q had no detectable effects on temporomandibular joints of young mice, but induced an increased cartilage thickness by more than 30% in joints of the 23- to 24-month-old aged mice.49 In another study, senolytic fibrates did not affect cartilage damage development in spontaneous OA mice.50 Collectively, these results indicate that senotherapeutic agents displayed variant effects depending on tissue type and age. In this study, compared with sham surgery, ACLT injury led to an increase in the number of p16-positive senescent cells in both young rats and aged mice. With the administration of SSK1, p16-positive senescent cells were effectively eliminated and the SASP factor MMP13 was downregulated in young and aged animal joints, demonstrating the benefits of senolytic treatment for removing senescent cells after injury, regardless of age.51

In addition to enhancing the anabolic process in the cartilage, IA injection of SSK1 also improved the subchondral bone structure in both young rats and aged mice. Senescent cells and SASP factors affect subchondral bone tissue microstructure, manifested by decreased bone density, trabecular bone, and relative bone tissue volume.52 Emerging evidence suggests that bone–cartilage cross-talk influences cartilage degeneration across OA progression to some extent. However, whether subchondral bone remodelling is associated with chondrosenescence has yet not been fully studied. Our data established evidence that the elimination of senescent chondrocytes by local administration of senolytics contributed to the improvement of subchondral remodelling and osteophyte formation in joints, which represents a close cross-talk between cartilage and its adjacent subchondral bone. In a recent study, intermittent systemic administration of D + Q, the first described senolytics in 2015,53 improved subchondral turnover and BV in temporomandibular joints in 23- to 24-month-old mice but not in those aged 4 months old.49 Another study found that only combined senolytic treatment could reduce osteophytes in aged mice.48 In our study, however, the protective effect of SSK1 on cartilage and subchondral bone of the knee joint is observed in both young and aged animals without systemic senolysis. One possible reason for this is that SSK1 has good tissue penetration capacity. Small-molecule compounds have strong permeability, but senolytics usually require higher concentrations, which are prone to aggregate formation and greatly reduce penetrability. However, the galactose side-chain in SSK1 residue can improve hydrophilic properties and prevent molecular self-assembly.54 Galactose conjugates demonstrated minimal aggregation and self-quenching in aqueous media and efficient in-depth staining.55 Here, IA administration of prodrug SSK1 as a therapeutic senolytic is successful for targeting the microenvironment in the subchondral bone to improve arthritic pain and OA development.

To reveal the potential mechanism by which SSK1 delays OA progression, we further investigated the effect of SSK1 on apoptotic signalling. Senolytics eliminate senescent cells by facilitating cell apoptosis.6,56 Our data indicate that SSK1 can significantly reduce apoptotic cells accumulation in vivo after intervention for 6 weeks, which is consistent with the rescued proliferative characteristics of chondrocytes in the joint cavity at this time point. However, the immune response is compromised as senescent cells accumulate in aged animals. The protein and microRNA profiles of extracellular vesicles (EVs) from the synovial fluid of young and old mice after treatment with UBX0101 are distinct,57 indicating different mechanisms of senolytic treatment after joint injury. In young mice with post-traumatic OA, increased levels of cartilage ECM-related proteins and decreased levels of proteases involved in cartilage degradation, were found, which did not emerge in aged mice. Mechanistically, the ACLT-induced OA model of young mice with senolytic treatment mainly regulates metabolic disease and molecular transport, energy production, nucleic acid metabolism, and small-molecule biochemistry, whereas senolytic treatment in aged mice induced primarily immunological changes in EV proteins.57 The evidence somewhat explains that the limited preventive effect of current senolytics on OA progression in aged mice may lie in their impaired immune activity.48 In addition, a recent study revealed that p16-positive fibroblasts can sense inflammatory signals and respond through their increased secretory capacity to promote tissue regeneration.58 The evidence suggests that, to some extent, senolytic administration in the articular cavity cannot totally eliminate senescent cells, which have the potential to sense microenvironment alterations thus activating regenerative signals.

The current study is not without limitations. Although the effectiveness of SSK1 treatment on human cartilage explants from patients with OA and post-traumatic animal models were evaluated, they do not encompass the full range of OA disease. Exploration of its potency in patients who have OA driven by other mechanisms, such as natural ageing or obesity, needs further investigation. RNA-seq analysis revealed that SSK1 affects pathways related to apoptosis cellular senescence, DNA replication, and cell cycle pathways in OA chondrocytes, but the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its senolytic effect remain unclear. Further studies are needed to elucidate how SSK1 modulates senescence-associated signalling and promotes tissue regeneration. Although the effect of oestrogen on the study was excluded, the use of only male animals was a shortcoming of this study. Intra-articular injection of SSK1 lacks cell-type specificity, other cell types including senescent synovial fibroblasts and MSCs may also contribute to the alleviation of OA. As a kind of prodrug, pharmacogenomic differences in patients may lead to patient-to-patient variability in SSK1 release if utilised in the future. In addition, further evaluation in large-animal models is necessary to establish the potential benefits of senolytic SSK1 therapy before its translational application. Other directions include the effectiveness of oral administration, optical dosage, and sustained drug delivery platforms.

In conclusion, our findings provide strong preclinical evidence that senotherapy with the prodrug SSK1 provides a therapeutic approach for the treatment of OA. The potential benefits of SSK1 treatment include the elimination of senescent chondrocytes, alleviation of cartilage degeneration, improved subchondral bone remodelling, and relief of arthritic pain. In terms of translational medicine, SSK1 opens new insights into the intervention of cartilage degeneration and thus provides a clinically relevant potential alternative for current treatment of post-traumatic OA progression.
