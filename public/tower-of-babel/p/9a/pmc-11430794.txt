Invited Perspective: The Exposome—An Exciting Opportunity for Collaboration, Not Competition
Authors: Christopher P Wild
Published: 2024

Safarlou et al.1 have written a stimulating and timely commentary in this issue of Environmental Health Perspectives as the exposome approaches its 20th anniversary.2 The authors make two statements, one a point of principle and one a definition, that I believe capture the essence of their case.

First, the principle. Toward the end of their article, the authors write, “The exposome concept should serve the mission of exposomics, not vice versa.”1 At first sight this appears curious, a case of putting the cart before the horse. The exposome is an entity, whereas exposomics is an approach to analyzing it. Both are needed, but precision in hierarchy is important to ensure a correct focus: As the old folk song implores, “Keep your eyes on the prize.” The overarching prize of analyzing the exposome is to identify risk factors for disease and consequently to reduce morbidity and mortality. Allowing the methodology to predominate would be counterproductive. The exposome serves exposomics only insofar as it creates the need for novel means of exposure assessment and data analysis. This article seeks, predominantly, to articulate a vision for exposomics as a novel approach to explore the exposome.

Second, the definition. Safarlou et al. define exposomics as “a research program in environmental health aimed at enabling a comprehensive and discovery-driven approach to identifying environmental determinants of human health.”1 There are two essential principles within this statement: exposomics is comprehensive and it is discovery-driven. Comprehensiveness is the defining, innovative feature of the exposome. Exposomics seeks to be faithful to this original call for breadth and depth. However, even the approach of exposomics remains limited, providing only a partial description of the exposome. Therefore, the authors’ suggestion to consider compiling components of the exposome is helpful. Indeed, this viewpoint opens the door to much-needed international cooperation on exposomics. The development and validation of methods should be supported by a global consortium of funders to ensure efficient coverage of different components of the exposome, with reference to exposure priorities in different world regions.

A corollary of the above point is that exposomics does not have a monopoly on describing the exposome. Although we might marvel at the discoveries furnished by the James Webb Space Telescope, we can also appreciate information garnered from space probes targeted to specific planets or the chemical composition of fragments of a meteorite. The point of each is to better understand the universe. Specific methods are subservient to that goal. This is true of the molecular and biochemical techniques of the “omics” variety widely employed in exposomics. At times, unraveling the exposome will require innovative questionnaires or Global Positioning System (GPS) data to capture broad, complex factors, such as economic status, psychosocial stress, or the built environment. On occasion, more targeted laboratory methods for specific components of the exposome will be justified. It is not that such approaches are irrelevant to the exposome; it is that alone they are not up to the task. By its nature, the exposome demands more than single-exposure, single-disease studies.

The definition given by Safarlou et al. also emphasizes that exposomics is characterized by a discovery-driven approach. The sheer breadth of interconnected exposures and biological responses captured by exposomics can generate novel hypotheses about etiology, often when applied to the large-scale prospective cohorts and biobanks established in recent years. This is the exciting, transformative power of exposomics. It is where exposomics comes closest to doing for the exposome what genomics has done for the genome. Thus, although exposomics cannot capture the entire exposome, this is no reason to denigrate its value. A partial description of the exposome is of great value when analyzed at the population level in the context of a specific study design. And as the authors acknowledge, the discoveries from exposomics may be complemented by subsequent hypothesis-driven research.

As an adjunct to the above discussion of definition, Safarlou et al. rightly emphasize throughout that although exposomics encompasses the environment, it goes much farther (even if environment is broadly interpreted). This point is perhaps best illustrated by their reminder that, in addition to the complexity of exposures, exposomics seeks to capture biological responses to exposure, processes that themselves may affect health and disease.

The exposome concept was born of frustration and hope. Frustration because the huge investment in sequencing the human genome was partially based on a promise to unravel the causes of human disease, even though five decades of epidemiology had already indicated those causes to be predominantly nongenetic; something more than the Human Genome Project was needed to combat the 21st century pattern of ill-health. Hope because the exposome could be addressed by some of the innovative technology from genomics and the allied fields of proteomics, metabolomics, biostatistics, and bioinformatics, transforming assessment of nongenetic exposures and their biological consequences. It was also hoped this harnessing of disciplines would trigger a reversal in the paucity of investment in exposure assessment methodology prevailing at the time.

Twenty years on, the exposome and exposomics still promise much. But we must remember this is a young field, barely out of adolescence. Notwithstanding the advances made, the ongoing need to define its identity is unsurprising. Amid this process, I sense Safarlou et al. worry that the referenced whispers, asides, and research rebranding may compromise future investment. It should not. Their case for support is strong. The original concept of the exposome was deliberately described as a complement to the genome. On a strategic level, this avoided pitting one scientific community against another. On an operational level, it emphasized the exciting opportunities for interdisciplinary research. Collaboration trumps competition. I believe the inclusive, collaborative vision for the exposome remains unaltered. The real surprise is how quickly and successfully the concept has transformed into the reality of a new, vibrant, and innovative field of biomedical research.
