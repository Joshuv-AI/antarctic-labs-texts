Mycoviruses from Aspergillus fungi involved in fermentation of dried bonito
Authors: Seiji Buma, Syun-ichi Urayama, Rei Suo, Shiro Itoi, Shigeru Okada, Akihiro Ninomiya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Seven new viruses were found from two Aspergillus spp. isolated from dried bonito.•Aspergillus chevalieri fusarivirus 1 has a unique bi-segmented genome.•Aspergillus chevalieri polymycovirus 1 suppressed the initial growth of the host.

Introduction

Fungi have been used for food fermentation around the world since ancient times. Fermentation enriches our diet by preserving food and adding unique flavors and nutritional value. For example, the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae is used to make bread, beer, and wine (Parapouli et al., 2020; Walker and Stewart, 2016); the filamentous fungus Penicillium roqueforti is used in the production of blue cheese (Coton et al., 2020); and Aspergillus oryzae is used in the production of misoand soy sauce, which are traditional Japanese fermented foods (Machida et al., 2008). However, despite this deep involvement of fungi in our food culture, the diversity of mycoviruses that infect the fungi involved in food fermentation is not well understood, except for mycoviruses that infect S. cerevisiae (such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae virus l-A) (Wickner et al., 2013; Schmitt and Breinig, 2006).

Dried bonito (ray-finned fish in the family Scombridae), called “katsuobushi” in Japanese, is an important processed marine product used primarily to make soup stock in Japanese cuisine. There are two types of dried bonito: arabushi and karebushi. The process of making dried bonito is as follows. The bonito is cut into four pieces, the fillets are boiled, and the bones are removed from the fillets. Then, water is removed by smoking to obtain arabushi. Arabushi flakes are widely used in home cooking in Japan. Karebushi is fermented arabushi; it is made by repeating the process of spraying fungi onto the surface of arabushi and drying it under the sun (Dimici and Wada, 1994). The fungi involved in the fermentation process are collectively called katsuobushi molds. During the fermentation process, katsuobushi molds produce various enzymes that contribute to the formation of the unique flavor of karebushi. Methyltransferases methylate volatile phenols contained in katsuobushi (Doi et al., 1989; Yamauchi and Doi, 1997), proteases hydrolyze proteins to produce amino acids (Aoki et al., 2013), and lipases hydrolyze lipids (Kunimoto et al., 1996). In addition, some compounds produced by katsuobushi molds have antioxidant activity and are believed to protect karebushi from oxidation (Miyake et al., 2009). Recent studies have revealed that Aspergillus spp. such as A. chevalieri and A. sydowii, are dominant on the surface of karebushi (Kadooka et al., 2020; Takenaka et al., 2020). At the beginning of the fermentation, the starter mold A. glaucus is sprayed on the katsuobushi.

Mycoviruses (i.e., viruses that infect fungi) were first discovered in 1962, approximately 70 years after the discovery of the first virus in the 1890s. An RNA virus was isolated as a pathogen of an edible mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) (Hollings, 1962). Mycoviruses have now been reported from various fungi, particularly those that are pathogenic to plants and humans. The number of reports has increased rapidly in recent years, and in 2021 alone the number of identified mycoviruses increased by 25 %, with >50 novel mycoviruses reported (Villan et al., 2023). A large number of mycoviruses have been reported from hosts belonging to the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Myers et al., 2020). Among these, a mycovirus was first discovered in a member of the genus Aspergillus in 1970 (Banks et al., 1970), and since then, mycoviruses have been reported from Aspergillus spp., including members of sections Nigri, Circumdati, Flavi, Clavati, and Fumigati (Kotta-Loizou and Coutts, 2017a). These viruses mainly belong to the families Partitiviridae, Chrysoviridae, Totiviridae, and Polymycoviridae.

Mycoviruses are believed to behave symbiotically without killing host cells (Ghabrial and Suzuki, 2009; Ghabrial et al., 2015). They have various physiological effects on their hosts, for example, on growth, primary metabolism, and phytopathogenicity (Urayama et al., 2024). Recently, studies have shown that they even affect enzyme production and secondary metabolism (Nerva et al., 2019; Song et al., 2020; Ninomiya et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022). Viruses infecting Aspergillus spp. can affect host growth, pigment production, pathogenicity, and toxin production (Schmidt et al., 1986; Özkan et al., 2017). For example, a chrysovirus infecting the human pathogenic fungus A. fumigatus caused decreased conidia production, resistance to oxidative stress, and toxicity to mice (Takahashi-Nakaguchi et al., 2019).

Various mycoviruses have been reported from Aspergillus spp., and therefore we hypothesized that katsuobushi molds also harbor mycoviruses. In this study, nine strains of katsuobushi molds of the genus Aspergillus were searched for mycoviruses using the fragmented and primer-ligated double-stranded RNA sequencing (FLDS) method (Urayama et al., 2016; Hirai et al., 2021). As a result, six viruses were found from two A. chevalieri strains [Aspergillus chevalieri victorivirus 1 (AchVV1), Aspergillus chevalieri polymycovirus 1 (AchPmV1), Aspergillus chevalieri partitivirus 1 (AchPV1), Aspergillus chevalieri chrysovirus 1 (AchCV1), Aspergillus chevalieri fusarivirus 1 (AchFV1), and Aspergillus chevalieri mitovirus 1 (AchMV1)], and one from an A. sulphureus strain [(Aspergillus sulphureus partitivirus 1 (AsuPV1)]. While the genomes of previously reported fusariviruses consist of a (+) single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), AchFV1 is suggested to have two (+) ssRNAs. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a fusarivirus with a segmented genome. Furthermore, to reveal the effect of mycoviruses on their hosts, antiviral drugs were used to eliminate the viruses, and then the phenotypes were compared between isogenic fungal strains with and without mycoviruses. The results showed that AchPmV1 suppressed the initial growth of its host. This study demonstrates previously unknown diversity and function of mycoviruses that infect katsuobushi molds.

Materials and methods

Fungi

The fungal strains used in this study are listed in Table S1. A. chevalieri strains JCM23047, JCM23061, JCM23062, and JCM23063 were provided by the Japan Collection of Microorganisms, RIKEN BioResource Research Center (RIKEN BRC; Ibaraki, Japan). A. chevalieri strain NBRC4090, A. conicus strain NBRC4046, A. sydowii strain NBRC4097, and A. wentii strain NBRC4107 were provided by the Biological Resource Center, National Institute of Technology and Evaluation (NBRC; Tokyo, Japan). According to the information available from RIKEN BRC and NBRC, all the strains used in this study were isolated from katsuobushi. Unless otherwise noted below, each strain was incubated at 26.5 °C

Detection of viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) by electrophoresis

Each strain of A. chevalieri was grown on minimal medium (Barratt et al., 1965) containing 20 % sucrose and 1.5 % agar (MMS20A). The mycelia were then cut out with agar pieces and inoculated into 100 mL of liquid minimal medium (Barratt et al., 1965) containing 20 % sucrose (MMS20) and incubated with agitation at 150 rpm for 1 week. Each strain of A. conicus, A. sulphureus, A. sydowii, and A. wentii was cultured in the same manner as A. chevalieri except that potato dextrose broth (PDB; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used for liquid culture and PDB containing 2 % agar (PDA) for agar culture. After liquid culture, the mycelia and culture supernatant were separated by filtration through Prowipe (Daio Paper Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The dsRNA fraction was obtained from crushed mycelia according to reported protocols (Morris and Dodds, 1979; Okada et al., 2013). The dsRNA fraction was concentrated by ethanol precipitation and subjected to 1.2 % agarose gel electrophoresis. dsRNA was detected using the fluorescent dye GelRed (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA).

Genome sequencing of mycoviruses

dsRNA fractions were obtained from A. chevalieri JCM23062, A. chevalieri JCM23063, and A. sulphureus NBRC4095 by the method described in Section 2.2. Sequence analysis of RNA in the dsRNA fraction was performed according to the FLDS method (Urayama et al., 2016; Hirai et al., 2021).

Reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR)

Each strain of A. chevalieri was cultured on MMS20A. The mycelia were then cut from agar plates, inoculated into 100 mL of MMS20, and incubated for 1 week with agitation at 150 rpm. A. sulphureus was cultured in the same manner as A. chevalieri except that PDA was used for agar culture and PDB for liquid culture. After liquid culture, the mycelia and culture supernatant were separated by filtration. Mycelia (0.1 g), 0.5-mm glass beads (0.3 g; Tomy, Tokyo, Japan), 500 μL of RNA extraction buffer [2 × sodium chloride-Tris-EDTA buffer (100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA), 1 % sodium dodecyl sulfate, 0.1 % β-mercaptoethanol], and 500 μL of phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) pH 5.2 (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) were put into 2-mL screw-cap tubes (Watson, Tokyo, Japan), and the mycelia were crushed using a bead crusher μT-01 (Taitec, Saitama, Japan) at 4000 rpm for 45 s. The homogenate was centrifuged (4 °C, 21,000 × g, 10 min), and 400 μL of 2-propanol was added to 400 μL of the supernatant and centrifuged (4 °C, 21,000 × g, 15 min). The supernatant was removed, and 75 % ethanol was added to the precipitate and centrifuged (4 °C, 21,000 × g, 5 min). The precipitate was dissolved in 50 μL of nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to afford a total RNA solution. RT-PCR was performed using the Express One-Step Superscript qRT-PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the total RNA from the mycelia as the template. Primers specific to the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP)-encoding gene of each virus were designed and used (Table S2). The reaction solution was then subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis. One-step RT-PCR was performed in the following conditions: reverse transcription (55 °C, 20 min; 60 °C, 10 min); PCR reaction (94 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 68 °C for 1 min; then 68 °C for 5 min).

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic trees were built on the basis of RdRP amino acid sequences. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT software v7.490 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). For each protein set, substitution models were tested, and the best-fit model was chosen by using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). Maximum-likelihood trees were constructed by using IQ-TREE software v2.2.2.6 (Minh et al., 2020).

Elimination of mycoviruses

The nucleoside analogs 2′-C-methylcytidine (2CMC; Combi-Blocks, San Diego, CA, USA) and ribavirin (Combi-Blocks) were used as antiviral drugs, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as the negative control. Spores of A. chevalieri suspended in 0.05 % Tween 20 were spread on yeast extract-peptone-20 % sucrose agar (YPS20A; 1 % yeast extract, 2 % peptone, 20 % sucrose, 2 % agar) containing 0.2 mg/mL 2CMC or 0.1 mg/mL ribavirin. After incubation, the colonies that emerged were transferred to agar plates without antiviral drugs and stored as strains. Each strain was cultured in yeast extract-peptone-20 % sucrose medium (YPS20; 1 % yeast extract, 2 % peptone, 20 % sucrose) with agitation at 150 rpm for 1 week and the mycelia were collected. RT-PCR was performed using total RNA extracted from the mycelia as described in Section 2.4.

Phenotypic analyses

To culture A. chevalieri, MMS20A, YPS20A, and malt extract-peptone-20 % sucrose agar (MPS20A; 2 % malt extract, 0.1 % peptone, 20 % sucrose, 2 % agar) were used. One hundred spores suspended in 0.2 μL of 0.05 % Tween 20 were dropped at the center of an agar plate, which was incubated. Colony diameters were measured every 12 h for 1 week. Three independently obtained virus-free strains were used for this experiment. Each strain was cultured in triplicate, and Welch's t-test was used to determine significant differences.

Results

Identification of mycoviruses from katsuobushi molds

dsRNA and ssRNA viruses infecting fungi can be detected using dsRNA as an indicator (Urayama et al., 2016). dsRNA fractions obtained from nine strains of katsuobushi molds (Table S1) were subjected to electrophoresis, which suggested that A. chevalieri JCM23062, A. chevalieri JCM23063, and A. sulphureus NBRC4095 contained dsRNAs (Fig. 1). The strains JCM23062 and JCM23063 yielded several (>5) bands with sizes ranging from ∼1 kb to ∼6 kb, which suggested co-infection of some viruses. The strain NBRC4095 yielded three bands, each of which seemed smaller than 2.0 kb. The dsRNA sequences were determined by the FLDS method (Urayama et al., 2016; Hirai et al., 2021), which enables determination of the full-length genome sequences of RNA viruses. As a result, six distinct mycoviruses (chrysovirus, fusarivirus, mitovirus A, mitovirus B, polymycovirus, and victorivirus) were found in A. chevalieri JCM23062; five (chrysovirus, fusarivirus, mitovirus A, mitovirus B, and partitivirus A) were found in A. chevalieri JCM23063; and one (partitivirus B) was found in A. sulphureus NBRC4095. To confirm that these viruses actually infected the hosts, total RNA was extracted from the hosts and RT-PCR was performed using virus-specific primers and the total RNA. Mitovirus B was not detected in either strain JCM23062 or JCM23063, and was thus considered to be derived from contamination. Characterization of dsRNA viruses

AchCV1 has three genomic RNA segments. The largest segment (segment 1, 3566 bp) encoded an RdRP; the second-largest segment (segment 2, 3029 bp) encoded a coat protein (CP); and the smallest segment (segment 3, 2858 bp) encoded a hypothetical protein, ORF3 (Fig. 2A). BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchCV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Aspergillus cibarius chrysovirus 1 (AcCV1; identity 75.54 %, coverage 99 %, GenBank accession number WBW48474.1). The CP of AchCV1 showed the highest similarity to that of AcCV1 (identity 72.27 %, coverage 99 %, GenBank WBW48475.1). ORF3 of AchCV1 showed the highest similarity to the hypothetical protein of AcCV1 (identity 78.22 %, coverage 100 %, GenBank WBW48476.1) (Table S3). Phylogenetic analyses based on amino acid sequences of RdRP suggested that AchCV1 belongs to the genus Alphachrysovirus (Fig. 2B). AchCV1 was included in a clade that consists of AcCV1 and Hulunbuir Chrys tick virus 1 (HCTV1). The species demarcation criteria within the genus Alphachrysovirus (Kotta-Loizou et al., 2020) include deduced amino acid sequence data (≤70 % and ≤53 % amino acid sequence identity in the RdRP and CP, respectively). AchPV1 has three genomic RNA segments (segment 1, 1953 bp; segment 2, 1827 bp; segment 3, 1707 bp), which encode an RdRP, a CP, and a hypothetical protein (ORF3), respectively (Figure S2A). AsuPV1 has three genomic segments (segment 1, 1678 bp; segment 2, 1507 bp; segment 3, 801 bp). Segments 1 and 2 encode an RdRP and CP, respectively. Partitiviruses have two essential dsRNA segments of 3–4.8 kb and may have additional genomic segments; the larger of the two essential segments usually encodes an RdRP and the smaller encodes a CP (Nibert et al., 2014; Vainio et al., 2018). In AchPV1 and AsuPV1, the largest segment encodes RdRP and the second-largest segment encodes the CP. BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchPV1 showed the highest similarity to an RdRP detected in a metagenome from a tick, Dermacentor silvarum (identity 94.87 %, coverage 100 %, GenBank WAK75571.1) (Table S3). The CP of AchPV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Xiaogan partiti-like virus 1 (identity 40.89 %, coverage 98 %, GenBank UUW20933.1). No protein showed similarity to ORF3 of AchPV1. BlastP search for AsuPV1 revealed that its RdRP showed the highest similarity to an RdRP detected in a soil metagenome sequence (identity 89.14 %, coverage 99 %, GenBank UDL14362.1) and the RdRP of Penicillium stoloniferum virus F (PsV-F; identity 85.07 %, coverage 99 %, GenBank DAD54825.1). The CP of AsuPV1 showed the highest similarity to that of PsV-F (identity 70.53 %, coverage 99 %, GenBank DAD54826.1). Phylogenetic analysis based on amino acid sequences of RdRP showed that AchPV1 and AsuPV1 belong to the genera Alphapartitivirus and Gammapartitivirus, respectively (Figure S2B). The species demarcation criteria within the genus Alphapartitivirus (Vainio et al., 2018) are ≤90 % and ≤80 % amino acid sequence identity in the RdRP and CP, respectively. Three RdRPs showed >90 % identities to the RdRP of AchPV1. However, the amino acid sequences of the corresponding CPs are unknown, because the RdRP sequences were derived from tick metagenome. No CPs showed >80 % identity to the CP of AchPV1. Thus, AchPV1 can be classified as a new species. The species demarcation criteria within the genus Gammapartitivirus are the same as those within Alphachrysoviridae. According to those criteria, AsuPV1 also can be classified as a new species.

The genome of AchVV1 consists of a single RNA (5113 bp) that encodes a CP and an RdRP (Figure S3A). BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchVV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Aspergillus foetidus virus slow-1 (identity 50.18 %, coverage 99 %, NCBI Reference Sequence YP_009508249.1) and the CP of AchVV1 to that of Beauveria bassiana victorivirus 1 (identity 53.82 %, coverage 93 %, GenBank AMQ11130.1) (Table S3). Phylogenetic analysis showed that AchVV1 is a member of the genus Victorivirus (Figure S3B). International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) defined the species demarcation criteria within the genus Victorivirus: viruses/viral genome sequences that share <70 % RdRP amino acid sequence identity will be considered to represent different virus species regardless of whether reported from the same or different host species (ICTV, 2023). According to this criterion, AchVV1 can be classified as a new species.

AchPmV1 has four genomic RNA segments (segment 1, 2427 bp; segment 2, 2258 bp; segment 3, 2045 bp; segment 4, 1369 bp) encoding an RdRP, a hypothetical protein (ORF2), a methyltransferase, and a proline-alanine‑serine-rich protein (PASrp), respectively (Fig. 3A). Viruses belonging to the family Polymycoviridae have overall genome sizes ranging from 7.5 to 12.5 kb consisting of 4–8 dsRNA segments (Kotta-Loizou and Coutts, 2022). Methyltransferases are involved in the formation of a cap structure (m7GpppX-) at the 5′- end of viral RNA in ssRNA viruses (Kanhayuwa et al., 2015; Ramdhan and Li, 2022). The formation of the cap structure promotes translation of viral genes. However, there is no experimental evidence showing that methyltransferases are involved in cap structure formation in polymycoviruses. PASrp can be involved in the formation of filamentous viral particles (Jia et al., 2017). Note that typical viral particles are not observed in many polymycoviruses. BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchPmV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Metarhizium brunneum polymycovirus 1 (identity 45.34 %, coverage 96 %, GenBank WBL45227.1) (Table S3). ORF2 of AchPmV1 showed the highest similarity to the Magnaporthe oryzae polymycovirus 1 hypothetical protein (identity 34.45 %, coverage 96 %, NCBI Reference Sequence YP_010086047.1). The putative methyltransferase of AchPmV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Aspergillus fumigatus polymycovirus 1 (identity 33.68 %, coverage 94 %, GenBank BBU42082.1). The PASrp of AchPmV1 showed the highest similarity to that of Beauveria bassiana polymycovirus 1 (identity 38.93 %, coverage 79 %, NCBI Reference Sequence YP_009352878.1). The results of phylogenetic analyses based on amino acid sequences of RdRP are shown in Fig. 3B. Characterization of (+) ssRNA viruses

We also performed molecular characterization of (+) ssRNA viruses, which have single-stranded positive-sense genomes. AchFV1 has two genomic RNA segments. An RdRP and a hypothetical protein (ORF2) are encoded by segment 1 (6322 kb), and another hypothetical protein (ORF3) is encoded by segment 2 (1963 kb) (Fig. 4A). Fusariviridae is a family of viruses with genome size 5.9–10.7 kb and a single (+) ssRNA genome (Chiba et al., 2024). However, unlike known fusariviruses, AchFV1, detected in this study, has two genomic segments. BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchFV1 showed the highest similarity to that from Penicillium aurantiogriseum fusarivirus 1 (PaFV1; identity 49.46 %, coverage 98 %, NCBI Reference Sequence YP_009182154.1) (Table S3). ORF2 of AchFV1 showed the highest similarity to the 53-kDa protein of PaFV1 (identity 31.01 %, coverage 53 %, NCBI Reference Sequence YP_009182155.1). No homologous proteins were found for ORF3 of AchFV1 in BlastP search. Phylogenetic analysis showed that AchFV1 is a member of the genus Alphafusarivirus (Fig. 4B). As in known fusariviruses, the RdRP of AchFV1 contained both RdRp-1 (pfam00680) and DEAD/DEAH box helicase (pfam00270) domains. The number of transmembrane helices in RdRP was predicted by using the TMHMM 2.0 tool (Sonnhammer et al., 1998; Krogh et al., 2001). The protein from AchFV1 has three transmembrane helices at the N-terminus; RdRP of other alphafusariviruses has 3–8 transmembrane helices. ORF2 contained a structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) domain (COG1196), which is consistent with known alphafusariviruses. Next, we set out to test the possibility that PaFV1 (Nerva et al., 2017), which was closest to AchFV1 in a phylogenetic tree based on RdRP amino acid sequences (Fig. 4B), has a second genomic RNA segment. Using an assembler rnaSPAdes (Bushmanova et al., 2019), de novo assembly was performed on the raw sequencing data (NCBI Sequence Read Archive SRR2127587) that was previously used to determine the PaFV1 genome sequence. A tblastn search was performed on the obtained contigs using ORF3 of AchFV1 as the query, but no homologous protein was found. AchMV1 has a single genomic RNA segment (2744 kb), which encodes an RdRP (Figure S4A). BlastP search revealed that the RdRP of AchMV1 showed the highest similarity to that of a mitovirus found in a metagenome from cattle feces (identity 58 %, coverage 93.63 %, GenBank URG16717.1) (Table S3). AchMV1 is a member of the genus Duamitovirus (Figure S4B). The species demarcation criterion within the family Mitoviridae is <70 % RdRP amino acid sequence identity (Walker et al., 2022). According to this criterion, AchMV1 can be classified as a new species.

Elimination of mycoviruses

Next, we attempted to eliminate mycoviruses from A. chevalieri JCM23062. Virus elimination was performed using a modified version of the method of Ikeda et al., 2022. The experimental workflow is summarized in Figure S5. When 2CMC was used as the antiviral drug, AchVV1 or AchPmV1-free strains were obtained, but no other viruses were removed (Figure S6). When ribavirin was used as the antiviral reagent, AcheVV1, AchPmV1, and AchMV1 were eliminated; the elimination rates of AcheVV1 and AchPmV1 were three and four times those for 2CMC, respectively (Figure S6). Elimination of AchPmV1 was also observed in DMSO-treated strains (negative controls). As a result, from strain JCM23062, isogenic strains lacking (1) AchPmV1; (2) AchVV1; (3) AchPmV1 and AchVV1; and (4) AchMV1 were obtained. We did not obtain any strains without AchCV1 or AchFV1 in the conditions used in this study. A. chevalieri JCM23063 was also treated with antiviral drugs in the same manner as strain JCM23062, but no virus was removed from strain JCM23063.

Effect of mycoviruses on katsuobushi mold

We set out to evaluate the effect of viruses on A. chevalieri JCM23062 by using isogenic strains lacking one or more viruses. Three independently obtained strains without one or more viruses were used for the phenotypic analyses. Because only one strain lacking AcheMV1 was obtained, we did not use this strain for phenotypic analyses. For convenience, we refer to A. chevalieri JCM23062 as the parental strain (Pc), the Pc-derived strains without AchPmV1 as Pc-Pm, and the Pc-derived strains without AchVV1 or AchPmV1 as Pc-VPm. The strains were inoculated in the center of agar media (MMS20A, YPS20A, MPS20A) and the colony diameters were measured over time (Figure S7A). On MMS20A, the AchPmV1-free strains (Pc-Pm, Pc-VPm) had larger colony diameters than the parental strain Pc at all time points (Fig. 5A and B). However, in the late growth phase (84–156 h), there was no significant difference in the average growth rate of Pc and the AchPmV1-free strains (Pc-Pm, Pc-VPm) (Fig. 5C). Discussion

In this study, seven new mycoviruses were discovered from katsuobushi molds (two strains of A. chevalieri and one strain of A. sulphureus), and their whole genome sequences were determined. To our knowledge, this is the first time that mycoviruses have been found in katsuobushi molds. In addition, there is no previous report of mycoviruses from either A. chevalieri or A. sulphureus.

Phylogenetic analysis of AchCV1 revealed that it is in a clade that includes AcCV1 and HCTV1. AcCV1 has characteristics that distinguish it from other alphachrysoviruses, and it was proposed to be included in a new genus, Gammachrysovirus, along with HCTV1 (Lutz et al., 2024). Most alphachrysoviruses have a protein containing a motif conserved in the ovarian tumor (OTU)-like cysteine protease (pfam02338) (Kotta-Loizou et al., 2020), but AcCV1 does not have a protein containing this domain (Lutz et al., 2024). While alpha- and betachrysoviruses usually have one structural protein, AcCV1 has two (Lutz et al., 2024). Similar to AcCV1, AchCV1 lacks a protein containing a motif common to OTU-like cysteine proteases. The number of structural proteins of AchCV1 is unknown because viral particles were not isolated in this study. However, the phylogenetic analyses undertaken here indicate that AchCV1 should be included in the proposed genus Gammachrysovirus, with AchCV1 as the second gammachrysovirus from fungi.

While known fusariviruses have a single genomic RNA, AchFV1 was found to have a bi-segment genome. No studies have previously reported on fusariviruses with segmented genomes. Segment 2 of the AchFV1 genome encoded a hypothetical protein (ORF3), and BlastP search using the amino acid sequence of ORF3 as a query did not identify any homologous proteins. We therefore tested the possibility that alphafusarivirus PaFV1 (Nerva et al., 2017), the phylogenetically closest known virus to AchFV1, may have a second genomic segment. The short-read data used for sequence analysis of PaFV1 was obtained, and de novo assembly was performed using rnaSPAdes (Kanhayuwa et al., 2015). A tblastn search was performed on the obtained contig sequences using the amino acid sequence of AchFV1 ORF3 as a query, but no hits were found in the sequences. There are two possible reasons: while PaFV1 has a homolog of AchFV1 ORF3, the similarity is too low for it to be detected; or, PaFV1 does not have an ORF3 homolog in its genome. Further study is needed to understand the diversity of fusariviruses with a segmented genome.

In this study, two antiviral drugs (2CMC and ribavirin) were used with the aim of eliminating viruses from fungi. As a result, AchVV1, AchPmV1, or AchMV1-free isolates were obtained from A. chevalieri JCM23062. However, no AchCV1 or AchFV1-free isolates were obtained. No virus was eliminated from A. chevalieri JCM23063. Although a chrysovirus and a mitovirus have been reported to be removed from A. fumigatus by 2CMC (Ikeda et al., 2022), we were unable to eliminate AchCV1 from A. chevalieri in this study. This result suggests that even viruses belonging to the same family have different susceptibilities to antiviral drugs depending on the host.

Comparison of the growth of A. chevalieri JCM23062 and isogenic strains lacking AchPmV1 suggested that AchPmV1 suppressed the initial growth of the host on MM20SA medium. Polymycoviruses have a variety of effects on their hosts, such as promotion of growth and conidia formation (Wang et al., 2023), induction of sectoring (Kotta-Loizou and Coutts, 2017b), reduction of phytopathogenicity (Jia et al., 2017; Zhai et al., 2016), promotion of pigmentation (Filippou et al., 2021), and increase of nikkomycin Z sensitivity (G. Sass et al., 2023). A polymycovirus has been shown to cause decreased growth of A. fumigatus (Takahashi-Nakaguchi et al., 2020; G. Sass et al., 2023); AchPmV1 could be the second known polymycovirus that inhibits the growth of Aspergillus spp. Note that we cannot deny the possibility that the growth delay was induced by interaction of several mycoviruses (Hillman et al., 2018). Moreover, the involvement of mycoviruses that was not detected by FLDS method in this study, cannot be ruled out.

Enzymes produced by katsuobushi molds play a major role in the formation of the unique flavor of karebushi, and A. chevalieri is one of the dominant species in the fermentation (Kadooka et al., 2020; Takenaka et al., 2020). Therefore, AchPmV1, which affects the growth of A. chevalieri, may affect the quality of dried bonito flakes in katsuobushi fermentation. However, this study only assessed the effect of AchPmV1 in agar culture; further studies are needed to clarify the effect of AchPmV1 on katsuobushi molds in conditions of katsuobushi fermentation. In addition, the results of this study were from nine strains of katsuobushi molds, and further exploration is needed to understand the full diversity of mycoviruses that infect katsuobushi molds.

Considering that filamentous fungi are widely infected with mycoviruses, it is of industrial importance to clarify the impact of mycoviruses on fermentation. This study is the first to elucidate the diversity of mycoviruses of filamentous fungi involved in food fermentation. If it becomes clear that useful functions are induced or inhibited by viruses, it will be possible to develop fungal functions by using or curing mycoviruses. Mycoviruses have been applied only for biological control of phytopathogenic fungi, and most of them are untapped resources. Further studies should be conducted to determine the functional effects of mycoviruses on filamentous fungi involved in food fermentation.
