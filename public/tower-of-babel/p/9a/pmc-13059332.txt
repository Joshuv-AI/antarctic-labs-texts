Could home management of COVID-19 cases alleviate strain on the fragile healthcare system in Damascus, Syria: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Marah Alsalkini, Ahmad Alkhaledi, Khaled Awad, Kassem Awad, Haitham Housni Zamzam, Ibrahem Hanafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global impact of COVID-19 was devastating, particularly in low-income countries. Conflict zones like Syria, already plagued by political and socioeconomic instability, faced additional hardships, resulting in a fast collapse of the healthcare system. The overwhelmed system necessitated the implementation of home-based management as a practical solution to alleviate strain on hospitals. This study documents the efforts of a volunteer team during the initial COVID-19 wave in Damascus, Syria (August-September 2020) in implementing management through 770 home visits and evaluates its outcomes in terms of recovery, hospitalization, and mortality three weeks after. Out of the moderate and severe cases managed, 81.4% (n = 276) reported full or partial recovery, with only 6.6% (n = 19) persistently experiencing low peripheral blood oxygen levels at follow-up. Nonetheless, 97 patients (28.6%) necessitated hospitalization, 49 (14.5%) were admitted to intensive care units, and 52 (15.3%) died. Age, male gender, and cardiac comorbidities were associated with a higher need for oxygen support and led to higher hospitalization and mortality rates. These home visits effectively steered most patients toward recovery and facilitated the timely identification and management of critical cases until their transition to hospital care when capacities allowed, proving to be a significant asset to the healthcare system in the face of such challenging circumstances.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12941-3.Keywords: COVID-19, Pandemic, Home management, Syrian conflict, Low-income countries, Mortality, Morbidity

Introduction

Soon after confirming the first cluster of COVID-19 cases in China [1], the number of infected patients increased worldwide, reaching more than 64 M cases and 1.5 M deaths by the end of November 2020 [2]. Although the pandemic posed a challenge for healthcare systems everywhere, no adequate attention was given to the situation low – and middle-income countries as most published research was produced in high-income countries [3, 4]. Populations of low-income countries suffered from greater disease incidence and mortality due to fragile healthcare systems, limited resources, lack of trust in the medical institutions, inadequate awareness, poor socioeconomic status, and poor compliance with governmental preventive measures [5, 6]. Moreover, the political and economic insecurity in conflict areas necessitated more than strict regulations and mass testing to contain the pandemic. For instance, displaced populations in the Middle East suffered from the unstable accessibility to basic healthcare services, fueling the spread of the disease [7–10].

In Syria, a decade of ongoing armed conflicts, displacement, and resource depletion exaggerated the situation and left the Syrian population to face the novel pandemic with an exhausted healthcare system. The Syrian crisis resulted in the destruction of healthcare facilities, migration of medical personnel, unavailability of medical supplies, and inadequate medical training [11–13]. Additionally, the forced sanctions aggravated the financial collapse and the shortage of drugs and medical equipment that were essential to cope with the pandemic [14–17]. The ongoing crisis and warfare in limited resources countries like Afghanistan and more specifically in Syria have significantly worsened socio-economic conditions, pushing many families into poverty and likely increasing the reliance on child labor. With the added burden of the COVID-19 pandemic, it is possible that children in affected households were further pushed into work to support their families, contributing to the cycle of poverty and inequality [18, 19]. On top of that, population displacement, soldiers’ transition around the country, and the lack of adequate testing, the hesitancy among the public towards COVID-19 vaccination and reporting exacerbated the spread of the disease and made it difficult to estimate the gravity of the pandemic [4, 8, 12, 20, 21].

]After the first case of COVID-19 in Syria was confirmed in March 2020 [22], the government issued preventive measures to control the spread of the disease. These restrictions lasted for a few months but were then gradually lifted to prevent further economic deterioration [12]. This led to the first overwhelming wave of COVID-19 cases, which lasted from June to August 2020 and was most critical in Damascus [23, 24]. The immense flow of cases in the Syrian capital during this period was beyond hospitals’ capacity, and many patients with mild to moderate cases were denied admission due to either lack of availability or the need to prioritize more severe cases. In addition, the shortage of intensive care unit (ICU) beds and mechanical ventilators as well as the poor management of personnel and supplies led to a soaring mortality rate [25], which demanded supportive outpatient solutions to contain the situation.

To avoid healthcare collapse worldwide, the World Health Organization (WHO) emergency guidelines recommended considering homecare and isolation for mild COVID-19 cases in situations with insufficient hospitalization capacity or medical resources [26]. Nevertheless, given the challenges in standardizing and documenting home visits, studies that explored the effectiveness of home treatment were few in developed countries and quite rare in developing countries, where they were most needed. Several of them also investigated telemedicine, which was shown to lessen the burden on medical services and enhance timely treatment [27] However, this was not feasible in many situations, such as the one in Syria, due to the poor infrastructure (i.e., power shortage and internet connection troubles) as well as the limited experience in implementing these methods. Alternatively, home visits offered a more feasible approach that could effectively support the management of mild cases, reducing the need for hospitalization and preserving dear resource [28, 29]. This study aims to put into words the experience of a voluntary medical team that solely provided homecare for COVID-19 patients in the Syrian capital and to assess the effectiveness of this approach during the first pandemic wave in terms of hospitalization and patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and settings

This prospective cohort study was conducted in Damascus and its suburbs between August and September 2020, covering the second half of the first COVID-19 wave in Syria [21]. It recruited patients who were home managed by the Syrian Medical Team (SMT) after complaining about COVID-19-like symptoms. The SMT was established in August 2020 simultaneous to the accumulation of COVID-19 cases in Syria and the publication of reports about the poor preparedness of the healthcare system to resist the impact of the new pandemic [4, 30, 31]. The mission of the team was to support the healthcare system in Damascus and to ameliorate the burden of the pandemic on Damascus inhabitants. Using social media platforms, the team raised awareness about COVID-19 symptoms, prevention, diagnostics, and when to seek care. The team also provided free-of-charge professional home management for suspected COVID-19 cases when the number of patients exceeded the hospitalization capacity of Damascus medical centers. The team consisted of 20 graduate resident physicians, four senior medical students, and 31 other team members, whose work for the team was completely voluntary. However, SMT received logistic support from the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and the Syrian Family Planning Association (SFPA), which included a vehicle, oxygen cylinders, handheld pulse oximeters, emergency bags, and sanitizers.

Patients’ recruitment

Inhabitants of Damascus or its suburbs who experienced COVID-19 symptoms were able to approach the SMT through two hotlines. Phone calls were answered by volunteers who were trained to classify the calls into three categories based on the recommendations of the latest WHO guidelines [32]: (1) calls regarding patients with mild cases, which were immediately transferred to doctors for telephone medical council and recommendations; (2) calls regarding critical cases with red flags like loss of consciousness or cyanosis, which were asked to immediately present to the nearest medical center; and (3) calls regarding moderate to severe cases that required professional medical care but were unlikely to be admitted for in-patient care due to the lack of capacity. Therefore, home visits by a resident physician were scheduled for patients within this third category.

Patient visits, diagnosis, and management

This study reported only COVID-19 patients who received home visits and were diagnosed by SMT members. Those who received another diagnosis upon the home visit were excluded. Multiple patients within the same household who met the inclusion criteria were reported separately. After collecting the demographic characteristics, medical history and complaints and performing the clinical examination. The physician arranged for necessary laboratory and radiological tests and adjusted the management based on the results. This further communication was performed mostly via WhatsApp, as the limited logistic resources did not allow for repeated home visits for all patients. Patients who could not afford these tests were managed based solely on their clinical presentation. Patients were prescribed therapeutic options that ranged from oral symptom-relieving medications to oxygen therapy, noninvasive ventilation, and intravenous antibiotics. Patients with peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) in room air between 80 and 92% received oxygen support, and those with SpO2 < 80% and refused to be transferred to the hospital received noninvasive mechanical ventilation at home. The target saturation to stop supplementary oxygen support was 92%. The equipment (e.g., an oxygen cylinder, a regulator, and masks) as well as necessary instructions to administer, monitor, and adjust ventilation support were also appropriately delivered to the caregivers of every critical case with close remote follow-up by the treating physician. Patients’ caregivers were responsible for funding and exchanging the oxygen cylinders when empty. On the other hand, a limited number of patients had critical cases (SpO2 < 80%) at the time of home visit and were referred immediately to the nearest available public hospital. However, a few patients with critical conditions refused hospitalization for several reasons, including fear of hospitals, immobility, and lack of accompanists, and accepted the risks of being managed at home based on their will.

The managing physicians documented the findings during the visit on a structured paper questionnaire designed for this study (Supplementary 1). The collected data included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical manifestations, vital signs, as well as the indicated medications and ventilation support. The size of the household and the number of residents and suspected cases in it were also collected.

Follow-up

Follow-up phone calls for all recruited patients were performed two to three weeks after the initial visit. Yet, due to the extensive number of phone calls and the time burden imposed by these follow-up conversations on our team, we were limited to a single outreach attempt per household. The follow-up calls were also conducted by a resident physician based on a structured paper questionnaire designed to cover the aims of this study (Supplementary 1). During these calls, the patients or their caregivers were asked about hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality in the period after the home visit. They were also asked to self-report information about the progression of their symptoms, the appearance of symptoms on other household members, and compliance with self-isolation.

Data entry, and analysis

The collected anonymous data was entered into Microsoft Excel 365 version 2011 (year 2020) and then imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Counts and percentages were used to report categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to test the association between variables. An Alpha value of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance with the Bonferroni correction to correct for multiple comparisons.

Ethics approval and participant consent

Ethical approval was granted for this study by the institutional review board of Damascus University (No. MD130623-106). All experiments were conducted in compliance with applicable guidelines and regulations, adhering to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki 2013 [33]. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary and had no impact on the medical care provided to the individuals. Informed consent for study participation was obtained from all patients during both the home visits and subsequent follow-up calls. Furthermore, explicit consent for the publication of the study findings was obtained during the home visits and follow-up communications.

Results

Population characteristics

During the study period, SMT received more than ten thousand phone calls; however, only 770 of them were eligible for home visits. Approximately one-third of the patients visited (n = 282, 36.6%) were above 65 years old. More than half of them (n = 391, 53.9%) lived with four or more household members, and 68.5% of them (n = 447) had a maximum of three rooms in their household. At the time of the home visits, more than 70% of the patients reported at least one more household member suspected of having COVID-19. Hypertension (n = 330, 42.9%) followed by diabetes mellitus (n = 193, 25.1%) were the most common comorbidities in the sample (Table 1).

Symptoms, vital signs, and management

The most commonly reported complaint was weakness (n = 661, 85.8%), followed by fever (n = 498, 64.7%) and dyspnea (n = 392, 50.9%). The average respiratory rate was 23.9 ± 6.5, while the SpO2 averaged 94.1 ± 6.3. Almost one-third of the patients required ventilation support (n = 209, 27.1%), which was mostly oxygen support (n = 199, 25.9%). One hundred twenty patients (15.5%) were prescribed antibiotics other than azithromycin; most of them received levofloxacin or ceftriaxone (Tables 2 and 3).

Follow-up findings

Three-quarters of the patients who could be followed (n = 259, 76.4%) still did not have a confirmed diagnosis by means of any diagnostic modalities, and 17% of those who received a confirmed diagnosis underwent only computed tomography (CT) scans. COVID-19 symptoms were completely or partially recovered most patients (n = 276, 81.4%), all patients reported improvement of their dyspnea, and only 19 (6.6%) patients still had oxygen saturation below 90% (Table 4). Only 19 patients (5.6%) reported not following the provided self-isolation instructions, and poor income was the most common reason for that. However, more than half of the patients reported new COVID-19 symptoms in at least one member of their households (n = 174, 51.3%), who did not have these symptoms at the time of the home visit; fifty-seven of them (16.8%) reported that for more than three of their household members.

Hospitalization and ICU admission occurred in 97 (28.6%) and 49 (14.5%) patients, respectively. Fifty-two patients (15.3%) died during the follow-up period, and the majority of them (n = 52, 90.4%) died after being hospitalized and admitted to an ICU. Overall, approximately 50% of the patients who were admitted to the hospital died (n = 47/97) in comparison to 2% of those who were completely managed at home (n = 5/242; P < 0.001). While 42 patients out of those who entered the ICU (85.7%) died, only five of those who were managed on the normal isolation ward (10.4%; P < 0.001; Fig. 1).

Most patients who did not receive home oxygen support (n = 195, 86%) did not require hospitalization, only nine (17.3%) died during the follow-up period, and only two had SpO2 of less than 90% at the follow-up. On the other hand, death occurred one third (n = 43, 38.4%) of the patients who received home oxygen support, and hospitalization was needed in approximately 60% of them. Symptoms including dyspnea improved significantly less than in the group without home oxygen support (P < 0.001). However, 52 of those who did not die (75%) had SpO2 of at least 90% by the end of the follow-up period (Table 4).

Risk factors for outcomes

Home oxygen support, hospitalization, ICU admission, and death occurred significantly more frequently in males (P < 0.001). While no patients under 45 years entered an ICU or died, almost one-third of the old adults were admitted to an ICU and died (P < 0.001). Also, about half of the old adult patients needed oxygen support (P < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus and cardiac comorbidities were significantly associated with more home oxygen support, hospitalization, ICU admission, and death. Notably, tachycardia, tachypnea, and the need for home oxygen support at the time of home visits were significantly associated with hospitalization, ICU admission, and death later (Table 5).

Discussion

This study documented the voluntary efforts of SMT in the home management of COVID-19 patients during the first two months of their response to the first wave of the pandemic in Damascus. The urgent need for such efforts and the heavy workload was expected in an overpopulated city facing economic collapse and severely weakened healthcare system, where the demand for care far exceeded the available hospital capacity. Diagnosing cases were challenging as it relied primarily on clinical presentation. The visited cases had mostly moderate severity, and more than a quarter of them required home oxygen support. Nevertheless, more than 70% of the patients visited reported full or partial recovery within three weeks and did not require hospitalization, suggesting significant alleviation of the burden on medical centers in Damascus.

The poor preparedness of the Syrian healthcare system for the pandemic rang the alarm bell early [4, 30, 34]. According to the human rights watch, COVID-19 cases exceeded hospitalization capacity soon in the first wave of the pandemic, forcing healthcare workers to deny admission to a decent number of patients [34–36]. Prioritizing the most severe cases for admission significantly delayed the possibility of hospitalizing less critical cases [25]. Private care was also out of the reach of the majority of the patients due to the collapsed economy [13, 37]. Similar scenarios were reported in countries with similar dramatic deterioration of the economy, such as the neighbor Lebanon [10]. The shift to public sector hospitals intensified the needs and created further shortages in supplies and personnel, which resulted in exaggerated mortality rates during hospitalization [25]. As a result, public fear increased, and the motivation to present to emergency rooms diminished, creating a preference for home visits [10]. Therefore, recruiting medical staff to implement a home visit and management system was a necessity to alleviate the pressure on public hospitals and to reduce the delay in COVID-19 case management. With this aim, the SMT organized the voluntary efforts of more than twenty physicians, received more than 160 telephone requests per day, and arranged 770 home visits during the first two months of its work (i.e., the second half of the first wave of the pandemic in Damascus). Those numbers align with a previous study that used mathematical simulations to expect that approximately 39% of the population of Damascus had been infected by September 2020 [38].

It should be noted that although the team was swiftly initiated and organized two months after the start of the first wave, the limited personal and logistic assets as well as the great load of requests forced the team also to prioritize and reserve the available human capacities to visit relatively more severe cases. This was clearly reflected in our sample, which was predominated by older adults with several comorbidities. For instance, 40% of our sample had hypertension, and a quarter were diabetic in comparison to 25% and 7%, respectively, in a home-treated sample in Italy. Moreover, dyspnea, which reflected disease severity, was reported in the Italian sample in less than one-third of the cases compared to half of them in our sample [39]. Although recommendations advised restricting home management to mild cases [40], our circumstances necessitated extending it to moderate and sometimes severe cases, as it was the only option available. It is worth mentioning that the long-term Syrian armed conflict and the relative safety of Damascus city resulted in a condensed population, leading to difficulty in breaking the circle of infection and containing the pandemic. Additionally, self-isolation was not easy to achieve due to the high person per room metric, especially among displaced populations [41]. This can explain surprisingly high transmission within households (> 50%). It has been shown that household and family members have an approximately 20% higher probability of being infected, especially in the presence of symptomatic patients [42]. In countries where over 25% of the population lives in poor crowded housing conditions and is unable to follow basic protective measures such as sanitation and social distancing like in Mexico, Lithuania, and Latvia showed yet greater transmission rates [43]. Unfortunately, the application of these measures in Syria was also quite challenging and, in some cases, not feasible due to the misinformed situation about the pandemic [13, 36, 38], poor health promotion efforts, cultural context, and most importantly poor economic status [16].

COVID-19 diagnosis was an absolute challenge due to the inaccessibility and unavailability of diagnostic modalities. Thus, about two-thirds of our sample were diagnosed merely based on clinical presentation. Nevertheless, the findings remain robust as the diagnostic tests were also not available for a significant proportion of hospitalized patients in Damascus public hospitals due to a shortage of supplies and poor coordination between COVID-19 centers [25]. Yet, mortality and respiratory failure rates were not significantly different between patients who were diagnosed based on the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) gold standard and those who were admitted merely based on their clinical presentation [25]. Moreover, although contamination with other infections cannot be completely excluded, the likelihood of including a significant number of non-COVID-19 cases is low given the clinical presentation, mortality rates, and high transmissibility. Finally, among patients who underwent diagnostic testing, only three were ultimately not diagnosed with COVID-19. This likely reflects an underestimation of COVID-19 incidence in official reports [36, 44], especially when we compare it to countries such as Italy, where the pandemic was reported to be “catastrophic” and only 16% of the home-treated patients were not diagnosed via PCR test [39]. On top of that, this diagnostic uncertainty possibly had inevitable therapeutic consequences, as antibiotics in our sample were prescribed more frequently than in other neighbouring countries [45]. It can be speculated that this pattern may stem from limited access to PCR testing during home visits, which led healthcare providers to prescribe antibiotics without confirmed diagnoses. Similar studies in Egypt and Ethiopia have also reported frequent antibiotic use for COVID-19 patients without bacterial confirmation [46, 47]. The overprescription may also reflect prophylactic use, driven by the high risk of secondary infections with antibiotic-resistant strains associated with the prolonged effects of the Syrian conflict [48].

About a quarter of our patients had respiratory insufficiency requiring home oxygen support. This happened significantly more in patients with risk factors such as being older or male or having diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or cardiac comorbidities. Patients who received oxygen support had higher hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality rates than their counterparts who did not require oxygen support at the time of home visit. Nevertheless, the follow-up of these critical patients showed that around 40% did not require hospitalization, 75% reached SpO2 of at least 90%, none of them reported persistent dyspnea, and more than 50% of them reported partial or complete recovery. These findings were not as good as the findings of an Italian study that reported positive outcomes for oxygen support at home even in moderate and severe COVID-19 cases, as all 18 patients fully recovered with no deaths [49]. This difference can be explained by the small sample size in the Italian study or by the fact that caregivers were in our case responsible for funding and providing oxygen support, a relatively great economic and logistic challenge for most Syrian families.

Twenty-nine of our patients were hospitalized, and 15% of them were admitted to an ICU during the follow-up period. These numbers were relatively high in comparison to other home-managed COVID-19 samples in Italy, France, and Belgium, where the hospitalization rate ranged between two and four percent [39, 44, 50]. They were also higher than other countries with limited resources like Pakistan, as 11% of the home-treated patients there required hospital admission [51]. The higher need for hospitalization in this study can be due to the limited assets available to the SMT and the great number of requests it received, which led the team to discount home visits for less severe cases, leading to proportionally worse outcomes for the whole sample. Nevertheless, it should be noted that critical cases, which needed hospitalization and ICU admission later, could already be identified at the time of the home visit. Demographic characteristics (e.g., male sex and older age), comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus and cardiac morbidity), and vital signs (e.g., tachycardia and tachypnea) were major determinants of these outcomes. These findings are quite important because improving home treatment algorithms and patients’ classification measures comes second on the priority scale after resolving the shortages in hospitalization capacity and diagnostic and therapeutic medical resources [52].

Fifteen percent of our patients died during the follow-up period, which is relatively high compared to none in similar studies in China [27], Italy [39, 49], and Pakistan [51]. However, it should be noted that 90% of the deaths occurred during hospitalization, leaving a mortality rate of only 2% for patients who were completely managed at home. This percentage is much lower than the mortality rate reported for hospitalized patients in Damascus [25]. It also showcases the tremendous effectiveness of the endeavor of the SMT when compared to the mathematical simulation of the situation of COVID-19 in Damascus, which expected 4,380 unreported COVID-related deaths only in Damascus by September 2020 [38]. Finally, taking into consideration the severe presentation and high comorbidity in our sample compared to previous home treatment reports [39, 44, 49, 51], we think that the home management system put in application by the SMT was able to significantly alleviate the burden of the pandemic on public hospitals and to lessen the psychological strain and anxiety among the patients [10].

One limitation of our study could be the high loss of follow-up, which was inevitable as the limited number of staff and the enormous workload did not allow for several attempts to reach the patients by phone. However, it is unlikely that our findings are significantly affected by attrition bias because the loss of follow-up ratio was equal among patient groups (Fig. 1), and the number of patients followed still exceeded the number of patients reported in other reports [27, 44, 49, 51].

Conclusion

Organized voluntary efforts for the home management of COVID-19 cases represented a temporary but essential coping mechanism that helped alleviate pressure on the Syrian healthcare system during the pandemic. The enormous workload of responding to the pandemic is exaggerated in densely populated areas, especially when compounded by displaced populations, subpar sanitation, and fragile healthcare infrastructure. Consequently, prioritizing home visits becomes imperative and can steer the majority of visited patients toward either full or partial recovery within three weeks, and reserve in-patient management for the more severe cases. This approach not only offers a means to alleviate strain on hospital resources but also serves as a crucial mechanism for the timely identification and management of critical cases, ensuring their safe transition to hospital care when hospitalization capacities permit. In the face of adversity, employing organized volunteer efforts in home management reflects the potential of collective action in the pursuit of public health.
