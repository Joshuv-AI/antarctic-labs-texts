CT-derived proximal tibial bone density imbalance is associated with knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Hogyeong Kang, Geonil Kim, Sungjoon Lim, Soonchul Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompartmental imbalance in proximal tibial bone density may contribute to the structural progression of knee osteoarthritis (OA), yet remains underexplored using quantitative imaging. This study investigated the association between compartmental proximal tibial bone density, measured using CT-derived Hounsfield units (HU), and knee OA.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 753 knees from patients who underwent lower-extremity CT, knee radiography, and orthoradiogram between 1995 and 2024 at a single tertiary center. The knees were categorized as OA (N = 238) or non-OA (N = 515) based on expert image review. The HU values were measured in three compartments: the medial plateau, lateral plateau, and central metaphysis. The absolute and relative HU differences between compartments (e.g., medial to lateral) were calculated. Associations with OA were assessed using multivariate logistic regression, adjusted for age and sex. The diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsIn unadjusted comparison, OA-affected knees showed lower HU across proximal tibial subregions than non-OA knees. Higher medial HU values (OR per 100 HU: 1.49; p = 0.016) and greater medial-to-lateral HU differences (OR: 1.27; p = 0.089) were associated with OA. These relative differences were exaggerated with advancing age. Medial HU values showed moderate discriminative power (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.682), whereas medial-to-lateral differences had limited standalone performance (AUC = 0.553).ConclusionsKnees with OA were associated with asymmetric remodeling patterns of the proximal tibia, including higher medial subchondral density and lower metaphyseal bone density, suggestive of a compartmental imbalance. CT-derived HU values, along with absolute and relative compartmental differences, may serve as exploratory radiologic markers to support OA risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06302-0.Keywords: Proximal tibia, Bone density, Knee osteoarthritis, Genu varum, Hounsfield unit

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) and osteoporosis (OP) are among the most common and disabling musculoskeletal conditions, especially in postmenopausal women and the elderly [1, 2]. OA involves focal cartilage loss and subchondral bone remodeling [3], while OP is characterized by systemic bone mass reduction [4]. This relationship remains complex and context-dependent, with some studies suggesting that subchondral sclerosis in OA may reflect compensatory changes with potential implications for bone strength or fracture susceptibility [5, 6]. Contrarily, others propose that systemic osteopenia may exacerbate joint degeneration via compromised load distribution and structural instability [7–9].

In the knee, where medial compartment OA with varus alignment predominates [10], mechanical loading asymmetry is central to disease progression [11]. Varus-aligned knees impose disproportionate axial loads on the medial tibiofemoral compartment [12, 13], driving subchondral sclerosis [14], cartilage attrition [15], and osteophyte formation [16]. Conversely, the lateral tibial plateau and adjacent metaphyseal regions often experience relative unloading and may undergo stress shielding, resulting in localized bone loss [17]. Such patterns suggest heterogeneous proximal tibial bone mineral density (BMD) changes with possible effects on malalignment and disease progression [18]. Yet, most studies have assessed global BMD via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or qualitative imaging, with few quantifying compartmental trabecular density variations [19–21]. The central metaphyseal region, despite its location between the load-bearing tibial plateau and diaphyseal shaft, and its potential role in mechanical subsidence and varus progression, remains underexplored [21]. A better understanding of regional bone density imbalance across this area may provide critical insights into the development of varus deformity and axial collapse in progressive OA.

Computed tomography (CT) allows opportunistic assessment of trabecular bone via Hounsfield units (HU), correlating with volumetric BMD and enabling spatial analysis without extra imaging [22–25]. Unlike radiography, which cannot quantify regional BMD, or MRI, which has limited accuracy for mineralized bone, CT provides high-resolution, compartment-specific, and depth-resolved quantification of proximal tibial density, making it particularly suited for evaluating the hypothesized compartmental imbalance in OA [26–30]. The capacity of CT-derived HU mapping to capture biomechanically relevant proximal tibial gradients in OA is largely untested.

This study investigates compartment-specific proximal tibial density patterns associated with OA using CT-derived HU values. We hypothesized that OA would be associated with characteristic compartmental imbalances, potentially serving as radiologic markers for early detection and risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cross-sectional study included patients who underwent lower-extremity CT, knee radiography, and orthoradiogram between 1995 and 2024 at the CHA Bundang Medical Center in Seongnam, Republic of Korea. CT examinations were obtained for clinical indications (e.g., evaluation of knee pain, deformity/alignment assessment, or preoperative planning) and not for OA screening or research; HU values were retrospectively extracted from these existing images for quantitative analysis. The initial dataset comprised 2,480 knees. The cohort selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Exclusion criteria were applied sequentially to eliminate conditions that could compromise the accurate assessment of the proximal tibial bone density. Exclusion criteria included tibial plateau fractures; proximal tibial bone tumors and primary cystic lesions (e.g., simple bone cyst, aneurysmal bone cyst); prior surgery or implants involving the proximal tibia (e.g., ligament reconstruction, fracture fixation, or arthroplasty); and poor-quality imaging. Degenerative subchondral bone cysts related to osteoarthritis were not exclusionary. Knees with isolated lateral compartment OA were excluded to focus on the more prevalent medial compartment OA pattern. A total of 753 knees from 697 unique participants were included. Among these, 238 knees were diagnosed with knee OA based on X-ray images showing Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2, as determined by experienced physicians, including musculoskeletal radiologists and orthopedic specialists. The remaining 515 knees that did not meet this radiographic criterion served as the non-OA control group (Fig. 1).

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA Bundang Medical Center (IRB No. 2022-12-025). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and use of anonymized clinical data.

Data collection and CT-based assessment of proximal tibial bone density

We extracted relevant clinical data, including age, sex, and medical history, from electronic medical records. The bone density of the proximal tibia was assessed by measuring the HU values on lower-extremity CT images (Fig. 2). All scans were performed using standardized acquisition and reconstruction parameters on institutional multidetector CT systems (e.g., Revolution CT; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) to ensure uniform HU quantification.

Measurement of proximal Tibia HU in CT images. Representative coronal CT slice at the midsagittal level of the proximal tibia showing the placement of three circular ROIs for trabecular bone density measurement: A Lateral tibial plateau, B Medial tibial plateau, and C Lower metaphyseal region, corresponding to the central proximal tibial metaphysis. All ROIs were placed within the trabecular bone to avoid cortical margins and artifacts. HU, Hounsfield unit; CT, computed tomography; ROI, region of interest

For each knee, a reference slice was selected in the coronal plane at the midsagittal level of the tibia, where the intercondylar eminence was centrally positioned between the medial and lateral condyles. On this slice, three circular regions of interest (ROIs) were placed within the trabecular bone: the medial tibial condyle, the lateral tibial condyle, and the central proximal tibial metaphysis (hereafter referred to as the ‘lower middle’ region) located just inferior to the tibial plateau and approximately aligned with the tibial tubercle in the coronal plane. ROI size was standardized to fit entirely within the trabecular bone, avoiding cortical margins, surgical artifacts, and pathological abnormalities, thereby ensuring consistent placement and reproducibility across all participants. The mean HU value from each ROI was recorded as a surrogate measure of the regional trabecular bone density. All measurements were performed using the INFINITT PACS software (INFINITT Healthcare Co., Seoul, Republic of Korea), and an experienced investigator reviewed the ROI placement for consistency.

In addition to the absolute HU values for each region (medial, lateral, and lower middle), we calculated the relative HU differences to quantify the imbalance in trabecular bone density between regions. For any two regions X and Y, the relative HU difference was computed as (HUX − HUY) / |HUY|. This method enables normalized comparisons even when the reference region’s HU value is low, preventing artificially inflated or undefined ratios that can occur in trabecular bone, where HU values may approach zero or become negative. For each knee, three key comparisons were performed: medial-to-lateral, lower middle-to-lateral, and lower middle-to-medial HU. A positive value indicates that region X is denser than region Y and is expressed as a ratio relative to Y’s HU.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R Statistical Software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as median (Q1, Q3) unless otherwise specified, while categorical variables are expressed as absolute counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted using group-wise summaries of age, sex, regional absolute HU values, and relative HU differences.

Participants were grouped into predefined age brackets, and comparisons were further stratified by sex to visualize age-related trends in HU values. Mean HU values and relative HU differences across the tibial regions were plotted by age group using line graphs and boxplots. Simple linear regression was used to evaluate the association between age and HU values, with R2 values and p-values reported for each model.

Both absolute HU values and relative HU differences were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex to assess the relationship between trabecular bone density and OA. As a sensitivity check for potential within-subject correlation from bilateral knees, we re-estimated all logistic models using participant-clustered (sandwich) standard errors at the participant level. To limit instability from collinearity among related predictors, each absolute HU measure and each relative HU difference was entered in separate models rather than jointly. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values were reported. Subgroup analyses were conducted separately for male and female participants. To reduce model dependence from linearity assumptions and to avoid extrapolation when covariate overlap is limited across ages, we additionally fit models with age modeled using natural cubic splines (df = 3) and repeated the regressions within prespecified age bands (< 50, 50–70, > 70 years) adjusting for sex. Variables with p-values less than 0.1 in the multivariable logistic regression models for the total population were included in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses. This threshold was chosen to capture not only statistically significant variables but also those with borderline significance, which may have clinical relevance. ROC analyses were limited to the total population to ensure sufficient statistical power and broader generalizability of the diagnostic performance. The model’s performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 753 knees were included in the final study cohort, comprising 238 knees with OA and 515 knees without OA (Table 1). These knees represented 697 unique participants, of whom 56 (8.03%) contributed both knees. The median age was 52.10 years (32.55, 71.36), with the OA group being older [72.35 (62.17, 81.80)] than the non-OA group [41.45 (28.89, 54.52)]. Females accounted for 69.6% of the total population, with a higher proportion in the OA group (81.1%) compared to the non-OA group (64.3%). In unadjusted comparison, across all three proximal tibial subregions, the CT-derived HU values were consistently lower in the OA group. The median lateral compartment HU was 64.79 (21.21, 110.73) in knees with OA and 128.66 (89.60, 169.80) in those without. Medial compartment values were 153.57 (102.68, 192.39) and 197.07 (144.66, 245.40), respectively, while the lower middle region showed values of − 2.00 (− 39.12, 32.78) in the OA group and 45.22 (1.53, 106.15) in the non-OA group. The medial-to-lateral HU difference was 0.63 (0.18, 1.28) in knees with OA and 0.51 (0.17, 0.93) in those without. Lower middle-to-lateral differences were − 0.85 (− 1.33, − 0.55) in the OA group and − 0.60 (− 0.94, − 0.28) in the non-OA group, while lower middle-to-medial differences were − 0.97 (− 1.18, − 0.79) and − 0.74 (− 0.97, − 0.52), respectively.

Age- and sex-based trends in proximal tibial HU values

Across all age groups, the medial compartment consistently showed the highest HU values, followed by the lateral and lower middle compartments (Suppl. Figure 1A). The HU values in all three subregions decreased with advancing age. Linear regression analyses demonstrated a statistically significant age-related decline in HU values in the lateral, medial, and lower middle compartments (all p < 0.001; Suppl. Figure 1B–D). When stratified by sex, this age-related decrease was consistently observed in both males and females across all compartments (Suppl. Figure 2). In each age group, HU values were generally higher in males than in females.

Relative differences in the proximal tibial HU values between the subregions also varied with age (Fig. 3A). The medial-to-lateral differences tended to increase across age groups, whereas both the lower middle-to-lateral and lower middle-to-medial differences decreased. Linear regression analyses showed a statistically significant increase in the medial-to-lateral difference with age (p = 0.00683; Fig. 3B) and significant age-related decreases in both the lower middle-to-lateral and lower middle-to-medial differences (both p < 0.001; Fig. 3C, D). When stratified by sex, the overall patterns of age-related changes in relative differences were generally similar between males and females (Suppl. Figure 3).

Association between proximal tibial HU values and OA risk

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between proximal tibial HU values and the presence of OA (Table 2). After adjusting for age and sex, higher medial compartment HU values were significantly associated with OA, with an OR of 1.004 per unit increase in HU (95% CI 1.001–1.007; p = 0.016). Every 100-unit increase in medial HU corresponds to a 49.06% increase in the odds of OA. A greater medial-to-lateral difference was also associated with increased OA odds, with an OR of 1.273 (95% CI 0.964–1.683; p = 0.089). In a sex-specific analysis adjusted for age, the association between medial compartment HU values and OA remained statistically significant in females (OR 1.004; 95% CI 1.001–1.008; p = 0.008). Spline-adjusted models produced estimates that were materially unchanged from linear-age models for both medial HU (OR 1.004; 95% CI 1.001–1.007; p = 0.018) and the medial-to-lateral ratio (OR 1.251; 95% CI 0.949–1.655; p = 0.113) (Suppl. Table 1). In age-stratified analyses, the medial-to-lateral ratio was associated with OA in the 50–70 year stratum (OR 1.618; 95% CI 1.105–2.403; p = 0.015), whereas medial HU was associated with OA in the > 70 stratum (OR 1.006; 95% CI 1.002–1.011; p = 0.010); no significant associations were observed in participants < 50 years (Suppl. Table 2). As a verification step, applying participant-clustered standard errors yielded identical OR estimates with minimal changes in precision—medial HU: OR 1.004, 95% CI 1.001–1.006, p = 0.014; medial-to-lateral ratio: OR 1.273, 95% CI 0.956–1.694, p = 0.098—leaving statistical interpretation unchanged.

Representative case illustrations of age-related changes in proximal tibial bone density and alignment

Three representative cases were selected from female patients aged 31, 53, and 85 years, all of whom underwent left-sided imaging to visually illustrate age-related changes in the proximal tibia (Fig. 5). The 31-year-old patient (Fig. 5A, D) had no significant degenerative findings and showed HU values of 166.4 (lateral), 170.4 (medial), and 66.8 (lower middle), with relative HU differences of 0.024 (medial-to-lateral), − 0.60 (lower middle-to-lateral), and − 0.61 (lower middle-to-medial). The hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle was 2.7° varus. The 53-year-old (Fig. 5B, E), who presented with degenerative changes and a subchondral cyst, had HU values of 72.5 (lateral), 192.6 (medial), and 15.1 (lower middle), with relative differences of 1.66, − 0.79, and − 0.92, respectively, and an HKA angle of 7.7° varus. The 85-year-old (Fig. 5C, F), with advanced OA and osteopenic changes, showed HU values of 12.9 (lateral), 304.3 (medial), and − 14.1 (lower middle), with relative differences of 22.55, − 2.09, and − 1.05, and an HKA angle of 14.5° varus. Across the three cases, the medial compartment HU values increased with age, whereas the HU values in the lateral and lower middle compartments decreased. The relative HU differences shifted accordingly, and mechanical alignment progressively worsened.

Discussion

In this retrospective cross-sectional study, we investigated the association between regional proximal tibial bone density and knee OA using CT-derived HU as a surrogate marker of trabecular bone density. Our findings revealed that the medial compartment HU values were consistently higher than those of the lateral and lower middle compartments across all age groups. Age-related trends demonstrated a significant linear decline in HU values in all regions. Relative HU differences between the compartments also varied with age; medial-to-lateral differences increased, whereas lower middle-to-lateral and lower middle-to-medial differences decreased. In multivariate logistic regression analyses, higher medial HU and greater medial-to-lateral HU differences were associated with increased odds of OA. Medial HU values showed moderate discriminative power for identifying OA, whereas relative HU differences demonstrated limited diagnostic ability.

Age-related patterns showed that older individuals exhibited higher medial HU values alongside lower lateral and metaphyseal HU values, consistent with progressive load concentration in the medial compartment and disuse-related bone loss in unloaded regions. These changes may reflect cumulative mechanical adaptation over time, potentially amplifying compartmental density imbalances in advanced age. Sensitivity analyses (spline-adjusted and age-stratified models) yielded estimates that were materially consistent with the primary analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings. Intriguingly, age-stratified models suggested heterogeneity by life stage: the medial-to-lateral ratio was associated with OA in the 50–70 year stratum, whereas medial HU was associated with OA in the > 70 stratum. This pattern raises the possibility that relative compartmental imbalance may be an earlier marker of risk, while absolute medial densification reflects cumulative remodeling in later life. In sex-stratified analyses, the association between higher medial HU, greater medial-to-lateral HU differences, and OA was significant in females but not in males. This discrepancy may relate to sex-specific skeletal and hormonal influences on bone remodeling; in particular, estrogen deficiency in postmenopausal women accelerates trabecular bone loss in unloaded compartments, thereby magnifying relative medial density [31]. Males, with higher baseline bone mass and potentially more uniform load distribution, may display less pronounced compartmental disparities detectable in cross-sectional analysis [32]. Together, these findings suggest that both age- and sex-dependent remodeling trajectories contribute to proximal tibial density imbalance in OA, underscoring the value of future studies incorporating hormonal status, alignment measures, and longitudinal designs to clarify temporal and mechanistic links.

The compartmental variation and age-related changes in proximal tibial HU values are consistent with previous studies, which demonstrate that medial compartment sclerosis and metaphyseal osteopenia are characteristic features of OA progression [33, 34]. Wolff’s law states that bones adapt to increased mechanical loading, which commonly results in increased medial subchondral BMD in the knee joint owing to varus-aligned loading patterns [35, 36]. Our observation that medial HU is positively associated with OA aligns with earlier reports that localized subchondral sclerosis in the medial plateau reflects both adaptive and pathological remodeling in response to chronic overload [33]. This apparent paradox of higher HU values in the medial compartment, despite overall osteoarthritic bone changes, likely reflects localized subchondral sclerosis in response to increased axial loading. Furthermore, the finding that the medial-to-lateral HU difference correlated with the presence of OA suggests that imbalance, rather than absolute HU alone, could serve as an early marker of joint deterioration. Despite its modest AUC, the relative HU metric we introduced, capturing compartmental imbalance rather than absolute density, offers a novel radiological marker that may reflect early biomechanical deterioration in OA. This regionally normalized approach has the advantage of mitigating inter-individual variability and may enhance clinical interpretability when integrated with functional assessments, such as gait analysis or joint alignment. Further research is required to validate its utility in broader populations and imaging settings.

The co-occurrence of subchondral sclerosis and metaphyseal osteopenia may contribute to biomechanical instability observed in progressive OA. Subchondral bone stiffening reduces its shock-absorbing capacity, accelerating cartilage wear, while osteoporotic metaphyseal bone undermines structural support and may facilitate varus deformity [34, 37]. This hypothesis is supported by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based studies, which show that bone marrow lesions and trabecular deterioration are often localized to the medial plateau in varus-aligned knees [33, 38]. As shown in our schematic model (Fig. 6), a mechanical overload of the medial compartment can trigger subchondral sclerosis, whereas the metaphyseal and lateral regions may undergo stress shielding and bone loss. Over time, this imbalance may exacerbate the varus malalignment, reinforcing a vicious cycle of uneven loading, deformity progression, and OA advancement. Our schematic interpretation suggests that the fibula provides lateral column support, preventing collapse. In contrast, the medial portion of the lower metaphysis lacks reinforcement, potentially associated with asymmetric subsidence. This preferential collapse of the medial metaphyseal region under increasing axial load may contribute to genu varum deformities, particularly in older individuals. These observations align with the emerging concept of the “osteoporotic OA phenotype,” in which systemic or regional bone loss accelerates joint degeneration due to insufficient subchondral support [39]. In addition to bone density metrics, secondary structural features—such as medial and lateral osteophyte severity and compartment-specific joint space narrowing—could provide complementary insights into the proposed mechanism linking compartmental density imbalance to cartilage degeneration and OA progression. Although these parameters were not evaluated in the present study, integrating such compartment-specific structural assessments with HU-based measurements in future work may enable a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between subchondral bone remodeling, cartilage loss, and mechanical alignment changes.

This study is the first to quantitatively assess both absolute and relative proximal tibial HU values across a large cohort of knees by incorporating age- and sex-based subgroup analyses. The inclusion of a large sample with broad age and sex representations enhances the generalizability of our findings to real-world populations. Our use of simple, reproducible CT-based measurements provides a clinically accessible method for evaluating knee bone health. In clinical settings, regional HU patterns may be observed opportunistically on existing lower-extremity CT scans, providing supplementary imaging information that reflects the underlying biomechanical adaptations in patients with medial compartment OA. Moreover, our introduction of relative HU difference metrics provides a novel lens for characterizing the bone density imbalance in OA. These findings may inform future risk stratification strategies, particularly for aging populations.

This study had several limitations. First, we did not use DXA to directly quantify BMD. Because CT was obtained for clinical indications rather than OA screening, our cohort reflects an opportunistic CT population and may not generalize to patients who do not undergo CT in routine care. Additionally, as CT scans were acquired for clinical rather than research purposes, variability in patient positioning or scan indications may have introduced a subtle bias. However, standardized ROI placement mitigated this risk. Although CT-derived HU values correlate with BMD, they are not volumetric BMD measures and may be influenced by factors such as marrow composition, trabecular structure, and CT acquisition parameters [22, 40, 41]. Accordingly, HU should be interpreted as a surrogate indicator of regional bone density. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevented a causal inference between bone density changes and OA onset or progression. Third, we excluded lateral compartment OA to preserve homogeneity; however, this limited the generalizability of our findings to varus-dominant medial OA. Finally, while medial HU showed moderate discriminatory power, the novel relative HU metrics demonstrated limited diagnostic utility. Future studies should investigate whether combining HU-based metrics with clinical features, gait analysis, or biochemical markers can improve diagnostic accuracy or support prognostic modeling.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that regional differences in proximal tibial HU values were associated with the presence of knee OA, and that these values showed moderate ability to discriminate OA status. We introduced a novel relative HU metric capturing compartmental imbalance, demonstrating its association with OA and its potential as a regionally normalized imaging feature for future research. Additionally, we identified age- and sex-specific remodeling patterns, with aging associated with increased medial HU and decreased lateral and metaphyseal values. Although CT-derived HU values are not routinely applied in OA evaluation, our findings suggest that both absolute and relative compartmental HU measures may serve as exploratory imaging markers for OA detection and risk stratification. These results warrant further validation through longitudinal and biomechanical studies.
