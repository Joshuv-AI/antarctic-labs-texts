An analysis of the evolution of the key indicators of the Spanish health system
Authors: María D Fernández-Motilva, Eduardo Jiménez-Fernández, Ángeles Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study provides a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of regional health system performance in Spain over the period 2002–2023, using the INCLANS (Indicadores Clave del Sistema Nacional de Salud) framework. The analysis focuses on the evolution of composite indicators capturing key dimensions of health systems with particular attention to regional disparities and temporal dynamics. The empirical strategy combines the construction of metric-based composite indicators with panel data models, convergence analysis, and clustering techniques. The use of a distance-based aggregation framework allows for the development of cardinal and comparable indicators, enabling a consistent assessment of regional performance over time. Sigma and beta convergence measures are employed to examine whether disparities across autonomous communities are narrowing or widening, while clustering methods identify groups of regions with similar temporal trajectories. The results reveal a heterogeneous and multidimensional pattern of regional dynamics. Input-related dimensions, such as health expenditure, human resources, and infrastructure, exhibit persistent divergence, reflecting structural differences across regions within Spain’s decentralized health system. In contrast, outcome-related indicators, particularly mortality, display convergence patterns, suggesting a gradual homogenization of health outcomes despite increasing disparities in resource allocation.Keywords: Regional health systems, Territorial inequalities, Composite indicators, Sigma and beta convergence, Health system performance, ClusteringSubject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The assessment of health system performance has become increasingly prominent in recent decades, driven by the need to evaluate equity, responsiveness, and the efficiency of resource allocation across diverse governance frameworks1,2. Spain is territorially organized into 17 autonomous communities and two autonomous cities (Ceuta and Melilla), which correspond to the 19 NUTS 2 or regions of Eurostat. From this point forward, the term ‘regions’ will be used throughout the text. Administratively, Spain has three levels of government: central, regional, and local. In this work, we focus on the regional governments of the 17 regions that manage healthcare in Spain since 2002. Ceuta and Melilla have a distinct administrative status and their healthcare competencies are more directly linked to the central government. The 17 Spanish regions studied in this paper are Andalusia, Aragon, Asturias, Balearic Islands, Basque Country, Canary Islands, Cantabria, Castile-La Mancha, Castile and Leon, Catalonia, Extremadura, Galicia, La Rioja, Madrid, Murcia, Navarre, and Valencian Community. Thus, Spain, with its decentralized National Health Systen, offers a particularly relevant study, raising fundamental questions about the extent to which decentralization has contributed to convergence or divergence in health outcomes and resource allocation.

This paper aims to conduct a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of the Spanish regional health system performance, focusing on studyng the regional disparities and the temporal dynamics from 2002 to 2023. The empirical analysis is based on the Spanish Ministry of Health’s INCLANS (Indicadores Clave del Sistema Nacional de Salud) framework, which provides a comprehensive and structured basis for evaluating the performance of the National Health System across the 17 CCAA. INCLANS comprises 248 indicators organized into 18 domains, integrating benchmarks from the OECD, WHO, and the European Community Health Indicators (ECHI), alongside international best practices3. These indicators cover multiple dimensions of health system performance, including healthcare resources, accessibility, quality of care, and health outcomes. Given the multidimensional and high-dimensional nature of the framework, it constitutes a particularly suitable tool for assessing regional disparities, but also requires an appropriate aggregation strategy. For this reason, the present study focuses on a consistent subset of indicators and constructs eight health composite measures that allow for meaningful comparison of regional performance and its evolution over time.

The conceptual framework for the use of composite health indicators has been extensively developed in the literature. Our study builds upon the seminal framework proposed by4, which conceptualizes health system performance through three interdependent dimensions: health outcomes, responsiveness (including patient experience and accessibility), and financial fairness. This approach provides a comprehensive structure for evaluating health systems by synthesizing multidimensional information into composite indicators. It has been widely adopted and further developed by institutions such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), both of which have constructed monitoring systems based on comparable indicator frameworks. A prominent example is the OECD’s health system performance reports, which use composite indicators to assess access, quality, and system functioning across countries5. Similarly,6 analyze cross-country differences in health system performance by combining outcome and input variables within a composite indicator framework.

In the case of the INCLANS framework, the heterogeneity of indicators, the diversity of measurement scales, and the large number of variables require the implementation of a process of normalization and aggregation to enable meaningful comparisons. In addition, the period analyzed in this study captures key structural phases in the evolution of the Spanish health system, including the consolidation of decentralization, the fiscal adjustment period following the 2008 financial crisis, and the subsequent recovery phase. These stages provide a suitable context to assess whether regional disparities have narrowed or widened over time, and to evaluate the dynamics underlying the evolution of composite indicators across regions.

Regarding this, the growing literature on healthcare decentralization highlights a complex trade-off between regional autonomy and territorial equity. While decentralization may enhance responsiveness and adaptability to local needs, it can also generate persistent disparities in the absence of effective coordination and redistributive mechanisms. In particular,7 emphasize that the effects of decentralization on equity and performance in Italy are highly dependent on institutional design, whereas8 provide empirical evidence showing that fiscal decentralization may exacerbate regional inequalities in health outcomes when differences in fiscal capacity remain significant. These findings are particularly relevant to the Spanish context, where regional heterogeneity and the uneven distribution of resources appear to have shaped the long-term evolution of the healthcare system’s performance.

Focusing on Spain, previous studies have documented significant regional heterogeneity in both resource allocation and health system performance.9 analyze the impact of decentralization on regional disparities, highlighting differences in the availability and use of healthcare resources across regions. Similarly,10 examine the evolution of public healthcare expenditure, identifying substantial territorial disparities linked to differences in fiscal capacity and management practices. More recently11, assess the structural challenges of the Spanish health system during the COVID-19 crisis, emphasizing the role of prior austerity measures in shaping regional resilience and system responsiveness. This evidence can be framed within a broader strand of the literature that combines institutional analyses of decentralization12, empirical approaches incorporating explanatory determinants of efficiency and performance13, and studies focusing on equity and interregional disparities14, complemented by macro-level assessments provided by international and national institutions such as the OECD and the Spanish Independent Fiscal Institution.

Unlike previous contributions, which typically focus either on institutional features of decentralization or on efficiency and inequality separately, this paper provides an empirical assessment of regional disparities in the Spanish health system over time. Our approach explicitly documents the presence and evolution of territorial heterogeneity without imposing strong assumptions on the underlying causal mechanisms. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has provided empirical evidence of the divergence across Spanish regions. In doing so, this paper overcomes key limitations of the existing literature by ensuring temporal comparability and offering a more robust characterization of disparities across regions.

To undertake this, the analysis conducted in this paper adopts an approach based on the construction of eight normalized composite indicators using a distance-based aggregation framework15. A key advantage of this methodology is that the composite indicator is that constructed as a distance measure, which provides a well-defined quantitative scale. This is particularly important because most composite indicators are used as if differences in their values were meaningful, even though their construction does not guarantee such interpretation. As a result, standard practices such as comparing gaps between regions or analysing their evolution over time may lack a solid measurement foundation. In contrast, the proposed approach ensures that differences in the indicator have a consistent quantitative meaning, allowing for coherent comparisons across regions and over time. Building on this framework, the empirical strategy of the paper combines panel data models and convergence analysis to examine the evolution of regional disparities in health system performance. Panel data models are used to identify common temporal effects while controlling for unobserved regional heterogeneity, whereas sigma and beta convergence measures are employed to assess the evolution of disparities and the presence of catch-up dynamics. In addition, a clustering approach is implemented to identify groups of regions with similar trajectories across different dimensions of health system performance.

By integrating these complementary methodologies, the paper provides a multidimensional perspective on the evolution of Spain’s decentralized health system. In doing so, it contributes to the literature by jointly analyzing temporal dynamics, convergence processes, and regional heterogeneity within a unified framework. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how decentralization interacts with structural inequalities, offering relevant insights for both academic research and policy design.

This contribution is guided by a set of hypotheses concerning the longitudinal dynamics of regional health system performance in Spain. First, it is expected that significant regional disparities persist over time, reflecting structural differences across regionss. Second, the evolution of these disparities is not uniform, and the empirical evidence may reveal divergence patterns in some dimensions alongside convergence processes in others. Third, the coexistence of beta convergence and sigma divergence is anticipated, suggesting that although some regions may exhibit catch-up dynamics, overall inequality may not necessarily decrease. Fourth, the relationship between different dimensions of health system is likely to be non-proportional, implying that improvements in certain indicators do not automatically translate into overall performance gains. Finally, the temporal evolution of regional performance is expected to be heterogeneous, with distinct clusters of regions following differentiated trajectories rather than converging towards a common national pattern.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. “Methods” section presents the methodological framework, including the construction of composite indicators, the econometric specifications, and the clustering methodology. “Results” section reports the empirical results, focusing on temporal dynamics, convergence patterns, and regional groupings. Finally, “Conclusion” section concludes.

Specifically, eight composite indicators are constructed to capture the analyzed dimensions for the full list of the 17 regions. Healthcare resources are proxied by measures of human capital, including the number of specialized care physicians, primary care physicians, and nursing staff per 1,000 inhabitants. These variables are closely linked to accessibility and quality of care, and their regional distribution provides evidence on inequalities in resource allocation17,18. Technological capacity is captured through the number of CT scanners and MRI units per 100,000 inhabitants, reflecting the system’s diagnostic capabilities19. Hospital infrastructure is measured by the number of day-hospital and operational beds per 1,000 inhabitants, which relates directly to system capacity and waiting times20. Public health expenditure per protected inhabitant is included as a measure of financial effort at the regional level. Public healthcare expenditure should be understood as a financing concept rather than a production-based one. In most advanced health systems, including that of Spain, public funds are not exclusively allocated to publicly owned providers but also channelled toward private entities through various contractual arrangements. These mechanisms include service outsourcing, public–private partnerships, and reimbursement schemes, whereby private providers deliver services that are ultimately financed by the public sector. Consequently, the boundary between public and private healthcare becomes institutionally blurred, as the public sector retains the role of financier and regulator, while the actual provision of care may be carried out by a mix of public and private agents. This distinction is crucial for accurately interpreting healthcare expenditure data and for assessing the efficiency and equity implications of different health system arrangements. To ensure comparability over time, nominal expenditure is deflated using standard price indices, allowing the analysis to focus on real changes in resource allocation.

On the other hand, health outcomes and system performance are captured through healthy life years at birth, patient satisfaction indicators, waiting times for elective surgery, and the number of patients on waiting lists. These variables reflect population health, system responsiveness, and accessibility1. In addition, age-adjusted mortality rates for major causes of death–including diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and respiratory conditions–are used to provide a comprehensive assessment of health outcomes. While these outcomes are not solely determined by the healthcare system, there is substantial evidence linking effective healthcare provision and preventive policies to reductions in avoidable mortality21 (see Table 1).

Health composite indicators

To analyze the evolution of regional health indicators (INCLANS) across Spain over the period 2002–2023, this study adopts a framework specifically designed to address key methodological challenges in the measurement of health system performance. Central to this approach is the construction of a composite indicators based on an unsupervised distance-based machine learning algorithm15, in which normalized health metrics are aggregated through a norm defined in . This formulation ensures that the resulting indicators preserve a fully quantitative interpretation, allowing for meaningful and consistent comparisons across regions and over time.

Given the inherently multidimensional nature of health system performance, the construction of composite indicators is required to synthesize heterogeneous information into a single quantitative measure. This aggregation reduces dimensionality and enhances interpretability, while mitigating issues of collinearity and redundancy among variables. However, the choice of aggregation procedure is not innocuous, as it directly determines whether the resulting indicator retains a cardinal interpretation and whether comparisons across units and time are theoretically valid.

A key limitation of widely used approaches is that they do not rely on a well-defined metric structure in . As a consequence, the resulting indicators are often ordinal or lack a clear cardinal meaning, which restricts their applicability in longitudinal and econometric analyses. In contrast, the methodology adopted in this study is explicitly grounded in a normed vector space, ensuring that distances between observations are well-defined and that the resulting composite indicators are quantitatively interpretable, scale-consistent, and directly comparable across regions and over time.

According to this, we construct eight composite indicators using an unsupervised distance-based approach in which each region’s performance is defined as its distance to a reference point in a multidimensional space of standardized health indicators. In the context of this study, the higher the composite indicator value, the better the effort and performance of the health system. Thus, the reference vector for calculating the distance to the hypothetical worst-case region would be constructed using the minimum values of the indicators with positive polarity and the maximum values of those with negative polarity (see Table 1). Additionally, the weighting structure is determined through a method grounded in a formal mathematical norm, where weights are endogenously derived via a fixed-point convergence process, ensuring consistency and stability in the resulting aggregation scheme.

Intuitively, the method can be understood as follows. Each observation (region) is represented as a point in a multidimensional space, where each coordinate corresponds to one of the indicators. The composite indicator is constructed from the relative position of each region with respect to a worst or best (depending on the polarity) region. In this framework, the notion of “distance” captures how far an observation is from this reference, thereby aggregating multiple indicators into a single quantitative measure.

Additionally, the results are compared with those obtained from alternative aggregation methods, providing further evidence on the consistency and reliability of the proposed approach.

Convergence analysis

To further examine the evolution of regional health disparities, this study incorporates both σ- and β-convergence analyses27,28. On the one hand, σ-convergence evaluates whether the dispersion of health indicators across CCAA decreases over time, providing a direct measure of inequality dynamics. On the other hand, β-convergence assesses whether regions with initially lower levels of the composite indicator exhibit faster improvements relative to better-performing regions. Together, these measures allow for a consistent characterization of convergence or divergence patterns, which is essential for understanding the extent to which decentralization is associated with persistent territorial heterogeneity.

Hierarchical clustering

To conclude, hierarchical clustering is applied to classify regions based on similarities in their health indicator trajectories. We use unsupervised learning techniques to provides valuable insights into regional groupings, facilitating the identification of clusters with shared characteristics and common developmental patterns. This study applies two distinct clustering approaches depending on the temporal variability of the indicator. If the indicator exhibits significant variation over years, a longitudinal clustering approach is used, considering the temporal evolution of each region. Conversely, if the indicator remains relatively stable over time, a static clustering approach is adopted, grouping regions based solely on average values.

To determine the most appropriate clustering methodology, the coefficient of variation (CV) of the indicator is computed for each region. If the average CV exceeds a fixed threshold, it suggests substantial temporal variation, which warrants the use of time-series clustering techniques. In this case, Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance is computed between the temporal profiles of each region, followed by a k-means clustering procedure to group regions with similar indicator trajectories. The optimal number of clusters is determined using the elbow method applied to the DTW-based distance matrix31,32. This approach allows for the identification of regions that share common temporal patterns, enabling a dynamic understanding of health disparities.

If the coefficient of variation is below a fixed threshold, indicating the relative stability of the indicator, a static clustering approach is applied. In this case, hierarchical clustering is performed using the Ward.D2 method, which minimizes intra-cluster variance and ensures compact, well-separated clusters33. The optimal number of clusters is determined using the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS) method, and the results are visualized using dendrogram representations. This static clustering approach facilitates regional comparisons based on absolute health indicator values, rather than their temporal dynamics34,35.

Results

Health composite indicators

Drawing on the indicators grouped into eight dimensions (Table 1), we construct eight composite health indicators using a distance-based unsupervised machine learning methodology36. The longitudinal evolution of these indicators across regions reveals substantial heterogeneity in regional performance. Based on Min-Max normalization and subsequent aggregation via an norm, the indicators are bounded between 0 and 1, where values closer to 1 indicate a greater distance from a reference extreme, or from the hypothetical region with the worst indicator values. The reference minimum and maximum are computed over the entire historical sample rather than on a year-by-year basis, ensuring intertemporal comparability of the indicators. For composite indicators higher values denote a more favourable position relative to the best-worst-performing region observed over the period. Outlier detection based on the interquartile range (IQR) criterion reveals only a very limited number of extreme observations, concentrated in specific regions and indicators. In particular, a small number of cases are identified for the Canary Islands (ES.CN) in the Expenditure and Mortality indicators. Overall, the results suggest that outliers are not a widespread issue in the dataset. Nevertheless, given the use of min–max normalization, some extreme values (e.g., 0 or 1) may reflect boundary effects rather than genuine anomalies.

This methodological construction has important implications for interpretation. Since the composite indicators measure relative distances to sample extrema rather than absolute levels, temporal changes do not necessarily reflect improvements or deteriorations in absolute terms, but rather shifts in relative positioning within the distribution. Consequently, an increase in the compisite indicator may simply indicate divergence from the reference region, not necessarily an absolute gain in performance. The marked heterogeneity observed across regions, together with the coexistence of converging and diverging dynamics across different domains, underscores the limitations of purely descriptive analysis. Within the INCLANS framework, where indicators are cardinal and comparable across regions and time, this calls for an econometric approach capable of disentangling common temporal effects from persistent regional heterogeneity and of formally assessing the statistical significance of the observed disparities (see Fig. 1).

The human resources indicator shows moderate dispersion with a clear degree of persistence over time. Most regions are concentrated within an intermediate-to-high range indicating a relatively homogeneous distribution of healthcare personnel compared to other dimensions. However, the presence of sustained upper and lower tails suggests that differences in workforce availability are structurally embedded. Additionally, some convergence is observed in later years, with several regions moving towards higher values, possibly reflecting generalised expansion in healthcare staffing. At the upper end of the distribution, Navarra (ES.NC) emerges as the most prominent outlier. This indicates a structurally higher availability of healthcare personnel relative to the national benchmark. Other regions such as Castilla y León (ES.CL), Aragón (ES.AR), and the Basque Country (ES.PV) also display persistently high values. These regions maintain relatively stable trajectories over time, suggesting a sustained above-average endowment of both medical and nursing staff. At the lower end, Andalusia (ES.AN) stands out as a clear and persistent outlier, with values frequently below 0.3 throughout most of the period, indicating a systematically lower relative availability of healthcare personnel. Similarly, regions such as Murcia (ES.MC) and the Valencian Community (ES.CV) tend to occupy the lower segment of the distribution. Although some upward movement is observed over time, these regions remain consistently below the national average. Their relative position suggests structural differences in workforce allocation, potentially linked to population dynamics, budgetary constraints, or organisational models of care provision.

The distribution of the health equipament indicator reveals a persistent degree of regional heterogeneity, with several regions systematically positioned above or below the system average. While most regions fluctuate around intermediate values, a subset of regions consistently occupies the upper and lower tails of the distribution. This suggests that disparities in hospital capital endowment are structural rather than transitory, and that convergence dynamics, if present, are weak. Among the regions with the highest levels of the indicator, Madrid (ES.MD) stands out as a clear upper outlier throughout most of the period, which indicates a systematically higher relative concentration of hospital equipment. This pattern is coherent with its role as a central hub for highly specialized healthcare services and the presence of large tertiary hospitals. Similarly, Navarra (ES.NC) and the Basque Country (ES.PV) exhibit persistently high values, although with less volatility than Madrid. These regions combine relatively small population sizes with high levels of per capita investment in healthcare infrastructure, resulting in consistently above-average equipment endowment. Their position in the upper tail of the distribution appears stable over time, suggesting a structurally high level of technological capacity. At the lower end of the distribution, several regions display persistently low values of the indicator. In particular, regions such as Castilla-La Mancha (ES.CM) and Extremadura (ES.EX) tend to occupy the lowest positions over much of the sample period. Their trajectories indicate limited relative growth in hospital equipment compared to the national benchmark.

The hospital beds indicator also suggests a moderate but persistent degree of regional heterogeneity. Most regions are concentrated within an intermediate band indicating broadly similar levels of hospital bed capacity relative to the national benchmark. However, a limited number of regions consistently occupy the upper and lower extremes, pointing to structural asymmetries in the distribution of inpatient resources. Among the regions with the highest values, Catalonia (ES.CT) clearly stands out as the most prominent upper outlier throughout the period. This pattern is likely linked to the historical role of a mixed provider network, including a significant presence of publicly financed but privately managed hospitals. Other regions such as the Basque Country (ES.PV), Castilla y León (ES.CL), and Aragón (ES.AR) also display persistently high values. These regions exhibit relatively stable trajectories over time, suggesting a sustained above-average level of inpatient capacity. At the lower end of the distribution, regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN), the Valencian Community (ES.CV), and Murcia (ES.MC) consistently record the lowest values, often below 0.2–0.3 in the case of Andalusia and around 0.1–0.2 for the Valencian Community. These values indicate a systematically lower relative endowment of hospital beds compared to the national average. Extremadura (ES.EX) and Castilla-La Mancha (ES.CM) also appear in the lower segment, although with slightly higher values and some upward movement over time. The persistence of low values in these regions suggests structural differences in healthcare organization, possibly reflecting lower population density, different models of care provision, or a greater reliance on outpatient and primary care services.

The health expenditure indicator captures the relative financial effort of each Autonomous Community within the Spanish National Health System. Importantly, this measure reflects total publicly financed healthcare, including expenditure channelled to private providers through contractual arrangements (conciertos), and therefore represents financing rather than ownership of provision. The overall pattern displays a clear common dynamic across regions: a steady increase up to the late 2000s, a pronounced decline during the post-financial crisis period (approximately 2010–2014), and a subsequent recovery culminating in peak values around 2019–2021. Despite this shared trajectory, substantial and persistent differences in relative expenditure levels remain across regions. At the upper end of the distribution, La Rioja (ES.LO) consistently emerges as the region with the highest relative expenditure, reaching values close to 1 in the final years of the period. Its trajectory is persistently above the national average, indicating a structurally higher level of per capita public health spending. Similarly, Navarra (ES.NC), the Basque Country (ES.PV), and Castilla y León (ES.CL) also occupy the upper segment of the distribution throughout most of the period. These regions show relatively stable trajectories and maintain their relative advantage even during the contraction phase. Their position is likely associated with structural factors such as ageing populations, lower population density, and higher unit costs of service provision. At the lower end, Andalusia (ES.AN) stands out as a persistent outlier. This reflects a lower level of public health expenditure per capita. Other regions such as Murcia (ES.MC), the Valencian Community (ES.CV), and Madrid (ES.MD) also tend to occupy the lower segment of the distribution. Although these regions follow the same cyclical pattern as the rest of the system, their relative position remains consistently below the national average.

The welfare indicator, measured as Healthy Life Years at birth, reflects the expected number of years an individual lives in good health and constitutes a synthetic outcome measure of population health. In this case, the indicator exhibits a relatively high degree of persistence. Most regions are concentrated within an intermediate-to-high range, suggesting broadly similar health outcomes across territories. However, there remain identifiable upper and lower tails, indicating persistent regional disparities in healthy life expectancy. At the upper end of the distribution, regions such as Navarra (ES.NC), Castilla y León (ES.CL), and Aragón (ES.AR) frequently attain values close to or above 0.9. These regions display relatively stable trajectories over time, indicating consistently higher levels of healthy life expectancy relative to the national benchmark. Additionally, regions such as the Basque Country (ES.PV) and Catalonia (ES.CT) also tend to occupy the upper segment, although with slightly more variability. The persistence of high values in these regions suggests favourable structural conditions, including demographic composition, lifestyle factors, and effective healthcare delivery systems. At the lower end, Andalusia (ES.AN) stands out as a clear and persistent outlier, with values frequently close to zero throughout much of the period. This indicates a systematically lower level of healthy life years relative to other regions. Similarly, regions such as Extremadura (ES.EX), the Valencian Community (ES.CV), and Murcia (ES.MC) tend to occupy the lower segment of the distribution. These patterns suggest structural disadvantages in health outcomes, potentially linked to socio-economic conditions, health behaviours, and differences in public health effectiveness.

The patient experience indicator shows moderate dispersion combined with noticeable temporal variability. Most regions lie within a relatively narrow band suggesting that patient-reported experience is more homogeneous than structural inputs but less stable than outcome indicators such as healthy life years. A common feature across regions is a marked improvement trend up to the late 2010s, followed by a generalized decline around 2020–2021, likely associated with systemic disruptions affecting healthcare delivery and communication. At the upper end of the distribution, Asturias (ES.AS) stands out as a consistent top performer, reaching values close to or equal to 1 in several years, particularly around 2010. Its trajectory remains systematically above the national average, indicating a comparatively high level of patient satisfaction with information provision. Other regions such as Navarra (ES.NC), the Basque Country (ES.PV), and Castilla y León (ES.CL) also exhibit relatively high values. These regions maintain stable positions in the upper segment, although with some fluctuations. Their performance suggests structurally stronger communication practices and possibly better patient–provider interaction frameworks. At the lower end, the Canary Islands (ES.CN) clearly emerge as a persistent outlier, with values frequently below 0.3 and even approaching zero in some years. This indicates systematically lower patient satisfaction regarding the clarity of information received in specialist consultations. Other regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN), the Valencian Community (ES.CV), and Murcia (ES.MC) also tend to occupy the lower segment of the distribution. While these regions show some improvement over time, their relative position remains below the national average.

The accessibility indicator captures the relative ease of access to healthcare services across regions. This indicator exhibits a substantially higher degree of volatility over time and less clear persistence in the cross-sectional distribution. At the upper end of the distribution, regions such as Catalonia (ES.CT), Galicia (ES.GA), and, in several years, the Valencian Community (ES.CV), frequently attain high values, unlike in other indicators, these positions are not consistently maintained over time, and significant fluctuations are observed. This pattern suggests that high accessibility is less structurally embedded and more contingent on short-term system performance. At the lower end, several regions–including Madrid (ES.MD), the Basque Country (ES.PV), and occasionally Andalusia (ES.AN)–display very low values in certain years, in some cases approaching zero. These extreme observations are notably more pronounced than in other indicators and tend to be episodic rather than persistent.

A key feature of the mortality indicator is that the pattern shows a clear and sustained downward trend across all regions over time, indicating a generalized improvement in mortality outcomes. This decline is particularly pronounced between 2000 and the early 2010s, after which the pace of improvement slows and stabilizes. In terms of dispersion, the indicator exhibits moderate but persistent regional heterogeneity. While all regions follow a similar downward trajectory, differences in levels remain relatively stable, suggesting limited convergence in relative mortality outcomes. At the upper end of the distribution, regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN) and Asturias (ES.AS) tend to exhibit the highest values, particularly in the earlier years of the period. Although these regions experience substantial improvements over time, they remain relatively above the national average throughout most of the sample. Other regions such as Galicia (ES.GA) and the Valencian Community (ES.CV) also appear in the upper segment, typically maintaining values above the bulk of regions, especially during the first half of the period. These patterns suggest a persistently higher mortality burden, potentially linked to demographic structure, prevalence of chronic conditions, and socio-economic factors. At the lower end of the distribution (i.e., better mortality outcomes), regions such as Madrid (ES.MD) consistently display the lowest values throughout the period, with a marked and sustained decline reaching the lowest levels by the end of the sample. This indicates comparatively better health outcomes in terms of mortality. Similarly, Navarra (ES.NC), Castilla-La Mancha (ES.CM), and Castilla y León (ES.CL) tend to occupy the lower segment of the distribution, with relatively favourable mortality profiles. These regions show steady improvements over time and maintain their relative advantage within the system. The persistence of regional differences indicates that inequalities in mortality remain structurally embedded. Unlike more volatile indicators such as accessibility, mortality exhibits strong temporal persistence and smoother dynamics, consistent with its nature as a long-term outcome influenced by both healthcare system performance and wider determinants of health.

Starting with human resources, both model specifications consistently indicate a gradual but persistent divergence across regions, although they capture this dynamic in different ways. In the time-dummies specification, the positive and statistically significant coefficients observed in the later periods reflect an increasing distance from the benchmark relative to the base year, pointing to a progressive widening of regional disparities over time. Complementarily, the random-effects specification with a linear trend yields a positive and statistically significant coefficient, which confirms the presence of a systematic upward trajectory in the indicator. Given that the composite indicator measures distance to a reference point, this result provides clear evidence of sustained divergence in cross-regional performance.

A similar, albeit less regular, pattern is observed for health equipment. The time-dummies estimates suggest that temporal effects are relatively weak in the initial periods but become stronger and more frequently significant in later years, indicating that divergence intensifies over time rather than emerging uniformly. This evidence is reinforced by the positive trend coefficient in the random-effects model, which, although smaller in magnitude, still supports the presence of an incipient divergence process. Taken together, both specifications suggest that disparities in health equipment are increasing, albeit at a slower and more uneven pace compared to human resources.

In the case of Hospital Beds, the temporal dynamics are more moderate and less pronounced across both specifications. In the time-dummies model, some positive and occasionally significant coefficients are identified, indicating periods in which regions move further away from the benchmark relative to the base year. However, these effects are neither systematic nor large in magnitude. This is consistent with the results from the random-effects specification, where the estimated linear trend is positive but relatively small, suggesting only a limited and gradual increase in disparities.

By contrast, health expenditure displays the most pronounced and systematic temporal pattern. The time-dummies specification shows consistently positive and highly significant coefficients across most periods, indicating a persistent increase in the distance to the benchmark. This pattern is strongly reinforced by the random-effects model, where the estimated linear trend is positive, large in magnitude, and highly statistically significant.

Turning to welfare, the empirical evidence is weaker and less systematic across both specifications. In the time-dummies model, the estimated coefficients do not display a clear or consistent pattern over time, with limited statistical significance across periods. This suggests the absence of a well-defined temporal dynamic in the distance to the benchmark. Consistently, the random-effects specification yields a small and only marginally significant trend coefficient, indicating that any increase in dispersion is modest and not robust. Both models point to limited divergence and provide no clear evidence of a systematic convergence process.

On the other hand, patient experience exhibits a more structured temporal evolution. The time-dummies specification reveals positive and statistically significant coefficients particularly in the middle and later periods, indicating that regions move further away from the benchmark relative to the base year during these stages. This pattern is complemented by the random-effects model, where the estimated linear trend is positive and statistically significant, confirming a gradual increase in dispersion over time. However, the attenuation of time effects towards the end of the sample suggests some degree of stabilization in more recent years, although without reversing the overall divergence trend.

Accessibility, in turn, does not exhibit a consistent or systematic temporal pattern across either specification. In the time-dummies model, the estimated coefficients are generally not statistically significant and do not follow a clear trajectory over time, indicating the absence of a structured evolution relative to the base year. This lack of pattern is mirrored in the random-effects specification, where the estimated linear trend is small and statistically insignificant. Taken together, these results suggest that changes in accessibility are primarily driven by idiosyncratic and short-term fluctuations rather than by a systematic process of convergence or divergence across regions.

Conversely, mortality displays a markedly different and highly consistent dynamic. The time-dummies specification yields negative and statistically significant coefficients across virtually all periods, indicating a steady reduction in the distance to the benchmark relative to the base year. This pattern is fully aligned with the random-effects results, where the estimated linear trend is strongly negative and highly significant. Given the interpretation of the composite indicator as a distance measure, both specifications provide robust and unambiguous evidence of convergence, as regions progressively reduce disparities in mortality outcomes over time.

Human resources exhibit a clear convergence pattern, albeit less pronounced than in other dimensions. Dispersion tends to decline over time, while lagging regions grow faster, indicating the coexistence of σ- and β-convergence. Nevertheless, distributional dynamics reveal substantial persistence, particularly at the extremes, suggesting that convergence occurs gradually and without major changes in the relative ranking of regions.

In contrast, equipment follows a more ambiguous trajectory. Although catch-up dynamics are present, as lower-performing regions tend to improve faster, dispersion remains broadly stable. The transition analysis confirms moderate persistence, especially at the bottom of the distribution, indicating that improvements are not sufficient to alter the overall structure of regional disparities.

A comparable configuration emerges in hospital beds. While growth patterns point to convergence, the absence of a clear reduction in dispersion indicates that inequalities remain largely unchanged. Strong persistence across quintiles further reinforces this result, as regions tend to retain their relative positions over time, with limited upward mobility from the lower segments.

Health expenditure also reflects partial convergence. On the one hand, lower initial levels are associated with higher subsequent growth, supporting the presence of catch-up dynamics. On the other hand, dispersion does not show a systematic decline, and mobility remains moderate, implying that relative improvements do not translate into a substantial compression of the distribution.

Regarding welfare, the evidence again points to convergence in growth rates without a corresponding reduction in disparities. Although lagging regions improve relatively faster, dispersion remains unstable and the distribution retains a noticeable degree of persistence. This suggests that convergence processes are heterogeneous and insufficient to fully homogenize regional outcomes.

Conversely, patient experience displays a more consistent convergence pattern. Dispersion declines over time and is accompanied by significant catch-up effects, indicating that regional differences are narrowing. Even so, some persistence remains, particularly at the extremes, with mobility concentrated in the middle of the distribution.

Accessibility presents a similar case of partial convergence. Despite clear catch-up dynamics, overall dispersion does not decrease significantly. The distributional analysis reveals persistence, especially among the lowest-performing regions, which appear to face barriers to upward mobility, thereby limiting the impact of convergence forces.

Finally, mortality stands out as the most robust case of convergence in dispersion terms. Regional differences narrow steadily over time; however, this process is not driven by stronger improvements among lagging regions. Instead, high persistence across the distribution suggests that convergence reflects a gradual compression rather than substantial reordering across regions.

Hierarchical clustering

Once convergence has been analyzed, clustering analysis is conducted to uncover latent patterns and regional groupings based on the temporal trajectories of the composite indicators across Spain’s autonomous communities. The clustering analysis based on the original algorithm reveals that all composite indicators exhibit substantial temporal variability across Spain’s autonomous communities. The average coefficient of variation exceeds conventional thresholds in all dimensions, supporting the use of longitudinal clustering to capture the dynamic evolution of regional trajectories. A robustness analysis conducted using alternative thresholds of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% confirms that the classification remains unchanged in all cases. Both the clustering strategy and the resulting partitions are identical across specifications, with pairwise adjusted Rand indices equal to one. This indicates that the identified clustering structure reflects stable patterns in the data rather than arbitrary methodological choices (see Table 3).

The degree of heterogeneity varies across indicators, as reflected in the number of clusters obtained. Equipment, health expenditure, and hospital beds display the highest level of fragmentation, each yielding nine clusters. Accessibility produces eight clusters, while patient experience, human resources, and welfare generate seven clusters. Mortality is the least fragmented dimension, with four clusters. This distribution suggests that input and infrastructure related indicators are characterized by highly differentiated temporal trajectories across regions, whereas mortality follows more homogeneous patterns over time, despite remaining heterogeneous (See Fig. 3).

Conclusion

This study provides a longitudinal assessment of the dynamics of regional health systems in Spain over 2002–2023. By integrating composite indicators across eight dimensions of health, panel data models, convergence analysis, and clustering techniques, the article develops a coherent, multidimensional approach to examine how regional disparities in health systems evolve within a decentralized institutional context. A key feature of the analysis is that the composite health indicators are based on an norm, so all findings are interpreted as relative distances to a benchmark, rather than absolute performance levels, which is crucial for understanding the empirical results. This metrics-based perspective allows for consistent comparisons across regions and over time, while emphasizing the relative nature of improvements and disparities.

Four main conclusions emerge. First, the evidence reveals that regional disparities in the health system are fundamentally structural and multidimensional. Differences are particularly persistent in dimensions such as human resources, hospital equipment, and health expenditure, where certain regions (notably Navarra (ES.NC), the Basque Country (ES.PV), and Castilla y León (ES.CL)) systematically outperform others, while regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN), Murcia (ES.MC), the Valencian Community (ES.CV), and Extremadura (ES.EX) remain consistently below the national benchmark. Additionally, a clear geographical pattern emerges, with many of the lagging regions concentrated in the south of Spain, suggesting that territorial and structural factors–beyond individual regional characteristics–play a relevant role in shaping these disparities. These patterns indicate that resource allocation and financing capacity are deeply embedded in institutional, demographic, and territorial factors.

Second, indicators such as mortality exhibit a clearly differentiated pattern. The results show a sustained reduction in dispersion, indicating that regions have become progressively more similar in their relative distance to the benchmark. This convergence is observed across most territories, including both high-performing regions such as the Basque Country (ES.PV) and Navarra (ES.NC), and lower-performing regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN) and Extremadura (ES.EX). However, the absence of statistically significant beta convergence indicates that this process is not driven by faster improvements among lagging regions, but rather by a relatively homogeneous adjustment across all regions. This suggests that common system-wide factors, such as national clinical standards or public health interventions, may play a more relevant role than region-specific dynamics, and points to a limited connection between the evolution of different dimensions.

Third, several indicators, including health expenditure, equipment, and human resources display the coexistence of beta convergence with stable or non-decreasing dispersion. For example, regions such as Murcia (ES.MC) or Andalusia (ES.AN), which start from lower levels, tend to experience higher relative growth rates, as reflected in the negative and significant beta coefficients. Nevertheless, these improvements are not sufficient to close the gap with leading regions such as Madrid (ES.MD), Navarra (ES.NC), or the Basque Country (ES.PV), where relative positions remain largely unchanged over time. This is further supported by the transition matrices, which show high persistence in both lower and upper quintiles. These results indicate that convergence in growth rates coexists with persistent structural differences and therefore should not be interpreted as a reduction in regional inequality.

Finally, the clustering analysis reinforces these conclusions by showing that regional performance is strongly dimension-specific. The regions do not belong to stable groups in all indicators, and their relative positions vary according to the dimension considered. For instance, regions such as Madrid (ES.MD), Navarra (ES.NC), and La Rioja (ES.RI) tend to be grouped among the best-performing clusters in dimensions such as expenditure or human resources, while occupying more intermediate positions in others. Conversely, regions such as Andalusia (ES.AN), Murcia (ES.MC), and Extremadura (ES.EX) are more frequently located in lower-performing clusters, although their position is not identical across all indicators. This variability confirms that there is no uniform pattern of convergence, but rather a set of heterogeneous trajectories shaped by structural and institutional factors across regions.

In summary, while previous studies have documented heterogeneous effects of decentralization across regions (Armenteros-Ruiz et al., 2024), this study expands upon that evidence by showing that this heterogeneity is also dimension-specific within a unified empirical framework. Likewise, our results differ from the evidence provided by Di Novi et al.8. While their analysis suggests that fiscal decentralization does not significantly affect disparities between regions, primarily due to the presence of equalization mechanisms, the findings presented here point to the persistence of substantial regional heterogeneity in Spain, statistically significant across several indicators. However, unlike Di Novi et al.8, this study does not identify the specific mechanisms driving these disparities, such as fiscal capacity or institutional differences, but rather documents their existence and dynamic behavior over time across different dimensions.

From a public policy perspective, our results suggest that reducing regional disparities requires more than simply promoting general improvements in resource allocation. The persistence of divergences in input-related indicators, coupled with only partial convergence in outcomes, underscores the need for stronger coordination mechanisms and targeted interventions that address structural differences between regions. Policies aimed at reducing disparities must consider both the distribution of resources and the mechanisms by which these resources translate into health outcomes.
