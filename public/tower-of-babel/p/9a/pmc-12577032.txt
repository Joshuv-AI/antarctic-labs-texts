Effectiveness of 0.01% atropine and orthokeratology for incipient myopia: a 1-year retrospective study
Authors: Xiaona Sun, Huihui Chu, Ru Zhang, Lingling Han, Jifeng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate and compare the effectiveness of 0.01% low-dose atropine (LA) and orthokeratology lenses (OK) monotherapy versus single-vision spectacles (SV) in controlling low myopia in clinical settings.MethodsTreatment-naïve Children with a first diagnosis of myopia using single vision spectacles (SV), LA, or OK for at least 1 year were included in this retrospective study. Data from 140 children’s eyes were analyzed at baseline (before the initiation of treatment) and after 1 year of treatment. ANOVA, ANCOVA, and Multiple Liner Regression were undertaken to identify factors significantly associated with axial length (AL) elongation and spherical equivalent refraction (SER) progression over this period.ResultsAfter controlling baseline variates, the adjusted 1-year AL growth of the LA group (0.22 ± 0.54 mm, adjusted p < 0.00) and the OK group (0.30 ± 0.23 mm, adjusted p < 0.00) was slower than that of SV group (0.57 ± 0.41 mm), and there was no statistically significant difference in AL growth between the LA group and the OK group (adjusted p = 0.29). The 1-year SER progression of each treatment group differed significantly (p < 0.00). Specifically, the slowest progression of SER occurred in the group receiving the LA intervention (-0.16 ± 0.38D) compared to the SV group (-0.56 ± 0.42D), followed by the OK group (-0.34 ± 0.34D). Baseline AL (β=-0.299, p < 0.00) and baseline SER (β=-0.255, p < 0.00) were negatively correlated with AL elongation and SER changes, respectively. Age and sex were not significantly associated with AL or SER changes during the study period. After stratification by age, in the subgroup aged 4–7, LA had more significant control effect on AL elongation (p < 0.00) and SER growth (p < 0.00) than SV, while OK only had more significant control effect on AL elongation than SV (P = 0.03). In the subgroup aged 8–12, the control effect of LA and OK on AL elongation and SER growth both became more significant than SV (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsIn the early stage of myopia, LA and OK showed similar efficacy in slowing the AL elongation and were significantly better than SV. For the progression of SER, LA had the best control effect, followed by OK.Keywords: 0.01% low dose atropine, Orthokeratology lenses, Myopia, Axial length, Spherical equivalent refraction

Introduction

The “myopia boom” has become a significant global public health issue, with a sharp rise in prevalence, especially in East Asia, where 80–90% of 18-year-olds are myopic, and 10–20% are highly myopic [1–3]. Prevalence rates of myopia among children aged 4–14 years from the entire Shanghai region and high myopia were 32.9% and 4.2%, respectively [4]. The implications of this are far-reaching, as high myopia is linked to severe ocular pathologies [5], including myopic macular degeneration, retinal detachment, cataract, and glaucoma, ect. The model of visual impairment and myopia suggested that each additional 1 D of myopia is associated with a 58%, 30%, 21%, and 20% increase in the risk of myopic maculopathy, retinal detachment, posterior subcapsular cataract, and open-angle glaucoma respectively [6]. They also predicted that 1 D of myopia control can prevent between 0.74 and 1.22 years of visual impairment for myopia levels of between − 3 and − 8 D [6].

Various strategies have been proposed and implemented to control myopia progression, including pharmacological interventions, optical corrections, and environmental modifications. 0.01% low dose atropine, a pharmacological approach, has been recognized for its role in myopia control, with research indicating a reduction in the progression of myopia in children [7–10]. The exact mechanism by which atropine exerts its antimyopic effects is not fully understood but is thought to involve modulation of retinal and scleral muscarinic receptors, as well as potential direct effects on scleral remodeling [11].

Orthokeratology lenses, by reshaping the cornea to provide clear vision during the day without the need for glasses or contact lens, has been confirmed by multiple studies to significantly control myopia progression [12–14]. The most likely mechanism by which OK delays myopia progression appears to be producing peripheral myopic defocus through corneal epithelial redistribution [15].

Although the myopia control effect of LA and OK has been established through clinical trials, reports of simultaneous comparing the effects of LA, OK and SV on incident myopia control in non-study-based patients are scarce. RCTs provide controlled environments with strict criteria and protocols, but these may not fully reflect real-world clinical situations. Thus, this retrospective clinical study aims to evaluate the effects of LA and OK on myopia in practice, highlighting real-life differences in myopia control strategies. Most importantly, this study primarily focuses on children with low myopia who have been diagnosed for the first time. Therefore, the purpose of this investigation was to explore the control effect of LA and OK on incipient myopia with low degree in the real clinical world. The results of this study will be instrumental in guiding clinical practice, aiding in the selection of the appropriate myopia control strategy for children.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a stratified retrospective cohort study conducted at Beijing children’s hospital Ophthalmic Centre (BCHOC), capital medical University, Beijing, China. Treatment-naïve patients with a first diagnosis of myopia with cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction rang from − 2.0 D to − 0.5 D were involved in this study.

Clinical records of patients with myopia who used SV, LA or OK between September 1, 2022, and July 30, 2024, were captured from electronic health record systems of BCHOC, including age, sex, visit time and the prescription. Analyses were based on one eye per child (the right eye, or left eye if right eye data were incomplete) to avoid intra-subject correlation. Patients at the beginning of treatment with no systemic disease, and no previous myopia control therapy were considered eligible for this study. Children with anisometropia (> 1.50 D), strabismus, amblyopia, or other ocular pathologies (e.g., congenital cataracts) were excluded. Patient records were excluded from the analysis if the patient chose other myopia control treatment or had no follow-up visit during the study period.

The treatment allocation was based on parental preference after informed discussion of risks/benefits of LA, OK, and SV, consistent with real-world practice. Patients who chosen the treatment of atropine eye drops received one drop of 0.01% atropine (Shenyang Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) every night before. The uncorrected visual acuity of children using LA alone exceeds 0.8. Additionally, all patients were assisted with a flipper for accommodation training during the treatment process. The OK lenses used in this study were four-zone reverse geometry lenses (Emerald Lenses; Euclid Systems Co., Herndon, VA) with an oxygen permeability coefficient of 100 × 10− 11(cm2/s) (mL O2/mL · mmHg). The patients were fitted with the lenses according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

The primary outcomes of this study were the change in AL and SER from baseline. The AL was measured at least five times in each eye with a Zeiss IOL Master, and the average was calculated. Cycloplegic refractive error (using either 1% cyclopentolate or 1% tropicamide, depending on the age of practitioner) was determined via autorefraction using the NIDEK Tonoref II (NIDEK, Gamagori, Japan) from an average of three measures per eye. More specifically, cycloplegic autorefraction was carried out in children aged < 8 years using 1% atropine eye gel (Shenyang Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China), with three drops in each eye per day, and refraction test was performed after 3 days. Cycloplegic autorefraction in children aged over 8 years was conducted following instillation of compound tropicamide (0.5% tropicamide plus 0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride; Shenyang Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China), with four drops in each eye instilled five minutes apart, and refraction test was performed after 40 min. Subjective refraction under natural pupil was tested within the next 7 days. For the OK group, 1-year SER was measured under cycloplegia after a 25-day washout period of lens wear to eliminate transient corneal reshaping effects.

Data process and statistics

The results obtained were presented as mean and standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare mean patient age, initial AL and SE, the changes of AL (1-year (dAL) and every 3 months) and SER (1-year, dSER). Bonferroni or Tamhane’T2 test was used to test specific differences after the ANOVA for post hoc analysis. Bonferroni for equal variances; Tamhane’s T2 for unequal variances (verified by Levene’s test). The chi-square test was used to compare sex between groups. To match the potential confounding factors between the three groups, the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to the data from the three groups. Covariates (age, sex, baseline AL, baseline SER) were included based on their known association with myopia progression [16–18] and preliminary correlation analysis. After an overall F test has shown significance, Bonferroni post hoc test was used to evaluate differences among specific means. In addition, the differences in the changes of AL or SER between the three groups were further analyzed by Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model. The model included age, sex, baseline AL or SER, and treatment strategy. Age, AL, and SER were treated as continuous variables, while sex and treatment were treated as categorical variables in the models. The standardized 1-year SER and AL changes from baseline were calculated. Furthermore, age was classified as younger age (4–7 years) and older age (8–12 years), determined by the median. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. All of the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 140 children met the inclusion criteria for the final analysis including treatment with LA (n = 42), OK (n = 54) or SV lenses (n = 44) within the study period. All patients who involved the study did the 12 months review. The mean patient age was 7.43 ± 1.82. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are described in Table 1. There were no significant differences between groups in terms of sex (p = 0.695). Since it was a retrospective study, there were significant differences between groups in some characteristics at baseline. Specifically, pairwise comparisons revealed the following statistically significant (p < 0.05) differences: the LA group was younger than the other groups; the LA group (-0.68 ± 0.29D) had lower values of SER than the OK group (-0.99 ± 0.36D) and the SV group (-1.17 ± 0.49D), and the OK group had lower values than the SV group; the OK group had higher values of AL compared to the SV group. Thus, the ANCOVA and MLR analysis were used to corrected for differences in these factors at baseline.

Primary outcomes: AL and SER

The statistical analysis is illustrated in Tables 2 and 3. Figure 1 shows the time courses of changes in AL in the three group. At 12 months, the 1-year mean AL elongation was 0.57 ± 0.41 mm (adjusted value 0.55 ± 0.06 mm) in the SV group, 0.23 ± 0.54 mm (adjusted value 0.20 ± 0.06 mm) in the LA group and 0.30 ± 0.23 mm (adjusted value 0.34 ± 0.05 mm) in the OK group. The results of the three groups differed significantly (p < 0.001). The AL growth of the LA group and the OK group was slower than that of SV group, and there was no statistically significant difference in AL growth between the LA group and the OK group. After controlling for baseline AL, the adjusted AL change difference showed a similar result (Table 2). AL elongation was reduced by 58.68% in the LA group and 47.56% in the OK group compared to SV within a year.

The 1-year mean change in SER was − 0.56 ± 0.42D (adjusted value − 0.62 ± 0.06D) for the SV group, -0.16 ± 0.38D (adjusted value − 0.09 ± 0.06D) for the LA group and − 0.34 ± 0.34D (adjusted value − 0.35 ± 0.05D) for the OK group. The results of the three groups differed significantly (p < 0.001). Specifically, the slowest progression of SER occurred in the group receiving the LA intervention compared to the SV group, followed by the OK group. After controlling for baseline SER, the adjusted SER change difference showed a similar result (Table 3). The SER was delayed to increase by 70.92% and 40.07% within 1 year in patients with LA and OK, respectively, as compared to patients with SV.

Multicollinearity was assessed (VIF < 2). In the MLR model (Table 4), baseline AL (β=-0.299, P = 0.000) and baseline SER (β=-0.255, P = 0.004) were negatively correlated with AL elongation and SER changes, respectively. In contrast, age and sex were not significantly associated with AL or SER changes during the study period. For AL changes, both LA (β=-0.375, P = 0.000) and OK (β=-0.238, P = 0.011) were statistically different from SV. Compared with SV, the AL growth of students using LA or OK was significantly lower than that of SV. The treatment of LA (β = 0.611, P = 0.000) and OK (β = 0.308, P = 0.001) were both positively correlated with SER changes (negative value) compared to the SV. This suggests that both LA and OK have a better effect on controlling the growth of SER than SV.

This study further analyzed the AL growth every three months in three groups. At 3 months, the 3-month mean AL elongation was 0.18 ± 0.20 mm in the SV group, 0.07 ± 0.10 mm in the LA group and 0.05 ± 0.17 mm in the OK group. There was a statistically significant difference between the three groups (P < 0.05). Specifically, the slowest progression of AL occurred in the LA group compared to the SV group, followed by the OK group. There was no statistically significant difference in the AL growth every three months after the third month among the three schemes.

A stratification analysis on age was performed to further reveal the baseline characteristics of children who would derive greater benefit from LA or OK compared to SV. After stratification by age, LA and OK demonstrated better efficacy in slowing down AL elongation in both younger aged 4–7 and older children aged 8–12 than SV (P < 0.05).

When it comes to controlling the progression of SER, younger children seemed to derive greater benefit from LA (-0.21 ± 0.33D) compared with SV (-0.58 ± 0.45D, p = 0.003). In older children, the control effect of LA (-0.04 ± 0.47D) and OK (-0.29 ± 0.30D) on SER growth both became more significant than SV (-0.55 ± 0.38D, P < 0.05).

Discussion

We conducted the current study to evaluate the treatment efficacy of LA and OK under diverse real-world clinical environments. The findings indicate that both LA and OK are effective for low myopia.

Several previous studies have demonstrated that the efficacy of LA and OK combined treatment is better than that of monotherapy [16, 19–23]. However, the combination therapy poses a burden on children and families in terms of both economic costs and treatment compliance [24], particularly for pediatric patients with low myopia. Parents often take into account the potential side effects of various treatment methods when managing their children’s myopia, including the impact of OK on the cornea, the hassle of nursing, the need for frequent follow-up visits and the side effects associated with atropine such as photophobia, allergy, and poor near visual acuity. Therefore, parents maybe prefer to choose a single treatment regime.

In present study, 1-year AL elongation was 0.23 ± 0.54 mm in the LA group, 0.30 ± 0.23 mm in the OK group and 0.57 ± 0.41 mm in the SV group. The 1-year mean change in SER was − 0.16 ± 0.38D for the LA group, -0.34 ± 0.34D for the OK group and − 0.56 ± 0.42D for the SV group. This study confirmed that both LA and OK had good control effect on AL elongation in the initial onset myopia, but LA had a slightly better control effect on SER progression than OK.

The result of 1-year AL elongation in the current LA and OK treatment was similar to that of the previous study of Zhao [25] et al. (LA 0.24 ± 0.12 mm, OK 0.29 ± 0.11 mm) and Guo [26] et al. (LA 0.22 ± 0.02 mm, OK 0.37 ± 0.01 mm). One multicentric, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial reports 1-year data proving efficacy of LA in reducing SER progression (-0.16 ± 0.4D) in Indian children having mild to moderate myopia without any significant effect on AL elongation (0.22 ± 0.2 mm) [27]. Our findings regarding the efficacy of LA are consistent with several major Asian trials such as ATOM2 [8] and LAMP [28], which demonstrated a significant slowing of myopia progression. In the ATOM2 study [8], AL and SER increased by 0.24 ± 0.19 mm and − 0.43 ± 0.52D, respectively, in children treated with LA over a period of 1 year. The SER progression of children treated with 0.5% atropine group in the ATOM2 study [8] was − 0.17 ± 0.47D, which was comparable to the SER progression of children treated with LA (-0.16 ± 0.38D) in the present study. In the LAMP study [28], the mean AL elongation and SER progression were faster than those observed in the present study in the LA group (-0.64 ± 0.56 VS. -0.16 ± 0.38D/year and 0.35 ± 0.24 VS. 0.23 ± 0.54 mm/year).

The CHAMP study, a large 3-year RCT conducted primarily in a Western population, reported that 0.01% atropine demonstrated a statistically significant but comparatively modest effect in slowing myopia progression (0.24D reduction in SER progression, 0.13 mm reduction in AL elongation) compared to placebo [29]. It is important to note that the trial’s predefined primary efficacy endpoint for the 0.02% concentration was not met. Notably, the 2-year PEDIG study found that 0.01% atropine had no significant effect on SER progression (-0.82D for atropine vs. -0.80D for placebo) or AL elongation (0.44 mm vs. 0.45 mm), concluding it was ineffective for US children [30]. These contrasting results likely stem from ethnic/environmental differences: East Asian children’s faster myopia progression may boost measurable treatment effects, while genetics, lifestyle (e.g., outdoor time, academic pressure), and baseline traits shape both myopia’s course and therapy response. Therefore, while 0.01% atropine works well in Asians, its effect may be more modest and variable in other populations. This highlights the importance of generating regional real-world evidence to guide local clinical practice, and suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to myopia management may not be appropriate.

The 1-year AL elongation of OK group (0.30 ± 0.23 mm) in the current study was similar to the 1-year results reported by Kinoshita et al. [20, 24] (0.28 ± 0.12 mm and 0.27 ± 0.11 mm, respectively) in the subgroup of subjects with SER of − 1.00 to − 3.00D, but was slightly more than results reported by Kinoshita et al. [20, 24] of subjects with SER of − 1.00 to − 6.00D and other previous study [16, 21] (range from 0.16 mm to 0.24 mm). In the ROMIO study [13], subjects had less 1-year AL elongation in the OK group (0.20 ± 0.15 VS. 0.30 ± 0.23 mm). Those discrepancies between studies may be that our children participated in present study were slightly younger and had lower spherical equivalents and smaller axial lengths than those studies.

Numerous studies on myopia control interventions have reported the relationship between age and treatment measures, with a wide array of divergent findings. In this study, the initial age was not associated with SER or AL changes during the study period. But the age-dependent effect was observed in Xu’s study [16]: the younger the age, the poorer the efficacy. There were significant negative correlations between AL elongation and baseline age in the SV, atropine, OK groups. Moreover, they found that Sex and baseline SER or AL values were not associated with SER or AL changes. In the present study cohort, baseline AL and baseline SER values were negatively correlated with axial lengthening and SER growth, respectively. This is consistent with several clinical investigations [12, 14, 17, 18, 31, 32], but contradictory to several other previous reports [13, 33, 34].

Our results showed that OK and LA had the best effect on myopia control during the first 3 months of the study period, indicating that the control effect of LA and OK mainly played a role in the early stage. Interestingly, there were 10 out of 42 (23.81%) patients who displayed a clinically meaningful reduction in axial length of at least 0.05 mm during the first year of treatment in LA group, while neither the OK group nor the SV group showed this phenomenon. Our research also indicated that, for both younger and older children, LA and OK were more effective than SV in retarding the elongation of AL (P < 0.05). When it comes to controlling the progression of SER, younger children aged 4–7 seemed to benefit more from LA only. While in the subgroup aged 8–12, both LA and OK had significantly better control effects on the progression of SER than SV (P < 0.05). The specific mechanism remains unclear, potentially linked to atropine’s impact on choroidal blood supply and scleral remodeling. The younger the age, the greater the biological plasticity of the sclera. Additionally, parents’ heightened awareness of myopia in young children, coupled with increased outdoor activities, may contribute to axial regression.

Good therapeutic adherence is a critical factor in controlling myopia. Previous studies [13, 35, 36] have reported that the dropout rate of OK ranges from 6% to 30%. Moreover, the adherence to the OK and LA combination therapy was worse compared to the monotherapy [20]. The adherence to the use of 0.01% atropine in the combination group in Nozomi’s study was worse compared to the adherence rate of 98.8% to the use of 0.01% atropine in the ATOM2 study [8]. Meanwhile, the rebound effect following cessation of treatment warrants attention. Chia et al. [9]. demonstrated that 12 months after atropine cessation, 0.01% atropine resulted in less myopic rebound, which led to a more sustained effect of myopia retardation and overall reduction of myopia. Zhu et al. [37]. reported no significant AL changes in patients with at 1-month after OK withdrawal. A within-subject crossover trial suggested that there may be a rebound effect after discontinuation of the OK [12]. Thus, the rebound effect after longer OK discontinuation remains to be studied. Moreover, side effects associated with 0.01% atropine were uncommon [8]. A five-year clinical trial demonstrated that LA were more effective in slowing myopia progression with less visual side effects compared with higher doses of atropine [38]. Although it is unclear what the true adverse event rate of OK, safety remains a concern due to the risk of potentially blinding microbial keratitis from contact lens wear [15].

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature introduces potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Although we adjusted for key baseline characteristics, factors such as time spent outdoors, near-work activity levels, and parental myopia history were not consistently available in the medical records and could influence myopia progression. Second, significant baseline differences existed between groups, particularly in age and refractive error, which were inherent to the real-world treatment selection process. While we used ANCOVA and MLR to statistically control for these differences, residual confounding may persist. Third, the use of different cycloplegic agents based on age, though clinically standard, could theoretically introduce measurement bias, despite our statistical adjustment for age. Fourth, the 1-year follow-up period is relatively short for evaluating long-term myopia control efficacy and safety. Finally, the sample size, though sufficiently powered for the primary outcome, may be underpowered for some subgroup analyses. Future prospective, randomized trials with longer follow-up, standardized measurements, and careful recording of environmental and genetic factors are warranted to confirm our findings.

In conclusion, our study showed that 0.01% dose atropine and OK treatment were equally effective in slowing the AL elongation over a 1-year treatment period. For the control of SER progression, LA showed a trend toward better efficacy compared with OK in children with low myopia over 1 year.
