Theta-nested gamma oscillations balance prediction and vigilance in spatial navigation
Authors: Kwan Tung Li, Ziqun Wang, Pulin Gong, Dongping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe brain’s ability to navigate to rewards is crucial for survival, relying on memory of paths while avoiding unpredictable threats. Our investigation, by building a biologically plausible spiking neural network model, reveals how theta-nested gamma oscillations (TGOs) enhance goal-navigation performance. We demonstrate that synfire chains within TGOs discloses the puzzling positive velocity–frequency correlation, resulting from an optimal trade-off between predicting future rewards and avoiding hazards. By explaining experimental observations under respective drug-induced impairments, this study clarifies the roles of theta oscillations, gamma oscillations, and their coupling, providing insights into the brain’s adaptive capabilities and the dynamical mechanisms underlying the prediction-vigilance balance for navigating both predictable rewards and unpredictable challenges.

Hippocampal place cells represent ongoing trajectories and fire at time-compressed “theta sequences” to provide a framework for encoding past, present, and future positions (1–4). They fire at progressively earlier theta phases as an animal moves through their place fields–a phenomenon known as theta-phase precession (2, 5). Theta-gamma coupling further enhances this representation, segregating spatial information into distinct gamma subcycles (3). Despite the recognized roles of theta-nested gamma oscillations (TGOs) in spatial navigation, the precise functional contributions of theta (4∼12 Hz) and gamma (40∼100 Hz) oscillations, whose disruptions impair navigational performance (6–9), remain elusive; especially the underlying dynamical mechanisms and benefits of their coupling are not fully understood (7, 8). Notably, the well-documented correlation between theta frequency and motion velocity suggests an adaptive neural strategy for processing spatial information (10–13), yet its computational significance remains unclear.

Recent findings reveal that hippocampal activity is dynamically coupled with locomotion (14, 15), with theta rhythms synchronizing with both movement speed and stepping frequency–particularly in complex navigation tasks–highlighting a close integration between cognitive representations and motor control (14). Contrary to conventional views that obstacle avoidance relies solely on sensory-motor processes (16), emerging evidence highlights the hippocampus’s involvement in integrating predictive planning with real-time environmental assessment (14, 15). This coordination suggests that the hippocampus contributes not only to higher-order cognitive functions but also to dynamic behavioral control, mediating both foresight and vigilance. While forward planning is essential for locating rewards, effective navigation also requires sustained alertness to unanticipated threats (4, 17).

Motivated by these insights, we develop a biologically plausible spiking network model that incorporates synfire chain (SFC) dynamics (18–20) (SI Appendix, Text), to investigate whether and how TGOs support this dual role by enabling both predicting future events and monitoring instantaneous environmental contingencies. Our findings reveal a fundamental trade-off: The positive velocity–frequency correlation optimally balances predictive planning with environmental vigilance. Within this adaptive regime, TGOs make distinct yet complementary contributions: Theta oscillations chunk spatial information, facilitate self-location encoding, and enhance situational awareness; gamma oscillations synchronize population activity, promote SFC slope, and extend predictive theta sequences; and theta-gamma coupling enables efficient use of theta cycles to integrate prediction with real-time hazard detection. Together, these mechanisms provide a computational framework for understanding how hippocampal oscillations jointly support goal-directed navigation and adaptive responsiveness to environmental uncertainty. Therefore, our study unravels the functional roles of TGOs in goal navigation, accounting for both predictable rewards and unpredictable challenges.

Results

SFC Dynamics.

To investigate SFC dynamics, we simulate a rat navigating a linear track at velocity v(t)=⟨v⟩±σvξ(t), where ξ(t) is a uniform random process between −1 and 1 (Fig. 1A). Our model comprises two key components (Fig. 1B): the medial septum (MS), internally generating theta oscillations, and the CA1 superficial layer (L1), where SFCs are formed. The MS contains two inhibitory (I) populations (MS1 and MS2), which fire in antiphase (21–23). MS1 inhibits L1 I neurons (24, 25), while MS2 dynamically modulates the gain of entorhinal cortex (EC) outputs. Specifically, EC inputs–modeled as Gaussian-shaped spatial profiles (26–29)–deliver location-specific excitatory (E) drive to L1 E neurons (putative place cells), and their amplitudes are rhythmically modulated by MS2 activity in a phase-dependent, nonlinear fashion (24) (Eq. 13). Given that theta oscillations in L1 and EC are observed to be out of phase (30), we assume MS1 and MS2 respectively project to L1 and EC. L1 is modeled as a continuous attractor neural network (CANN) (19, 31, 32), in which excitatory-to-excitatory connectivity decays exponentially with distance. This architecture encodes spatial positions and incorporates intrinsic neuronal adaptation and short-term synaptic depression (STD), which together support the formation and propagation of SFCs (Eqs. 2 and 9 in Materials and Methods) (31, 33).

Mutual inhibition between MS1 and MS2 leads to their alternating activation, which segments SFCs into two distinct phases (Fig. 1C): Self-location phase (yellow dots, MS1 inhibited, MS2 activated) and Look-ahead phase (blue dots, MS1 activated, MS2 inhibited), corresponding to encoding of present and future locations, respectively. During the self-location phase, MS2 permits EC inputs to reach L1, while reduced MS1 activity disinhibits L1 I neurons, leading to their activation. These I neurons suppress the excitability of place cells in L1, confining firing to neurons representing the subject’s current position. This forms a stable activity bump in the CANN. Importantly, variations in the location-dependent input width and the E connectivity radius have minimal impact on the estimated self-location (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B–E). In the look-ahead phase, MS1 becomes active and suppresses L1 I neurons, reducing inhibition in L1. Meanwhile, MS2 deactivates, weakening EC inputs. This disinhibition in L1 enables forward SFC propagation, allowing the network to predict upcoming positions. Within the CANN framework, L1 E neurons are strongly and symmetrically connected with spatially adjacent neurons (Materials and Methods). Initially activated place cells excite nearby neurons, but over time, intrinsic spiking adaptation and STD reduce their activity and synaptic efficacy. This dynamic supports forward propagation of activity into novel spatial locations. Together, these alternating phases allow SFCs to continuously encode both current and future positions in a temporally structured manner.

The generated SFCs exhibit TGO properties consistent with experimental observations (Fig. 1D). Theta rhythms originate in the MS, with their frequency fθ governed by slow potassium currents in the MS (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Gamma oscillations emerge from the E-I loop within L1, driven by synaptic current delays between E and I populations (34, 35). Phase-amplitude coupling in the resulting TGOs aligns with physiological findings (36): Gamma amplitude peaks during the look-ahead phase, when MS1 is active and predictive representations are enhanced (Fig. 1E and Materials and Methods). Moreover, our model replicates theta-phase precession (Fig. 1F), wherein place cell firing shifts to earlier theta phases as the rat traverses its place fields (2, 5). This precession arises naturally from the chaining of self-location and predicted future positions by sequential SFCs (Fig. 1G). We define two key SFC properties essential for navigation (Fig. 1G): SFC length, the spatial extent represented by a single SFC, and SFC separation, the spatial distance between the initiation positions of two consecutive SFCs (Fig. 1G).

The model captures essential features of hippocampal sequence dynamics by leveraging prelearned spatial representations within the CANN framework. SFC dynamics provide a mechanism for trajectory prediction, rapidly integrating past and future spatial information within the cognitive map (37) and enabling efficient recall, adaptive planning, and real-time navigation decisions (1, 2, 4, 17).

Simulating Goal-Navigation Task and Trap Avoidance.

To examine the functional role of SFCs in navigation, we simulate a goal-directed task in a cross maze (Fig. 2A), which consists of a main track and three arms (Left, Middle, and Right), each spanning 50 cm. Goal representation is implemented by extending the model to incorporate a deep CA1 layer (L2, Fig. 2B), which stores the reward position (38, 39). Both L1 and L2 form a 3-branched CANN, where each branch represents a trajectory toward a specific arm. L1 provides spatial inputs to L2, and synaptic plasticity between them supports memory formation (Materials and Methods). The task requires the rat to find a fixed reward (a predictable event) while avoiding a randomly placed trap along the main track (an unpredictable event). The simulation unfolds into two phases: a learning phase, in which the rat explores the maze to locate the reward in the absence of a trap, and a task phase, where it recalls the reward location while simultaneously avoiding an unpredictable trap (Materials and Methods).

During the learning phase, synaptic connections from L1 to L2 at the reward site undergo potentiation when the rat reaches that location (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–E). This is mediated by the coincidence of reward-induced neuromodulator signals and L1→L2 sequential firings, triggering reward-based spike-timing-dependent plasticity (R-STDP, Fig. 2E and Materials and Methods) (40, 41). As a result, L2 neurons become selectively responsive to L1 inputs at the reward-associated branch, effectively encoding the learned reward location (Fig. 2C), consistent with the idea that L2 is predominantly involved in processing reward-related information (39). In contrast, L1 neurons encode all traversed spatial trajectories through internally generated SFCs, regardless of behavioral relevance or reward (SI Appendix, Text), thus supporting parallel representation of all possible movement sequences (Fig. 2D). This labor division reflects a key modeling assumption: While L1 provides a comprehensive representation of experienced paths, L2 functions as a readout layer that flags reward-relevant locations via learned L1→L2 connections.

In the task phase, the rat must choose the correct arm at the maze fork. Sequential activation of L1 E neurons via SFCs simulates prospective trajectories (Fig. 2D). When the SFC’s look-ahead part reaches the reward location, previously potentiated L1-to-L2 synapses preferentially activate L2 neurons that encode the correct arm (cyan bars in Fig. 2 D and F). A selection mechanism in L2 determines the decision outcome, with selection probability proportional to the strength of the activated representation (Materials and Methods). Consequently, the arm with the highest L2 firing rate is chosen, enabling successful reward retrieval. In the absence of synaptic potentiation, L2 activation is nonspecific, leading to chance-level decisions. Moreover, due to intrinsic neuronal adaptation and STD (Materials and Methods), recently activated paths become less likely to be reselected, promoting exploratory sampling–reminiscent of vicarious trial and error (VTE) observed in rodents (17).

Beyond goal-seeking, the rat must also avoid a trap randomly placed along the main track (Fig. 2 A and D). In real situations, the rat may perceive the trap from afar and slow down or turn around. To simplify, we assume the trap is detectable only when the hippocampal representation of self-location coincides with the trap’s position, avoiding distant awareness. This aligns with recent findings that hippocampal representations must match the rat’s physical location before its forelimbs touch the ground (14). If self-location encoding fails to register the trap in time, the rat is caught. If an SFC transiently encodes the trap’s position, we assume the rat successfully detects and avoids it, neglecting reaction time.

This dual functionality–reward prediction and hazard detection–highlights the role of hippocampal sequence generation in integrating cognitive planning and real-time environmental responsiveness.

Velocity-Dependent Adaptation of Theta Frequency Optimizing Goal-Navigation Performance.

We now examine how the interplay between theta frequency (fθ) and mean velocity (⟨v⟩) optimizes navigation performance. Success is measured as the overall reward acquisition rate, integrating both prediction rate (correct trajectory selection) and dodge rate (trap avoidance). While sensorimotor factors contribute to dodging, we assume this contribution remains constant during exploration. Effective navigation requires balancing two competing demands: longer SFCs, which improve reward prediction by extending look-ahead sequences (Fig. 3A), and shorter SFC separations, which enhance self-location coverage and improve dodge ability (Fig. 3B). However, maximizing one inherently compromises the other (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A).

Both theta frequency fθ and mean velocity ⟨v⟩ strongly influence SFC dynamics (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F–H). Increasing fθ shortens SFCs (Fig. 3C) and reduces SFC separations (Fig. 3D), thus impairing reward prediction (Fig. 3E), but enhancing dodge performance (Fig. 3F). Increasing ⟨v⟩ has little effect on SFC length (Fig. 3C), but reduces the number of SFCs before reaching the maze fork (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B) and increases SFC separations (Fig. 3D), thereby degrading both prediction and dodge rates (Fig. 3 E and F). Specifically, the number of SFCs scales as 1/⟨v⟩, while SFC separation scales approximately as ⟨v⟩/fθ (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), as consecutive SFCs are temporally separated by roughly one theta period 1/fθ. Consequently, at higher ⟨v⟩, increasing fθ yields a smaller reduction in prediction rate (Fig. 3E), but a more substantial improvement in dodge rate (Fig. 3F). This shift in trade-off moves the optimal performance toward higher fθ values as ⟨v⟩ increases (Fig. 3 G and H), reflecting a velocity-dependent adaptation of theta frequency. Together, these results suggest that dynamically modulating theta frequency in response to movement velocity enables the hippocampal network to balance long-range predictive planning with immediate spatial vigilance–thereby optimizing navigation performance in unpredictable environments.

Theta Oscillation Impairment Inducing Loss of Self-Location Encoding.

To examine the functional role of theta oscillations in adaptive navigation, we dynamically modulate theta frequency fθ(t) according to the rat’s instantaneous velocity v(t) (Fig. 3H), increasing by 1 Hz per 5 cm/s above a 4 Hz baseline at v(t)=20 cm/s, and fθ(t)=4 Hz if v(t)<20 cm/s. This velocity-dependent modulation reflects experimental observations linking locomotion speed with hippocampal theta frequency. To simulate theta disruption, we modeled the effects of Muscimol–a GABA receptor agonist known to inactivate the MS and impair spatial encoding by place cells, thereby degrading navigation performance (6, 8). Accordingly, we doubled the inhibitory synaptic conductance within MS1 (gMS1I→I), within MS2 (gMS2I→I), and between MS1 and MS2 (gMS1↔MS2I→I), effectively dampening the rhythmic output from the MS (Muscimol condition; control: Normal as in Fig. 2).

Muscimol application markedly suppresses MS2 activity (black dots in Fig. 4A, and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B), weakening its modulation on location-dependent EC inputs and disrupting proper SFC termination. This results in abnormally prolonged SFCs, resulting in less frequent representation of current location (dark yellow dot in Fig. 4B) and three major deficits: 1) Loss of self-location encoding: The less frequent representation of current location induces substantial reduction in spatial field information (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). 2) Spatial decorrelation: The extended duration of unitary SFCs leads to a very long time period of prediction without current location representation, which causes the prediction to lose its reference (current location) (Fig. 4B–D), consistent with experimental observations (6, 7, 42); 3) Reduced theta and gamma power: Theta oscillations diminish due to the impaired MS2 drive (Fig. 4E), while sustained inhibition on L1 I neurons disrupts the E-I loop, also reducing gamma power (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Further analyses reveal disrupted coordination: Both theta-phase-gamma-coupling (modulation index, MI) and theta-phase precession (circular-linear correlation, clcorr) (Materials and Methods) are substantially reduced in the Muscimol condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E and F), matching prior empirical findings (6).

Functionally, these disruptions impair both learning and performance. During the learning phase, L1-to-L2 synapses at the reward site fail to potentiate (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G), as spatial decorrelation mentioned above causes the absence of current location representation when the rat receives reward, where neuromodulator is released (Fig. 4C). In the task phase, failure of potentiation of L1-to-L2 synapses fails to reliably activate L2 neurons at the reward site (Fig. 4 A and D), leading to similar firing rates across all arms and consequently a lower prediction rate (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, prolonged unitary SFCs increase SFC separation (Fig. 4B, F, and G), impairing self-location coverage and thereby degrading the dodge rate (Fig. 4I). These deficits substantially diminish overall reward acquisition (Fig. 4J).

These results highlight the critical role of theta oscillations in organizing the spatiotemporal structure of hippocampal activity. MS-driven theta rhythmicity ensures coordinated SFC propagation, enabling reliable self-location encoding and future-path prediction. Disrupting this rhythm decouples internal planning from external context, impairing both prediction and vigilance. Our model thus provides a mechanistic account of how theta oscillations support adaptive navigation in dynamic environments.

Gamma Oscillation Impairment Degrading Synchronization and SFC Propagation.

We further examine the role of gamma oscillations in supporting theta-phase precession, SFC propagation, and task performance under the same adaptive setting. Experimental studies have shown that gamma disruption impairs theta-phase precession (7), an effect induced by injecting clonidine, a noradrenergic autoreceptor agonist that lowers noradrenaline levels, reduces gamma power, and degrades neural synchronization (43). We model this by halving the background input IL to L1 E neurons. It lowers L1 excitability (Fig. 5 A and B) and neural synchronization (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), flattening the SFC slope (SFC propagated distance/time occupied; Fig. 5 C and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C) and shortening SFCs (Fig. 5 C and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Shorter SFCs limit prediction distance (Fig. 5C–E) (43), impairing reward prediction (Fig. 5 D and E) and reducing the prediction rate (Fig. 5I), while the dodge rate remains unchanged (Fig. 5J) as SFC count and separation are unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and E). Overall reward acquisition declines (Fig. 5K). Reduced synchronization also lowers gamma power (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B), weakens theta-gamma coupling (lower MI in SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), and impairs theta-phase precession (lower clcorr in SI Appendix, Fig. S5C), underscoring gamma’s role in coordinating theta-phase coding (7).

Together, these results highlight the critical role of gamma oscillations in dynamic predictive coding during goal-directed navigation. Longer SFCs are accompanied by stronger gamma power (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E), while gamma disruption impairs the temporal precision of neuronal spikings and degrades SFC propagation, thereby compromising reward prediction. Our findings provide computational support for theoretical predictions (44, 45), demonstrating how background excitation modulates SFC propagation via gamma-dependent changes in SFC slope.

Impairment of Theta-Phase-Gamma-Amplitude Coupling Degrading Self-Location Chunking and SFC Propagation.

Finally, we examine the role of theta-phase-gamma-amplitude coupling in self-location chunking and SFC propagation under the same adaptive setting. Disrupting this coupling is expected to impair the temporal coordination of neural activity, thereby altering sequence generation and navigation performance. To probe the underlying mechanism, we introduce a “phase lag” ϕL between the theta phase of MS2 activity and the phase modulating EC inputs (Fig. 6 and Materials and Methods). A larger ϕL delays the arrival of peak EC inputs at L1, shifting the peak gamma amplitude (Fig. 6B) and disrupting theta-gamma coupling. The effective time window T−TL for SFC propagation (shaded in Fig. 6A, Left) spans from the arrival of peak EC inputs to the peak of MS2 activity. During this window, L1 I neurons recover, allowing SFCs to advance before being terminated by peak L1 inhibition.

Intuitively, increasing ϕL reduces this window, thereby shortening SFCs and limiting prediction distance (Fig. 6 C and D), a trend observed up to ϕL=π (Fig. 6E). However, the relationship between ϕL and SFC dynamics is more nuanced: 1) Under the Normal condition (ϕL=0), maximal MS2 activity terminates an ongoing SFC while maximal EC inputs simultaneously initiate the next one, efficiently partitioning each theta cycle into propagation segments. At this alignment, maximal EC input coincides with strong L1 I activity, stabilizing the activity bump at the rat’s current location and supporting sustained self-location encoding (Fig. 6C). 2) As ϕL increases toward π, the propagation window shrinks, and the overlap between EC input and L1 I activity weakens. This results in less stable bump dynamics and shorter durations of self-location encoding. 3) At the critical phase lag (ϕL=π), both the look-ahead length and the duration of self-location encoding are minimized due to misaligned peaks in EC input and L1 inhibition. Interestingly, when EC input is sufficiently strong, it can override the terminating influence of MS2 output, allowing SFCs to occasionally extend beyond their designated propagation window (e.g., first SFC in Fig. 6D). These extensions can persist until they are eventually shunted by subsequent maximal EC inputs (shaded in Fig. 6A, Right). 4) Beyond ϕL=π, the timing shifts further: EC input begins to coincide with peak MS2 activity. This increased alignment drives the generation of more full-extended SFCs that extend beyond the window T−TL, while the increased L1 I activity during peak EC input also prolongs the duration of self-location encoding.

These dynamics explain our findings: 1) As ϕL increases toward π, SFCs shorten substantially, reducing prediction distance (Fig. 6E); 2) When ϕL>π, look-ahead sequences and SFC lengths partially recover due to extended propagation under delayed EC inputs; 3) SFC separation increases substantially with ϕL up to π, then declines (Fig. 6F), mirroring the nonmonotonic trend in SFC duration; 4) Near ϕL=π, the prediction rate decreases (Fig. 6G) and the dodge rate drops markedly (Fig. 6H), resulting in overall performance degradation (Fig. 6I). Together, these results demonstrate that theta-phase-gamma-amplitude coupling enables efficient use of theta cycles by coordinating SFC propagation and stabilizing self-location representations. While moderate phase lags preserve functionality, strong decoupling (ϕL≈π) impairs both prediction and real-time spatial encoding. Our results reveal the dynamical mechanism of how theta-gamma coupling enhances self-location chunking and prediction for promoting navigation performance.

Discussion

This study reveals how TGOs support adaptive navigation by balancing prediction and vigilance. By building a biologically plausible spiking network model, we demonstrate that TGOs shape sequential firing chains (SFCs) to mediate goal-directed behavior and immediate spatial vigilance, explaining the functional role of the positive velocity–frequency correlation and the distinct contributions of theta oscillations, gamma oscillations, and theta-gamma coupling. Our results show that this correlation is not simply a byproduct of locomotion but reflects an adaptive mechanism. As movement accelerates, cognitive demands rise–particularly the need for rapid trap detection–necessitating more frequent self-location updates. Higher theta frequency compensates by shortening theta cycles, enhancing spatial chunking and maintaining hazard awareness. Gamma oscillations promote SFC slope, extending predictive reach, while theta-gamma coupling optimizes the allocation of each theta cycle for both prediction and self-location encoding.

We designed a task requiring both reward prediction and hazard avoidance, capturing the dual necessity of prospective and immediate spatial processing. In this framework, L2 encodes reward locations and mediates decision-making, and L1 generates SFCs that integrate current location and predictive trajectories (38, 39). As motion speed increases, longer SFCs improve prediction, but only if balanced with short-term self-location representation to detect nearby threats. This trade-off, constrained by TGOs, naturally leads to a positive velocity–frequency relationship (10–13). Our model embeds TGOs within explicit circuit mechanisms: MS-driven modulation on L1 inhibition (24, 25) and EC inputs pace theta rhythms and gate SFC propagation (21–23). As MS theta frequency scales with velocity (46), our model adapts to motion dynamics and environmental uncertainty.

Importantly, the proposed framework generalizes beyond specific architectures. Any model that integrates recurrent SFC dynamics with reward-driven enhancement and decision-making via trajectory-based voting could exhibit similar trade-offs. Our findings thus provide mechanistic insight into how hippocampal oscillations may support adaptive navigation in unpredictable environments. To our knowledge, this mechanism has not yet been demonstrated experimentally; however, our model generates specific, testable predictions that can inform and guide future experimental studies (SI Appendix, Text).

Rationale and Uniqueness of Our Biologically Plausible Model.

In this study, we present a biologically plausible spiking network model comprising two main regions: MS (theta pacemaker) and CA1 (CANNs). MS internally generates theta oscillations via a slow potassium current (47), while CA1 is split into two layers (38, 39) with each forming a CANN. L1 generates SFCs and gamma oscillations, whereas L2 encodes the reward site and supports decision-making through plastic feedforward connections from L1 to L2 via R-STDP at the reward site. Direct projections from MS1 to L1, as evidenced in experiments (24, 25), intrinsically drive L1 to generate theta oscillations and terminate ongoing SFCs. Meanwhile, MS2-modulated EC inputs to L1 inform the rat’s current location and initiate subsequent SFCs, naturally accounting for various experimental findings (26–28, 48), e.g., the out-of-phase theta oscillations between EC and L1, as well as theta-phase precession. Unlike previous models that impose theta oscillations as external inputs (49–51), our model generates TGOs intrinsically through direct MS modulation, aligning more closely with their biological basis by leveraging MS control to drive hippocampal theta oscillations (52). Oscillatory interference models (50) generate TGOs and theta-phase precession by interfering multiple oscillators with distinct frequencies, but they require fine-tuned parameters and may not fully capture place cell behavior. Similarly, somato-dendritic models (51) tie theta generation to intraneuronal interactions, tightly coupling place cell activity to external inputs and limiting their capability to predict upcoming positions. In contrast, direct projections and modulation from MS enable our model to explain theta oscillation impairment under MS disruption, such as through pharmacological interventions, and also coordinately divide the generated SFCs into self-location and look-ahead parts for spatial information processing.

The other key aspect of our model lies in SFC dynamics: generation, propagation, and termination. First, SFC generation is achieved via statistically symmetric connectivity, where the connection probability decays with distance–a basic component in CANN for creating bump attractors (44, 49). This sparse connectivity is empirically supported by experimental observations of similar neuronal activities during forward and backward travels (53) and is assumed to reflect a prelearned spatial map, potentially inherited from CA3 (54) and supported by the observation of preplay (55). Actually, SFCs can be generated by the mixture of extrinsic and intrinsic mechanisms (54), where the former can be implemented via feedforward projections from CA3 to CA1 through STDP (56). Second, SFC propagation depends on the directional drive from location-dependent EC inputs, which induce a directional moving bump along the spatial map, facilitated by STD and intrinsic neuronal adaptation [also required for VTE (17)]. Notably, the propagation is enhanced by gamma oscillations, which are intrinsically generated within the L1 E-I loop (57). Finally, SFC termination is governed by MS theta oscillations, which regulate inhibition on L1 I neurons via MS1 and modulate location-dependent EC inputs to L1 via MS2. Thus, our model captures the essential mechanisms underlying SFC dynamics, providing a solid framework for studying the roles of TGOs in navigation.

Our MS-modulated CANN model not only captures place cell firing properties for encoding both current location and future positions but also reproduces biologically plausible TGOs by leveraging the detailed SFC dynamics, which are governed by statistically symmetric connectivity, neuronal and synaptic adaptations, directional drive from EC inputs, and MS-mediated modulation of inhibition in L1. This unified framework enables us to investigate the functional roles of theta and gamma oscillations, as well as their coupling–key biological constraints that previous models (31, 54, 58, 59) have largely overlooked. While earlier studies have primarily focused on the generation of theta sequences and the investigation of their functions, they have often neglected the intricate interplay between gamma oscillations and theta rhythms. In contrast, our approach demonstrates that the orchestrated SFC dynamics underlying TGOs enhance navigation performance, consistent with experimental findings (1, 8, 9, 43, 60), and further elucidates the distinct contributions of theta oscillations, gamma oscillations, and their coupling, as well as the corresponding underlying dynamical mechanisms. Thus, by integrating MS modulation with CA1 intrinsic properties, our model provides a comprehensive framework that unifies TGOs and SFC dynamics, offering fresh insights into how hippocampal networks support both current-location encoding and future trajectory prediction during goal navigation.

Limitations of the Study.

This work has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our model assumes a fixed gamma frequency across all movement speeds. However, experimental studies suggest that gamma oscillations may adapt to behavioral states and speed to improve coding efficiency (11). Future work should explore how speed-dependent modulation of gamma frequency could enhance spatial encoding and temporal coordination. Second, our model employs a fixed SFC structure that does not adapt with velocity. In contrast, biological systems likely leverage additional mechanisms–such as phase precession and velocity-dependent recruitment of predictive neurons (61, 62)–that support more flexible forward planning and efficient obstacle avoidance, particularly at higher speeds. Incorporating such mechanisms could improve behavioral performance beyond what is achieved in the current framework. Third, the cognitive map in our model, which has no prior path preference, is predefined and embedded within a CANN. This simplification overlooks the contribution of CA3, which is believed to support cognitive map formation through experience-dependent Hebbian plasticity (30, 54). Extending our model to include the learning of the cognitive map via CA3 dynamics could provide a more comprehensive account of how trajectory selection and value-based evaluation are shaped by learning and memory. It would also clarify how plasticity mechanisms can dynamically reshape internal representations without disrupting goal-directed behavior. Finally, the current model focuses on 1D spatial navigation. Extending it to include EC grid cells and 2D spatial tasks would help determine whether similar SFC-based mechanisms underlie predictive coding in more complex environments (4). Together, these limitations point to several promising directions for future research. Our findings nonetheless suggest that TGOs are not merely passive oscillatory rhythms, but active computational tools that flexibly balance predictive inference and self-location to guide adaptive behavior.

Materials and Methods

Circuit Architecture and Dynamics.

Our model featured a trilayered structure, comprising MS, L1 and L2. MS comprises 500 I neurons, divided into MS1 and MS2, each consisting of 250 I neurons (21, 22). Neurons were randomly connected with a probability of P=0.2, where synaptic conductance gMS1↔MS2I→I was set for mutual inhibition between MS1 and MS2, and gMSI→I for self-inhibition inside each subpopulation. Both L1 and L2 consisted of 2,000 E neurons (nE), and 500 I neurons (nI). Each E neuron i was associated with a preferred firing location xi. Neurons within the same layer were randomly connected at a probability of P=0.2, except for E neurons. E neurons were interconnected at a probability of pij=e−|xi−xj|/d with d denoting the connectivity length, for both intralayer connections and those from L1 to L2. A periodic boundary condition was applied to the maze, with |xi−xj|=1 if neuron i and j encoded the starting point and endpoint of the maze, respectively, resulting in a continuous attractor (19, 31). To avoid confusion, connections from the end to the start are removed to prevent the SFC from end-to-start propagating.

Simulation Details.

The linear maze (Fig. 1A) was encoded by 1,000 L1 E neurons, representing 100 distinct locations, each location LocI spanning 1 cm and encoded by 10 neurons, that is, Count(xi∈LocI)=10. The cross maze (Fig. 2A) was divided into four sections, each covering 200 locations and represented by 2,000 E neurons in both L1 and L2. Throughout the simulations (except when trapped), the rat moved at velocity v=⟨v⟩±σvξ(t), where ξ(t) is a uniform random process between −1 and 1. The deviation amplitude, σv, was set equal to ⟨v⟩. The simulation unfolds into two phases: 1) Learning phase: The rat explores the maze to locate the reward in the absence of a trap; 2) Task phase: It recalls the reward location while avoiding the unpredictable trap.

Learning phase.

Each branch was traversed twice. Upon reaching the randomly assigned reward site, a neuromodulatory signal was induced to the L2 E neurons encoding that location (xi=LocR), activating R-STDP to strengthen the L1-to-L2 synapse at the reward site (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and D).

Task phase.

R-STDP was no longer applicable. Upon reaching the fork, the rat made a decision, choosing arm i with probability P(armi)=∑j∈armiRjL2/∑k∑j∈armkRjL2, where RjL2 denotes the firing rate of neuron j in L2 E, accumulated from the start of the trial to the decision point. A trap was randomly placed along 3 cm of the main track. As the rat approached the trap region ([Loctrap−3cm,Loctrap), where Loctrap is the trap’s exact location), the total synaptic trace at each location was computed every 0.05 ms: zT(LocI,t)=∑xi∈LocIzi(t). The location with the highest zT at time t was identified as the rat’s recognized location: Recog(t)=argmaxLocIzT(LocI,t). If any recognized location Recog(t) fell within the trap region (∃Recog(t)∈[Loctrap−3cm,Loctrap)), the rat successfully dodged the trap and continued toward the reward. Otherwise, it was trapped, and the trial was terminated.

Measurements.

Oscillation power in L1 was computed by Fast Fourier Transforming (FFT) the detrended mean membrane potential of all E neurons in L1 within a 10-second interval, with the summation of power in the theta band (4∼12 Hz) for theta power, and that in the gamma band (40∼100 Hz) for gamma power. Subsequently, an inverse FFT was applied to obtain the band-passed theta and gamma oscillations, respectively.

SFC detection.

To detect SFCs, we first identified the a-th time taloc when place cells corresponding to the subject’s current location were activated (yellow dots in Fig. 1C). This time corresponds to the coincidence of the subject’s current location (y) and the location with the maximum synaptic trace zi(t) (synaptic trace of neuron i) for each location LocI (spanning 1 cm and encoded by 10 neurons, e.g., Count(xi∈LocI)=10). The a-th SFC was defined as the spike trains fired during the period [taloc,ta+1loc). The displacement during this period was calculated as the difference between the locations with maximum synaptic trace at the start and end of the period: ΔLocSFC=argmaxLocIzi(ta+1loc−δt)−argmaxLocIzi(taloc), where δt represents an infinitesimal time. This value represents the SFC length in terms of location, and the SFC number was counted from the starting point to the fork along the main track.

Modulation index.

The intensity of theta-phase-gamma-amplitude coupling can be measured by a modulation index (MI) as described (64). MI can be obtained by calculating the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence DKL(D‖U), where D represents a normalized distribution of the mean gamma amplitude across various theta phases binned into 360 1°-intervals (0∼360°), while U is a uniform distribution over theta phases. A higher MI signifies a stronger coupling between theta phase and gamma amplitude. Neuronal synchrony Kij measures the probability of the neuron pair i and j firing in coincidence, given as Kij=∑kBi(k)Bj(k)∑kBi(k)∑kBj(k) with Bi(k)(Bj(k)) being the spike train of neuron i(j). Bi(k)=0(1) denotes no spike (one spike) generated within the kth 1-ms bin. The average of all pairs of excitatory neurons in L1 was defined as the synchrony index.

Field information.

To quantify the correlation between the subject’s location and the SFCs in L1, that is, how well neuronal activities encoded the spatial information (6). It was defined as ∑xp(x)λi(x)λilog2λi(x)λii, where x denotes the location in the maze, and p(x) is the probability of the subject occupying the location x; λi(x) represents the firing rate of neuron i at location x, while λi denotes the average firing rate of neuron i across the entire maze. A higher value of the field information indicates a better encoding of the subject’s location by the activity of excitatory neurons in L1.

Theta-phase precession.

Theta-phase precession was quantified by clcorr between theta phase and the distance to the neuron’s preferred location. It was calculated using the Circstat toolbox (65), based on the formula (rcx2+rsx2−2rcxrsxrcs)/(1−rcs2), rcx=corr(cos(θtheta),x), rsx=corr(sin(θtheta),x), with x denoting the distance between the spiking location and the preferred location, corr and θtheta being the Pearson correlation and the theta phase, respectively. θtheta is calculated by applying FFT on the mean membrane potential in the simulation, and then band-pass filtered to the theta band and Hilbert transformed at last. A higher clcorr indicates that the spike locations away from the preferred location correlate more with the theta phase.

Statistical Analysis.

Data processing, figure generation, and statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks) and MATLAB toolbox, hatchfill2 (66). All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. The exact sample size (e.g., simulation trials) of each experiment is indicated in the figure legends. For the comparison of parameters including only 2 conditions, unpaired Student’s t test and Pearson’s correlation were used. For the comparison of more than 2 conditions within the same graph, ANOVA was performed. Statistical outcomes were based on P<0.05 and displayed throughout figures as follows: NS: not significant; *P<0.05; **P<0.01; ***P<0.001; and ****P<0.0001.
