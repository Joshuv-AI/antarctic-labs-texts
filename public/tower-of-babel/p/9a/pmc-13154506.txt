A community-based contraceptive education intervention study on inter-spousal communication, approval, and support for contraceptive use among married couples in selected districts of Jimma zone, southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Fekadu Yadassa, Gurmesa Tura, Zewudie Birhanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong many other factors, inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval, and partner support significantly influence the utilization and continued use of family planning. However, there is limited evidence on the use of behavior change models to structure contraceptive education. The purpose of this study was to determine the effect of contraceptive education on inter-spousal communication, partner’s contraceptive use approval, and support among married couples in Jimma Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based quasi-experimental study was conducted among married couples (control = 383; intervention = 383). Participants currently using contraceptives were selected from family planning registration book of Kebeles; health posts, Data were collected using a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire by trained data collectors and analyzed using SPSS version 23.0. The data were analyzed using a random-effect mixed-model statistical method.ResultsThe random-effect mixed-model logistic regression analysis showed that participants in the intervention kebeles had 3.6 times higher odds of improved communication at end line compared to those in the control kebeles [OR = 3.6; 95% CI: 2.90–4.50; P < 0.001], after adjusting for baseline differences and kebele-level clustering. Approval increased by 90% in the intervention group compared to the control group over time [OR = 1.90; 95% CI: 1.50–2.40; P < 0.001]. Similarly, participants in the intervention group had 3.1 times higher odds of supporting contraceptive use at end line compared to the control group [OR = 3.1; 95% CI: 2.50–3.85; P < 0.001], controlling for kebele-level clustering.ConclusionHigher levels of inter-spousal communication, partner’s contraceptive use approval, and support were reported in the intervention group compared to the control group. The use of a structured behavioral model in family planning education, along with male involvement, improves spousal discussion, approval, and support for contraceptive use, thereby enabling couples to use family planning more effectively.Trial RegistrationThe trial was registered retrospectively with a unique identification number of PACTR202107529608380. Introduction

Family planning refers to the ability of individuals and couples to anticipate and achieve their desired number of children by appropriately spacing and timing births through the use of contraceptive methods and the treatment of involuntary infertility [1].

Contraceptive use among couples varies worldwide and plays a significant role in managing family size and reducing unintended pregnancies. Globally, the prevalence of modern contraceptive use is estimated at approximately 57%, although this varies considerably across regions. Female-controlled methods, such as injectable and oral pills, account for the majority of contraceptive use [2, 3].

Although women often bear the primary responsibility for family planning, men frequently exert considerable influence on final contraceptive decisions [4, 5]. Therefore, the involvement of both partners is essential for the successful, effective, and sustainable implementation of reproductive health programs. Evidence indicates that when both spouses actively participate in family planning decisions, couples are more likely to achieve their desired family size, improve maternal and child health outcomes, and maintain consistent and continued use of contraceptive methods [6–8].

Inter-spousal communication, partner approval, and support for contraceptive use are closely interconnected components of couple dynamics that influence reproductive health behavior. Inter-spousal communication refers to the exchange of thoughts, feelings, intentions, and information between married partners about family planning, reproductive health, and other household matters. Contraceptive approval refers to a partner’s acceptance of or agreement with the use of contraception, while contraceptive support refers to the financial and non-financial assistance provided by male partners to help their wives initiate and continue using contraceptive methods [9–11]. Male involvement in family planning has been shown to enhance spousal communication, increase partner approval and support for contraceptive use, and improve women’s autonomy, all of which are strongly associated with higher contraceptive utilization, particularly in developing regions [12].

Effective communication between partners plays a pivotal role in enhancing shared decision-making and improving reproductive outcomes. In particular, it helps address psychological barriers to contraceptive use, including misinformation, emotional distress, and sociocultural taboos, which may otherwise lead to discontinuation or non-use of contraceptive methods [12].

The study on spousal communication and perceived social support for contraceptive practice among Malaysia women indicated that the false perception of one partner about the other results in the discordance of the perception of one another’s attitude towards the approval and support of contraceptive use, hence lead to nonuse of contraception [5, 13]. While it is common for men to report more favorable responses due to social desirability bias, women often underestimate their partner’s support, whereas men may overstate their approval-a pattern observed in studies conducted in Bangladesh [14, 15].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that inter-spousal communication about family planning significantly improves each partner’s understanding of the other’s attitudes, knowledge, support and approval regarding contraceptive use and this mutual comprehension promotes adherence to contraceptive methods, reduces misconceptions, and facilitates joint decision-making-particularly within sociocultural contexts where male dominance in reproductive decisions is prevalent [9, 16, 17].

Male partner approval and support are particularly important in sub-Saharan Africa because men often play a significant role in household decision-making. Research suggests that women who perceive their partners as supportive of contraceptive use are significantly more likely to adopt modern contraceptive methods, conversely, lack of partner approval can create barriers to contraceptive uptake [13].

The lack of communication between spouses has been strongly associated with unintended pregnancies and unmet contraceptive needs. Studies across sub-Saharan Africa have shown that women who do not discuss family planning with their partners are more likely to experience reproductive coercion and have lower rates of modern contraceptive uptake [18, 19]. Consequently, spousal communication is now recognized as a critical entry point for improving sexual and reproductive health outcomes and has significant programmatic implications for family planning initiatives [6, 20].

These discussions can foster partner approval and support-both of which are essential for sustained use, especially in patriarchal societies where male partners often hold decision-making power over reproductive matters [21–26], at a couple level helping partner’s contraceptive use approval and support.

Therefore, engaging men in contraceptive education is increasingly seen as a strategic priority. Evidence suggests that men exposed to family planning information, education, and communication (IEC) interventions are significantly more likely to discuss contraception with their wives and approve their use of contraceptives by up to four times more compared to those not exposed [17, 23, 27–30].

. While prior studies have assessed the influence of family planning education on contraceptive uptake among individual non-users [31–34], and others have explored the general association between spousal communication and contraceptive use [9, 23, 35], there is limited evidence on the effect of use of behavior change models specifically information-Motivation-Behavioral Skill Model (IMBS) driven conceptual framework to increase awareness of contraceptive methods through improvement of spousal communications, contraceptive use approval and support in the rural community of Ethiopia, specifically in Jimma Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

Though, currently the community health workers providing health services in rural community there is still high contraceptive discontinuations rate and low uptake of contraception when compared to urban that require continuous provision of education to enhance use continuation. Thus the authors hypothesize that structured contraceptive education; grounded in behavior change model (Information Motivation Behavioral Skill Model), can significantly enhance inter-spousal communication, improve partner approval of contraception, and strengthen support for wives’ contraceptive use. Thus, the purpose of this study was to assess the effect of contraceptive educational intervention on spousal communication, husbands’ approval, and support for contraceptive use among married couples in selected districts of Jimma Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting and design

A quasi-experimental community-based educational intervention study was conducted in the Kersa and Goma districts of the Jimma zone, southwest Ethiopia, which is 352 km away from the countries capital city Addis Ababa. The intervention took place from September 2020 to August 2021. The major ethnic group in the Jimma zone is Oromo (87.6%), followed by Amhara (4.05%). The principal language is Oromiffa, spoken as a first language (90.43%), followed by Amharic (5.33%). Most inhabitants were Muslims (85.65%), followed by 11.18% Orthodox Christians [36].

Study participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria

The source population for the study consisted of all married couples in the Jimma Zone, where the women were of reproductive age and using family planning methods. The study population specifically included women who were new acceptors of reversible modern contraceptive methods during the study period. The reason for selection of new accepters of contraception was that the umbrella of the project was to determine the effect of educational intervention on contraceptive use continuation that also could be mediated by other variables. Eligibility criteria included married couples who had lived in the area for at least six months, were currently living with their husbands, and intended to use family planning for at least the next two years.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software, with a 95% confidence interval, 80% power, and assumed proportions of P1 = 25.5% and P2 = 17% (P1 and P2 represent the proportions of contraceptive use discontinuation in the control and intervention arms, respectively, and were used to determine a larger sample size compared with other outcome variables, as contraceptive discontinuation was one of the outcomes addressed in another paper).This resulted in a sample size of 383 participants for each group (Control: N1 = 383; Intervention: N2 = 383).

Two districts, Kersa and Goma, were purposefully selected from the 21 existing districts based on their previous year’s performance in family planning services, particularly in recruiting new family planning acceptors, where they outperformed other districts. Additionally, their geographic locations are at opposite ends with one district acting as a buffer zone between them made them ideal for implementing the intervention while minimizing the risk of information exchange and potential bias.

The two districts are divided into smaller administrative units called Kebeles, which in turn are composed of households. Each Kebele has a health post staffed by health extension workers, providing services to approximately 5,000 people. Goma district comprises 41 Kebeles, while Kersa has 36. Thirteen Kebeles were selected for the study; however, adjustments were made based on geographical location and accessibility. The sample size for each district was then distributed among the Kebeles assigned to the control and intervention groups. To select study participants, lists of contraceptive users were obtained from the registration books of the respective health posts and selected.

Contraceptive educational intervention

Before the intervention, baseline data were collected. Following this, a family planning educational intervention was delivered to the intervention group while the control group was receiving the usual counseling services by health workers. Both male and female partners received the intervention in group settings as well, individually through home-to-home visits when participation in group is impossible. The training was carried out by fourteen trained health extension workers on the contraceptive education training materials.

Health education was given in four sessions over two consecutive months. The family planning education was given separately for the women and men study participants to ensure freedom and free discussion among similar gender group. Each group had four educational sessions and each session has further session break down for various groups of 5–8 participants, based on the number of participants in the respective kebeles, but the length of time for each sub-session was 60 min. No payment was done for the study participants for their involvement in the study.

We used charts, pictures and samples of contraceptive methods for teaching, and field notes. As this study is part of the mega project from which the first paper was published, the educational intervention was also guided by the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills (IMB) model, discussed in detail in the first article [36].

The content of the contraceptive education were population growth and development, the concept of family planning, benefits of family planning (health, socio-economic, environmental), types of family planning methods, advantage and disadvantages, side effects, misconceptions, myths, perceptions, male involvement (significance, inter-spousal communication, approval, and support).,

The study groups either control or intervention arm, the participants of the study, the data collectors and supervisors were kept unaware of which group is the intervention group, so that the bias which may arise from these conditions may be controlled. We assumed that the study units in the control and the intervention arms are almost similar in their back ground and doesn’t affect the research outcome and as such there would be little or no introduction of bias arising from differences in participants’ background. The report of this research was done using a Trend statement check list and it was attached at the index of the paper.

Data collection procedure and tool

Data were collected using a pre-tested, interviewer-administered, semi-structured questionnaire. The standardized data collection tool previously used in a published study was adapted, with particular emphasis on inter-spousal communication, the husband’s approval of contraceptive use, and his support for his wife’s use of family planning methods [36]. Inter-spousal communication-used interchangeably with “discussion between the husband and wife” was measured by the proportion of women, men, or couples who reported having discussed on topics such as, family planning or contraceptive use, and the desired number of children in the future, contraceptive use approval, support and frequency of discussion, how they reach on decision. Both husbands and wives were individually asked whether they had communicated or discussed these relevant topics over the past 12 months. Husbands were asked: Did you discuss on use of family planning, number of children to have (spacing, limitation) with your wife during past 12months? Wives were asked: Did you discuss on use of family planning, number of children to have (spacing, limitation) with your husband during past 12months? Their responses were recorded as binary variables: “Yes” indicating the presence of communication (coded as 1), and “No” indicating the absence of communication (coded as 0).

For contraceptive use approval both partners were asked whether such approval existed during past 12 months. The wives were asked: “Did your husband permit you to use contraceptive method in the past 12months?” Their responses were recorded as binary variables: “Yes” indicating the presence of approval (coded as 1), and “No” indicating the absence of approval (coded as 0). The husbands were asked: “Do you permit your wife to use contraceptive method if she wishes?” Their responses were recorded as binary variables: “Yes” indicating the presence of approval (coded as 1),

On assessment of partner support both the husbands and Wives were asked separately questions> Husbands were asked: Did you support your wife to use contraceptive methods in the past 12 months? Their responses were recorded as binary variables: “Yes” indicating the presence of support (coded as 1), and “No” indicating the absence of support (coded as 0).

Wives were asked: Did your husband support you to use contraceptive methods in the past 12months? Their responses were recorded as binary variables: “Yes” indicating the presence of support (coded as 1), and “No” indicating the absence of support (coded as 0).

Data collectors underwent two days of training, while supervisors were trained for one day. A total of 24 data collectors were recruited from the same locality, sharing similar socio-cultural backgrounds. This was intended to facilitate the collection of sensitive sexual and reproductive health information.

The data collection process was led by health extension workers. Data collectors moved from household to household using house numbers obtained from family folders at the local health post. To ensure privacy, data were collected separately from both husbands and wives by different data collectors. This approach helped protect the confidentiality of responses, particularly in cases where women were using family planning methods without their husband’s knowledge.

Prior to data collection, the purpose of the study was explained to each participant, and informed consent was obtained. If either the husband or wife was unavailable at the time of the visit, the data collectors made return visits to ensure their participation.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was developed following a thorough review of the literature and was pretested on 5% of a sample drawn from a similar population outside the study site but within the same zone. This pretesting aimed to ensure clarity, logical sequencing, and appropriate wording of the questions. The reliability of the instrument was assessed using Cranach’s alpha and yielded a coefficient of 0.79. Additionally, during data collection, questionnaires were checked daily for completeness.

Data analysis

The data were cleaned, coded, and entered into a computer, then analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 for Windows. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to generate frequency distributions of the participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, the proportions of inter-spousal communication on family planning use and future fertility, approval and support for contraceptive use.

The difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis approach was performed based on aggregated percentage responses of male and female spouses to estimate group-level changes over time demonstrated a positive change in inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval and support in the intervention group relative to the control group. The finding showed that the intervention significantly improved inter-spousal communication (with 95% CI at P < 0.05.

Furthermore, a random-effects mixed model to evaluate the effect of the intervention on inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval, and support while accounting for the clustered study design and repeated measurements. The model included fixed effects for time (baseline vs. end line), study group (intervention vs. control), and their interaction (time × group), with kebele specified as a random effect to account for intra-cluster correlation. To account for clustering with in Kebeles and repeated measurements over time random-effects logistic regression model were fitted for each outcome:

Whereas uj represents the random intercept for each kebele, Group × Time interaction estimates the effect of the intervention over time. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All the outcomes were balanced at a baseline across intervention and control with no statistically significant difference.

Recruitment, enrollment, assignment and follow up

The recruitment the study was carried out during the two months of July and August 2020. The calculated sample of couples (766) was proportionately assigned to the seven kebeles based on their total new family planning acceptors during the study period. Then, the study unit was selected randomly using lottery method checking for exclusion and inclusion criteria which includes being new acceptor of family planning, the partners currently live together, already lived in the area for at least six months, have plan to use contraceptives for the next two years. The 766 couples equally assigned to control and intervention arm (N1=383, and N2=383) and base line data was collected for one month and baseline data was analyzed. Because of incompleteness of data two couples from both the intervention and control were omitted. Then the intervention was commenced with 381 couples for both control and intervention each. Then follow up was done till the end of August 2021. All the study participants competed the follow up study data was analyzed. (Fig. 1).

Result

The socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A quasi-experimental study was conducted in two selected districts of the Jimma Zone, located in the southwest of Ethiopia. The study included married couples of reproductive age, with participants divided into control and intervention groups. A total of 762 couples participated, yielding a high response rate of 99.5%. Both the control and intervention groups consisted of equal sample sizes, with 383 couples each currently using contraceptive method. The age distribution of the couples was similar across both groups. Most women in the intervention and control groups were aged 25–34 years, accounting for 247 (64.9%) and 245 (64.3%) respectively. In contrast, the majority of men in the intervention group (182; 47.8%) and control group (216; 56.6%) were between 35 and 44 years of age, indicating that men were generally older than their spouses in both groups. Regarding educational status, a majority of women in the intervention group were illiterate, while most in the control group had attained junior primary education (56.7%). Among men, 27.8% in the intervention group were illiterate, compared to 63.3% of the control group who had completed junior primary education. Over 90% of participants identified as Oromo by ethnicity and Muslim by religion. In terms of occupation, the vast majority of women in both the intervention (369; 96.9%) and control groups (367; 96.6%) were housewives. Most men in the intervention (379; 99.5%) and control (358; 94%) groups were farmers. Nearly all participants resided in rural areas (Table 1).

Comparison of couples’ report of Inter-Spousal Communication, approval, and support for contraceptive use

The study showed that in the control category, couples reported higher inter-spousal communication (76.2%) in the end-line assessment, compared to baseline assessment (73.9%); approval for contraceptive use were also reported higher (73.4%) in the end-line assessment, compared to baseline assessment (72.2%); and support for contraceptives were also higher (70.9%) in the end-line assessment, compared to baseline assessment (66.4%). In the intervention category, assessment post intervention on inter-spousal communication was higher (82.8%) than pre-intervention assessment (72.0%). On approval for contraceptive use, post intervention assessment were also reported higher (82.6%), compared to pre-intervention (71.6%). Similarly, supports for contraceptives were also higher (79.6%) post intervention, compared to pre-intervention assessment (70.4%). The finding also showed that husbands consistently reporting slightly higher levels of inter-spousal communication, approval of contraceptive use, and support than their wives did across both the control and intervention (Table 2).

The random-effect mixed model logistic regression analysis on communication, contraceptive use approval and support

This is the primary analysis conducted using a random-effects mixed model to evaluate the effect of the intervention on inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval, and support while accounting for the clustered study design and repeated measurements. The model included fixed effects for time (baseline vs. end line), study group (intervention vs. control), and their interaction (time × group), with kebele specified as a random effect to account for intra-cluster correlation. To account for clustering with in Kebeles and repeated measurements over time random-effects logistic regression model were fitted for each outcome:

logit(P(Yijk = 1))=β0 + β1Groupij + β2Timek + β3(Group×Time)+ujlogit(P(Yijk = 1))=β0 + β1Groupij + β2Timek + β3(Group×Time) + uj, Whereas uj represents the random intercept for each kebele, Group × Time interaction estimates the effect of the intervention over time. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All the outcomes were balanced at a baseline across intervention and control with no statistically significant difference.

The finding showed that participants in intervention kebeles had a 3.6 times higher odds of improved communication at end line compared to control kebeles, adjusting for baseline and kebele clustering and approval increased by 90% compared to the control over time. Similarly, participants in the intervention group had 3.1 times higher odds of supporting at end line compared to control, controlling for kebele-level clustering. Overall the result shows that the intervention had strong positive effects on inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval and support in intervention group compared with the control.

The aggregate difference-in-differences analysis demonstrated a positive change in contraceptive support in the intervention group relative to the control group; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, the random-effects mixed model, which utilized individual-level data and accounted for clustering at kebele level and repeated measurements, revealed a statistically significant intervention effect.

The discrepancy between the two approaches is likely attributable to methodological differences. The aggregate DiD analysis relied on summarized percentage values, thereby reducing statistical power and not explicitly accounting for intra-cluster correlation. Conversely, the mixed-effects model partitioned variance components appropriately and produced more efficient standard error estimates, resulting in improved statistical precision and detection of a significant intervention effect (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study assessed the effect of a community-based intervention on inter-spousal communication, partner approval, and support for contraceptive use among married couples. in selected districts of Jimma Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

The study showed that there was some degree of discordances in the proportion of reported Interspousal communication, contraceptive use approval and support between spouses within couples of both the control and intervention groups, but these disparities were proportionally narrowed at the end line particularly in the intervention couples.

With respect to the discordances in the report of inter-spousal communication contraceptive use approval and support of the partners between the women and men of the respective cohort, the finding showed that the variation was narrowed down to the extent that the husbands and wives reported contraceptive use approval and support equally at the end line among the intervention group when compared to the control group. These changes may be associated with the intervention which can be explained the fact that the educational intervention might have facilitated understanding and trust between the husband and the wives so that the spouses could have communicated, approved and expressed their supportive attitude. This is not only limited to the intra-couple differences but also inter-couples differences which was addressed using the random-effect mixed model analysis in this chapter. The finding was consistent with the study findings of Urban Kenya which assessed the perceived partner concordance among women and men Kenya [6, 37] and women often underestimate their partner’s support, while men may overstate their approval a pattern observed in studies from Bangladesh [14].

On the other hand, the findings indicate that the intervention improved couples’ inter-spousal communication, contraceptive use approval and support when analyzed using random-effects mixed model that accounted for clustering at the kebele level. These results suggest that interventions targeting both partners can positively influence relational factors that are known to facilitate contraceptive uptake and continuation. The random-effects mixed model showed that participants in intervention group were found to report inter-spousal communication 3.6 tomes when compared to the control group. The improvement in inter-spousal communication observed in the intervention group is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that improved awareness towards contraception enhances partners communication aligning with results from a study in Nepal on the effects of a radio drama on spousal communication and family planning adoption [18, 26, 35].

The study also showed that intervention showed improvement in participants’ report of contraceptive use approval (90% more likely) and support (3.1 times) in the intervention group compared to the control group. This finding is consistent with the findings from a study in Iran, which showed increased male participation, has improved partner approval and contraceptive us support after educational interventions [29].

The chi-square tests for provision of crude comparison between the husband and wives within couples and assessment of sex specific differences across intervention and control groups showed that there were differences observed at the end line among men and women (P < 0.001) indicating more level of communication, approval and support in the intervention group. This finding also has been reflected in the couple level comparisons with the analysis of random-effect mixed model above and the finding was consistent with existing evidences [18, 26].

Male partner approval has been widely recognized as an important factor influencing women’s reproductive health decisions in many low- and middle-income countries, where men often play a dominant role in household decision-making. Previous research in Ethiopia and other African settings has demonstrated that women whose partners approve of family planning are significantly more likely to use contraceptives than those whose partners disapprove or remain neutral [38, 39] Interventions that involve men and encourage couple dialogue have therefore been recommended as effective strategies to improve family planning outcomes and promote shared responsibility for reproductive health [27].

As a programmatic implication, communication between partners helps clarify fertility preferences and encourages mutual approval of family planning, which can subsequently translate into supportive behaviors that facilitate contraceptive adoption and continuation [3, 6]. Thus the findings highlight the importance of addressing relational and social dynamics within couples when promoting family planning. Programs that encourage partner communication, male engagement, and supportive attitudes toward contraceptive use may contribute substantially to improving reproductive health outcomes. These approaches are particularly relevant in these settings where gender norms and decision-making dynamics influence contraceptive behavior.

Strengths and limitations of the study

First, the use of a cluster-based intervention design at the kebele level minimized contamination between participants and reflected real-world implementation of community-based family planning interventions. Second, the inclusion of baseline and end line measurements enabled assessment of changes over time and strengthened causal inference regarding the intervention effect. Third, the application of difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis allowed estimation of the net intervention effect by accounting for pre-existing differences between intervention and control groups. In addition, the use of a random-effects mixed model appropriately accounted for the hierarchical structure of the data and intra-cluster correlation, improving the robustness of the estimates. Another important strength is the inclusion of both male and female spouses, which provided a more comprehensive understanding of couple dynamics related to inter-spousal communication, contraceptive approval, and support.

Despite these strengths, some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study relied on self-reported measures, which may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive topics such as contraceptive use and partner communication. Furthermore, the relatively small number of clusters (kebeles) may limit the precision of cluster-level variance estimates. Finally, potential interaction between participants from intervention and control areas cannot be entirely ruled out, which may have diluted the observed intervention effects and .the unmeasured contextual or district-level factors could have influenced the results, making it difficult to attribute the observed changes solely to the intervention.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study evaluated the effect of a community-based intervention on inter-spousal communication, partner approval, and support for contraceptive use among married couples in clustered kebele settings. The findings indicate that the intervention contributed to improvements in couple communication and supportive attitudes toward family planning. Although the difference-in-differences analysis showed limited statistical significance for some outcomes, the random-effects mixed model which accounted for clustering and hierarchical data structure—demonstrated significant intervention effects. These results suggest that programs targeting both spouses and encouraging joint discussion of reproductive intentions can positively influence relational factors that facilitate contraceptive decision-making.

Overall, the study highlights the importance of addressing couple dynamics in family planning interventions. Strengthening inter-spousal communication and male partner support may enhance the effectiveness of reproductive health programs and contribute to increased contraceptive uptake, continuation and improved reproductive health outcomes in similar settings.

Family planning programs should incorporate couple-centered approaches that promote communication between partners and encourage shared decision-making regarding reproductive health. Health interventions should actively engage men as supportive partners in family planning education and counseling, as male approval and support play a critical role in contraceptive use.

Healthcare providers should emphasize inter-spousal dialogue during family planning counseling, helping couples discuss fertility intentions and contraceptive options.

Community-level awareness programs should address gender norms and misconceptions that limit open communication about family planning within couples.

Future studies should include a larger number of clusters and longer follow-up periods to improve the precision of intervention effect estimates and assess the sustainability of behavioral changes.
