Karyomegalic interstitial nephritis without FAN1 mutation: a rare cause of chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis
Authors: Olha Shelest, Katarzyna Sobczyńska, Krzysztof Okoń, Anna Sączek, Katarzyna Krzanowska, Marcin Krzanowski
Published: 2025

A 34-year-old male with a diagnosis of chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage G3b of undetermined etiology was admitted due to progression of CKD to stage 4 over the past 8 months. His medical history included progressive deterioration in exercise tolerance and weakness, as well as multiple episodes of upper respiratory infection within the past year. He had no family history of kidney disease and no significant exposure to drugs or environmental toxins, including heavy metals. Upon admission, biochemical tests revealed a creatinine level of 179 µmol/L (reference range, 62–106 µmol/L) and an estimated glomerular filtration rate CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) of 43 mL/min/1.73 m² (reference range, >90 mL/min/1.73 m²). Urinalysis showed trace proteinuria without hematuria or leukocyturia; a 24-hour urine protein level of 0.43 g (reference range, <0.15 g); and a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 3.86 (reference range, <3). An abdominal ultrasound revealed slightly increased echogenicity of the kidneys. To further the diagnostic workup, a kidney biopsy was performed. The histopathological examination revealed several characteristic alterations in the renal tubular cells, including large, polymorphic, hyperchromatic nuclei (karyomegaly) (Fig. 1) and prominent nucleoli (Fig. 2). These changes were associated with mild interstitial fibrosis and inflammation (Fig. 1). Following the diagnosis of karyomegalic interstitial nephritis (KIN), additional diagnostics were performed. Molecular testing of upper respiratory swabs and stool samples ruled out viral infections, including adenovirus. All blood cultures, both bacteriological and mycological, were negative, and parasitic infections were excluded. A comprehensive immunological panel revealed no secondary causes of KIN. Genetic testing revealed no pathogenic mutations in the FAN1 gene, which is vital for DNA repair and has been linked to KIN.

Based on the patient’s overall clinical presentation and clear histopathological findings supporting a diagnosis of KIN, we concluded that the disease could be linked to genetic predisposition and undetected mutations in other genes. The literature suggests that mutations in the PRODH gene may play a pathogenic role. Unfortunately, we did not investigate this mutation. There is no treatment protocol for KIN. We recommended supportive nephroprotective measures, including lifestyle modifications and the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. During the subsequent 6-month period, kidney function remained stable.

KIN is a rare chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis that can be triggered by drugs, toxins, infections, or immunological conditions in genetically susceptible individuals. Kidney biopsy is essential for diagnosis, and the relative contributions of genetic predisposition and additional triggers remain unclear.
