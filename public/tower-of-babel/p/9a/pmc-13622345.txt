Comparison of 6-Week Doxycycline vs 6-Week Rifampicin or 3 Weeks of Doxycycline Plus Rifampicin in the Treatment of Onchocerciasis: A Randomized, Placebo-Controlled, Double-Blind, Phase 2 Trial
Authors: Sabine Specht, Ute Klarmann-Schulz, Alexander Y Debrah, Linda Batsa Debrah, Sabine Mand, Jubin Osei-Mensah, Bettina Dubben, Nana K Ayisi-Boateng, Yusif Mubarik, Yeboah Marfo-Debrekyei, Rolf Fimmers, Peter Konadu, Arcangelo Ricchiuto, Kenneth Pfarr, Barbara Gruetzmacher, Jennifer Nadal, Francesca Tamarozzi, Kelly Johnston, Mark J Taylor, Achim Hoerauf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepletion of Wolbachia has proven to be a validated curative treatment for filariasis. This trial investigated alternatives to the current treatment regimen for onchocerciasis: 6 weeks of doxycycline (DOX) at 200 mg daily.MethodsThis randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, phase 2a trial with 5 treatment regimens was conducted in Ghana to determine (1) whether treatment times could be shortened as compared with 6 weeks of DOX at 200 mg daily by using a combination of DOX and rifampicin (RIF) for 3 weeks or (2) whether a dosage reduction to 6 weeks of DOX at 100 mg or a change to 6 weeks of RIF at 10 mg/kg daily would result in noninferior efficacy.ResultsThe primary outcome—the absence of onchocercomata with worms showing normal embryogenesis at 20 months—revealed that DOX at 200 mg or 100 mg for 6 weeks was superior to all other interventions. DOX + RIF for 3 weeks was less effective but superior to RIF alone and to placebo, suggesting that shorter regimens can be designed by combining antiwolbachials. Finally, depletion of Wolbachia in nodules 6 months after study onset strongly correlated with antiparasitic effects at 20 months.ConclusionsReduction of DOX from 200 to 100 mg daily facilitates implementation of this regimen, by aligning it to other regimens using 100 mg daily with a larger safety profile. The results also indicate that a combination of antiwolbachials might deliver effective regimens with shorter treatment times. Finally, the ability to predict 20-month end points of the slow-acting antiwolbachials by using Wolbachia depletion at 6 months will accelerate follow-up end points earlier than macrofilaricidal end points.

Onchocerciasis, caused by Onchocerca volvulus nematodes, has a disproportionate impact on the affected population of 21 million, with 1.26 million disability-adjusted life years lost in 2021 [1]. Symptoms are caused by the first-stage larvae (microfilariae [MF]), leading to dermatitis and blindness.

Community-directed treatment with ivermectin (IVM) as mass drug administration (MDA) has dramatically reduced onchocerciasis in Africa. The World Health Organization’s road map for neglected tropical diseases (2021–2030) [2] calls for elimination, but challenges exist; for example, long-term delivery of IVM with high coverage [3] is difficult to achieve in hard-to-reach areas. In addition, evidence of suboptimal response to IVM [4, 5] concerns the scientific community, and many Loa loa coendemic areas are still excluded from MDA, since adverse events (AEs)—namely, fatal encephalitis—were documented in patients with high L loa MF loads (>20 000/mL blood) after IVM treatment for onchocerciasis [6, 7]. The latter issue also impedes the use of the newly approved microfilaricide moxidectin [8–10]. Therefore, it is clear that current MDA will not eliminate the infection for many years to come [3, 11], and stakeholders have advocated the need to develop safe macrofilaricidals to overcome these hurdles.

Antibiotic-based therapy targeting Wolbachia endosymbionts has, so far, delivered doxycycline (DOX) as the only safe macrofilaricide with superior activity to IVM. Advantages of DOX are its long-term/permanent sterilizing effect on adult female worms and the death of adult parasites. DOX is affordable and has a well-known safety profile. However, due to contraindications (pregnant women, children <8 years old) and adherence issues for multiweek regimens, new drugs and shortened treatment regimens are required. The A·WOL consortium was founded to develop new anti-Wolbachia drugs with a period ≤14 days and utility in excluded populations and to optimize regimens of existing drugs such as DOX for use in more restricted target populations (https://awol.lstmed.ac.uk/ [12, 13]).

In murine filariasis with L sigmodontis, rifampicin (RIF) alone or in combination with DOX for 14 days depleted Wolbachia, inhibited embryogenesis, and led to worm sterility [14, 15]. Thus, in our trial, we aimed to investigate whether the combination of DOX + RIF would allow shorter antiwolbachial treatments against onchocerciasis than with DOX alone. The second aim was to determine whether reduction of the DOX dose from 200 mg daily to 100 mg daily for 6 weeks would lead to equivalent long-term sterilizing and macrofilaricidal effects [16, 17]. The third aim investigated whether a 10-mg/kg RIF-only regimen was noninferior to DOX.

METHODS

Study Design and Participant Consent Statement

This randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, phase 2 trial was conducted from 2009 to 2015 (https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN68861628) and approved by the Committee on Human Research Publication and Ethics, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi, Ghana; the Research Ethics Committee of the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine, Liverpool, United Kingdom; and the Ethical Committee, University Hospital Bonn, Bonn, Germany. All participants provided written/thumb-printed informed consent. A Data Monitoring and Ethic Committee followed the data acquisition and approved the final datasets before deblinding.

Participants

Healthy volunteers aged 18 to 55 years with at least 1 palpable Onchocerca nodule (onchocercomata) were included in the trial (detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria: https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN68861628). Participants were recruited from 39 villages adjacent to the river Offin in Ghana (Upper and Lower Denkyira Districts, Central Region; Amansie Central and Adanse South Districts, Ashanti Region). MDA has been implemented since 2001 in Upper and Lower Denkyira Districts and from 2008 in the Amansie Central and Adanse South Districts. However, MDA compliance was not very high, and a considerable number of people had infrequently or not taken part in IVM MDA.

Interventions

Individuals were randomized according to their MF status (positive/negative) to 1 of the following 5 treatments:

DOX200 (n = 150): 6 weeks of DOX at 200 mg (Doxydoc, 100 mg/capsule; docpharm) and placebo matching RIF

DOX200 (n = 150): 6 weeks of DOX at 200 mg (Doxydoc, 100 mg/capsule; docpharm) and placebo matching RIF

DOX100 (n = 100): 6 weeks of DOX at 100 mg plus placebo matching DOX at 100 mg and placebo matching RIF

DOX100 (n = 100): 6 weeks of DOX at 100 mg plus placebo matching DOX at 100 mg and placebo matching RIF

DOX-RIF (n = 100): 3 weeks of DOX at 200 mg and RIF at 10 mg/kg/d (Rifampicin Hefa-N, 150 mg/capsule; Riemser Arzneimittel AG), followed by 3 weeks of placebo matching DOX and RIF

DOX-RIF (n = 100): 3 weeks of DOX at 200 mg and RIF at 10 mg/kg/d (Rifampicin Hefa-N, 150 mg/capsule; Riemser Arzneimittel AG), followed by 3 weeks of placebo matching DOX and RIF

RIF (n = 100): 6 weeks of RIF at 10 mg/kg/d and placebo matching DOX

RIF (n = 100): 6 weeks of RIF at 10 mg/kg/d and placebo matching DOX

Placebo (n = 50): 6 weeks of placebo matching DOX and RIF

Placebo (n = 50): 6 weeks of placebo matching DOX and RIF

The placebos were manufactured at the University Hospital Bonn under the supervision of its pharmacy. The study drugs were administered by directly observed treatment for 6 weeks; 150-µg/kg IVM was distributed at 6 months to ensure that trial participants remained in an annual MDA treatment cycle.

To ensure blinding of patients and the research team in the field, drugs were packaged according to a computerized randomization list and marked with an individual code, leaving no trace of the treatment regimen. The first 100 patients with >2 nodules and >1 nodule site were randomized separately because they were scheduled for an additional nodulectomy of 1 site at the 6-month follow-up.

Trial Procedures

At recruitment, volunteers were palpated for nodules [18]. For MF analysis, 2 skin biopsies of 1 to 3 mg were taken from the buttocks, processed, and evaluated as described [17, 19]. Nodulectomies were performed at 6 and 20 months under local anesthesia in the hospital [4, 20]. Patients were hospitalized for 1 day for postoperative observation. Wound dressing was continued in the villages until wounds were healed.

The extirpated onchocercomata were processed, and worms were assessed for vitality, sex, status of embryogenesis, and presence of Wolbachia by 2 experienced parasitologists (S. S., B. D.) with at least 8 sections across the nodules as described and fully blinded to the treatment groups [4, 17, 20]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Wolbachia ftsZ and O volvulus actin was carried out as described [20].

Statistical Analysis

Sample Size

Power calculation was performed in SAS (SAS Institute Inc). A detailed description of the underlying assumptions can be found in the Supplementary material.

Statistical Methods

The computer software packages SPSS 22/29 (IBM Corp) and SAS 9.2/9.4 were used. Study treatments were tested first for superiority to placebo and second for noninferiority to DOX200. This stepwise testing maintained an overall level of α = 0.833% for both comparisons of the experimental treatment. The overall level for the tests of all 3 experimental treatment groups is α = 2.5%, 1-sided.

A 1-sided P < .0083 and 2-sided P < .0167 were considered significant for all primary and secondary outcomes at 20 months. All baseline comparisons, as well as the only descriptive comparisons at 6 months, were done with a 2-sided P < .05.

For the comparisons, alternating logistic regression (Proc Genmod; SAS) was chosen for the histologic outcomes, while a generalized linear model was used for the PCR analyses. Both models allowed correction for possible dependency of the observation on several nodules/worms from 1 patient.

Based on the assumptions chosen for the power calculation, it was determined that noninferiority would be assumed if the upper confidence limit for the odds ratio of the treatment difference effect was <3. Statistical comparisons for noninferiority to DOX200 were performed only if the previous comparison vs placebo had rejected the null hypothesis.

Analysis sets were prepared before deblinding: intention to treat (ITT), per protocol (PP), and safety. The primary PP set included all participants who completed treatment without any violations of the protocol and underwent nodulectomies at 20 months. A secondary PP set was created for the participants who underwent surgery at 6 months. The safety set, describing all AEs, included all patients who took the drugs at least 1 time. The ITT set included all patients who were randomized, and it was used for the baseline data and confirmation of the PP analysis. ITT analysis of the histologic and PCR results included all operated cases, and missing values were not replaced. The use of the PP analysis as primary analysis was chosen and prespecified in the statistical analysis plan because for noninferiority trials the ITT analysis is generally not conservative and its role should be considered carefully (International Council for Harmonisation guideline E9: “Statistical Principles for Clinical Trials, https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/E9_Guideline.pdf).

RESULTS

In total, 537 patients with onchocerciasis from 39 villages were randomized into the treatment arms (Figure 1). Of these, 508 received treatment (DOX200, n = 153; DOX100, n = 100; DOX + RIF, n = 102; RIF, n = 102; placebo, n = 51). At 20 months, 421 of 508 (82.9%) fulfilled the ITT criteria, whereas 355 (69.9%) fulfilled the PP criteria (ie, present at all follow-ups).

Trial profile: At the 6-month follow-up, 102 participants with >3 nodules in >1 site underwent surgery, and the nodules from 1 site were removed. Of the 102 patients, 5 (DOX, 200 mg, 6 weeks, n = 2; DOX + RIF, 3 weeks, n = 2; RIF, 6 weeks, n = 1) did not have any evaluable nodules (no Onchocerca nodules [NON], n = 3; no worm sections found [NWSF] n = 2). In the remaining 97 participants (PP, n = 93), 268 nodules were found and removed. Of the 268 nodules, 12 (DOX, 200 mg, 6 weeks, n = 4; DOX, 100 mg, 6 weeks, n = 2; DOX + RIF, 3 weeks, n = 3; RIF, 6 weeks, n = 3) were not evaluable (for PCR only, n = 1; not good quality [NGQ], n = 1; NON, n = 6; NWSF, n = 4), so the results of 256 nodules (PP, n = 243) were used for further analyses. In total, 421 participants attended the 20-month follow-up. Of these, 10 could not undergo surgery because they did not have any palpable nodules left (DOX, 200 mg, 6 weeks, n = 2; DOX, 100 mg, 6 weeks, n = 5; DOX + RIF, 3 weeks, n = 1; RIF, 6 weeks, n = 2). Of the 411 patients who underwent surgery, 18 did not have any evaluable nodules (DOX, 200 mg, 6 weeks, n = 5; DOX, 100 mg, 6 weeks, n = 3; DOX + RIF, 3 weeks, n = 4; RIF, 6 weeks, n = 4; placebo, n = 2; ganglion, n = 2; lymph nodes, n = 6; no nodule after incision, n = 6; NON, n = 1; NWSF, n = 3). Primary Outcome: Rates of Onchocercomata With Worms Showing Normal Embryogenesis as Assessed by Histology 20 Months After Treatment Onset

Long-term absence of embryogenesis indicates sterility and is important to reach interruption of transmission (Figure 2A–C). As previously shown, both DOX-only groups (6 weeks) are sufficient to achieve long-term absence of MF. Albeit not formerly powered to confirm noninferiority between 100- and 200-mg dosing, both DOX groups appeared essentially equivalent in this trial (Tables 2 and 3), as >98.8% of the nodules contained only sterile or dead female worms, rendering both groups superior to all other treatments; this result also confirmed our earlier studies [4, 16, 17]. Importantly, dose reduction to DOX at 100 mg daily was sufficient for long-term sterility.

The extirpated onchocercomata were fixed in 80% ethanol or 4% phosphate-buffered formaldehyde solution. During further processing, the nodules were embedded in paraffin and cut. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin or the Gomori method for iron, or they were immunostained with antibodies against cathepsin D–like lysosomal aspartic protease (ASP) of Onchocerca volvulus for worm vitality or against Dirofilaria immitis Wolbachia surface protein or Brugia malayi Wolbachia peptidoglycan-associated lipoprotein for presence of Wolbachia. Characteristics for death of a worm included the absence of ASP staining, the loss of nuclei of all organs, and the loss of body wall integrity. Very degenerated worms, though still ASP positive, were grouped in the category “dead” and classified as moribund. The designation “living” refers to worms judged as being alive at the time of nodulectomy [20, 21]. Developmental stages from the 2-cell stage to stretched microfilariae (MF) in the uterus were classified as “embryos.” When only the stretched MF were degenerated and the other embryo stages were not, embryogenesis was recorded as “normal” for the purpose of this doxycycline (DOX)–oriented study, since previous ivermectin treatments would have conferred precisely these effects—specifically, degeneration of intrauterine-stretched MF (in addition to killing of skin MF) but not earlier developmental stages [22]. The ITT analysis confirmed the results of the PP analysis (Supplementary Tables 1c and 1e).

As expected, no differences in embryogenesis were observed among the groups at 6 months (Supplementary Tables 1a, 1b, and 1d). Secondary Outcomes

The analysis of embryogenesis per worm confirmed the primary outcome (Supplementary Tables 2a and 2b). We analyzed nodules containing free and living MF (Figure 2D). The outcome at 20 months, with 1.4% and 0% in the DOX200 and DOX100 groups, respectively, reflected the low rate of worms with intact embryogenesis in these groups. This parameter also showed clear superiority to the DOX + RIF, RIF, and placebo groups, with 11.7%, 27.8%, and 17.7% (Supplementary Tables 3a–3f).

Prior to treatment, skin MF-positive cases were equally distributed with 83.1% across the groups (Table 4, Figure 3, Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Figure 1). At 6 months, microfilaridermia remained nearly unaffected by treatment, except for the DOX200 group, which had a slight decrease (P = .014). In contrast, at 20 months, in both DOX groups, skin MF-positive cases were strongly reduced (7.5% and 1.6%, respectively). Therefore, both DOX groups were superior to the DOX + RIF, RIF, and placebo groups (46.7%, 67.1%, and 69.4% MF positive). Since all groups in the PP set had received IVM after 6 months, the high positivity rate in these 3 latter groups indicates recovery after IVM but not after DOX, which sterilized the adult female worms.

As seen previously [17], no differences in the number of live and dead worms occurred after 6 months. Patients Without Female Worms With Normal Embryogenesis

For regulatory registration of new chemical entities or expansion of an existing indication, focus is on the patient rather than on the worm or nodule. Our analyses take the dependence of worms or nodules in 1 participant into account, but the new aspect is that the study drugs are evaluated as effective only if the study participant no longer has a single living female worm with normal embryogenesis. Adverse Events

At least 1 AE was reported by 345 of 508 (67.9%) participants. In total, 722 AEs were documented during treatment (Table 11, Supplementary Tables 9a–9c). Discoloration of urine, nausea, stomach pain, vomiting, and diarrhea were differently distributed among the 5 groups. A higher percentage was expected for discoloration of urine in the 2 RIF groups because it is a described RIF AE. Nausea, stomach pain, and vomiting occurred at a higher level in the DOX200 group. Serious AEs were reported for 2 participants who died for reasons unrelated to treatment between the 6- and 20-month follow-up.

Prediction of Embryogenesis and Microfilaridermia

The uniqueness of this study is the dataset of 74 study participants who had nodules removed at 6 and 20 months. This dataset allowed us to determine if an outcome at 20 months can be predicted at 6 months. To find the best predictive parameters, the following variables were used: proportions of live female worms with (1) Wolbachia at 6 months, (2) normal embryogenesis at 6 and 20 months, (3) degenerated embryogenesis only at 6 and 20 months, and (4) presence/absence of MF or MF/mg skin at 6 and 20 months.

The parameter “proportions of live female worms without Wolbachia at 6 months” was the best for predicting absence of MF at 20 months (Cohen κ = 0.706, Spearman ρ = 0.747; Figure 4A) and absence of live female worms with normal embryogenesis at 20 months (Cohen κ = 0.703, Spearman ρ = 0.669; Figure 4B).

This shows for the first time in a randomized controlled trial that the 6-month time point for new antiwolbachials is a well-chosen time point to test the initial efficacy in pilot studies.

DISCUSSION

The World Health Organization’s neglected tropical disease road map (2021–2030) highlights the development of a macrofilaricide as a critical action to accelerate interruption of transmission for onchocerciasis [2].

Targeting Wolbachia endosymbionts in O volvulus has been extensively validated as a curative therapy with DOX, which is macrofilaricidal for 70% of adult female worms at 2-year follow-up when administered at 200 mg daily for 6 weeks and induces permanent sterility in the remaining worms, as observed by irreversible damage of the uterus epithelia [23]. Additional trials with 100 mg daily for 6 weeks confirmed the permanent sterilization and macrofilaricidal outcomes. Meta-analysis from several DOX trials using the Epi-Oncho model showed that the difference in macrofilaricidal performance between 100-mg daily and 200-mg daily DOX is not statistically significant [24].

The present study shows noninferiority of the 100-mg daily dose in a direct comparison with the 200-mg daily treatment arm within the same randomized trial. Using a lower daily dose would facilitate the implementation of DOX, since the level of experience is much higher than for 200 mg daily, as a dose that has been used for shorter times (eg, for Lyme disease) or when treating severe bacterial infections (eg, brucellosis). DOX at 100 mg daily in malaria chemoprophylaxis has been adopted by the Royal Society of Tropical Medicine and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention without restrictions on duration [25] and by the German Society for Tropical Medicine [26].

The second study outcome concerns whether the combination of 2 effective antiwolbachials can reduce treatment times. In previous trials, partial efficacy of RIF had been observed [27, 28]. Therefore, we included a DOX + RIF 3-week treatment. Although the overall treatment outcome was inferior to the DOX 6-week arms and would therefore not be recommended, the combination showed superiority to placebo and the nonefficacious RIF-only arm for the end points Wolbachia depletion and normal embryogenesis at 20 months. Thus, these data indicate that a combination of antibiotics may be one way to shorten treatment times, which is a focus of current trials.

The insufficient performance of RIF for 6 weeks may be explained by new studies on pharmacokinetic modeling of RIF data in rodents and humans. Accordingly, the doses used in the animal studies [14] are bioequivalent to a daily dose of RIF in humans that is 4-fold higher than the normally used dose of 600 mg daily (up to 2.4 g daily in patients who have tuberculosis with relatively mild adverse reactions [29]). This higher dose is now being used in 2 clinical pilot trials for the treatment of onchocerciasis (PACTR202009704006025, ISRCTN38954299). Data from Brugia malayi infection in rodents show that a correspondingly lower dose does not sufficiently deplete Wolbachia or cause antiparasitic effects [30]. The modeling algorithms were newly developed within A·WOL and were not available at the initiation of the present trial; nevertheless, they underscore how important thorough pharmacokinetic modeling will become for future trials.

Another deliverable of the trial that is important for the design of future studies is confirmation of the earlier finding [20] that Wolbachia quantitative PCR can be performed from Onchocerca nodule sections intermittent to those used for histology and that PCR delivers results concordant with histology. Exact quantification of Wolbachia by histology is impossible. This is due to the high density in untreated worms, resulting in underassessment, and to the known variation in density in different parts of the worm [31]. A consensus from earlier trials is that Wolbachia needs to be reduced by >90% to observe the known antiparasitic effects (macrofilaricidal, embryotoxic) [6, 32]. This figure is not obtained quantitatively from histology, as this technology allows only a noncontinuous judgment of “many,” “few,” or “no” Wolbachia in a given section (90% reduction here pertains to 90% of worms showing few or no Wolbachia). Quantitative PCR of Wolbachia provides a quantifiable assessment of anti-Wolbachia therapy in future trials.

An impediment to faster development of macrofilaricidal, antiwolbachial drugs has been the long follow-up times in the trials because death of adult worms is slow, taking between 18 months and 2 years (a desired effect to minimize AEs from rapid killing of worms). The strong correlation between Wolbachia reduction at 6 months and the parasitologic outcome at 20 months, in particular skin MF (Figure 4A) as observed in this study, enables shortening new drug pilot trials (phase 1b/2a) considerably. Thus, analysis of Wolbachia depletion at 6 months can identify the dose needed to achieve later macrofilaricidal or, equivalently, permanently sterilizing effects in female adult worms.
