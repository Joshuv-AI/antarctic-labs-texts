Modelling US health equity impacts of emicizumab for severe haemophilia A: aggregate distributional cost-effectiveness analysis
Authors: Thomas Majda, Janet S Lee, Randall Curtis, Stacey L Kowal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesEmicizumab is the first bispecific antibody approved for prophylaxis in people with haemophilia A with or without factor VIII inhibitors. Aggregate distributional cost-effectiveness analysis assesses health equity impacts by evaluating how health effects and costs from funding an intervention are distributed among population subgroups. The objective was to evaluate how funding emicizumab for people with severe haemophilia A (PwSHA) impacts population health and health disparities in the USA.DesignPopulation-level model of PwSHA from the perspective of the US healthcare system, using published sources and considering a lifetime time horizon.InterventionEmicizumab versus other haemophilia A prophylaxis treatments.Primary outcome measuresQuality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) gained and change in Atkinson index of inequality in quality-adjusted life expectancy.ResultsWhen an estimated 6512 PwSHA in the USA were treated with emicizumab, the US healthcare system would save US$160 billion over those individuals’ lifetimes. If these cost savings fund additional healthcare interventions in the overall population, funding emicizumab would improve overall US population health (1 068 903 QALYs gained, using a threshold of US$150 000/QALY) and reduce existing overall US inequities (−0.01% on the Atkinson index).In all scenarios tested for sensitivity, increased emicizumab and prophylaxis utilisation led to further reductions in health disparities and greater increases in population health. Results were robust to deterministic variations in the allocation of cost savings due to emicizumab use.ConclusionFunding emicizumab treatments for PwSHA improves overall population health and reduces overall health inequities in the USA. Cost savings from the use of emicizumab free up important resources that can be leveraged to support other healthcare interventions, but decisions on how these funds are used have large consequences for equity.Keywords: Health Equity, Health Care Costs, Quality of Life

Introduction

Haemophilia A is a rare X-linked bleeding disorder that causes deficiency of coagulation factor (F) VIII.1 People with haemophilia A (PwHA) have spontaneous, recurrent bleeds that commonly occur in joints and lead to haemophilic arthropathy.2 In the USA, approximately 33 000 males and an undetermined number of females live with haemophilia, of whom 80–85% have haemophilia A.3 4 Among PwHA, estimates of the proportion with severe disease (FVIII level <1%) range from 40% to 43%.2 5 6 PwSHA have a substantial risk for complications, including bleeding events and chronic arthropathy, leading to a lower quality of life.7 8 PwSHA receive lifelong treatment and face substantial medical costs, which impose significant economic burdens on individuals and the US healthcare system.9 A retrospective review of healthcare utilisation records estimated average total costs of approximately US$300 000 annually for PwSHA receiving continuous prophylaxis.10

Historically, the standard of care for severe haemophilia A entailed prophylaxis with FVIII replacement therapies,4 which have evolved from plasma-derived concentrates to recombinant forms of FVIII, driven by aims to eliminate human and animal product use in production and to extend the circulating half-life.11 12 However, FVIII prophylaxis has challenges, including difficult venous access from repeated cannulation11 and development of neutralising antibodies (inhibitors) against FVIII concentrates in up to 32% of PwSHA.313,15 PwHA with inhibitors have greater healthcare resource utilisation,16 17 medical costs >3 times those of PwHA without inhibitors18,20 and larger quality of life impacts.21 Furthermore, disparities in inhibitor prevalence have been observed; non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic PwSHA are more likely to have inhibitors than non-Hispanic White PwSHA.22,26

Non-factor therapies for PwHA are now available, such as emicizumab and the gene therapy valoctocogene roxaparvovec,27 and more are in development. Emicizumab is a recombinant, humanised, bispecific monoclonal antibody that bridges activated FIX and FX to restore the function of missing activated FVIII that is needed for effective haemostasis.28 29 Emicizumab has demonstrated effective bleeding prevention in PwHA irrespective of inhibitor status, disease severity and age,30,33 and has become the standard of care since its approval.

Given the high cost associated with haemophilia A treatment, health technology assessment agencies have relied on economic modelling to evaluate the clinical and economic impacts of interventions. Public health officials often use cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) when considering coverage and reimbursements for interventions because they help identify interventions that provide the greatest cost benefit (ie, improvement in health per dollar spent). CEAs from the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER), an independent research institute that conducts evidence-based reviews of healthcare interventions in the USA,34 have determined that emicizumab treatment is cost-effective versus bypassing agents and FVIII products for PwHA with and without inhibitors, respectively.35 36

With mounting awareness of inequity in health, national governments are tasked with making policy and funding decisions that promote equitable distribution of healthcare, and informing these decisions with CEA has inherent limitations. Distributional CEA (DCEA) is an extension of CEA and an established method of evaluating health equity that is accepted by external stakeholders, such as ICER.37,41 DCEAs assess the impact of funding an intervention on the distributions of health effects and costs among subpopulations of interest, while considering trade-offs between improving health in the overall population and reducing overall health inequality.38 42 43 In situations where a full DCEA is not possible or practical, an aggregate DCEA (A-DCEA) is used to scale average CEA results to the population level to assess equity impacts based on funding costs and current patterns of disease burden.42 43

While the cost-effectiveness of emicizumab prophylaxis for severe haemophilia A per conventional CEA is well documented, its health equity impacts are unknown. Furthermore, researchers have recently identified disparities in mortality rates based on race and ethnicity among PwHA in the USA,44 and addressing health disparities is a priority for patient advocacy groups.45 Our objective was to use A-DCEA to evaluate how funding emicizumab for PwSHA impacts overall health and health equity in the USA.

Methods

A-DCEA overview

An A-DCEA method was preferred over DCEA due to the limited available data showing consistent distributional effects in outcomes in subgroups, which may be due to the small number of PwHA. In contrast, distributional differences in FVIII inhibitor prevalence are well documented in the literature.22,2646 47 Thus, it was important to incorporate differences in FVIII inhibitors in investigations of equity impacts, but there was a lack of consistent evidence to justify making changes to the underlying CEA.

Lifetime incremental health in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) and individual costs were extracted from ICER assessments for PwSHA with or without inhibitors treated with emicizumab and comparators (figure 1, table 1).35 36 48 In summary, ICER reviewed the cost-effectiveness of emicizumab three times using Markov models following individuals’ progression over their lifetime.35 36 48 In PwSHA with inhibitors, lifetime direct costs were significantly lower and QALYs were slightly increased with emicizumab versus bypassing agents in a model using incremental costs and QALYs of those starting treatment at ≥12 years of age.35 As a conservative approach, these findings were applied to PwSHA of all ages (table 1). In PwSHA without inhibitors, ICER reported that emicizumab treatment resulted in lower lifetime direct costs and equal QALYs compared with FVIII replacement (table 1).36 Additionally, in PwSHA without inhibitors, emicizumab was associated with fewer lifetime QALYs and higher direct costs than valoctocogene roxaparvovec, on average (table 1).48

For the current A-DCEA, all incremental costs and QALYs extracted from ICER findings were from the healthcare system perspective. Incremental costs and QALYs extracted from ICER assessments were weighted to account for proportions of PwSHA using each prophylaxis option according to inhibitor status (Genentech, data on file). The emicizumab comparator for PwSHA with inhibitors was bypassing agents, and the comparators for PwSHA without inhibitors were FVIII replacement therapies, including efanesoctocog alfa and gene therapy (valoctocogene roxaparvovec). While efanesoctocog alfa was not included in the previous ICER assessments, it has had considerable uptake in the USA since its approval in 2023. Therefore, efanesoctocog alfa was included in the A-DCEA using the assumption of equal lifetime cost-effectiveness as all other FVIII products. Incremental costs and QALYs were further weighted to account for inhibitor prevalence in each racial and ethnic subgroup. Because non-Hispanic Black PwHA are more likely to have inhibitors, the underlying cost-effectiveness of emicizumab versus blended comparators is skewed more toward the incremental costs and QALYs found by ICER for emicizumab versus bypassing agents in non-Hispanic Black PwHA with inhibitors. Costs from the ICER analyses were inflated to 2023 US$ using the Medical Care Consumer Price Index.49

ICER results were scaled to the US population level by considering the US age-adjusted prevalence of severe haemophilia A,50 prevalence of inhibitors (overall, 25%51 adjusted by race or ethnicity22 and prophylaxis utilisation (data from American Thrombosis & Hemostasis Network (ATHNdataset)). The US prevalence of severe haemophilia A was extrapolated from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates of federally funded haemophilia treatment centre (HTC) populations,50 which account for approximately 75% of people with haemophilia treated in the USA.17 The proportion of PwSHA assumed to receive any prophylaxis was 83%, based on a previous analysis of the ATHNdataset from federally funded HTCs.17 Among PwSHA receiving prophylaxis, 63.75% were assumed to receive emicizumab, based on a weighted average of emicizumab’s market share among those with or without inhibitors, which was 65% and 60%, respectively (Genentech, data on file).

A targeted literature review collected distributional inputs by race and ethnicity or socioeconomic factors using PubMed and Google Scholar (online supplemental table S1). Five studies documented higher inhibitor incidence in Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black PwSHA than in non-Hispanic White PwSHA.22,26 Among PwSHA treated at federally funded HTCs from 1998 to 2008 (n=5651), Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black individuals were 1.4 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.7) times and 1.6 (95% CI 1.3 to 1.9) times, respectively, more likely to have prevalent inhibitors relative to non-Hispanic White individuals (table 1).22 No evidence was found on disparities in access to prophylaxis treatment.

Pre-emicizumab and post-emicizumab health distributions were compared to assess the change in health equity from funding emicizumab treatments in the USA. Quality-adjusted life expectancy (QALE) was used to represent existing US health disparities at baseline. QALEs for 25 equity-relevant subgroups were based on previously established estimates in which US registry data were used to calculate QALE from birth for 99% of the US population stratified by race and ethnicity and county-level social vulnerability index quintiles (table 2).52

Only PwSHA treated with emicizumab had potential health gains from emicizumab, whereas the full US population had potential health losses from the cost of funding emicizumab over other interventions (opportunity costs). Opportunity costs were converted into population health impacts using an opportunity cost threshold of US$150 000/QALY (US$100 000/QALY and US$50 000/QALY thresholds were also tested; online supplemental tables S2 and S3).53 In the base case, 100% of opportunity costs were reallocated across the population equi-proportionally (ie, proportionately allocated to each equity-relevant subgroup based on relative population size) to interventions that would improve population health and health equity.

Health equity

Changes in health inequality were evaluated via changes in the Atkinson index inequality in QALE at birth, whereby the Atkinson index is estimated before and after accounting for the impact of funding emicizumab. Estimation of social welfare impacts leverages an inequality aversion parameter (IAP), which reflects the degree to which the general US population is willing to accept potential tradeoffs in overall health gains for a more equal distribution of health.42 The IAP is used to weight QALY gains across equity-relevant subgroups to produce equity-weighted QALYs that reflect elicited societal preferences, indicating the proportion of population health willing to be sacrificed to improve health equity.

The base-case analysis assumed an IAP of 12, based on a recently disseminated survey of the general US population,54 which implies that health gains in the population quintile with the lowest health (in QALE) are weighted 6–7 times more than health gains in the quintile with the highest health (in QALE). A range of IAPs (0–20) was tested in each scenario. To further contextualise the magnitude of health equity impacts, the Atkinson index was also expressed as the change in population-level burden of health inequality, estimated as the average individual QALE impact scaled to the US population.

Base-case and scenario results were visualised using the equally distributed equivalent health (EDEH) plot (figure 2). Scenario analyses

Scenarios with varying treatment rates and allocations of opportunity costs of funding emicizumab treatments over other interventions were analysed (table 3). In treatment scenarios, utilisation rates of overall prophylaxis, emicizumab and bypassing agents varied. In healthcare system cost scenarios, the cost savings from funding emicizumab and the equitable distribution of those savings varied.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Role of the funding source

Results

Base-case results of A-DCEA

In the base-case model considering 6512 PwSHA in the USA, the use of emicizumab as opposed to other treatment comparators was projected to save the healthcare system US$25 million per individual treated with emicizumab over their lifetime (table 4), driven primarily by the reduction in direct lifetime costs of emicizumab relative to bypassing agents and FVIII prophylaxis. Emicizumab use was associated with an average of 0.06 QALYs gained per individual and was projected to increase QALYs at the individual level in all equity-relevant subgroups (table 4), with a total population health benefit of 377 QALYs derived solely from treating PwSHA. Subgroups with lower baseline QALE, particularly non-Hispanic Black subgroups, experienced larger relative health gains (0.13–0.14% relative individual QALE gains for non-Hispanic Black PwSHA) than those subgroups with higher baseline health (0.08% for non-Hispanic White PwSHA) (online supplemental figure S1). Larger QALY gains and lifetime cost savings in non-Hispanic Black PwSHA were driven by the higher prevalence of inhibitors in this subgroup, since emicizumab has relatively greater cost-effectiveness among PwSHA with versus without inhibitors based on previous ICER reports.

Given that emicizumab is cost-saving over a lifetime horizon, when individual cost savings are aggregated across 6512 PwSHA receiving emicizumab (table 2), the US healthcare system saves >US$160 billion, which translates to a population-level net health benefit of 1 068 903 QALYs across the US population (table 4), using an opportunity cost threshold of US$150 000/QALY. When these health gains were weighted by preferences of the US population for reducing inequalities, emicizumab was projected to provide 1 080 520 equity-weighted QALYs to the US population and reduce overall disparities in the USA by 0.010% (table 3). Alternative opportunity cost thresholds yielded similar population-level health gains from cost savings of funding emicizumab increasing with lower thresholds (online supplemental tables S2 and S3).

Treatment scenarios

When emicizumab utilisation was increased by 10%, total population equity-weighted QALY change (1 250 013) increased, and overall health disparities were reduced by 0.011% (table 3). Similar to the base case, increased emicizumab utilisation led to a notable improvement in outcomes for non-Hispanic Black PwSHA. When emicizumab utilisation was increased in the most vulnerable subgroups (ie, those with the lowest QALE), minor increases in population health benefit (1 117 433 equity-weighted QALYs) and a consistent reduction of overall population-level health disparities were observed. As all American Indian and Alaska Native quintiles fell into the top 15% most vulnerable, these subgroups had notably improved outcomes relative to others. Among the treatment scenarios tested, increasing the prophylaxis treatment rate from 83% to 100% (ie, all PwSHA receive prophylaxis with the assumed market basket of treatments) resulted in the greatest population-level QALY gains (1 307 344 equity-weighted QALYs) and the largest reduction of overall health disparities (0.012%). When emicizumab utilisation was increased by 20% and bypassing agent utilisation was decreased by 20% in PwSHA with inhibitors, a population health benefit was observed (1 165 267 equity-weighted QALYs), including a notable increase for non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic subgroups.

Healthcare system cost savings scenarios

In the base case, 100% of resources freed up by funding emicizumab over other interventions were assumed to be used by the system to improve population health and reduce health inequities. Instead, if either 50% or 10% of these cost savings were reallocated, drastic decreases in population health gains (540 446 and 108 386 equity-weighted QALYs, respectively) and much smaller reductions in overall disparities (0.005% and 0.001%) were observed. Confining the resources freed up by funding emicizumab to only 15% of the population had large impacts on disparity measures. When savings from emicizumab use were allocated solely to fund interventions for the 15% most vulnerable (lowest QALE), overall health disparities in the USA decreased by 0.338%, and American Indian and Alaska Native subgroups had the most to gain from these resources relative to other subgroups.

Discussion

An A-DCEA projected equity impacts of funding emicizumab for severe haemophilia A on the entire US population by considering 25 equity-relevant subgroups based on race, ethnicity and county-level social vulnerability index quintiles,52 finding that funding emicizumab treatment improves equity and is cost-effective for the US population. In the base case, funding emicizumab treatment resulted in >1 million QALYs gained and >US$160 billion in financial savings to the US healthcare system over the lifetime of treated PwSHA. The US population of PwSHA is diverse, with broad distributions of age, race, ethnicity, geographic region and education level.17 Therefore, understanding how different options for prophylaxis can directly and indirectly affect the individual and population health of prominent subgroups in the USA is crucial. Each racial and ethnic subgroup gained QALYs and had reduced direct costs when emicizumab was funded. Non-Hispanic Black individuals had the greatest health benefits and highest cost savings, which is notable given known disparities in inhibitor prevalence and mortality among Black PwSHA.22,2644 Our DCEA explores potential ways in which resources can be distributed (eg, increasing overall prophylaxis rates) to mitigate these disparities. Several haemophilia A therapeutics, including recombinant proteins, small interference RNAs and antibodies targeting various anticoagulant proteins (non-factor therapies), are in development,11 and equity implications should be continually investigated for high-cost treatments for diseases with high costs of care.

Investigating the impacts of treatments on health equity is particularly crucial given the scrutiny that equity initiatives often face. However, most Americans are inequality averse and prioritise addressing health disparities,54 and US health decision makers report that the equity impacts are among the most important considerations for treatment value beyond clinical impact and cost.41 Our study represents an effort to reduce health disparities by demonstrating the quantitative benefits of funding a health intervention. US payers, providers and patient advocacy groups are placing growing emphasis on equity considerations for assessing product values and healthcare decision-making.41 The insights generated by methods like DCEA and A-DCEA are being increasingly used to support health equity considerations by these stakeholders.37 38

The cost savings reported here may be underestimated for several reasons. Populations of PwSHA were estimated using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Community Counts age-adjusted prevalence,50 which relies on populations seen at federally funded HTCs; however, 25% of PwSHA did not visit a federally funded HTC in the most recent year of the ATHNdataset.17 The model assumed that PwSHA treated at and outside of federally funded HTCs had the same characteristics, but PwSHA without access to a federally funded HTC, who were not captured in this analysis, may be more socially vulnerable, which would influence health equity impacts. For example, HTC population profiles reflect an under-representation of Black individuals based on national demographics.17 55

Importantly, these results assume that cost savings gained from funding emicizumab are efficiently reallocated to fund other health interventions in the overall population. Health disparities were reduced by an estimated 0.01% when emicizumab was funded. The US$160 billion in cost savings may substantially impact equity in the US healthcare system, but such reallocations are subject to political, institutional and budgetary constraints that were outside the current model’s scope. However, health disparities were reduced by an estimated 0.3% when modelled cost savings were reallocated to the most vulnerable population, highlighting the importance of sufficient and equitable care for haemophilia to the overall US population. DCEA is an emerging methodology with few benchmarks for comparison and limited evidence on the translation into meaningful, real-world changes in equity, but similar analyses in the contexts of medicines for COVID-19 and Alzheimer’s disease yielded 0.003% and 0.009% reductions in health disparities, respectively (IAP of 11 used for both).56 57 Our results are therefore on par with other interventions that were deemed successful, which is notable considering that haemophilia A is a rare disease and only a small population is directly impacted by the funded intervention.

Certain limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The ICER reports may not accurately reflect current treatment patterns or real-world effectiveness, which would affect the results. Since the ICER reports were published, data on the real-world effectiveness of emicizumab58,61 have been similar to data from randomised clinical trials that informed the ICER analysis.62,67 Additionally, market uptake of emicizumab has increased since the ICER reports were published, theoretically enhancing the benefits of funding emicizumab treatment that were estimated here. The model assumed that each subgroup had identical utilisation of different therapies, as data regarding utilisation of prophylactic agents across racial and ethnic groups are not available. However, prophylaxis use and emicizumab uptake likely vary by race and ethnicity, and this assumption may obscure access-related disparities. Additionally, cost-effectiveness models commonly base inputs on average effects among large populations, which inherently masks distributional differences in equity-relevant factors. The model also assumed that the effectiveness of newer agents was similar to that of emicizumab due to the absence of head-to-head or real-world data. When these data become available, they should be incorporated into future analyses. A-DCEA was used rather than DCEA due to limited subgroup-specific data. However, this method may reduce granularity in understanding existing disparities and may mask heterogeneity in outcomes within subgroups that alter overall equity impacts. The racial and ethnic subgroups considered were also granular, which may have obscured important differences in their subpopulations (eg, Korean American versus Japanese American), but were based on the available US census-defined groups.

Additional factors that may substantially influence outcomes that were not included in the model should be included in future analyses as DCEA methodology is further honed. Disparities in other outcomes reported in the literature, including mortality,44 physical functioning,68 69 quality of life70 71 and immune tolerance induction therapy,72 73 were not included in the model due to the lack of a cost-effectiveness framework to incorporate them. Similarly, broader societal impacts, including work productivity loss, caregiver burden and education disruptions, were not accounted for, which may be significant for PwSHA. Inclusion of these factors would likely increase the overall savings and health equity improvements among PwSHA treated with emicizumab. These results are based on US-specific data and have limited generalisability to other economies and healthcare systems. Finally, future studies would benefit from involving PwSHA and other members of the public in developing and executing equity-informed research, especially in the development of the underlying CEA.

Conclusions

This was the first A-DCEA conducted to analyse the impacts of funding emicizumab for PwSHA, which was projected to improve overall population health and reduce health inequities in the USA, with larger relative health gains in QALE observed for non-Hispanic Black subgroups of PwSHA in particular. Cost savings from funding emicizumab treatment over other interventions freed up substantial financial resources that can be leveraged to support other health interventions in the USA, but decisions on how these funds are used have large consequences for equity.
