Fluorescent Detection of Aqueous N‑Nitrosodimethylamine via Photochemical Transformation and Affinity Capture
Authors: Chi-Hsien Wang, Hsuan-Min Hung, Robert G. Croy, John M. Essigmann, Bradley L. Pentelute, Timothy M. Swager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N-Nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) is a water-soluble carcinogen typically quantified using mass spectrometry coupled with chromatographic separation, which requires extensive sample preparation and sophisticated instrumentation. Here, we report a fluorescence-based detection strategy for NDMA in aqueous media that replaces column-based separation with affinity capture. Photochemical transformation of NDMA, followed by reaction with a biotinylated polymeric reagent, generates a biotin-labeled fluorescent product that can be selectively enriched through streptavidin–biotin interaction, enabling effective separation of the analytical signal from background. The assay is performed entirely in water without sample pretreatment, preconcentration, or solvent exchange. Under optimized conditions, quantitative detection is achieved in a plate-based format using only 100 μL samples, affording a limit of detection of 235 ppb. The chemical transformation is completed within 22 min, followed by rapid affinity capture via streptavidin-coated agarose beads or plates. The method is rigorously validated through spectroscopic characterization, selective capture experiments, and quantitative analysis, and demonstrates consistent performance in spiked commercial water samples. These results establish a chemically defined platform for chromatography-independent optical NDMA detection and provide a basis for further development toward scalable sensing formats built on streptavidin-functionalized solid phases.

Introduction

N-Nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) is a low-molecular-weight water-soluble nitrosamine of substantial toxicological concern due to its potent carcinogenicity. Upon metabolic bioactivation, NDMA forms electrophilic methylating species capable of covalently modifying DNA. , Environmentally, NDMA can be present in soil, air, and water; however, in aqueous environments it exhibits notable persistence. , It is generated within treatment and distribution networks through chloramination-related reactions, yielding concentration profiles that evolve across both space and time. − Recognizing NDMA’s toxicity, regulatory limits are set at extraordinarily low concentrations (ng L–1 to subng L–1), including EPA’s drinking water health advisory level of 0.07 μg L–1. These low levels require analytical methodologies capable of resolving trace NDMA quantities in chemically complex aqueous matrices. From an analytical standpoint, NDMA presents distinct challenges: it is small, polar, and largely optically silent. Quantification has consequently been dominated by enrichment coupled with chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric analysis. − EPA Method 521, which integrates large-volume solid-phase extraction with tandem mass spectrometry, continues to define the regulatory sensitivity benchmark, with a reported detection limit of 0.28 ng L–1 for NDMA. However, this workflow remains inherently separation-centric, relying on organic solvents, centralized laboratory instrumentation, and specialized personnel.

Optical detection strategies have been explored to reduce the reliance on mass spectrometry. Among these, chemiluminescence − and fluorescence-based methods − offer high sensitivity and a broad dynamic range; however, in many implementations these methods primarily serve as postseparation readouts rather than enabling a truly chromatography-independent solution. Photolysis-assisted approaches and derivatization schemes frequently require multimodule reactors, milliliter-scale sample handling, or chromatographic infrastructure. Thus, analytical complexity often persists even when reliance on mass spectrometry analysis is not needed. More broadly, optical signal generation is rarely coupled to analyte confinement in a manner that eliminates the dependence on chromatographic separation.

Herein, we demonstrate a chromatography-independent fluorescence assay for aqueous NDMA that integrates a rapid photochemical transformation and an ultrahigh-affinity and specificity molecular capture. Building on our prior observation that NDMA undergoes photonitrosation of naphtholsulfonate (1) to generate an ortho-quinone oxime (2). We now have added a subsequent transformation to enable intermolecular cyclization into a fluorescent naphtholoxazole derivative (3) bearing a biotin handle (Figure ). This transformation encodes the analyte into a capture-ready fluorophore, which can be selectively enriched via the streptavidin–biotin interaction (K d ≈ 10–15 M), which is one of the strongest known noncovalent associations and widely leveraged in biological applications. − Instead of resolving analyte species through column-based separation, this design spatially concentrates the transformed analyte via affinity capture, conceptually analogous to affinity-based bioanalytical assays, but is applied here to small-molecule environmental detection. − The assay operates on 100 μL aqueous samples and completes the 2-step chemical conversions in less than one-half hour without pretreatment, solvent extraction, or chromatographic infrastructure, achieving a limit of detection of 235 ppb NDMA. Notably, the entire detection workflow proceeds in aqueous media. In addition, affinity capture onto streptavidin beads provides a demonstration of solid-phase signal localization that may facilitate future signal amplification and translation toward solid-supported sensing formats. By replacing separation-driven detection with transformation-encoded affinity confinement, this work establishes a chemical framework for chromatography-independent optical analysis of a target traditionally governed by column-based methodologies.

Results and Discussion

Photochemical Formation of the Oxime Intermediate

The first step of the assay relies on the photochemical nitrosation of sodium 1,4-naphtholsulfonate (1) by NDMA in water to generate the corresponding ortho-quinone oxime intermediate (2). This transformation was established in our prior colorimetric detection platform, where formation of 2 was confirmed by comparison with an independently synthesized standard, and the effects of irradiation time and photonitrosation yield were evaluated. The prior study also showed that this aqueous photonitrosation is not restricted to a single laboratory irradiation setup, as the reaction could also be driven by a hand-held UV-A flashlight and by sunlight. Selected interference associated with this photochemistry was also examined. Nitrite was identified as interfering, consistent with its ability to generate related reactive NO x species under irradiation; however, filtration through anion-exchange resin beads removed nitrite from spiked water without suppressing the NDMA response. These prior results establish the shared photonitrosation step and its compatibility with simple aqueous operation. We therefore focused the present work on using intermediate 2 as a chemically addressable precursor for fluorogenic activation and affinity capture.

Design of the Biotinylated Polymeric Reagent

Having established intermediate (2) as the product of NDMA photonitrosation, we endeavored to use this species as a chemically addressable precursor for signal generation. Accordingly, a biotinylated polymeric reagent was designed to enable fluorogenic activation in conjunction with tailored solubility control and affinity tagging (Scheme ). The synthetic material was characterized by NMR and FTIR spectroscopy (Figure S5–S7). The polymer scaffold, poly­(styrene-co-4-vinylpyridine), was selected due to its insolubility in aqueous media, allowing facile separation of the reagent from the assay solution after reaction. This phase separation prevents the unreacted biotinylated reagent from competing with the product for streptavidin binding sites, thereby preserving the specificity of the affinity capture step and improving detection sensitivity. The pyridine units embedded within the polymer provide a platform for partial quaternization into pyridinium reactive sites capable of reacting with the oxime intermediate.

Structure of the Biotinylated Polymeric Reagent

The degree of functionalization was controlled at 0.13 per repeat unit to balance the reactivity and solubility. At higher levels of quaternization, an increased hydrophilicity compromises phase separation. To address the limited reactivity of the heterogeneous system, a PEG4 linker was introduced to enhance interfacial accessibility, resulting in an improved cyclization efficiency. Finally, the incorporation of a biotin tag enables selective labeling of the product, which, upon formation, is released into the aqueous phase as a biotinylated naphtholoxazole fluorophore (3). Together, these elements integrate chemical transformation, phase separation, and affinity capture into a coherent detection strategy.

Fluorogenic Activation of the Biotinylated Product

The biotinylated naphtholoxazole (3) was independently prepared and characterized (Figure S11–S13), and its photophysical properties were examined in aqueous solution (Figure a). Upon excitation at 360 nm, the product 3 exhibits an emission maximum at 510 nm, whereas the starting reagent 1 shows emission centered at 410 nm under the same excitation condition (Figure b). The red-shifted emission of 3 is consistent with formation of an extended conjugated naphtholoxazole chromophore. Conversion of 1 to 3 therefore results in a distinct change in emission profile, although partial spectral overlap remains due to the strong blue emission of 1. Consistent with the assay design, we next evaluated whether the biotinylated product could be selectively captured and removed from the assay solutions through affinity interactions.

Demonstration of Affinity-Driven Signal Localization

To examine our capture method, a mixture of 1 and 3 was prepared in aqueous solution (see the Supporting Information for details), with concentrations selected to produce clearly resolved dual emission features (Figure c). Upon addition of streptavidin-coated agarose beads (SA beads) and incubation for 1 min, the emission band associated with 3 is no longer observed in the supernatant after filtration, while the signal from 1 remains. This result is consistent with the rapid and selective capture of the biotinylated fluorophore by SA beads and its removal from solution. Solid-state fluorescence measurements of SA beads further support this assignment (Figure e). Washed beads exhibit minimal background emission (Figure e-I), whereas beads exposed to the mixture display a strong fluorescence signal corresponding to immobilized 3 (Figure e-II). In contrast, beads treated with a solution containing only 1 show no comparable signal (Figure e-III). These results validate the assay design and demonstrate that affinity interactions between the biotinylated product and streptavidin enable selective capture and spatial separation from the background signal in an aqueous solution without chromatographic separation. The rapid capture (within 1 min) further supports its potential for implementation in field-deployable detection formats.

Optimization of Detection Conditions and Analytical Performance

Following validation of affinity-driven capture using SA beads, a quantitative detection format was established using streptavidin-coated 96-well plates in combination with a microplate reader for fluorescence detection. The assay involves two sequential chemical transformations. The photonitrosation step has been established previously and was conducted under UV irradiation for 20 min, which provides sufficient conversion at low NDMA concentrations. Optimization of the cyclization step was performed using synthetic oxime intermediate 2. Solutions containing 120 μM of 2 were reacted with the biotinylated polymeric reagent at room temperature and at 60 °C over time intervals ranging from 0.5 to 120 min. As shown in Figure a, elevated temperature accelerates green fluorescence development, with maximum emission reached within 2 min at 60 °C, whereas slower conversion is observed under ambient conditions. These optimized conditions were applied to NDMA detection in aqueous samples (8–12 ppm). After 20 min UV irradiation, the reaction mixture was treated with an excess of the biotinylated polymeric reagent and heated at 60 °C for 2 min. An aliquot (100 μL) of each post photolysis sample was transferred to streptavidin-coated wells, and fluorescence was measured after incubation. Optimization of incubation conditions were further evaluated (Figure S1) to ensure consistent signal and linear response. The fluorescence intensity increases linearly with NDMA concentration over this range (Figure b), affording a limit of detection of 235 ppb.

Detection of NDMA in Drinking Water Samples

The performance of the assay was evaluated using commercially available water samples directly after purchasing from local market, including bottled water with and without added minerals, spiked with NDMA at concentrations of 8, 10, and 12 ppm. As summarized in Table , the measured concentrations in bottled water without minerals show good agreement with the spiked values, with recoveries ranging from 97.5% to 105.5% and relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 3.3%. In mineral water samples, slightly increased variability was observed, with recoveries ranging from 91.5% to 101.2% and RSD values of up to 6.5%, likely reflecting the presence of dissolved ions and matrix components. These results indicate that the assay maintains reliable performance in the commercial water samples, while more complex field-relevant matrices with higher UV absorbance or natural organic matter contents remain important targets for future development.

Conclusion

We report a fluorescent detection method for aqueous NDMA that integrates chemical transformation with affinity-enhanced detection. NDMA is converted in water into a biotin-labeled fluorescent product that can be selectively isolated through streptavidin–biotin interaction, eliminating the need for chromatographic separation. The biotinylated polymeric reagent plays a central role by enabling both product formation and selective capture while maintaining phase behavior that preserves specificity during the capture process. The assay operates entirely in aqueous media without preconcentration or solvent exchange, and achieves quantitative detection using 100 μL samples with a detection limit of 235 ppb. The chemical transformation is completed within one-half hour, followed by rapid capture on streptavidin platforms and shows reliable performance in spiked commercial water samples. Although the present sensitivity remains above regulatory thresholds, this work establishes a framework in which chemical reactivity and molecular recognition replace separation-based workflows. The SA beads experiments further demonstrate that the analyte-derived fluorescent product can be localized on a solid-supported affinity phase, providing an experimental basis for future larger-volume sample processing and signal concentration. This work thus enables chromatography-independent optical detection of small, otherwise optically silent contaminants through transformation-enabled affinity capture.
