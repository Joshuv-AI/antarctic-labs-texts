Is professionalism associated with patient safety attitudes? Insights from a cross-sectional study among medical residents
Authors: Mehdi Raadabadi, Fatemeh Keshmiri, Roghayeh Gandomkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient safety is a fundamental component of healthcare quality, with professional commitment playing a key role in shaping safety-oriented attitudes and responsible clinical behavior. This study aimed to examine how professionalism is associated with different dimensions of patient safety attitudes among medical residents.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 151 medical residents at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences in Iran in 2021. Data were collected using the Medical Student Safety Attitudes and Professionalism Survey (MSSAPS). Descriptive statistics, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) to assess the overall multivariate effect and linear regression analyses were used to examine the predictive role of professionalism on three key dimensions of patient safety attitudes, supported by model diagnostics.ResultsOut of the 160 questionnaires distributed, 151 were completed (response rate: 94.4%). The mean age was 30.42 years (SD = 5.90), 57.6% were female, and 59.6% were married. Among patient safety attitude dimensions, perceived experiences with professionalism scored highest (M = 3.40, SD = 0.70), while error disclosure culture scored lowest (M = 2.98, SD = 0.72). The initial MANOVA indicated a significant overall effect of professionalism on the combined safety attitude dimensions (Pillai’s Trace = 0.091, p = 0.003). Linear regression showed a moderate, statistically significant association between professionalism and the culture of error disclosure (p = 0.002, η² = 0.060), a small-to-moderate effect on teamwork culture (p = 0.024, η² = 0.034), and no significant relationship with safety culture (p = 0.952).ConclusionProfessionalism among medical residents was associated with improved error disclosure attitudes and stronger teamwork. These findings highlight the importance of targeted training in professionalism, ethical communication, and structured error reporting, alongside organizational support, to enhance patient safety behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09156-0.Keywords: Professionalism, Patient safety, Medical residents, Teamwork culture, Safety culture, Error disclosure

Introduction

Healthcare systems are becoming increasingly complex and specialized, with widespread adoption of advanced technologies. This has highlighted the critical importance of patient safety and quality of care [1]. Patient safety refers to the prevention and mitigation of adverse outcomes or harm resulting from healthcare processes, aiming to protect patients, their families, and healthcare workers [2]. In high-income countries, nearly one in ten patient experiences harm during hospital care, with almost half of these incidents considered preventable [3]. The burden of unsafe care is especially severe in low- and middle-income countries: according to WHO estimates, as many as 134 million adverse events occur in hospitals each year in these settings, contributing to approximately 2.6 million deaths [4].

Despite substantial investments in quality and safety initiatives, achieving sustainable patient safety also depends on human, cultural, and professional factors within organizations [5, 6]. Growing evidence highlights that professionalism—alongside safety culture and organizational structures—plays a central role in either strengthening or undermining patient safety. Professionalism is a multidimensional construct encompassing adherence to ethical principles, honesty, accountability, mutual respect, commitment to continuous learning, and prioritizing patient interests in decision-making [7]. It involves a set of attitudes and behaviors in healthcare professionals and students that consistently place patient welfare above personal interests [8].

Research shows that lapses in professionalism may lead to reduced patient trust, dissatisfaction, impaired teamwork, concealment of errors, and ultimately an increased likelihood of medical errors [9–11]. For instance, unprofessional behaviors such as disregarding communication standards, resisting error reporting, or making decisions influenced by personal gain can jeopardize patient safety [12]. Conversely, adherence to professional principles fosters a culture of safety, enhances communication among healthcare team members, and promotes transparency in addressing mistakes [13].

Evidence from Iran indicates that medical errors and patient safety problems are common and variably reported across healthcare settings. Systematic reviews and national assessments have documented a high prevalence of clinical and medication errors in Iranian hospitals, accompanied by substantial heterogeneity in underlying care processes and reporting practices [14]. Repeated evaluations of hospital patient safety culture have revealed uneven performance across key domains, particularly regarding non-punitive responses to error, management support for safety, and open communication [15]. Persistent barriers such as fear of blame, inadequate feedback mechanisms, and weak reporting infrastructures continue to limit effective incident reporting and organizational learning from errors [16].

Beyond organizational level, growing evidence highlights important gaps in patient safety education within Iranian health professions training programs. Studies among medical and nursing students have consistently reported limited understanding of patient safety principles, ambiguous attitudes toward error disclosure, and insufficient training in teamwork and communication skills [17, 18]. Although the Ministry of Health and Medical Education has initiated efforts to integrate patient safety and quality improvement content into undergraduate and postgraduate curricula, implementation remains inconsistent across universities and residency programs [19, 20]. Moreover, faculty development in patient safety and professionalism is still evolving, with limited alignment between formal curricular objectives and the prevailing clinical learning environment [21].

Taken together, this evidence suggests that while individual professionalism is a necessary foundation for ethical and responsible clinical practice, patient safety attitudes among medical residents are strongly shaped by broader educational and organizational contexts. In settings where patient safety education is fragmented and institutional support systems are underdeveloped, the translation of professional values into safety-oriented behaviors—such as error disclosure and teamwork—may be constrained. Accordingly, examining the association between professionalism and distinct dimensions of patient safety attitudes among Iranian medical residents offers contextually relevant insights into how professional commitment operates within a developing and resource-constrained medical education system.

Medical residents play a crucial role in patient care and must integrate principles of professionalism and patient safety into their practice to ensure positive outcomes [22]. However, studies reveal that medical trainees [23] and residents [24] often possess limited knowledge regarding medical errors, their causes, prevention, and other patient safety issues. This gap extends to ethics and professional behavior, highlighting the need to embed patient safety and professionalism education throughout medical training [25].

Medical residents, as physicians in training responsible for direct patient care, their attitudes and clinical behaviors develop during this formative stage [26]. Therefore, enhancing professionalism among residents supports both their personal development and directly impacts care quality and patient safety. Studies have shown that targeted professionalism training and professional role models reduce medical errors, improve interprofessional collaboration, and increase accountability [27, 28]. Furthermore, studying these issues within Iran is particularly relevant, as the country faces resource constraints, high patient loads, and varying institutional safety cultures—factors that may shape both professionalism and patient safety attitudes in unique ways.

This study aims to examine the associations between residents’ attitudes toward patient safety and their level of professionalism. Findings will provide practical insights to guide educational reforms and policy decisions within medical training environments.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional, descriptive-analytical was conducted in 2021 among medical residents at the School of Medicine, Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran. Data were collected from early January to late June 2021.

Study population and sample

The study population included all medical residents (N = 272) enrolled in residency training programs at the University, rotating through various departments across three university-affiliated hospitals. The sample size (n = 160) was calculated using a standard single-population proportion formula for finite populations:

Parameters included: Z = 1.96 (corresponding to a two-tailed α = 0.05), p = 0.50, q = 0.50 (maximum variability, based on previous national studies and to ensure adequate precision), d = 0.05 (margin of error), Population size = 272 residents.

Out of the 160 targeted participants, 151 completed the questionnaires, resulting in a response rate of 94.4%. Nine questionnaires were excluded from the analyses due to incomplete responses. All completed responses were included in the final analyses.

Inclusion criteria were: enrollment as a medical resident at Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences and active rotation in one of the three affiliated hospitals during the data collection period. Exclusion criteria were: refusal to participate, incomplete questionnaires, and residents on leave during the study period.

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy. The research team conducted on-site visits to the three university-affiliated teaching hospitals during multiple working shifts (morning, evening, and night) to capture variability in clinical activity and resident availability. During these visits, all residents encountered from different specialties and residency years were invited to participate.

Data collection instrument and procedure

Medical Students’ Safety Attitudes and Professionalism Survey (MSSAPS), with several questions added to gather demographic information were used.

a) Demographic questionnaire

The first section gathered demographic data including age, gender, marital status, number of monthly shifts, average number of patients seen per shift, overall GPA, and living situation.

b) Medical students’ safety attitudes and professionalism survey (MSSAPS)

To assess patient safety attitudes and professionalism among medical residents, the MSSAPS tool was utilized [29]. This questionnaire consists of 31 items across four dimensions: safety culture, teamwork culture, error disclosure culture, and experiences with professionalism. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale, with response anchors ranging from 1 = “Completely Disagree” to 5 = “Completely Agree”, allowing participants to indicate the degree of agreement with each statement. Example items include: for safety culture, “I receive appropriate feedback on my performance” and “We adhere to procedures, guidelines, and standard protocols related to infection control”; for teamwork culture, “The quality of care patients receive is affected by teamwork” and “I collaborate well with other healthcare team members”; for error disclosure culture, “If an error occurs, the patient or their family is informed” and “I am encouraged by my colleagues to report errors to the patient or their family”; and for experiences with professionalism, “Members of the healthcare team always treat patients with respect and dignity” and “Healthcare team members, regardless of their organizational rank, treat each other with respect and professionalism.”

The instrument was translated and culturally adapted into Persian using a forward-backward translation process, reviewed by a bilingual committee of three experts, and refined based on feedback from an expert panel of 13 professionals with ≥ 5 years of experience in patient safety and medical education. Validity of the MSSAPS was confirmed through expert review, with a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.69 and a content validity index (CVI) of 0.84. Any items requiring modification were adjusted to ensure cultural appropriateness while preserving the original constructs. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation identified a four-factor structure corresponding to safety culture, teamwork culture, error disclosure culture, and experiences with professionalism, explaining 62% of the total variance. All factor loadings exceeded 0.40 and were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) further supported the four-factor structure. Model fit indices indicated acceptable fit: χ²/df = 4.96, CFI = 0.91, GFI = 0.92, NFI = 0.90, RMSEA = 0.08, and SRMR = 0.06. All standardized factor loadings were significant (p < 0.001), ranging from 0.52 to 0.83. The EFA and CFA analyses were conducted using the dataset of the present study.

Reliability and convergent validity of the MSSAPS subscales were assessed for all five dimensions. Internal consistency was strong across subscales, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.769 for experiences with professionalism to 0.892 for Safety Culture. Composite reliability (CR) coefficients were similarly robust, ranging from 0.866 experiences with professionalism to 0.918 (Error Disclosure Culture). Convergent validity, assessed using average variance extracted (AVE), was satisfactory for all subscales, ranging from 0.510 (Safety culture) to 0.683 (experiences with professionalism), exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committees at Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Participation was voluntary, and all respondents provided informed consent prior to completing the anonymous questionnaire, which was administered in paper format. Data collection was conducted on-site at the university and affiliated hospitals.

Data analysis

The data were entered into and analyzed using SPSS version 21. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and scores related to professionalism and the dimensions of patient safety attitudes.

Linear regression analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between experiences with professionalism and three correlated dimensions of patient safety attitudes — safety culture, teamwork culture, and error disclosure culture. Given the intercorrelation among these outcomes, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was employed to assess the overall effect of professionalism across the combined patient safety attitudes dimensions, providing a more powerful and accurate test than separate univariate analyses.

Multivariate tests, including Pillai’s Trace, Wilks’ Lambda, Hotelling’s Trace, and Roy’s Largest Root, were performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and observed power and effect sizes (Partial Eta Squared) were reported. Subsequently, Tests of Between-Subjects Effects were used to evaluate the significance of professionalism on each dimension separately.

Key MANOVA assumptions were assessed before interpretation of results. Diagnostic scatterplot matrices displaying observed values, predicted values, and standardized residuals were examined to assess linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity. Correlations among dependent variables were inspected to justify the multivariate approach, and no violations of assumptions were detected.

Results

Of the 160 questionnaires distributed, 151 were completed. The residents had a mean age of 30.42 years (± 5.90), with the majority (63.6%) aged between 25 and 30 years. Most participants were female (57.6%), married (59.6%), and lived in rented or leased accommodation (45%). The average GPA was 16.31 (± 1.36), with roughly half (50.3%) scoring between 16 and 18. Regarding clinical workload, residents completed an average of 11.28 (± 3.75) monthly work shifts and saw a mean of 13.07 (± 4.61) patients per day (Table 1).

The Teamwork Culture dimension had the second highest average score (3.32 ± 0.49). The top-rated item was, “I collaborate well with other healthcare team members” (3.59 ± 1.17), whereas the lowest was, “Differences of opinion in this hospital are resolved appropriately” (2.99 ± 1.20).

The Safety Culture dimension showed a mean score of 3.05 ± 0.59. The highest-rated item was adherence to “procedures, guidelines, and standard protocols related to surface infection” (3.54 ± 1.08), while the lowest was “I receive appropriate feedback on my performance” (2.66 ± 1.26).

Finally, the Error Disclosure Culture dimension had the lowest overall mean score (2.98 ± 0.72), with the highest-rated item being “Reporting errors to the relevant units is a key component of patient safety” (3.61 ± 1.06) and the lowest-rated item was “I have received necessary training on how to report medical errors to patients” (2.56 ± 1.19).

A multivariate analysis was conducted to examine the overall effect of the Experiences with Professionalism dimension on the combined dimensions of patient safety. Results demonstrated a statistically significant multivariate effect of Experiences with Professionalism (Pillai’s Trace = 0.091, F(3, 147) = 4.923, p = 0.003). This indicates that variations in Experiences with Professionalism are associated with meaningful differences across the set of dependent variables. Similar significance was observed across Wilks’ Lambda, Hotelling’s Trace, and Roy’s Largest Root tests, confirming the robustness of the finding. The effect size was moderate (Partial Eta Squared = 0.091), suggesting that Experiences with Professionalism accounts for approximately 9% of the variance in patient safety attitudes outcomes. Observed power was high (0.905), reflecting a strong likelihood of detecting a true effect (Table 3).

Multivariate analyses were conducted to examine the specific effects of Experiences with Professionalism on each dimension of patient safety attitudes. No significant effect was observed on safety culture (F (1,149) = 0.004, p = 0.952, Partial Eta Squared = 0.000). However, a statistically significant effect was found for teamwork culture (F (1,149) = 5.182, p = 0.024, Partial Eta Squared = 0.034), indicating a small to moderate effect. The most substantial effect was observed in error disclosure culture (F (1,149) = 9.593, p = 0.002, Partial Eta Squared = 0.060), suggesting a moderate association. Although these effect sizes may appear modest, they are meaningful given the complex, multi-factorial nature of social constructs such as organizational culture and professional behavior (Table 3).

Discussion

Understanding healthcare providers’ attitudes toward patient safety is essential for improving clinical outcomes and fostering a culture of quality and accountability. In the present study, we aimed to assess residents’ perceptions across four key dimensions related to patient safety attitudes and experiences with professionalism. The analysis examined associations between professionalism and the safety-related attitudes, recognizing professionalism as a core competency in medical training that may be related to safe clinical behavior, without implying causality.

The descriptive analyses demonstrated that residents generally operate within a training environment that supports professionalism and teamwork; however, concerns were raised regarding safety culture, particularly the culture of error disclosure. Residents reported feeling comfortable speaking up about unprofessional behaviors and described positive interactions and mutual respect within healthcare teams. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in residency training within our context [21, 30–32], which may reflect the impact of recent training initiatives aimed at strengthening professionalism and teamwork in medical universities in Iran [33–36]. Furthermore, a systematic review of hospital settings reported that “teamwork within units” consistently received the highest safety culture scores, suggesting that collaborative professional relationships may be relatively well established even when other safety domains remain underdeveloped [15].

In comparison, residents reported limited formal training in error disclosure, and indicated that medical errors were not routinely disclosed to patients and families. Many also felt insufficiently encouraged to report errors or raise patient safety concerns. While residents were expected to adhere to established clinical guidelines, they reported a lack of appropriate feedback on their performance, a gap that may negatively influence patient safety practices. Our findings suggest that residency programs may need to move beyond general professionalism initiatives and implement focused training in error disclosure behaviors. Our results align with growing literature describing persistent barriers to transparent communication about errors despite increasing institutional emphasis on openness. For example, Schwappach and Boluarte reported that physicians often feel unprepared to disclose errors due to insufficient training and concerns about legal or professional consequences [37]. Likewise, O’Connor et al. highlighted the ongoing need for comprehensive communication skills training focused specifically on error disclosure, an area that remains insufficiently integrated into many residency curricula [38].

Multivariate analyses demonstrated that residents’ experiences with professionalism were moderately associated with the combined dimensions of patient safety and error disclosure culture dimension and to a lesser extent with teamwork culture. In contrast, no significant relationship was observed between experiences with professionalism and the safety culture domain. These findings suggest that professionalism may primarily influence individual and interpersonal levels of safety—such as communication, collaboration, and disclosure practices— rather than the organizational level where safety culture is largely constructed.

The moderate association between professionalism and combined patient safety dimensions aligns with prior literature emphasizing professionalism as a foundational competency enhancing patient safety competencies and clinical performance among healthcare professionals [39]. Similarly, the observed small-to-moderate association with teamwork culture supports the interpretation that professionalism may translate more readily into respectful interactions, communication, and collaborative practice within clinical teams. The moderate relationship between professionalism and error disclosure culture further reinforces evidence that professional norms encourage ethical responsibility and openness regarding medical errors. Studies conducted in Iran have repeatedly identified non-punitive response to error as the weakest component of safety culture, indicating that while individuals may value professionalism, organizational environments often fail to fully support transparent reporting [40]. International evidence similarly shows that error reporting behavior depends heavily on leadership support and psychological safety rather than individual professionalism alone, suggesting that professional values must be reinforced by institutional systems to translate into consistent disclosure practices [41].

However, the absence of a significant relationship between professionalism and overall safety culture aligns with prior Iranian research showing that structural elements of safety culture—particularly non-punitive response to error and organizational support—remain weak despite acceptable professional behaviors among staff [42, 43]. This finding supports the view that professionalism alone is insufficient to establish a mature safety culture and must be complemented by institutional policies, psychological safety, and supportive reporting systems [44]. Furthermore, overall safety culture remains moderate to weak despite acceptable teamwork scores, suggesting structural barriers such as hierarchical systems, fear of blame, or limited organizational learning processes [15], reinforcing the need for system-level interventions alongside professionalism training [45].

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into patient safety attitudes and professionalism among medical residents, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which are inherently susceptible to social desirability bias—particularly regarding sensitive issues such as professional conduct and error reporting. To mitigate this, participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality, and the questionnaires were administered in a manner that minimized direct observation, encouraging honest responses. Second, the study was conducted using convenience sampling in a single province of Iran and within one academic institution and its affiliated hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions, healthcare systems, or cultural contexts. However, we recruited participants across multiple hospitals and residency years to enhance representativeness within the institution. Furthermore, the final sample included 151 participants, slightly fewer than the calculated minimum of 160, which may have had a minor impact on statistical power. However, the high response rate and the observed effect sizes suggest that the main findings remain robust. Finally, the factor analyses indicated a borderline but acceptable model fit; consequently, the results should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

This study identified key dimensions of patient safety attitudes and professionalism among medical residents, offering critical insights into the educational needs necessary to enhance safety culture and professional behavior. The results indicated significant associations between professionalism and specific aspects of patient safety attitudes, particularly teamwork culture and error disclosure practices. In contrast, no significant relationship was observed between experiences with professionalism and the safety culture domain. These findings suggest that professionalism training in residency programs may contribute meaningfully to interpersonal and behavioral aspects of patient safety, particularly teamwork and error disclosure practices, but may have limited impact on organizational safety culture unless supported by systemic change [46]. Residency programs in Iran may therefore benefit from integrating professionalism education with institutional strategies that promote leadership engagement, psychological safety, and non-punitive error management systems to achieve broader improvements in patient safety culture.
