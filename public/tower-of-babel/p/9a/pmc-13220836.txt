Clinical Remission of Crohn’s Disease and Closure of a Rectovaginal Fistula After Autologous Non-Myeloablative Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation
Authors: Roberto Luiz Kaiser Junior, Lilian Piron-Ruiz, Raissa Zamperline Tomiatti, Maria Laura Silveira De Castro, Luiz Gustavo Quadros, Milton Artur Ruiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrohn’s Disease is a chronic, heterogeneous, recurrent and remitting inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any part of the digestive system. To date there is no cure for this disease. Clinical treatment aims to reestablish a balance and avoid crises that are common in an immune-mediated disease that promotes intestinal dysregulation. Anti-inflammatory medications, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants and biologics are part of the therapeutic arsenal for the disease. Obstructions, strictures and intestinal fistulas are complications that require repeat surgery in most patients. Fistulas in the anal or perianal regions are common with rectovaginal fistulas being a serious and complex complication that is difficult to control. Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be employed for select patients with severe, refractory autoimmune diseases that have failed to respond adequately to conventional treatments.Case PresentationA 31-year-old Brazilian patient underwent several unsuccessful perianal surgeries for a complex rectovaginal fistula during her twelve years with active Crohn’s Disease. Stool elimination was mostly through the vagina as she had rectal stenosis. Consequently, rectal amputation and permanent colostomy implantation were recommended but refused by the patient. Instead, she underwent a non-myeloablative autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Four years later, she is without active disease, with no manifestation of a rectovaginal fistula. Previous descriptions of rectovaginal fistula closure with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation were not found in a review of the literature.ConclusionFor patients who refuse radical surgery, the presence of a fistula is not a contraindication for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The procedure provided progressive improvement not only with apparent cure of the Crohn’s disease but with no manifestation of the rectovaginal fistula four years after the transplantation.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic, heterogeneous, recurrent and remitting inflammatory bowel disease of unknown immune-mediated etiology linked to intestinal dysregulation. The clinical picture can manifest at any age and consists of fever crises, abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss and systemic manifestations, all of which affect the patients’ quality of life. Complications, including the development of fistulas, strictures and intestinal occlusions that usually require repeated surgeries, are frequent during its evolution.1

As transmural inflammation involves the entire thickness of the bowel wall, it facilitates the extension of ulcers and infectious processes into adjacent structures. This process can lead to the formation of a fistula, an abnormal epithelialized connection between two lumens or an internal lumen and the skin.2

The development of a rectovaginal fistula represents a severe, often debilitating, perianal manifestation. These fistulae pose a significant therapeutic challenge due to the high recurrence rates and substantial negative impact on a patient’s quality of life, often presenting with symptoms such as passage of flatus or stool per vagina, purulent discharge, dyspareunia, and recurrent genitourinary infections.3

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the modality of choice for characterizing anorectal vaginal fistulas as it provides excellent soft-tissue contrast, allowing for the accurate mapping of the course of fistulas and identification of associated abscesses. Additionally, endoscopic ultrasound can provide high-resolution imaging of the sphincter muscles and the internal opening of the fistula.4

Anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents, such as infliximab, are foundational therapy for fistulizing CD. While effective for some perianal fistulas, the closure rate for rectovaginal fistulas can be lower, often requires long-term maintenance and long-term prognosis frequently involves constant recurrences.3

Rectovaginal fistulas in CD represent around 9% of all fistulas; their management and treatment constitute a major challenge.3 New surgical techniques in selected patients are more successful thus providing better results with the best surgical results being observed in patients who receive associated drug therapy.5–7 In this scenario, due to the severity of certain cases, some patients have an indication for radical surgery involving rectal amputation and fecal diversion with the implantation of a definitive colostomy being necessary.8

However, the indication of this surgery is not acceptable to all patients and, as the disease is active, they seek other treatment options, including alternatives based on cell therapy such as autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (aHSCT).9,10

aHSCT is an intensive therapeutic modality employed for select patients with severe, refractory autoimmune diseases that have failed to respond adequately to conventional immunosuppressive and biologic agents. The fundamental rationale for using aHSCT is to “reboot” the immune system to induce a state of self-tolerance. As far as we know there are no reports in the literature about the effectiveness of aHSCT in treating rectovaginal fistulae.

The objective of this report is to present the case of a patient with severe CD refractory to conventional treatment who presented with a rectovaginal fistula. As surgical indication of rectal amputation and fecal diversion with definitive colostomy implantation was not acceptable to the patient, she elected to undergo non-myeloablative aHSCT.

Case Description

A 31-year-old female Brazilian patient of Caucasian descent had been diagnosed with CD 13 years previously. Her evolution is illustrated in Figure 1. She reported that the first main symptom was constipation. A year later, she began to experience abdominal pain, nausea, mouth ulcers and bloody diarrhea and reported the appearance of lesions and a fistula in the perianal region. At that time, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pelvic region demonstrated a small accumulation of fluid located in the subcutaneous planes of the right anterior perineal region, in addition to diffuse parietal thickening of the rectum and sigmoid and the presence of numerous reactive lymph nodes, characterizing inflammatory bowel disease. During colonoscopy, enanthem of the mucosa in the left colon and rectum was identified as well as edema with friable lesions covered by fibrin. Because of the perianal lesions, she underwent three surgical procedures to occlude the fistula. After the onset of symptoms, the patient took sulfasalazine, mesalazine and corticosteroids for two years without improvement and so she was enrolled in a program of azathioprine and infliximab treatment, which was maintained for three years. Treatment was suspended due to lack of response to the medication even after optimization.

Two years pre-transplant a colonoscopy was performed up to the rectosigmoid transition that identified extremely friable mucosa with bleeding upon insufflation and the presence of uniform erosions covered by thick fibrin. Progression of the colonoscope was impossible through the rectosigmoid transition due to a stricture.

An MRI one year later showed thinning with apparent discontinuity of the internal sphincter muscles in the anterior wall of the anal canal from 10 to 2 o’clock with a fistula located in the anal canal close to the anal verge, presenting an anterior trans-sphincteric path extending to the posterior wall of the vaginal introitus (Figure 2). Consequently, rectal amputation and permanent colostomy implantation were recommended but as the patient refused, she was referred for evaluation by the bone marrow transplant and cellular therapy team of the Associação Portuguesa de Beneficência in São José de Rio Preto, SP, Brazil. At this time, the patient was being treated with fortnightly doses of adalimumab, a biologic that she had been taking for more than three years.

Five months prior to the transplant, the patient had active CD as evidenced by clinical and laboratory exams, with rectal stenosis and extreme difficulty in bowel movements for several consecutive days, in addition to the elimination of gases and feces through the vagina.

Moreover, abdominal tomography was suggestive of active inflammatory bowel disease and a colonoscopy showed rectal sub-stenosis, signs of CD in the distal-middle rectum, and edematous friable ulcerated mucous with sub-stenosis about 7 cm from the anal border (Figure 3). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy showed mild distal erosive esophagitis associated with a sliding hiatal hernia and mild enanthematous gastritis of the antrum. Due to the severity of the clinical condition with active CD, refractoriness to previous conventional treatments using anti-inflammatories, immunosuppressants and three biologics, and her refusal to undergo rectal amputation, she fulfilled all the aHSCT criteria and was included in the institution’s auto Crohn’s study protocol. This project was authorized by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (Associação Portuguesa de Beneficência: IRB- CAAE 20,894,719.1.0000.5629) and registered with the US Clinical Trials (NCT #03000296). The inclusion criteria for aHSCT have been published elsewhere.11

Laboratory exams identified mild anemia: hemoglobin (11.2 g/dL), hematocrit (34%), total leukocytes (8.53 x 109/L) with normal distribution and platelets (490 x 109/L). Biochemistry tests revealed inflammation: C-reactive protein (20.1 mg/dL – normal range: 0.3–1.0 mg/dL), Albumin (3.8 g/dL), Ferritin (9.2 mg/L – normal range: 11–307 mg/L) and Serum iron (23 µmol/L), Beta-2 microglobulin (3.2 mg/L - normal range: 0–2.4 mg/L) and calprotectin 1200 µg/mg (normal range: 50–200 µg/mg). All antibody tests were negative or non-reagent.

It is noteworthy that the patient was positive for the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) B27 which is strongly associated with ankylosing spondylitis and other associated inflammatory diseases, including Crohn’s disease.

During mobilization, the patient received 60 mg/kg cyclophosphamide (CY) and 10 µg/kg/day granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) starting on the 5th day after CY administration until the end of harvesting peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cells (HSC).

A total of 17.56×106 CD34+/kg were harvested in one leukapheresis session on Day +10 after the start of mobilization. Peripheral blood HSCs were infused on the day following the end of conditioning, which consisted of 200 mg/kg CY and a total dose of 6.5 mg/kg rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin split over four days.

The patient received all prophylactic care prior to and during hospitalization for aHSCT and did not present any major complications other than the expected hematological toxicity related to the procedure. During mobilization, she presented with anemia, with a hematocrit value 25% below normal range; this required the transfusion of two packed red blood cell units. For five days she presented neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count <500 x 109/L) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count <50 x 109/L) for three days however no platelet transfusion was needed.

During conditioning, the patient presented with anemia, with a hematocrit value 25% below the normal range, which required the transfusion of three packed red blood cell units. She presented neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count <500 x 109/L) for six days and thrombocytopenia (platelet count <50 x 109/L) for four days with the transfusion of one platelet concentrate unit. The administered blood components had previously been irradiated and were infused using leukocyte reduction filters. There was low overall toxicity during the mobilization and conditioning period.

Neutrophil engraftment was defined as an absolute neutrophil count ≥0.5 x 109/L on two consecutive days; this occurred on Day +10 after the HSC infusion. The following day the patient was discharged from hospital.

Follow-up after aHSCT occurred on Days +30, +90, +180 and +365 and every six months thereafter according to the study protocol. Prophylaxis was maintained for six months using fluconazole, acyclovir, sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim.

Shortly after aHSCT, the patient reported an improvement in her general condition and quality of life with the beginning of bowel movements. Abdominal pain improved but elimination of feces through the vagina continued. Three months after aHSCT, the patient caught COVID-19 with flu-like symptoms. However, she did not require treatment or hospitalization and no changes in the manifestations of Crohn’s disease were noted. At this time, although she continued with asthenia, her abdominal pain and bowel movements improved with elimination of gases and feces through the vagina diminishing.

Six months post-HSCT, her physical condition had improved with less tiredness; the passage of gases through the rectovaginal fistula continued even though the passage of feces had stopped and she maintained normal bowel movements 4–6 times per day.

An abdominal MRI eight months after aHSCT showed diffuse parietal thickening of the rectosigmoid with mucosal enhancement occurring in association with engorgement of adjacent vessels and fibroadipose proliferation. However, the caliber of the intestine was normal with preserved wall thickness and without signs of edema. Additionally, areas of stenosis, fibrosis, or dilatation were not observed. These findings suggest chronicity with mild active inflammation.

At nine months after aHSCT, the patient reported significant clinical improvement with very mild symptoms of the disease and improvement with normal defecation (3–5 times daily). However, small quantities of gases continued to be eliminated through the fistula. An improvement in all symptoms was evidenced however a colonoscopy identified moderate CD classified as Crohn’s Disease Endoscopic Index of Severity (CDEIS) = 6, Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s disease (SES-CD) = 6 and Crohn’s disease activity index (CDAI) = 46.

In the 10-month evaluation, an MRI of the pelvic region was requested due to the absence of symptoms and the clinical improvement that had been observed. This exam showed persistence of the fistula located in the anal canal at the anal verge, but with a reduced diameter compared to a study prior to aHSCT. Its trans-sphincteric path extended to the posterior wall of the vaginal introitus. A reduction in parietal thickening in the rectum and sigmoid colon was identified, as well as reduced inflammation of the adjacent adipose planes, vascular engorgement and fatty hypertrophy, data suggestive of a diminished inflammatory process (Figure 4). At 12 months post-HSCT, the CD was in clinical remission with a CDAI = 20 and an inflammatory biomarker (C-reactive protein) normal. The quality of life of the patient had improved; she had no pain, bleeding or abnormalities during bowel movements 3–4 times daily and no stenosis. However, occasionally gases passed through the rectovaginal fistula.

A colonoscopy at 16 months identified a terminal ileum ulcer, terminal ileum substenosis, scar retractions in the left colon and pseudopolyp in the rectum. Thirty months post-aHSCT, the patient had no symptoms, no elimination of gases or feces through the vagina, with the CD in remission (CDAI = 20) and no altered inflammatory activity markers. The patient maintained normal social and work activities.

An MRI performed two years and six months after the transplant shows a small fistulous orifice in the anal canal at 12 o’clock with anterior course in the direction of the vaginal introitus without signs of communication with the vagina and with fibrotic scarring (Figure 5). A colonoscopy at the same time (Figure 6) shows endoscopic remission of Crohn’s disease (CDEIS = 0/SES-CD = 0). Discussion

Rectovaginal fistulas are an abnormal connection between the rectum and the vagina that can result in the passage of gases and/or stools through the vagina, causing significant discomfort and embarrassment with poor quality of life. Both medical and surgical approaches can be used in the treatment of rectovaginal fistulas depending on the size, location and underlying cause.8,12,13

Antibiotics may be prescribed to control infections associated with these fistulas and to prevent their spread. Anti-inflammatory medications should be associated in cases where inflammation is a contributing factor; the aim of treatment is to reduce inflammation and promote healing.

Dietary modifications, such as increasing fiber intake, can help maintain stools soft and promote regular bowel evacuation. Stool softeners may be recommended to ease bowel movements and help prevent straining during defecation, which can exacerbate the condition.14

Medical treatment alone may not be considered sufficient to close every rectovaginal fistula, especially if the fistula is large or if there is an underlying inflammatory or infectious process. However, clinical management can be part of the overall treatment plan and biologics can facilitate the closure of fistulas.15–19 Treatment with anti-tumor necrosis factor alpha, such as infliximab, is considered an effective biological therapy for fistulizing perianal CD. However, the effect of anti-tumor necrosis factor therapy appears to be more modest in CD-related rectovaginal fistulas, possibly due to the poor vascularization of the rectovaginal septum and decreased penetration of medications into diseased tissues. Studies have shown significantly lower complete response rates to infliximab in patients with rectovaginal fistulas compared to perianal fistulas. Despite this, some patients with CD-related rectovaginal fistulas have responded to infliximab treatment, with fistula closure reported at 10 and 14 weeks after three doses of the drug.13,17

While clinical treatment can address some aspects of the condition, surgical intervention is often necessary for the definitive closure of the fistula. Surgical options may include a fistulotomy,20 advanced flap repair,21 sphincteroplasty22 and in severe cases, fecal diversion and temporary or definitive colostomy.23

The main complications associated with rectovaginal fistulas in CD include high recurrence rates (34–59%), functional disorders and local septic complications. To control these complications, it is essential to achieve remission of the CD, minimizing perianal disease activity to ensure adequate healing. Furthermore, the surgical approach must be adapted to the anatomy of the fistula and the quality of surrounding tissues to optimize the repair.

Temporary fecal diversion is indicated in severe cases of complex anal fistulas: extensive cryptoglandular fistulas, associated with severe anorectal CD, recurrent rectovaginal fistulas, fistulas induced by radiation and those with necrotizing fasciitis. The goal is to divert fecal flow to control infections and prevent trauma by repairing tissue. After the fistula has healed, the diverting stoma is closed.14

One study on rectovaginal fistula repair observed that fistulas of traumatic origin, such as those caused by obstetric and iatrogenic injury, had a significantly better prognosis compared to those of inflammatory origin, such as anastomotic, infectious and CD-related fistulas.8

aHSCT is an option to treat severe CD refractory to conventional treatment. Its aim is to achieve immunological reprogramming and consequently long-term clinical remission with the suspension of medications and surgical procedures.11 The presence of fistulas is not an exclusion criterion for performing aHSCT.11

The presentation of this case describes the progressive improvement in the symptoms of CD. The severity of this case is confirmed by the complex, difficult-to-control fistula that was an indication for radical surgery.

Previous descriptions of rectovaginal fistula closure with aHSCT were not found in a review of the literature.

Conclusions

Fistulas are common in patients with CD with the treatment of rectovaginal fistulas being extremely complex. Different strategies for the clinical and surgical management of rectovaginal fistulas exist however, treatment is frequently accompanied by relapses two years later, independent of the type of procedure. aHSCT is an alternative procedure for some patients, especially those with severe disease that is refractory to conventional treatments. As in this case, in which the patient refused to undergo radical surgery, the presence of a fistula is not a factor that prevents a transplantation from being carried out. This report describes the progressive evolution of the rectovaginal fistula with complete resolution two years and six months after aHSCT with images showing the difference before and after the procedure. Currently, four years after the Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant, the patient remains asymptomatic, in clinical and endoscopic remission of the CD. Thus, the current clinical status and images suggest not only closure of the rectovaginal fistula but also an apparent cure of the CD.
