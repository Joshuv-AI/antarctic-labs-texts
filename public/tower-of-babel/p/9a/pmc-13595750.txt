Differential responses of citrus root systems to biostimulants: the role of γ-aminobutyric acid in root promotion, phytohormones, and rhizosphere microbiology
Authors: Yuheng Wang, Huanyu Zhao, Yucui Sun, Xiaojun Shi, Huaye Xiong, Yueqiang Zhang, Fusuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn intensive citrus plantation systems, root growth is often limited by various abiotic factors, leading to poor nutrient uptake and consequently thus low fruit yield and quality. Therefore, selection of effective biostimulant type to promote well-developed root system is fundamental for citrus production. However, the effects of varied biostimulants on citrus crops remain poorly understood. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effects of fourteen biostimulants on citrus root growth following application.ResultsOur results revealed that the citrus root system exhibited differential responses to the various biostimulants. Among all treatments, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) proved to be the most effective in promoting root growth, significantly increasing root biomass, enhancing phytohormone levels, and stimulating root morphogenesis. Compared to the CK treatment, GABA application increased the total root length, surface area and volume by 19.3%, 36.3%, and 23.4%, respectively. GABA application also induced shifts in the rhizosphere microbial community structure. Structural equation modeling further confirmed that GABA application had a negative direct effect on microbial community composition, which indirectly promoted root phytohormone synthesis and ultimately enhanced citrus root morphological development.ConclusionThis study expands our understanding of how biostimulants promote crop root development and identifies GABA as a promising effective biostimulant for enhancing citrus root growth. Consequently, GABA application represents an effective strategy for promoting root development in intensive citrus plantation systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09253-8.

Introduction

In intensive cultivation systems, plant roots are the primary organs responsible for nutrient uptake from the soil [1]. Root system architecture is largely influenced by soil nutrient availability, cultivation management practices, and seasonal climatic variations [2, 3]. However, the excessive use of chemical fertilizers has become pervasive across modern agricultural production systems [4–6]. Such practices lead to soil acidification and degradation, ultimately constraining root development and function [7, 8]. China, as the world’s largest citrus producer, faces widespread overapplication of fertilizers in orchard management [9, 10]. Citrus is characterized by a shallow root system, with most roots distributed within the upper 45 cm of the soil profile [11], rendering it highly susceptible to fluctuations in soil physicochemical conditions. Prolonged excessive fertilization not only alters soil chemical and physical properties [12] but also disrupts the rhizosphere microbial community structure [13], further diminishing root nutrient absorption capacity and metabolic efficiency. Accumulating evidence suggests that improper fertilization practices, coupled with the intrinsic characteristics of citrus roots, have emerged as key constraints on root development and yield formation in citrus orchards [14]. Therefore, developing innovative regulatory strategies to optimize citrus root architecture and functionality is essential for mitigating the adverse impacts of excessive fertilization and for improving nutrient uptake efficiency, root health, and overall plant productivity.

In recent years, biostimulants have emerged as a class of substances capable of alleviating multiple environmental stresses, improving root morphology, and enhancing nutrient uptake in crops, and have thus been increasingly adopted in modern agricultural systems [15, 16]. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that biostimulant applications increased average crop yield by 17.9% under field conditions, highlighting their considerable potential in promoting root growth and metabolic activity [17]. Extensive research has shown that biostimulants stimulate root development and modify root system architecture through diverse physiological pathways. For instance, protein hydrolysates enhance lateral root formation in tomato by increasing salicylic acid accumulation [18], and oligosaccharide-based biostimulant promote shoot biomass production and root system development [19]; humic acid application improves root elongation and biomass accumulation in maize [20]; and seaweed extract enhances root growth in sugarcane [21]. Mechanistically, biostimulants improve root morphology and nutrient acquisition by regulating carbon and nitrogen metabolism [22], modulating phytohormone homeostasis [23], and restructuring the rhizosphere microbial community [24, 25]. Meanwhile, exogenous γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) application has been shown to enhance root system development in poplar [26]. However, most current studies have focused on vegetables and short-cycle horticultural crops [27], whereas the mechanisms underlying their effects on citrus and other perennial fruit trees remain largely unexplored. Therefore, identifying and characterizing effective biostimulant types that regulate citrus root growth and functionality holds great promise for improving root performance and nutrient use efficiency in citrus orchards [28].

In addition, research indicates that the stimulatory effects of biostimulant application on crop root growth and development are influenced by multiple factors. Moreover, the promoting effects of biostimulant on crop growth primarily occur through direct and indirect pathways [15]. Direct effects mainly involve the active components within biostimulants acting directly on crop growth and development; for example, seaweed extracts contain substantial amounts of amino acids and peptides that can be directly absorbed and utilized by plant roots upon application [29]. Meanwhile, when applied to the soil, biostimulants can alter plant hormone levels or modify microbial community characteristics, thereby indirectly regulating plant growth. For instance, exogenous application of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) significantly increases auxin content in citrus roots, thereby promoting plant growth [30]. However, the underlying mechanisms by which biostimulants regulate root architecture in citrus crops remain unclear, particularly regarding their interactions with phytohormone regulation and rhizosphere microbial communities. Therefore, elucidating the physiological functions and regulatory mechanisms of biostimulants in citrus root development is of great significance for promoting root formation and improving nutrient uptake efficiency.

Our previous study demonstrated that pre-grafting application of root-promoting compounds significantly improved root morphology, activity, and nutrient uptake in grafted citrus trees [28]. However, the physiological and ecological mechanisms by which specific biostimulant, particularly GABA, regulate citrus root development remain largely unknown. This study aims to systematically evaluate the effects of exogenous application of GABA and other representative biostimulant on citrus root development, and to elucidate their potential physiological regulatory mechanisms. Using controlled pot experiments, a comprehensive analysis was conducted integrating root morphological traits, physiological and biochemical parameters, soil nutrient dynamics, and rhizosphere microbial community composition to reveal the systemic regulatory role of GABA on citrus root structure and function. We hypothesize that: (i) the effects of different biostimulants on citrus root morphology vary, and (ii) GABA enhances root growth and function by modulating hormonal balance and restructuring the rhizosphere microbial community.

Materials and methods

Experiments design

To explore the responses of citrus root morphology to biostimulant application, two experiments were conducted under outdoor or greenhouse conditions from March to November 2024. Before the experiment, surface soil (0–20 cm) was collected from a citrus orchard at the Changshou District Experimental Station (29°58’58” N, 107°13’13” E) and transported to the National Monitoring Station of Soil Fertility and Fertilizer Efficiency on Purple Soils (29°48’27” N, 106°25’54” E). The soil was air-dried and passed through a 1 cm sieve prior to use. Subsamples were further sieved through 2 mm and 0.15 mm meshes for the determination of basic soil physicochemical properties. Meanwhile, to investigate whether bio-stimulants consistently promote root development in citrus crops, this experiment 1 used 1-year-old seedlings grafted with the rootstocks ‘Ziyang’ (Citrus x junos Siebold ex Tanaka) and ‘Ehime’ (Citrus reticulata Blanco), which the major citrus cultivars in orchards. In addition, two main types of citrus rootstock, Poncirus trifoliata (L.) Raf. and Citrus junos Sieb. ex Tanaka were used in the experiment 2. All plant materials in this study were purchased from the Germplasm Resources Center of the Citrus Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Experiment 1

One-year-old citrus seedlings (Ehime, Citrus x junos Siebold ex Tanaka) were planted in plastic pots (17.5 cm height and 15.5 cm diameter) containing 10 kg of air-dried soil. The initial soil properties were as follows: pH 6.12, organic matter 23.6 g kg⁻¹, total nitrogen 0.75 g kg⁻¹, available phosphorus 35.4 mg kg⁻¹, and available potassium 194 mg kg⁻¹. Basal fertilizers were applied at rates of 100 mg N kg⁻¹, 50 mg P₂O₅ kg⁻¹, and 100 mg K₂O kg⁻¹ of soil, thoroughly mixed before transplanting. The experiment included a non-biostimulant control (CK) and fourteen biostimulant treatments. The biostimulant tested were humic acid (HA), fulvic acid (FA), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), arginine (ARG), polyaspartic acid (PASP), polyglutamic acid (PGA), spermidine (SPD), seaweed extract (SA), alginate oligosaccharide (ADO), astaxanthin (AST), chitosan (CHO), chito-oligosaccharide (COS), chitin (CHI), and phosphite (PHO), including the main types of biostimulants used in agricultural planting systems [15, 16]. All compounds were purchased from certified agricultural suppliers. Biostimulant was applied once per month, for a total of five applications during the trial period. In this experiment, the application concentrations for each treatment were determined based on data collected by the research team and the results of preliminary experiments (Table S1). The compounds were dissolved in distilled water prior to application, whereas control plants (CK) received only distilled water. All other cultivation and management practices were consistent across treatments. Each treatment included six replicates, and irrigation was performed when the substrate surface became visibly dry. The experiment was conducted in an outdoor condition at a sited with a subtropical monsoon climate, characterized by an annual average temperature of 20.3 °C and an annual average precipitation of 1,170 mm. Meteorological data for the study period are shown in Fig. S1.

Experiment 2

In this experiment, two rootstock major varieties used for citrus grafting, Poncirus trifoliata (L.) Raf. and Citrus junos Sieb. ex Tanaka, were planted in plastic containers under greenhouse condition. The experimental treatments, application rates, and management practices were the same as those in experiment 1. Four replicates were established for each treatment, resulting in a total of 120 pots. At the end of the experiment, samples from experiment 1 were subjected to nutrient determination, physiological analysis, and microbial community analysis. Experiment 2 focused solely on measurement of plant phenotypic data. Finally, the effectiveness of the different biostimulant was evaluated based on the results obtained from both the citrus seedling experiment (Experiment 1) and the rootstock experiment (Experiment 2).

Sampling and determination of plant parameters

All citrus plants were sampled at the end of the experimental period. In experiment 1, the entire plants were carefully washed with distilled water and separated into leaves, stems, and roots. The biomass of each organ was determined using an electronic balance. Leaf chlorophyll content was measured using a chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502 PLUS, Konica Minolta Investment Ltd., Japan). Plant height, stem diameter, and fresh weight were recorded using an electronic scale and a soft measuring ruler. After measurement, plant samples were transported to the laboratory, oven-dried at 75 °C to a constant weight, and ground into fine powder. The powdered samples were digested with H₂SO₄–H₂O₂, and the nitrogen (N) content was determined by the Kjeldahl method, phosphorus (P) by the spectrophotometric method, and potassium (K) by flame photometry [31]. Fresh root samples were collected and immediately stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses of phytohormone concentrations and enzyme activities. In experiment 2, plant biomass and root biomass were determined using an electronic scale, and plant height was measured with a flexible ruler.

Soil sampling and determination

At the end of experiment 1, all soil samples were collected using a soil auger for nutrient analysis. Soil organic matter (SOM) was determined by the H₂SO₄–K₂Cr₂O₇ oxidation–FeSO₄ titration method in a heated oil bath. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (soil: water) suspension using a pH meter. Total nitrogen (N) was determined using the Kjeldahl method after H₂SO₄–H₂O₂ digestion (K9840 Kjeldahl Nitrogen Analyzer, Hanon Instruments, Jinan, China). Available phosphorus (P) was extracted with NaHCO₃ and quantified using the molybdenum–antimony colorimetric method, while available potassium (K) was determined by ammonium acetate extraction followed by flame photometry. All analytical procedures were conducted according to the methods described by Wang et al. [32]. After plant harvest, intact citrus plants were removed from the pots, and excess soil was gently shaken off from the roots. The soil that remained tightly adhered to the roots was collected as rhizosphere soil, while the remaining detached soil was designated as bulk soil. All soil samples were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from soil samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the extracted DNA was assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). DNA samples were stored at − 80 °C until further use. For amplification, 10 ng of DNA was used as the template in a 15 µL reaction mixture containing Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, MA, USA) and forward and reverse primers (2 µM each). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed under standard cycling conditions. The purified PCR amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing and library preparation were carried out by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) [33]. The target fragments and primer sequences used for bacterial and fungal community analysis are listed in Table S2.

Citrus root morphological analysis

At the end of experiment 1, the entire citrus root systems were carefully excavated and washed with water to remove adhering soil particles. The roots were then spread out evenly on a transparent plexiglass tray filled with water to prevent overlap among root segments [34]. Root images were captured using an Epson Expression 10000XL optical scanner (Seiko Epson Corp., Japan). Root morphological parameters, including total root length (TRL), root surface area (RSA), and root volume (RV) per plant, were quantified using WinRHIZO Pro Vision 2004b image analysis software (Regent Instruments Inc., Canada). In addition, root activity was determined using the triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) reduction method following Chen et al. [21].

Analysis of root phytohormones, enzyme activities, starch, and soluble sugars

For phytohormone analysis, an appropriate amount of frozen root sample was weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of isopropanol–water solution (80:20, v/v). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, homogenized at 40 Hz for 4 min, and sonicated in an ice–water bath for 5 min. This procedure was repeated three times, and the mixture was then allowed to stand at − 20 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and 800 µL of the supernatant was collected, evaporated to dryness under vacuum, and re-dissolved in 160 µL methanol–water solution (50:50, v/v). After centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to chromatographic analysis. Phytohormone quantification was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) on a Waters ACQUITY I-Class system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, the autosampler temperature was set to 10 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL [35].

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and malate dehydrogenase (MDH) were determined using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Keming Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The SOD activity was determined using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) method at 560 nm [36], the CAT activity was determined according to the method outlined by Li et al. [37], with optical density measurements taken at 240 nm. MDH activity was estimated using the procedure described by Heath and Packer [38], with absorbance values of the supernatant at wavelengths at 532 nm and 660 nm, respectively. For soluble sugar, 100 mg of dry powdered root tissue was extracted with 5 mL of 80% ethanol at 80 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask. The extraction was repeated three times, and soluble sugar content was determined spectrophotometrically at 620 nm [39]. For starch determination, the remaining pellet after ethanol extraction was extracted with 2 mL HClO₄, incubated in a water bath, and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected, and starch content was determined spectrophotometrically at 620 nm [40].

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analyses of soil microbial communities were performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using the “plspm” package in R software (version 3.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Co-occurrence network diagrams were constructed using Gephi software (version 0.10.1). Data processing and visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and Origin 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data were subjected to the Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for mean separation at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Effects of various biostimulants application on growth characteristics of citrus plants

For citrus seedlings (Ehime, Citrus reticulata × C. sinensis), biostimulant application enhanced growth, although the effects varied among treatments (Fig. S2). Compared with the CK treatment, all biostimulant treatments resulted in average increases of 13.5%, 12.5%, and 4.77% in citrus plant height, total biomass, and root biomass, respectively. However, the PASP, SPD and CHI treatments exhibited lower root biomass than the CK treatment (Table 1). Interestingly, biostimulant application promoted citrus plant growth while simultaneously decreasing the root-to-shoot ratio. Meanwhile, the FA, GABA, ARG, and AST treatments were more effective in promoting root biomass compared to other treatments. Among all biostimulant treatments, GABA exhibited the most pronounced promoting effect on citrus seedling growth and development. Compared with the CK treatment, GABA treatment resulted in increases of 16.0 cm in plant height, 21.2% in plant biomass, and 17.2% in root biomass.

In addition, the different citrus rootstocks exhibited various growth characteristics under biostimulant application (Fig. S3). For Poncirus trifoliata (L.) Raf. rootstock, the HA, FA and GABA treatments showed significantly greater root-promoting effects. Specifically, they increased plant height by 3.9 cm, 26.0 cm, and 1.1 cm, and root biomass by 23.3%, 30.0%, and 26.7%, respectively. For Citrus junos Sieb. ex Tanaka rootstock, GABA, PGA, and ADO treatments exhibited greater root biomass than the control, with values of 3.03 g, 3.15 g, and 3.10 g, respectively (Table 2). Therefore, based on the results of experiments involving citrus seedlings and various rootstock varieties, GABA application has the most consistent and reliable effect on promoting root growth in citrus crops.

Effects of biostimulants application on nutrient accumulation in citrus plants and soils

After determining the effects of different biostimulants on the growth of citrus seedlings, we analyzed the nutrient contents in the plants and soil. For citrus seedlings, N content was highest in leaves, followed by roots, and lowest in stems (Fig. 1a, b and c). Among all treatments, the application of humic acid (HA treatment) and amino acid-based biostimulant significantly increased N content in leaves, whereas no such trend was observed in AST or PHO treatments. The P content of citrus plants was highest in the leaves and lowest in the stems. However, there was no significant change in the P content of the plants due to the application of various biostimulants, however, phosphite application (PHO treatment) significantly increased P content in all plant parts, resulting in the highest levels observed (Fig. 1d, e and f). Compared with N and P content, the K content exhibited different distribution characteristics, with the lowest concentration in roots and higher levels in leaves than in stems (Fig. 1g, h and i). For soil nutrients, no significant differences in soil pH or SOM content were observed among biostimulant treatments. For total soil N, biostimulant application significantly increased soil N content, with average values of 0.83 g kg⁻¹ in the CK treatment and 0.92 g kg⁻¹ across all biostimulant treatments. Among all treatments, phosphite (PHO) resulted in the highest soil available P and available K contents (Table S3). Overall, biostimulant application had a greater impact on the nutrient status of citrus plants than on soil nutrients.

Effects of biostimulants application on citrus root morphology, phytohormones and physiology

The morphological characteristics of citrus crops under different biostimulant treatments were also analyzed (Figs. 2 and 3). Compared with the CK treatment, biostimulant application increased average total root length, surface area, and volume by 4.77%, 16.0%, and 8.49%, respectively. Among all treatments, GABA treatment exhibited the highest values for all root morphological parameters. Root phytohormones were quantified using UHPLC (Table 2). 1-Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) content did not differ significant among all treatments, with average contents of 37.5 and 38.2 ng g− 1 under CK and biostimulant application, respectively. 3-Indoleacetic acid (IAA) content increased in all biostimulant treatments except PGA and AST treatment, with the highest content of 2.65 ng g− 1 observed in the GABA treatment. Similarly, the highest strigolactone (SL) and gibberellic acid (GA₄) contents were also recorded in the GABA treatment. Interestingly, biostimulant application significantly reduced salicylic acid (SA) content by an average of 65.7% compared to the CK treatment. Regarding physiological indexes in citrus roots, biostimulant application increased catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, with average increases of 17.3% and 24.8%, respectively, compared with the CK. Root activity was also improved in the pot experiment, averaging 0.57 mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹ across biostimulant treatments versus 0.40 mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹ in the CK. No significant differences were observed in soluble sugar or starch content in the citrus root among treatments (Table S4). Overall, GABA treatment resulted in the most favorable root morphology and the highest phytohormone content.

The effect of γ-aminobutyric acid application on soil microbial communities

Based on the experimental results, GABA was identified as the most effective biostimulant for promoting citrus root growth (Fig. 2; Tables 1, 2 and 3). To investigate the effect of GABA application on soil microbial communities, 16s rRNA and ITS sequencing were used to analyze the bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. As shown in Fig. S4, the rarefaction curve gradually stabilizes, indicating that the sequencing results were reliable. Principal component analysis indicated significant differences in the bacteria and fungi community structures between bulk and rhizosphere soils (Fig. S4e, f). Following biostimulant application, bacterial community indices differed significantly between bulk and rhizosphere soils under CK and GABA treatments. Regarding bacterial α-diversity, the rhizosphere soil of CK exhibited higher diversity than the bulk soil of CK. However, the opposite trend was observed under GABA application (Fig. S4c). A similar trend was observed for fungal α-diversity (Fig. S4g). Furthermore, a difference was observed between soil microorganisms after GABA application in the Venn graph (Fig. 4a, d). At the phylum level, the bacterial community across all samples was dominated by Pseudomonadota (24.7–29.3%), Acidobacteriota (19.2–22.2%), Chloroflexota (11.4–13.4%), Bacillota (7.70–11.3%), Actinomycetota (7.50–11.0%), Gemmatimonadota (2.97–3.99%), Verrucomicrobiota (2.67–3.77%) and Verrucomicrobiota (2.10–3.77%) (Fig. 4b). Notably, under GABA treatment, the relative abundances of Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota were higher, whereas that of Myxococcota was lower compared to CK treatment. In the fungal community, phyla Ascomycota (40.5–48.5%), unclassified_k_Fungi (37.8–42.0%), Mortierellomycota (5.39–6.22%), Basidiomycota (2.18–7.43%), Fungi_phy_Incertae_sedis (1.38–3.83%) and Glomeromycota (0.06-4.00%) were predominant (Fig. 4e). Ascomycota showed higher relative abundance in rhizosphere soil samples, while Basidiomycota and Glomeromycota decreased under GABA application. Co-occurrence network analysis showed that in the bacterial community, the number of edges and nodes decreased in rhizosphere soil compared to bulk soil across all samples (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, the number of nodes, the number of edges and average degree were decreased in soil samples under GABA application conditions (Fig. 4c; Table S5), while negative links within the bacterial community increased. Similar trends were observed for the fungal community (Fig. 4f; Table S5). The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA > 3.5, p < 0.05) identified 40 and 23 significantly different taxa in bacterial and fungal communities, respectively (Fig. S5). Bacterial taxa were predominantly enriched in rhizosphere soil, whereas fungal taxa exhibited the opposite trend. Collectively, these findings suggest that GABA application reshaped the citrus root-associated microbial community.

Relative variance contribution rate of factors to root morphology under γ-aminobutyric acid application

In this study, the effects of GABA application on citrus root morphology were evaluated under different factors (Fig. 5). The structural equation model (SEM) revealed that the GABA application had significant direct positive effects on soil properties (pH, SOM, TN, AP and AK) in this experiment (p < 0.01). In contrast, negative direct effects on microbial communities (bacteria and fungi) were observed under GABA application. Soil properties exerted negative effects on both microbial community and phytohormones (IAA, JA, GA4, SL), whereas microbial community had direct positive effects on phytohormones. Furthermore, phytohormones exhibited significant direct positive effects on root morphology (RB, TRL, RV and RSR) in citrus (p < 0.01). Among all factors, the phytohormones had the largest effect on root morphology.

Discussion

Citrus exhibited differential responses to various types of biostimulants application

Consistent with our hypothesis, the results showed that biostimulant application altered citrus root systems, while roots responding differently to various types of biostimulants (Fig. S2 and S3). This difference was attributed to variations in biostimulant composition and sources [15, 41]. Our previous findings also indicated that both biostimulant type and the crop species are key factors influencing crop root growth. For example, cucumbers exhibited different growth responses to biostimulant from organic and inorganic sources [42], and different crop species show variations under identical biostimulant application conditions [43]. Therefore, citrus root morphology exhibited different changes under various biostimulant application conditions. Similarly, previous studies had found that biostimulant played essential roles in promoting root development in agricultural system, including maize [25], legumes [44], poplar [45] and basil [46]. Generally, the efficacy of biostimulant in agriculture stems from their direct effects on crops and indirect effects on the soil environment [15]. For example, seaweed extracts typically contain abundant bioactive compounds that promote plant growth and modify rhizosphere microbial communities, thereby enhancing maize growth [22, 25]. Therefore, seaweed extracts, humic/fulvic acid, and amino acids such as GABA demonstrated greater root-promoting effects in this study. In addition, biostimulant application not only affects the growth of citrus seedlings and their root systems but also alters plant nutrient content across treatments (Fig. 1). This difference was primarily influenced by biostimulant composition, as their raw materials contain nutrients that modify soil and plant mineral content [47, 48]. The functional characteristics of biostimulants are determined by the diversity of their components and active ingredients, which explains the varying responses of citrus crops observed in this study. Therefore, selecting the appropriate biostimulant type is essential for promoting root growth in agricultural production systems.

γ-aminobutyric acid application effectively promotes citrus root morphology formation

Among all biostimulant treatments, GABA application exerted the most stable promoting effect on root morphological development in citrus seedlings and rootstocks (Table 2; Fig. 3). Previous research has shown that GABA application promotes root growth in poplar [26], and increases auxin content in citrus roots [30]. Consistently, the present study found that GABA application significantly increased IAA content in citrus roots, as well as the availability of nutrients essential for root growth. The appropriate concentration of indole-3-butanoic acid can significantly promote the growth and development of plant roots [49]. GABA played a central role in coordinating carbon and nitrogen metabolism and functions as an important intermediate metabolite by entering the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby influencing the synthesis of organic acids and phytohormones [50–52], and also strengthen plant defense responses, including antioxidant capacity and stress tolerance [53]. Therefore, GABA application promoted root morphology in citrus crops. Furthermore, GABA is recognized as a key regulator of plant growth and signal transduction [54, 55], effectively modulating cell division, elongation, and proliferation [56–58], thereby promoting overall plant growth and development. In this study, GABA application significantly increased the activities of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in citrus roots (Table S4), which may contribute to enhanced structural integrity and improved stress tolerance of the root system. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that GABA application increases CAT activity while reducing H₂O₂ accumulation in plants [59], thereby effectively alleviating oxidative damage induced by environmental stressors [60]. In intensive citrus cultivation systems, excessive fertilizer inputs often lead to soil acidification and subsequent impairment of root growth [61, 62]. In this context, exogenous GABA application appears to alleviate such adverse effects, thereby promoting root morphological development and overall root growth in citrus. Moreover, GABA has been shown to play a critical role in plant carbon and nitrogen metabolism as well as energy synthesis [63]. Exogenous GABA treatment has been reported to enhance photosynthesis in pepper [64] and maize [65], thereby promoting plant growth through increased photosynthetic assimilation. Collectively, these findings suggest that GABA application enhances stress resistance and carbohydrate-energy metabolism in citrus roots, which in turn facilitates root growth and development.

Citrus root morphology by regulating phytohormones and microbial communities under γ-aminobutyric acid application

Consistent with hypothesis ii, variations in phytohormone profiles and microbial community composition across treatments were identified as the primary drivers of root morphological differences (Table 3; Fig. 4). Among all treatments, GABA application resulted in the highest levels of IAA, SL, and GA₄, phytohormones closely associated with plant growth and defense [51, 66]. The elevated IAA concentration under GABA treatment is consistent with its typical accumulation in rapidly growing tissues. Similarly, the larger root volume observed in GABA-treated plants aligns with the known role of strigolactone (SL) in promoting lateral root development [67]. Meanwhile, phytohormones are believed to alleviate the inhibitory effects of various abiotic stresses on crop growth [68]. Structural equation modeling further revealed a significant positive effect of GABA application on phytohormone levels and subsequent root formation in citrus (Fig. 5), corroborating previous findings in poplar where GABA increased endogenous IAA and GA contents and promoted root growth [26]. Root system plasticity is also modulated by microorganisms, and the application of biostimulants can substantially influence microbial communities [69, 70]. In this study, GABA treatment increased the abundance of the orders Pseudomonadales and Actinobacteria (Fig. 4). Notably, Pseudomonas species in the crop rhizosphere have been reported to effectively promote lateral root growth and enhance nitrogen utilization [71]. This order contains many well-characterized plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) [72]. The PGPR-mediated promotion of plant growth is often mechanistically linked to the production of phytohormones and the solubilization of nutrients [73]. Rhizosphere microorganisms both synthesize phytohormones (e.g., auxins, cytokinins) and utilize them as carbon/nitrogen sources, creating a dynamic feedback loop that shapes community assembly and hormone biosynthesis [74, 75]. Similar to our observations with GABA, the application of other biostimulants like ĸ-carrageenan has been shown to dramatically alter plant amino acid and alkaloid profiles [76], hinting at a common downstream effect on plant nitrogen metabolism and secondary metabolism. Functional profiling of the dominant microbial populations revealed a primary enrichment in pathways associated with carbon and nitrogen metabolism and organic matter decomposition. This enrichment may result from increased phytohormone levels in citrus roots, which selectively recruit microbial taxa capable of utilizing carbon sources in the rhizosphere. Similar findings have been reported in various crops; for example, Pseudomonas putida strain 1290 utilizes IAA as a carbon source [77, 78], and biostimulant application in maize has been shown to reshape the rhizosphere bacterial community [79]. Previous studies have demonstrated that applying biostimulants in combination with improved planting substrates can effectively promote crop growth. By extension, when combined with soil condition improvement in citrus orchards, GABA application may be expected to exert a more pronounced positive effect on root growth [80]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GABA application effectively modulates phytohormone levels in citrus roots and shifts the rhizosphere microbial community, thereby promoting the morphological development of the citrus root system.

Conclusion

These experiments provide empirical evidence that the application of different biostimulant has varying effects on citrus root growth promotion, and clearly demonstrates that GABA with the most effective root growth promotion in citrus. Meanwhile, the results indicated that GABA promotes citrus root growth by regulating phytohormones and microbial communities. This study expands our understanding of biostimulant in promoting crop root development and identifies the most effective biostimulant type for enhancing citrus root growth, and encouraged the application of biostimulants in sustainable agriculture. Consequently, it serves as an effective strategy for promoting crop root development in the intensive citrus plantation system.
