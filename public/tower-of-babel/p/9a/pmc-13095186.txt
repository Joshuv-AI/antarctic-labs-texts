Patterns of Colloid in Thyroid Fine-Needle Aspiration Smears
Authors: Chung-Yen Huang, Ming-Hsiang Weng, Tsu-Yao Cheng
Published: 2026

Colloid is a ubiquitous finding in thyroid fine-needle aspiration specimens. The presence of an “abundant colloid” – a hallmark of the benign colloid nodule – serves as a critical diagnostic exception to standard adequacy criteria, which otherwise necessitate the presence of at least six groups of ten well-preserved follicular cells [1]. Conventionally, colloid is bifurcated into two distinct morphologic categories: thin (watery) and thick (dense) [2]. To standardize evaluation, some cytopathologists employ a semi-quantitative threshold, designating thin colloid as “abundant” when it occupies ≥50% of the background of a single slide [3]. However, this assessment requires diagnostic caution as the translucent appearance of a thin colloid may be mimicked by cyst fluid or blood serum. Furthermore, thick colloid typically manifests as discrete, localized patches, precluding a reliable estimation of background coverage percentage. Given these quantitative challenges and the potential for diagnostic mimicry, we investigated the various morphologic patterns of colloid as a more reliable diagnostic indicator.

We describe herein the characteristics of a “patterned colloid,” which is defined as either thin or thick colloid manifesting with a structured morphology of repetitive lines, curves, or distinct geometric configurations. This feature is easily identifiable by both cytotechnologists and cytopathologists across multiple staining platforms, including Romanowsky and Papanicolaou stains. A variety of distinct architectural arrangements have been documented in the literature, including “parched earth,” “stained glass-like panes,” and the “spiderweb” pattern [2]. Other recognized morphologies include “Swiss cheese” formations, cloud-like, and “wrinkled plastic wrap” textures [2], as well as bubble-like [4] and flower-like [5] configurations. The diverse morphologic patterns observed in thyroid colloid likely reflect discrete physical and chemical transformations occurring during specimen maturation and slide preparation. The “thousand islands” or “thousand sails” configurations are the result of material aggregation following the inspissation of thick colloid. In contrast, linear morphologies – such as the “parched earth” or “spiderweb” appearances – may be attributed to a porcelain crackle-like crazing effect, a phenomenon driven by differential surface drying. Curvilinear arrangements are likely facilitated by surface tension, which acts to minimize surface area by rounding the edges of the colloid film. Furthermore, the wavy appearances observed in some smears may emerge from the kinetic interaction between the mechanical driving forces of smear preparation and the restoring force of surface tension. Finally, highly structured geometric variants, such as flower-like morphologies, may indicate localized crystal formation or the synergistic culmination of complex processes. While earlier literature has linked such morphologies to the effects of chemotherapy or radiotherapy [5], our findings demonstrate that these patterns can emerge in the absence of such clinical triggers, suggesting an intrinsic physical or biochemical origin.

Although specimens characterized by abundant colloid are generally categorized as benign, several distinct manifestations of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) can masquerade as benign, colloid-rich lesions. First, fine-needle aspiration smears of the macrofollicular variant of PTC often exhibit low cellularity against a background of abundant colloid. In such cases, pathognomonic nuclear features – including longitudinal grooves and intranuclear cytoplasmic inclusions – may be subtle or only focally distributed, contributing to a high incidence of preoperative misdiagnosis as benign [6]. Second, PTC may arise synchronously within a pre-existing benign nodular goiter, where the abundant colloid may obscure malignant foci. Finally, cystic variants of PTC frequently present with hypocellularity and thin, watery proteinaceous fluid that closely mimics the morphological appearance of thin colloid [2]. Clinicians and pathologists must maintain a high index of clinical suspicion in such colloid-rich specimens, particularly when concurrent imaging reveals atypical or sonographically suspicious features.

In conclusion, the characterization of these discrete geometric configurations provides a more objective framework than simple surface area estimation. Recognizing these patterns may reduce unnecessary “unsatisfactory” classifications, improve reporting consistency, and bolster diagnostic confidence in the identification of benign colloid nodules by serving as a qualitative marker for colloid abundance.
