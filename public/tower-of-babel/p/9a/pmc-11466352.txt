Prognostic value of morning blood pressure surge in chronic kidney disease
Authors: Zhilan Ma, Yan Wang, Xiaoyan Guo, Lan Ma, Yonghua Liu, Xiaoling Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis was a retrospective study. This study investigated the occurrence of a composite endpoints (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, end‐stage renal disease, and death) in 153 patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of in chronic kidney disease (CKD). Based on morning blood pressure surge (MBPS) defined as ≥35 mm Hg, patients were divided into two groups: with MBPS (n = 50) and without MBPS (n = 103). All patients were followed up for at least 1 year. Baseline demographic, laboratory and follow‐up data were collected. The clinical characteristics of the two groups were compared. The relationships between MBPS and endpoint events were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and Cox regression model. In total, 153 patients (mean age 41.8 years; 56.86% males) were included in this study. During the follow‐up period (mean 4.3 years), 34 endpoint events occurred. After adjustment for the covariates, the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, end‐stage renal disease and death remained significantly higher in patients with MBPS (hazard ratio [HR] and 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.124 [1.096–9.130]]) Among the other variables, systolic blood pressure, and night‐time and daytime pulse pressures remained significantly associated with outcome in patients of CKD (1.789 [1.205–2.654], 1.710 [1.200–2.437], and 1.318 [1.096–1.586], respectively]. In conclusions, MBPS was identified as an independent prognostic factor for composite endpoint events (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, end‐stage renal disease and death) patients with chronic kidney disease patients.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, end‐stage renal disease, morning blood pressure surge, prognosis

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an irreversible change in the structure and function of the kidney caused by a variety of pathogenic factors. This condition can last for months or years and is a severe public health problem worldwide, with a prevalence of 10.8% in China. Approximately 120 million adults aged ≥ 18 years have CKD, although only 12.5% are aware that they are affected by the condition. 1 Strategies to delay the progress of CKD and effectively protect kidney function are urgently required. Previous studies have shown that hypertension is not only a factor that promotes the progression of CKD to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), but is also a risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. 2

Studies of CKD patients with hypertension have revealed that blood pressure variability is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in CKD patients. 3 , 4 Morning blood pressure surge (MBPS) is a typical manifestation of increased blood pressure variability, with high mortality and disability rates and posing a significant threat to the health and quality life of affected individuals. 5 Previous studies have shown that increased MBPS is associated with stroke, left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, ischemic heart disease and arterial stiffness in patients with sustained hypertension. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 In addition, studies of untreated and treated hypertensive patients of various ages. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 There study shown that circadian BP changes were independently associated with an increased cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk. Recent research demonstrated that MBPS levels correlated with deterioration of diastolic function parameters in patients with masked hypertension. 16 However, the impact of MBPS on CKD patients remains to be fully clarified. In this study, we hypothesized that MBPS has a negative impact on CKD and increases the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. We also evaluated the prognostic value of MBPS in CKD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient

Records from 153 CKD patients with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, ABPM) treated at Ningxia Medical University General Hospital between January 2012 and December 2017 were analyzed retrospectively. The following inclusion criteria were applied: age ≥18 years; complete clinical data; A follow‐up period of at least 1 year. The exclusion criteria were as follows: severe liver disease and tumor disease; maintenance dialysis patients (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis). The Specific screening process for the enrollment of patients is shown in Figure 1.

Screening procedure for enrollment of patients.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis of CKD was based on the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcome (KDIGO) 17 guidelines. MBPS was calculated as the average BP value within 2 h after waking up minus the minimum value during night‐time sleep (average of 1 h including the lowest value). 18 Based on MBPS defined as ≥35 mm Hg, patients were divided into two groups: with MBPS (n = 50) and without MBPS (n = 103). The clinical characteristics and laboratory indicators of the two groups were compared.

ABPM

All of the patients underwent ABPM on a typical working day. BP was recorded every 30 min during the day (between 06:00 and 22:00) and every 1 h at night (between 22:00 and 06:00) using the oscillometric method with an automatic monitoring device (Welch Allyn ABPM 6100) and the data were analyzed using the associated software (I.E.M. GmbH). During each reading, participants were instructed to remain still and document their activities in a diary. A recording was considered valid and satisfactory if at least 80% of the total scheduled readings were obtained. The mean values of daytime and night‐time BP, and SBP and DBP were calculated for each patient on the basis of hourly averages of ABPM recordings. When wearing the detector, patients were asked to maintain normal life activities and avoid influential factors such as strenuous activities and emotional instability.

Echocardiography

Ventricular septal thickness (IVST), left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW) thickness, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (LVDd) values, and systolic function and diastolic function of the LV were assessed using two‐dimensional echocardiography. LV mass (LVM) was calculated using the ASE formula 19 as follows: 0.8 × {1.04 × [(IVSd + LVEDd + PWTd)3‐ LVEDd3]) + 0.6}. Left ventricular mass index (LVMI) was calculated as the LVM/the body surface area (BSA). BSA was calculation using Stevenson's formula 20 as follows: BSA = 0.0128 × weight (kg) + 0.0061 × height (cm) − 0.1529.

Estimated glomerular filtration rate

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated according to the CKD‐EPI 21 formula.

Follow‐up

Patients were followed up in our hospital outpatient clinic. The occurrence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events and end‐stage kidney disease were recorded during follow‐up visits or by telephone interview of the patient followed by a clinical visit. The primary endpoint of our study was all‐cause death. Secondary endpoints were as follows: (i) Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, including fatal and non‐fatal myocardial infarction, heart failure requiring hospitalization, and fatal and non‐fatal stroke. (ii) ESRD including peritoneal dialysis, hemodialysis or eGFR < 15 mL/min/173 m2. The follow‐up deadline was December, 2018; the study endpoint was reached.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 software. Measurement data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and differences between groups were compared using independent sample t‐tests. Non‐normally distributed measurement data were represented by quartiles and differences between groups were compared using non‐parametric tests. Counting date were presented as proportions (%) and χ2 tests were used to compare differences between groups. Cox regression analysis was used to estimate univariate and multivariate associations between factors and outcome. In the univariate analysis, factors with an index of p < .1 were included in the multivariate Cox regression model analysis with results expressed as a risk ratio with corresponding 95% confidence interval. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier product‐limit method and compared with the log‐rank test. p < .05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

RESULTS

In total, 153 patients (mean age 41.8 years; 56.86% males) were included in this study. Renal biopsy was performed in 62 cases, including 16 cases of IgA nephropathy. Based on MBPS defined as ≥35 mm Hg, patients were divided into two groups: with MBPS (n = 50) and without MBPS (n = 103). Demographic data, risk factors and blood pressure measurements of the patients in the two groups are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Body mass index and creatinine levels were higher in patients with MBPS. In addition, systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure as well as daytime systolic and diastolic BP were significantly higher in CKD patients with MBPS, while high‐density lipoprotein levels were significantly lower. Hypertension was also more common in CKD patients with MBPS.

During the follow‐up (mean 4.3 years, range 1−6.7 years), 34 patients (19 males) experienced endpoint events (Table 3). In the group with MBPS, 17 endpoint events (six cardiovascular events, three cerebrovascular events, and eight ESRD) occurred, with an incidence rate of 34%. In the group without MBPS, 17 endpoints events (two cardiovascular events, two strokes, two death, and 11 end‐stage renal disease) occurred, with an incidence rate of 16.5%.

DISCUSSION

Blood pressure shows a circadian rhythm, with systolic and diastolic pressures decreasing during sleep and rising steeply after awakening. The amplitude of change in blood pressure after waking up varies among individuals. 22 In this study, we investigated the prognostic value of MBPS in CKD. Our study is retrospective in nature, and we designed it with a follow‐up period of at least 1 year. This decision was based on the understanding that CKD is a progressive condition that typically requires longer observation to detect significant changes in renal function and related clinical outcomes. Patients were revisited every 2−4 weeks to closely monitor their condition and capture any acute changes that might occur. This frequent follow‐up schedule was designed to provide a more detailed view of the patients' clinical course and the effects of morning peak blood pressure. At the 1‐year mark, a comprehensive examination was performed to assess the overall progression of CKD and the effects of morning peak blood pressure. This included a battery of tests to assess kidney function and other relevant clinical parameters.

Recent studies have shown that MBPS is closely related to damage in target organs such as the heat, brain, and kidney. 23 , 24 , 25 The cut‐off values for MBPS described in the literature are inconsistent, possibly due to various definitions of MBPS applied in different studies. 26 , 27 In a large study by Li and coworkers, 37 mm Hg was applied as the cut‐off for MBPS, which is very close to the value used in our study. 28 Our study revealed that patients with CKD, MBPS is associated with increased risk of composite endpoint events (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, ESRD and death) independently of other ambulatory blood pressure parameters and risk markers. These findings suggest that further control of MBPS in patients of CKD might better manage the risk of adverse events in this population. However, we did not detect any relationship between left ventricular mass index and morning BP surge in the multivariate analysis, possibly due to the small sample of relatively young patients with CKD and short follow‐up time in our study. We also found that high‐density lipoprotein levels were higher in the MBPS high group than those the MBPS low group. High‐density lipoprotein has anti‐atherosclerosis effects and improves vascular endothelial function, thereby reducing the occurrence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. 29 Therefore, the possibility that high‐density lipoprotein has an effect on endpoint events cannot be excluded.

In our study, we observed a noteworthy difference in the prevalence of hypertension between MBPS and non‐MBPS groups. Although eGFR appeared to be somewhat lower in the MBPS group, this did not reach statistical significance. This apparent discrepancy may be ascribed to multiple factors, including the relatively youthful demographic of our patient sample, the brevity of the follow‐up period, and the heterogeneity in the chronicity of hypertension. We recognize that eGFR is subject to modulation by blood pressure levels and concur that the interplay is complex, being influenced by a spectrum of elements beyond mere blood pressure.

Moreover, we affirm that blood pressure variability is a pivotal factor implicated in the advancement of kidney disease, irrespective of the presence of hypertension. Morning surge hypertension, serving as an indicator of such variability, has been correlated with the deterioration of renal function. 30 Our research thus accentuates the significance of MBPS as a candidate risk factor for the progression of chronic kidney disease.

Since MBPS is also closely related to sympathetic nervous activity, dosing with antihypertensive drugs at bedtime is effective for reducing morning BP. Administration of angiotensin receptor blockers using this regimen was shown to improve microalbuminuria more effectively than morning dosing, especially in patients with morning hypertension. 31

We are aware that our study has certain limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study with limited cohort size, which could lead to potential bias. Second, the use of antihypertensive drugs is an important part of the management of patients with CKD, but different drugs may have different effects on morning peak blood pressure. We will include the use of antihypertensive medications in future studies to better assess the relationship between morning peak blood pressure and the risk of cardiovascular events and death. Third, cystatin C, as a sensitive indicator of renal function, may provide more information about the status of renal function. In future studies, if conditions permit, more renal function‐related indicators such as cystatin C should be included as much as possible to more comprehensively assess the status of patients with CKD.

CONCLUSIONS

We have demonstrated for the first time that MBPS is independently associated with increased risk of composite endpoint event (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, ESRD and death) risk in CKD patients. Therefore, MBPS is implicated as an important parameter that can be monitored to determine clinical management strategies and prevent organ damage in patients with CKD.
