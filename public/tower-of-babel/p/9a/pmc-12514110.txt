Exploring the experiences and perceptions of mental illness among family caregivers of persons with mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality, Upper East region, Ghana
Authors: Dennis Bomansang Daliri, Gifty Apiung Aninanya, Timothy Tienbia Laari, Nancy Abagye, Richard Dei-Asamoah, Eric Ansu Obeng, Afaya Agani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental illness remains a global public health concern, affecting individuals and their families across diverse cultural contexts. The perceptions of family caregivers about mental illness are essential as they form the foundation for stigma and discrimination. However, little is known about the experiences and perceptions of family caregivers on mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality in Ghana.ObjectiveTo explore the experiences and perceptions of mental illness among family caregivers of persons with mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality, Ghana.MethodsThis qualitative exploratory study was conducted in the Bolgatanga Municipality. Fifteen family caregivers of mentally ill patients were purposively sampled from two hospitals. Individual in-depth interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically analysed through an inductive approach using NVivo 12 pro.FindingsThe perceptions of family caregivers regarding mental illness were categorized under three emerging themes, including causes of mental illness, symptoms of mental illness, and stigmatization/discrimination. Participants identified the causes of mental illness to be biological, psychological and spiritual. They perceived the symptoms of mental illness as unusual behaviours, sleep disturbances, paranoid beliefs and hallucinations. They also recounted experiences of stigmatization and discrimination directed towards both persons with mental illness and themselves as caregivers.ConclusionThe study reveals that caregivers’ beliefs about the causes and symptoms of mental illness particularly spiritual attributions strongly influence their experiences and attitudes, often discouraging the use of formal mental health services. These beliefs also reinforce stigma and discrimination toward both individuals with mental illness and their caregivers. To address these challenges, targeted educational interventions are needed to improve mental health literacy and dispel misconceptions. Moreover, both quantitative and qualitative research is essential to deepen understanding of how cultural beliefs shape treatment choices, thereby guiding the development of culturally appropriate interventions that enhance access to formal care.

Introduction

Mental illness is a growing public health concern worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where the burden is compounded by limited access to mental health services (MHS), deeply rooted cultural beliefs, and widespread stigma. Despite global efforts such as the World Health Organization’s Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP), which aims to scale up care for mental, neurological, and substance use disorders by integrating services into primary healthcare [1], utilization of formal MHS remains low in many LMICs [2]. One key barrier is the prevailing perception of mental illness as a spiritual affliction rather than a medical condition. In many African settings, including Ghana, explanatory models often attribute mental disorders to witchcraft, curses, ancestral punishment, or possession by evil spirits, leading families to initially consult faith-based or traditional healers rather than formal MHS [3, 4]. This spiritual framing not only delays access to appropriate treatment but also influences caregivers’ attitudes toward the mentally ill, reinforcing misconceptions and discriminatory practices [5].

Perceptions about the manifestations of mental illness are similarly influenced by cultural norms, which affect the recognition and interpretation of symptoms. Behaviours such as inappropriate laughter, self-neglect, aggression, or talking to oneself are often pathologized in ways that reflect community expectations rather than clinical diagnostic criteria [6, 7]. In some cultures, emotional distress or hallucinations may be interpreted as signs of spiritual awakening or divine communication, while in others, the same experiences may be seen as symptoms warranting urgent psychiatric intervention [8]. These culturally variable interpretations affect help-seeking pathways and contribute to either early engagement or significant delays in seeking care. Notably, studies have shown that what is perceived as a severe symptom in one context may be normalized in another, depending on prevailing belief systems [9].

Stigma and discrimination remain significant obstacles to mental healthcare across cultures, with evidence suggesting that misconceptions about causation and symptomatology directly contribute to societal rejection and internalized stigma [10]. In Ghana, individuals with mental illness often face social exclusion, marginalization, and even abuse [11]. Stigma is not only directed at those diagnosed with mental illness but may also extend to their families, particularly primary caregivers, who are sometimes viewed as bearers of shame or moral failure. This secondary stigma creates barriers to social support, employment, and healthcare access [12]. Additionally, research indicates that stigma can exacerbate mental health conditions, deter individuals from adhering to treatment regimens, and reduce the likelihood of long-term recovery [13]. Understanding the nature and roots of stigma within local cultural frameworks is therefore critical to designing effective public mental health interventions.

While prior studies in Ghana have explored mental illness perceptions among various groups, including students, religious leaders, and urban caregivers, most of this research has focused on southern regions such as Accra and Cape Coast [14, 15] which are generally more urbanized and may be influenced by Western biomedical frameworks. In contrast, northern Ghana, including the Bolgatanga Municipality, is characterized by distinct sociocultural traditions, extended family structures, and a strong reliance on indigenous healing practices. This cultural divergence necessitates a localized investigation to uncover how mental illness is conceptualized, recognized, and responded to within the northern context. There is currently a paucity of studies examining how family caregivers in Bolgatanga perceive mental illness, including its causes, symptoms, and the stigma surrounding it. Addressing this gap, the present study sought to explore the experiences and perceptions of family caregivers of persons with mental illness patients in the Bolgatanga Municipality. Specifically, it aims to answer the question: What are the experiences and perceptions of family caregivers about mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality, Ghana?

Methods

Research design

The qualitative exploratory study design [16] was used to explore and describe the perception of family caregivers of mentally ill patients about mental illness. The report of the study was guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [17].

Research setting

The study was conducted in the Bolgatanga Municipality in the Upper East Region of Ghana. With a total population of 139,864 (73,257 females and 66,607 males), nearly 64% live in urban parts of the municipality [18]. The healthcare system of the municipality mainly consists of state-owned primary-level facilities like Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) centres, clinics, and health centres. However, the Presbyterian Psychiatric Hospital and the Upper East Regional Hospital are secondary-level facilities in the municipality. The Presbyterian Psychiatric Hospital in the Bolgatanga Municipality is the only psychiatric hospital in the northern/savannah ecological zone of Ghana. The hospital is manned by one psychiatrist, psychiatric nurses, and support staff. The hospital offers in-patient, outpatient, and community MHS to people with mental illnesses and their families. The hospital also serves as the major referral psychiatric hospital for all five regions of northern Ghana and parts of some neighbouring countries (Burkina Faso and Togo). Also, the Upper East Regional Hospital has a psychiatric department that offers mental health care to people with mental illnesses mostly by psychiatric nurses. Being the only healthcare facilities that offer MHS in the Bolgatanga Municipality, the Regional Hospital and the Psychiatric Hospital were purposively chosen for this study.

Target population and inclusion criteria

Family caregivers of people with mental illness constituted the target population for the study. Family caregivers who were 18 years or older, had at least six months of caregiving experience and consented to participate were included in the study. However, those who were service users themselves were excluded from the study. These were excluded to reduce confounders in the data analysis.

Sampling method and sample size

Participants were purposively sampled [19] based on their knowledge and experiences with caring for their relatives with mental illness. Fifteen caregivers participated in the study, which was defined by data saturation; the point at which no new information emerges [20].

Data collection instrument and method

A semi-structured interview guide was used to collect data for the study. The interview guide was adopted from a previous study by Devkota et al. [9] and adapted to suit the objectives of the current study. The interview guide was initially piloted among three family caregivers to detect and correct flaws. From February to March 2023, the first author (with experience in psychiatry and conducting interviews) conducted in-depth individual interviews in secluded offices in the hospitals during less busy working hours. The participants signed or thumb-printed the informed consent forms before the commencement of the interviews. A total of fifteen interviews were conducted: eleven in English language and four in Grune (local language) lasting 45 min to 1 h.

During the interviews, open-ended questions were asked, and probes were used to elicit further responses. Sufficient time was given to the participants for them to comprehensively express their perceptions about mental illness. Iterative questioning was used to obtain clarity of ambiguous responses. The participants gave permission for the interviews to be audio-taped and field notes taken.

Data analysis and management

The data were analysed through thematic analysis using NVivo 12 pro software. Verbatim transcription of the interviews was done immediately after each interview. Each transcript was read and reread several times by the first and second authors to familiarise themselves with the data. The transcribed data were imported onto the NVivo 12 pro software with line-by-line reading and coding done. Then phrases about the perception of mental illness relevant to the codes were identified and collated. Probable themes were searched for and the relationships between the codes and themes were identified to form the main themes. Some themes were modified, and others were broken down into sub-themes. The candidate themes were then defined, refined, and the data within them were analysed. The report was then written from the data to present the narratives clearly and logically within the themes. For easy retrieval of data, soft copies of anonymised transcripts were saved on a computer with access only to the researchers.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, the framework of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability as proposed by Lincoln and Guba [21] was applied. Confirmability was ensured through member checking by returning the transcripts to participants to ensure that the participants’ statements were accurately presented [22]. Transferability was ensured through thick description by providing sufficient details about the study setting, sample features, and processes. Credibility was ensured through space triangulation, where data were collected from different sites (Presbyterian Psychiatric Hospital and the Upper East Regional Hospital) [23]. Peer debriefing and strict adherence to verbatim transcriptions ensured dependability [24].

Theme 1: causes of mental illness

This theme represents the perception of the family caregivers about the causes of mental illness. While some of the family caregivers perceived the causes of mental illness to be biological and psychological, others perceived the causes of mental illness to be spiritual. Three subthemes emerged and they were as follows: biological causes, psychological causes and spiritual causes.

Subtheme 1: biological cause of mental illness

Most caregivers perceived those mental illnesses had biological causes. Almost all participants indicated that the smoking of marijuana (cannabis) was a common cause of mental illness.“Well, I always think that it is a result of drug abuse, marijuana, and other things like that” (45-year-old female caregiver).

Theme 2: symptoms of mental illness

The family caregivers also narrated their perceptions about the symptoms of mental illness. While some of them perceived unusual behaviour and poor sleep as symptoms of mental illness, others perceived paranoid beliefs and hallucinations as symptoms of mental illness. Four subthemes emerged under this theme including [1] unusual behaviour [2], experience of poor sleep [3], paranoid beliefs, and [4] hallucinatory experiences.

Subtheme 1: unusual behaviour

The family caregivers perceived unusual behaviour to be a symptom of mental illness. They believed that people with mental illness behave abnormally and do things that contrast with their usual selves.“When it comes, she becomes difficult to understand, like leaving her motorbike on the road and walking home, attempting to remove her clothes, and forgetfulness”. (40-year-old male caregiver).“…You will see her laughing alone, picking people’s things and entering people’s rooms” (59-year-old female caregiver).

Theme 3: stigmatization and discrimination

The family caregivers reported that mental illness was associated with discrimination and stigmatization. This stigmatization was either directed toward the ill relative or the family caregiver. The two subthemes under this theme were “stigma against family caregivers” and “stigma against mentally ill persons”.

Subtheme 1: stigma against family caregivers

The family caregivers narrated that they themselves were stigmatised in their communities and described the stigma as a common occurrence in their communities. The discrimination was done in several ways including making fun of the caregiver.“Hmmm, here the stigma is too much, there have been times when people intentionally ask me, how is your boy doing? Only to look aside and laugh at me” (45-year-old female caregiver).

“Hmmm, here the stigma is too much, there have been times when people intentionally ask me, how is your boy doing? Only to look aside and laugh at me” (45-year-old female caregiver).

Subtheme 2: stigma against mentally ill persons

The family caregivers in this study reported that their mentally ill relatives were also stigmatized in their communities. This was either by passing derogatory comments against mentally ill persons or neglecting/ignoring them. Such acts of stigma against mentally ill patients were evidenced in the following comments:“Hmmm, you see we are in a village where the illiteracy rate is high so any little thing, they say the fellow is mad so forget about him. They don’t consider them as human beings and do not take them seriously. They don’t consider them as important in society so generally, I will say there is stigmatization” (40-year-old male caregiver).“As for my community people, they will insult you and call you mad. If you argue with someone, they will say it’s because you are mad. Others even accused my daughter of witchcraft. Some have also said her condition is because she is refusing to accept witchcraft (a spiritual gift)” (58 year old female caregiver).

Discussion

This study explored the experiences and perceptions of family caregivers regarding mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality of Ghana. Three key thematic areas emerged from the data: perceived causes of mental illness, symptom recognition, and experiences of stigma and discrimination. Caregivers identified biological, psychological, and spiritual causes of mental illness. Reported symptoms included poor sleep, paranoid beliefs, hallucinatory experiences, and unusual behaviours. Participants also discussed stigma directed at both individuals living with mental health conditions and their families. These findings provide insight into the culturally embedded understandings of mental illness in the Bolgatanga Municipality.

A central finding of the study was the range of perceived causes of mental illness, which included biological, psychological, and spiritual factors. Participants frequently cited drug use, particularly cannabis, as a biological cause of mental health problems, which aligns with findings from other studies that have explored substance use as a risk factor for mental illness. For example, Choudhry et al. [6] similarly reported that caregivers in Nepal identified substance misuse as a major contributor to mental health conditions. Additionally, psychological causes such as financial hardship, stress, and family conflict were commonly reported in this study. This resonates with findings by Ofori-Atta et al. [25], who highlighted the significance of social and economic stressors in the mental health landscape of Ghana. The inclusion of spiritual causes, such as witchcraft and divine punishment, reflects the enduring role of traditional beliefs in shaping mental health narratives in sub-Saharan Africa. This is consistent with studies in South Africa and Ghana where both caregivers and spiritual leaders attributed mental illness to supernatural factors, thereby influencing help-seeking behaviours toward traditional or religious healers rather than formal MHS [14, 26, 27]. The multiplicity of these explanatory models underscore the need for culturally sensitive mental health education initiatives that validate local beliefs while integrating evidence-based understanding of mental illness.

The second major finding pertains to how symptoms of mental illness are recognized and interpreted by caregivers. Participants reported behavioural changes such as talking to oneself, laughing inappropriately, suspiciousness, and poor sleep as indicative of mental illness. These observations are consistent with the findings of Son and Park [28], who found that caregivers in Korea often identified similar signs when describing individuals experiencing mental health conditions. However, the range of symptoms identified in this study was relatively limited, suggesting the need for broader public mental health education to enhance early recognition and understanding of diverse clinical presentations. Importantly, attributing some symptoms to spiritual causes, such as curses or ancestral wrath, further demonstrates the cultural lens through which these behaviours are interpreted. As observed in Luhrmann et al.’s [29] cross-cultural studies, interpretations of hallucinations and other symptoms differ significantly across cultures, with some viewing them as spiritually meaningful while others perceive them as signs of pathology. These findings reinforce the necessity of integrating local explanatory models into mental health assessments through tools such as the Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI), which enables practitioners to understand symptom expression within the patient’s cultural framework [30, 31].

The third key finding from this study is the stigma and discrimination associated with mental illness, affecting both individuals experiencing these conditions and their caregivers. Caregivers reported being labelled or ostracized due to their association with family members living with mental health conditions. This aligns with findings from Devkota et al. [9] in Nepal, and Monnapula-Mazabane and Petersen [32] in South Africa, who documented similar patterns of stigma extending beyond the individual to affect entire families. In both contexts, stigma was shown to deter affected individuals and their caregivers from seeking formal mental health services, thereby contributing to delayed treatment and poor adherence. In this study, stigma was rooted in beliefs that mental illness results from spiritual failure, divine punishment, or moral wrongdoing. Such misconceptions contribute to internalized stigma, shame, and community rejection. The social consequences of stigma, including isolation and marginalization, demonstrate the profound impact of negative societal perceptions on mental health outcomes. These findings support calls for community education campaigns that aim to reduce stigma and promote inclusive attitudes toward people experiencing mental health challenges.

Strengths and limitations

The qualitative exploratory design allowed for a detailed exploration of the perceptions of family caregivers, providing rich insights into their experiences and beliefs regarding mental illness in a specific cultural context. To the best of our knowledge, no such study has been conducted in the Upper East Region; hence, findings can inform policy in designing and implementing mental health education programs in the region aimed at demystifying mental illnesses, thereby enhancing MHS utilization. These strengths notwithstanding, certain limitations need to be considered. The study’s participants were limited to family caregivers, potentially excluding the perspectives of individuals with mental illness themselves, healthcare providers, or other community members who might have additional perceptions and experiences of mental illness that can inform targeted interventions for enhancing mental health care. Also, translating interviews from the local language to English may have introduced some loss of nuance or cultural context, potentially affecting the accuracy of the data interpretation. This was, however, minimized with member checking. Also, using purposive sampling makes the findings less generalisable; however, the purpose of a qualitative study is to understand the lived experiences of participants, which cannot be explored through quantitative studies. Furthermore, the study was conducted in the Bolgatanga municipality; hence, finding may be limited to the municipality.

Implications for practice, policy and research

The findings of this study have significant implications for mental health care in the Bolgatanga Municipality. In clinical practice, the diverse beliefs about the causes and symptoms of mental illness highlight the need for individualized and culturally sensitive care. Mental health professionals should adopt a holistic approach that incorporates biological, psychological, social, and spiritual factors, using tools such as the Cultural Formulation Interview to better understand the cultural context of their patients. At the policy level, efforts should focus on reducing stigma and discrimination through targeted public education campaigns. These campaigns should engage trusted community figures, including traditional and faith-based healers, to promote accurate understanding and acceptance of mental illness. Strengthening partnerships between formal mental health services and indigenous healing systems can enhance access and cultural relevance of care. For future research, there is a need for both qualitative and quantitative studies to examine the relationship between cultural beliefs and treatment choices among recipients of care. Such evidence can inform the design of culturally tailored interventions that promote the uptake of formal mental health services. These studies are needed to further the research agenda, inform practice and policy that will enhance mental health care in the region.

Conclusion

This study found that family caregivers in Bolgatanga Municipality view mental illness through a combination of biological, psychological, and spiritual lenses. Symptoms were recognized in various ways, and stigma towards both individuals and their caregivers emerged as a significant barrier to using mental health services. The findings suggest that mental health care in this context must be culturally sensitive and holistic. Clinicians should be trained to incorporate patients’ cultural beliefs into care using tools like the Cultural Formulation Interview. Policy efforts should focus on reducing stigma through community education involving traditional and religious leaders. In addition, there is a pressing need for both quantitative and qualitative research to better understand how cultural beliefs shape treatment choices among individuals receiving mental health care. Quantitative studies can clarify patterns and associations, while qualitative research can offer in-depth insights into the meanings and experiences that underlie those choices. Future research should also investigate regional and cultural variations in mental health perceptions and evaluate the effectiveness of culturally adapted interventions—particularly those that integrate traditional healing practices with biomedical approaches. Generating such evidence is crucial for designing culturally responsive mental health strategies that enhance awareness, increase service utilization, and improve outcomes in diverse contexts such as northern Ghana.
