Hippocampal subfield radiomics improves identification of in-hospital aggressive behavior in schizophrenia: an interpretable machine-learning study
Authors: Jin-xin Wang, Ran-chao Wang, Feng-ling Xu, Yang Li, Yu Yang, Yue-feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate hippocampal subfield radiomics for identifying aggressive behavior in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia and to validate the incremental benefit and interpretability of a combined model integrating clinical variables, whole-brain structural MRI information, and hippocampal subfield radiomics.MethodsThis retrospective single-center cohort included 247 hospitalized patients with schizophrenia, randomly split (7:3) into training and test sets using outcome-stratified sampling. Aggression during hospitalization before discharge was assessed with the Modified Overt Aggression Scale (MOAS); clinically significant aggression was defined as a weighted total score ≥4. Whole-brain gray matter volume (GMV) features and hippocampal subfield radiomics features segmented using the FreeSurfer pipeline were each subjected to maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR) and 10-fold least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) for feature selection to derive sMRI-Radscore and Hip-Radscore, respectively. Three extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models were compared. Discrimination, incremental value, calibration, and net benefit were evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement/integrated discrimination improvement (NRI/IDI), Brier score, and decision curve analysis (DCA). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used to interpret the combined model.ResultsFive GMV and nine hippocampal subfield radiomics features were retained. sMRI-Radscore showed lower discrimination (AUC = 0.681) than Hip-Radscore (AUC = 0.729). The combined model achieved the highest AUC (0.875), outperforming both single models, with the lowest Brier score (0.119) and higher net benefit across most threshold probabilities. SHAP indicated Hip-Radscore as the top contributor. Hip-Radscore and its key constituent features were significantly associated with aggression severity.ConclusionHip-Radscore may help identify aggressive behavior risk in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia, and the combined model showed improved discriminative performance. These findings provide imaging-based evidence for early risk stratification and management during hospitalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08338-w.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Aggressive behavior, Radiomics, Hippocampal subfield segmentation, Interpretable machine learning

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder with a neurodevelopmental basis, characterized by a prolonged course and high disability, and it can involve multiple functional domains including cognitive processing, perceptual experience, emotional regulation, and behavioral control. Prior evidence indicates that, among psychiatric disorders, individuals with schizophrenia are at a comparatively elevated risk of aggressive behavior [1–3]. Clinically, aggressive behavior is typically defined as verbal or physical acts with hostile intent, encompassing both direct aggression toward others and outwardly directed behaviors such as property destruction. Psychiatric healthcare workers are among the primary victims of such behaviors, and a cross-sectional survey has shown that approximately 30% of psychiatric nurses have experienced severe assaults during their careers [4]. Aggressive behavior can cause immediate physical and psychological harm and may also increase the use of coercive measures, such as restraint and seclusion, thereby exacerbating healthcare resource utilization and family burden [5]. Therefore, achieving early identification and early risk warning of potential aggressive behavior during hospitalization has become an important objective of psychiatric clinical management. However, existing risk assessment methods are mainly based on clinical interviews, scale assessments, and medical history information. Although these approaches have important clinical value, their ability to characterize individualized risk may remain limited when used alone. In-hospital aggression is influenced by multiple factors, including demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, behavioral control ability, and potential neurobiological abnormalities. Therefore, while preserving the value of clinical information, further integration of key imaging indicators may help complement individual risk characterization from different dimensions. On this basis, a multidimensional prediction model integrating clinical features and key imaging indicators may provide a new strategy for individualized identification of aggression risk in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia.

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), a fundamental noninvasive tool for assessing brain structural alterations, has been widely used in schizophrenia research [6–9]. Prior studies suggest that conventional macroscopic sMRI measures (e.g., volumetric metrics), although capable of capturing schizophrenia-related structural abnormalities, remain limited in predictive utility for further discriminating individuals at high risk of aggression within patient populations [10, 11]. A single macroscopic morphometric measure may be insufficient to comprehensively reflect the multilevel pathological alterations implicated in schizophrenia, particularly microlevel processes such as disrupted synaptic connectivity, imbalances in excitatory/inhibitory regulation, and potentially related neuroinflammation. Radiomics extracts high-dimensional quantitative descriptors of intensity, texture, and shape, enabling a more sensitive characterization of tissue heterogeneity and underlying pathological information [12, 13]. In the field of psychiatric disorders, studies have reported that radiomics-based features from bilateral hippocampal subfields can, to some extent, differentiate patients with schizophrenia from healthy individuals [14]. In addition, studies have shown that fusion strategies integrating radiomics features derived from multiparametric MRI of the corpus callosum can improve the discriminative performance of auxiliary diagnostic models for schizophrenia [15].

The hippocampus is considered one of the key brain structures involved in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia and its clinical phenotypes. This structure exhibits marked functional heterogeneity and comprises multiple subfields with distinct functional specialization and differential vulnerability, including CA1, the subiculum, and the hippocampal tail [16]. As a key node of the limbic system, the hippocampus, together with the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal regulatory circuits, is involved in threat appraisal and behavioral inhibition, and alterations in its structural and microstructural heterogeneity may be related to aggression-associated phenotypes [17, 18]. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, studies focusing on hippocampal and hippocampal subfield radiomics features for predicting the risk of aggressive behavior in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia remain lacking.

Building on the above background, this study used FreeSurfer to automatically segment bilateral hippocampal subfields and extract corresponding radiomics features, and constructed a prediction model integrating whole-brain sMRI metrics and hippocampal subfield radiomics information. We systematically evaluated its ability to predict aggressive behavior in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia and its model interpretability, aiming to provide more objective and generalizable imaging-based evidence for clinical risk warning and stratified interventions. We hypothesized that hippocampal subfield radiomics features would provide greater discriminative value than conventional whole-brain GMV features, and that a combined model integrating clinical variables, whole-brain sMRI information, and hippocampal subfield radiomics features would further improve identification of clinically significant aggression during hospitalization.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study initially screened 280 patients with schizophrenia who were hospitalized at Zhenjiang Mental Health Center between January 2023 and October 2025 and underwent brain MRI at admission. Among them, 12 patients were excluded because of MRI image quality-control failure, and 21 were excluded because of missing key clinical or scale data, leaving 247 patients with schizophrenia for the final analysis. All participants were allocated into a training set (n = 173) and a test set (n = 74) at a 7:3 ratio using outcome-stratified random sampling to ensure balanced proportions of the aggressive group and the non-aggressive group across the two sets. The final analytic cohort of 247 patients with schizophrenia was aged 18–62 years. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10); (2) no restriction on sex, age ≥ 14 years; (3) an educational level of primary school or above, with the ability to understand and cooperate in completing scale assessments; and (4) a prior outpatient or inpatient diagnosis of schizophrenia and having received antipsychotic treatment for ≥ 6 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) comorbid intellectual disability or organic brain disease; (2) severe physical illness or severe adverse drug reactions; (3) severe psychiatric deterioration or marked agitation; (4) visual or auditory perceptual impairment; (5) pregnancy or lactation; and (6) contraindications to MRI or poor image quality. In addition, medical records were reviewed for documented alcohol use disorder, illicit drug use, or psychoactive substance abuse/dependence. No patient in the final cohort had a clearly documented alcohol or substance use disorder; therefore, this variable showed no variation across participants and was not included in the analytic framework.

Based on the Modified Overt Aggression Scale (MOAS) assessments during hospitalization, participants in the training set were classified into a schizophrenia with clinically significant aggression group (n = 52, MOAS weighted total score ≥ 4) and a schizophrenia without clinically significant aggression group (n = 121, MOAS weighted total score < 4); in the test set, there were 23 participants in the clinically significant aggression group and 51 in the non–clinically significant aggression group. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University, and the study purpose and significance were explained to the participants and their legal guardians; written informed consent was obtained from both the patients and their guardians. All data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Clinical data collection

Demographic variables collected included age, sex, marital status, years of education, residence, employment status, caregiver, monthly family income, duration of disease, first hospitalization, length of hospitalization, antipsychotic dose, family history of schizophrenia, and the management mode during hospitalization. Medication-related information, including current antipsychotic use at admission, antipsychotic type, and daily antipsychotic dose, was extracted from medical records. Daily antipsychotic doses were converted to chlorpromazine-equivalent doses according to published dose-equivalence methods. Clinical scales included the Insight and Treatment Attitudes Questionnaire (ITAQ) [19], Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) [20], Dickman Impulsivity Inventory (DII) [21]. Baseline information and ITAQ, BPRS, and DII ratings were obtained from routine assessments completed within 3 days after admission by two psychiatrists with more than 5 years of clinical psychiatric experience who had received unified scale-assessment training, using standardized instructions and with assistance from the patient and/or legal guardian when necessary. In cases of disagreement, a senior psychiatrist with more than 10 years of clinical psychiatric experience reviewed the information and made the final determination. Records with missing key variables or total scale scores were excluded. Among the 268 patients with MRI data that passed quality control, 21 were excluded because of missing key clinical or scale data, including 4 with missing ITAQ scores, 2 with missing BPRS scores, 5 with missing DII scores, 8 with missing monthly family income, and 2 with missing caregiver information, while MOAS/aggression-related records were verifiable for all 268 patients and no patient was excluded because of unverifiable MOAS or aggression-related information. Because complete length of hospitalization was available only after discharge and could be influenced by aggressive behavior during hospitalization, it was not included in the final prediction model. Previously recorded aggressive behavior referred to aggressive events clearly documented in the medical records before the current admission. However, because this retrospective study did not systematically collect this information using a uniform standard for all patients and some records could not be reliably verified, it was not included in the main analysis.

Because this study focused on actually recorded and verifiable aggressive behavior during hospitalization rather than general aggression tendency or trait aggression, the Chinese version of the Modified Overt Aggression Scale (MOAS) was selected as the outcome assessment tool [22, 23]. The MOAS scoring criteria were mainly based on previous studies applying the MOAS to violence risk assessment among psychiatric inpatients in China, and the definition of clinically significant aggression as a MOAS weighted total score ≥ 4 followed prior studies on aggression prediction in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia [5, 24]. The MOAS was administered by the above psychiatrists before discharge to evaluate overt aggressive behavior throughout the entire hospitalization period. The MOAS is an observer-rated, event-based scale comprising four aggression domains: verbal aggression, aggression against property, auto-aggression, and physical aggression against others. Each domain is rated from 0 to 4 according to severity and is then weighted to calculate the total score. Example events include shouting, abusive language, or verbal threats; throwing or damaging objects; self-injurious behavior; and pushing, hitting, or other physical attacks against others. Clinically significant aggression was defined as a MOAS weighted total score ≥ 4 and served as the grouping criterion. MOAS scoring was based on verifiable clinical materials during hospitalization, including physician progress notes, nursing records, aggression or impulsive-event records, restraint or seclusion records, and pre-discharge psychiatric review. For patients with complete medical records but no documented aggressive behavior, the MOAS score was recorded as 0; only when relevant information was missing, inconsistent, or unverifiable was aggression information considered unverifiable and the patient excluded.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

All participants underwent brain MRI on a 3.0T scanner (Siemens Magnetom Skyra, Germany), positioned supine and scanned using a dedicated head coil. Three-dimensional T1-weighted images were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.26 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm × 256 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, and no inter-slice gap. All T1 images first underwent N4 bias field correction to address intensity inhomogeneity and were then processed using FreeSurfer version 7.2.0, including skull stripping, cortical and subcortical segmentation, registration to the built-in FreeSurfer fsaverage standard template, cortical surface reconstruction, and hippocampal subfield segmentation [25–27]. All images underwent quality control by two independent researchers with more than 3 years of neuroimaging analysis experience to identify processing failures, severe artifacts, or segmentation errors.

Using the FreeSurfer automated cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation pipeline, anatomical segmentation was performed for each T1-weighted image. Gray matter volume (GMV) measures were extracted for 68 whole-brain cortical regions and a total of 16 bilateral subcortical gray matter structures, and were normalized by total intracranial volume (TIV) to correct for inter-individual differences. Specifically, TIV normalization was performed using proportional correction, calculated as normalized GMV = raw regional GMV / individual TIV. Hippocampal subfields were segmented using the built-in FreeSurfer module, yielding 12 subfields per hemisphere and 24 regions of interest (ROIs) in total, including CA1, CA3, CA4, GC-DG, HATA, fimbria, molecular layer, subiculum, presubiculum, parasubiculum, hippocampal tail, and hippocampal fissure. Hippocampal subfield segmentation results were retained in each participant’s native T1 space, and their three-dimensional masks were used for subsequent radiomics feature extraction (Fig. 1).

Radiomics feature extraction and model development

Based on the 24 hippocampal subfield ROIs segmented with FreeSurfer, 107 radiomics features were extracted from each ROI using the PyRadiomics package, covering shape features, first-order statistical features, and multiple texture feature classes, including GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, GLDM, and NGTDM. In total, 2,568 features were obtained. Given the relatively limited sample size and high-dimensional radiomics feature space, the original radiomics features were not directly entered into the final machine-learning model. Instead, a stepwise dimensionality-reduction strategy was applied exclusively within the training set to reduce feature redundancy and mitigate overfitting risk. Feature standardization, univariate screening, and mRMR/LASSO-based feature selection were performed exclusively in the training set; standardization parameters were estimated from the training set and then applied to the test set. All imaging features were standardized using z-score normalization. First, the Mann–Whitney U test (P < 0.05) was used for preliminary dimensionality reduction, followed by the maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR) method to remove redundant features and retain those with greater information content. Finally, the most representative features were further selected using 10-fold cross-validated LASSO-logistic regression. To assess feature-selection stability, the selection frequency of each retained feature was defined as the proportion of cross-validation folds in which that feature obtained a non-zero LASSO coefficient. The Radscore was calculated as Radscore = Σ(βi × Xi), where βi denotes the LASSO-logistic regression coefficient and Xi denotes the standardized feature value. This approach follows commonly used strategies for constructing radiomics signatures [12, 28].

Three XGBoost-based prediction models were then constructed. For the sMRI-Radscore model, the 84 GMV features were screened using the same feature-selection procedure described above, and the resulting sMRI-Radscore was used as the model input. For the Hip-Radscore model, hippocampal subfield radiomics features were selected and linearly combined to generate Hip-Radscore, which was then entered into the XGBoost model. For clinical variables, demographic features, disease-related variables, and scale-based indices, including ITAQ, BPRS, and DII scores, were initially considered as candidate predictors in the analytic framework. For the combined model, sMRI-Radscore, Hip-Radscore, and four clinically meaningful variables showing significant between-group differences in the training set, including sex, duration of disease, DII score, and monthly family income, were integrated as input features. Continuous clinical variables, including duration of disease, DII score, and monthly family income, were standardized using z-score normalization based on the training set, and the same transformation was applied to the test set; sex was entered into the model as a binary variable.

The XGBoost classifier was implemented using the xgboost package in R. Hyperparameters were optimized only within the training set using 10-fold cross-validation, with the area under the ROC curve as the optimization metric. The candidate parameter grid included nrounds, max_depth, eta, subsample, colsample_bytree, and min_child_weight. After the optimal parameter combination was determined, the final model was refitted using the entire training set and then validated in the test set. Detailed parameter settings are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2), with a two-sided significance level set at P < 0.05. The Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test were used to assess normality and homogeneity of variance, respectively. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Effect sizes for continuous variables were expressed as Cohen’s d (using the pooled standard deviation) with 95% confidence intervals, and effect sizes for categorical variables were expressed as Cramér’s V [29].

Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the AUC and its 95% confidence interval reported, and differences between models were compared using the DeLong test. In the training set, the optimal threshold was determined from predicted probabilities using the Youden index, and this threshold was then fixed and applied to the test set; Sensitivity, Specificity, Accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for the training and test sets accordingly. To enhance robustness, all metrics were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples and averaged. The incremental value of the combined model over the single models was quantified using the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Calibration curves were plotted in the test set and the Brier score was calculated to assess goodness of fit, while decision curve analysis was used to evaluate clinical net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. To enhance model interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was used to provide both global- and individual-level explanation analyses of the combined model. To avoid information loss due to dichotomization, the continuous total MOAS score was further used to assess the correlations of Hip-Radscore (and its constituent features) with aggression severity.

Models based on structural MRI gray matter volume

A total of 84 whole-brain GMV features were extracted, including 68 cortical regions and 16 bilateral subcortical gray matter structures. After screening using the Mann–Whitney U test and the maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR) method, 14 candidate features with higher relevance and low redundancy were retained. Subsequently, feature reduction was further performed using 10-fold cross-validated LASSO-logistic regression, resulting in five key GMV features: the right hippocampus, left amygdala, right lateral orbitofrontal cortex, right rostral anterior cingulate cortex, and left ventral diencephalon. Detailed LASSO coefficients and selection frequencies are provided in Supplementary Table 4-A. An sMRI-Radscore was constructed based on these features, and an XGBoost prediction model was subsequently developed.

This model achieved a mean AUC of 0.681 (95% CI: 0.597–0.766, bootstrap-estimated) for predicting aggressive behavior during hospitalization in patients with schizophrenia, with sensitivity and specificity of 67.3% and 66.1%, respectively (Fig. 2A–C; Table 2).

Hippocampal subfield radiomics model

Bilateral hippocampi were automatically segmented using FreeSurfer 7.2.0, yielding 24 hippocampal subfields. A total of 107 radiomics features were extracted from each subfield, resulting in 2,568 raw features. After screening using the Mann–Whitney U test and mRMR, 32 informative and non-redundant features were retained. Subsequently, 10-fold cross-validated LASSO-logistic regression was applied, and nine key features were ultimately selected: L-CA1_GLCM_Entropy, R-Subiculum_GLSZM_LargeAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis (R-Subiculum_LAHGLE), R-CA1_FirstOrder_Entropy, L-GC-DG_FirstOrder_Skewness (L-GC-DG_Skewness), L-CA3_GLRLM_ShortRunEmphasis (L-CA3_SRE), R-Presubiculum_GLCM_DifferenceVariance (R-Presubiculum_DifferenceVariance), R-Fimbria_GLCM_Contrast (R-Fimbria_Contrast), L-Tail_Shape_SurfaceArea (L-Tail_SurfaceArea), and R-Subiculum_Shape_VoxelVolume (R-Subiculum_VoxelVolume). Detailed LASSO coefficients and selection frequencies are provided in Supplementary Table 4-B. Hip-Radscore was calculated based on these features, and an XGBoost prediction model was subsequently developed. This model achieved a mean AUC of 0.729 (95% CI: 0.651–0.808, bootstrap-estimated), with sensitivity and specificity of 76.9% and 69.4%, respectively (Fig. 2D–F; Table 2).

Combined model and performance improvement analysis

A combined XGBoost model was developed by integrating sMRI-Radscore, Hip-Radscore, and four significant clinical variables (Sex, Duration of disease, Impulsive, and Income). In the training set, the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.821–0.929, bootstrap-estimated), which was significantly higher than that of the sMRI model (AUC = 0.681; DeLong test: P < 0.001) and the Hip-Radscore model (AUC = 0.729; DeLong test: P = 0.008; Table 2; Fig. 3A–C). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) analyses showed that, in both the training and test sets, NRI/IDI indicated incremental value of the combined model over the single models (Table 3). In the test set, the combined model yielded the lowest Brier score (0.119), compared with 0.203 for the sMRI model and 0.182 for the Hip-Radscore model (Fig. 3D–F). Decision curve analysis further demonstrated that, across a broad range of risk thresholds, the combined model consistently provided higher clinical net benefit (Fig. 3G–I).

Model interpretability analysis (SHAP)

To enhance interpretability and transparency, SHAP analysis was performed for the combined model. The global feature importance ranking showed that Hip-Radscore (1.816) and Impulsivity (DII score, 1.269) had the highest mean absolute SHAP values, followed by sMRI-Radscore (0.689), Income (monthly family income, 0.593), Duration of disease (0.547), and Sex (0.052) (Fig. 4A–B). Further feature-importance analysis based on the native XGBoost gain metric showed that the ranking of predictors was consistent with the SHAP results (Fig. 4C). Hip-Radscore exhibited a stable positive contribution to the model output, such that higher Hip-Radscore values corresponded to a higher predicted probability of clinically significant aggression during hospitalization. At the individual level, one high-risk patient and one low-risk patient were selected as representative cases for SHAP analysis (Fig. 4D–E). For example, in the high-risk participant, Hip-Radscore and sMRI-Radscore were the primary positive contributors to the model output, whereas in the low-risk participant, lower Hip-Radscore and lower impulsivity contributed substantially to risk reduction.

Correlation between Hip-Radscore and MOAS scores

Spearman rank correlation analysis showed a significant positive correlation between Hip-Radscore and the total MOAS score (ρ = 0.52, P < 0.001), indicating that higher hippocampal subfield radiomics–based risk scores were associated with greater aggression severity. At the individual feature level, L-CA1_GLCM_Entropy (ρ = 0.45, P < 0.001), R-Subiculum_LAHGLE (ρ = 0.36, P < 0.001), and L-CA3_SRE (ρ = 0.31, P < 0.001) were significantly positively correlated with MOAS scores. In contrast, R-Subiculum_VoxelVolume (ρ = −0.40, P < 0.001) and L-GC-DG_Skewness (ρ = −0.35, P < 0.001) were significantly negatively correlated with MOAS scores (Fig. 5). No statistically significant associations were observed between the remaining four features and MOAS scores (all P > 0.05). The above single-feature correlation analyses were exploratory, and no multiple-comparison correction was applied.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated structural MRI metrics and hippocampal subfield radiomics features to develop and validate a multidimensional prediction model for aggressive behavior during hospitalization in patients with schizophrenia. The results showed that the hippocampal subfield radiomics model had better discriminative performance for predicting aggressive behavior than the conventional GMV model, and that the combined model achieved the best overall performance. In the training set, the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.875 and remained high in the test set (AUC = 0.842), markedly outperforming both the GMV-only model and the hippocampal radiomics model. The DeLong test results further supported the statistically significant improvement of the combined model. Notably, in the test set, the combined model maintained high sensitivity (80.8%) and specificity (79.2%), suggesting a relatively balanced performance in identifying high-risk individuals and ruling out low-risk individuals. In addition, the relatively high negative predictive value (NPV) suggests that the model may be more suitable for risk ruling-out in clinical settings, which may help reduce unnecessary restrictive interventions. Across multiple metrics, including NRI, IDI, the Brier score, and decision curve analysis (DCA), the combined model outperformed the single models. SHAP-based interpretability analyses further indicated that Hip-Radscore was the most influential predictor, followed by impulsivity. The significant positive correlation between Hip-Radscore and the total MOAS score suggests that it carries dual relevance in structural characterization and in reflecting aggressive behavior.

We found that the model based on hippocampal subfield radiomics demonstrated relatively better discriminative performance for predicting aggressive behavior than the conventional GMV model. Compared with the whole-brain GMV model, the radiomics model showed an AUC increase from 0.681 to 0.729, with some improvement in sensitivity and specificity. Whole-brain GMV represents a macroscopic structural measure that primarily reflects global morphometric differences, but it is often insufficient to detect subtle, focal alterations associated with aggression risk. In studies of aggressive behavior in schizophrenia, macroscopic volumetric differences may be less sensitive, whereas regional microstructural abnormalities may have greater biological relevance [30]. In contrast, hippocampal subfield radiomics features quantify gray-level distributions and their spatial arrangement patterns, providing a finer-grained structural characterization beyond conventional volumetric metrics and potentially better capturing subfield-level structural heterogeneity associated with aggressive behavior [31, 32]. In addition, we found that the selected radiomics features spanned multiple key subfields (e.g., CA1, CA3, GC-DG, the subiculum, and related output pathway structures), and that each region exhibited distinct tissue characteristics. This not only supports prior meta-analytic evidence indicating widespread abnormalities across all hippocampal subfields in schizophrenia, but also further suggests that patterns of microstructural alteration may be region specific [33]. Compared with global measures that may dilute lesion-related signals, Hip-Radscore enables more precise localization and quantification of microstructural variability in key brain regions, thereby markedly improving the sensitivity and discriminative ability for detecting aggressive behavior during hospitalization in patients with schizophrenia. In addition, the right hippocampal GMV feature selected in sMRI-Radscore reflects macroscopic volume changes of the whole hippocampus, whereas Hip-Radscore summarizes hippocampal subfield-level radiomics features and captures finer-grained shape, gray-level distribution, and texture heterogeneity. Therefore, the inclusion of both scores in the combined model was intended to integrate complementary structural information rather than to redundantly include hippocampal indicators.

In this study, features significantly associated with MOAS scores were primarily concentrated in key hippocampal subfields, including CA1, CA3, DG, and the subiculum, suggesting that microstructural and morphometric alterations within the hippocampus may be involved in modulating the severity of aggressive behavior [34, 35]. Specifically, L-CA1_GLCM_Entropy was positively correlated with MOAS, suggesting that increased complexity of gray-level distribution in CA1 may reflect greater local microstructural heterogeneity, thereby affecting contextual information integration [36, 37]; The positive correlation for L-CA3_SRE further suggests that altered gray-level structural patterns in CA3 may be related to changes in contextual memory processing [38, 39]. In contrast, L-GC-DG_Skewness was negatively correlated with MOAS, suggesting that abnormal gray-level distribution shape in the dentate gyrus may impair contextual discrimination, making individuals more prone to exaggerated responses in ambiguous situations [40, 41]. Notably, R-Subiculum_LAHGLE was positively correlated with MOAS, whereas R-Subiculum_VoxelVolume was negatively correlated, suggesting that this output subfield may exhibit a combined pattern of increased texture complexity and reduced volume, reflecting coordinated changes in structural integrity and microstructural tissue characteristics [13, 42]. Meanwhile, the inclusion of R-Presubiculum_GLCM_DifferenceVariance, R-Fimbria_GLCM_Contrast, and L-Tail_Shape_SurfaceArea indicates that alterations in output-related hippocampal subfields and tail morphology may also contribute to the overall risk-related structural pattern [43, 44]. Overall, the coordinated abnormalities reflected by these nine features across key intra-hippocampal nodes and output-related structures may together constitute a structural basis potentially associated with aggressive behavior, whereas Hip-Radscore integrates the combined information from these multi-subfield features.

Interpretable machine learning approaches help identify key drivers of complex models, thereby improving clinical interpretability and usability [45]. In clinical practice, this is particularly important because model complexity may compromise the transparency and interpretability of the results. Because XGBoost is a nonlinear tree-based model and lacks directly interpretable regression coefficients, we additionally incorporated SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis to enhance model transparency and clinical interpretability [46–48]. SHAP is a method for interpreting machine-learning model predictions by quantifying the magnitude and direction of each feature’s contribution to the predicted outcome. At the global level, overall importance is assessed by calculating the mean contribution of SHAP values for each feature, whereas at the individual level, case-specific SHAP values are used to show the positive or negative influence and effect size of each feature on a given prediction. In the global SHAP analysis, Hip-Radscore was the most influential variable in the combined model, exceeding both conventional structural MRI metrics and clinical variables, further underscoring the central role of hippocampal subfield radiomics in precisely predicting aggressive behavior during hospitalization in patients with schizophrenia. Notably, impulsivity was the second most important predictor after Hip-Radscore, suggesting that impaired behavioral inhibition provides important predictive information beyond imaging features. This finding is clinically plausible because elevated impulsivity may reflect reduced inhibitory control and an increased tendency toward rapid responses to perceived threats, emotional arousal, or interpersonal conflict, thereby increasing the risk of verbal or physical aggression. At the individual level, SHAP plots enabled interpretable individualized prediction. For example, in a high-risk participant, Hip-Radscore, sMRI-Radscore, and high impulsivity were the top three contributors to the predicted risk of aggressive behavior during hospitalization in schizophrenia, consistent with the observed outcome. Such interpretability may help psychiatrists more accurately assess the risk of aggressive behavior early during hospitalization, thereby optimizing risk-stratified management and, when necessary, enabling more cautious and appropriate use of interventions such as restraint or seclusion, reducing the risk of injury to healthcare workers and the associated family and healthcare burden [5, 49]. Compared with models that provide only a binary outcome, an XGBoost–SHAP framework can provide both the risk level and its key drivers, offering more targeted information to inform clinical decision-making.

It should be noted that this study focused on predicting clinically significant aggressive behavior during hospitalization, and the model is mainly intended for risk stratification and early warning in inpatient settings. For patients with schizophrenia who have aggressive or severe violent behavior but are not under ordinary inpatient management, such as community patients, forensic psychiatric patients, or patients with a history of severe violence, the context, severity, and risk structure of aggressive behavior may differ. Lu et al. recently reported gray matter alterations in frontotemporal and insular regions in male patients with schizophrenia and severe violent behavior, suggesting that violence-related structural abnormalities may extend beyond the hippocampal system [50]. In contrast, the present study characterized structural heterogeneity associated with clinically significant aggression during hospitalization from the perspective of hippocampal subfield radiomics. Future studies should further validate the cross-setting applicability of this model in community samples, forensic psychiatric samples, and patients with a history of severe violence. Regarding outcome assessment, MOAS was selected as the primary assessment tool because it is an observer-rated and event-based scale that is suitable for evaluating overt aggressive behavior that actually occurs during hospitalization and can be verified from medical records [5, 50]. In contrast, the Buss-Perry Aggression Questionnaire mainly evaluates self-reported aggression traits or tendencies and may be more susceptible to insight, cooperation, and reporting bias; therefore, it was not used as the main outcome assessment tool for actual in-hospital aggressive behavior in this study.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this study was based on a single-center retrospective cohort and lacked external validation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative evidence and model discrimination related to in-hospital aggressive behavior, rather than causal evidence or definitive prospective prediction. Second, although feature selection was performed only within the training set and mRMR, cross-validated LASSO, bootstrap resampling, and independent test-set evaluation were used to reduce overfitting risk, the sample size remained limited relative to the high-dimensional radiomics feature space. Therefore, model stability and generalizability require further validation in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts. Third, aggression assessment was based on MOAS scores combined with clinical observations and hospitalization records, and may have been influenced by record completeness and ward management practices. In addition, aggressive behavior during hospitalization may not fully represent long-term aggression risk after discharge or aggression-related characteristics in non-hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. Finally, although SHAP analysis improved model interpretability, the correspondence between radiomics features and specific neurobiological mechanisms remains to be further explored.

Conclusions

This study suggests that hippocampal subfield radiomics may provide complementary structural information for identifying clinically significant aggressive behavior in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. The combined model integrating clinical variables, whole-brain structural MRI information, and hippocampal subfield radiomics showed improved discriminative performance and interpretability. These findings support the potential value of hippocampal subfield radiomics for in-hospital aggression risk stratification, although further validation in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts is warranted.
