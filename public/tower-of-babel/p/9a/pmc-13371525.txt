Modulation of antioxidant defense and organic acid profiles in green beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) via salicylic acid and passive modified atmosphere packaging
Authors: Onur Tekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGreen bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is a vegetable species characterized by its short postharvest storage life. This study investigated the effects of different doses of salicylic acid (SA; 1 and 3 mM), passive modified atmosphere packaging (passive MAP), and the combined applications of SA and passive MAP (hereafter SA + MAP) on postharvest physiological and biochemical changes in the 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean cultivars.MethodsPods were stored at 7ºC and 80 ± 5% relative humidity, and changes in respiration rate, external ethylene production, headspace gas composition (O2 and CO2), antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD, and APX), MDA content, and organic acids (oxalic, citric, malic, succinic, fumaric, and tartaric acids) were examined.ResultsThe study revealed significant physiological and biochemical differences between the 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' cultivars. In 'Gina', 1 mM SA + MAP was the most effective treatment for suppressing respiration, reducing it by 49% relative to the control, while external ethylene production under this treatment decreased by 30%. In 'Gevaş', 3 mM SA + MAP was the most effective treatment, reducing respiration and external ethylene production by 64% and 75%, respectively, compared with the control. Under these most effective treatment conditions, 'Gina' showed enhanced acid accumulation, with significantly higher oxalic (149%), succinic (55%), citric (40%), and fumaric acid levels (21%) than the control. Conversely, 'Gevaş' exhibited more pronounced changes in organic acid profiles, with greater depletion of fumaric (73%), succinic (79%), and tartaric acids (62%), while oxalic (97%) and malic acid levels (5%) remained above the control, and citric acid was maintained at a level comparable to the control. Furthermore, enhanced antioxidant defense effectively suppressed lipid peroxidation, reducing MDA levels by 41.8% and 69.4% relative to the control, respectively. Collectively, these findings indicate that the combined application of SA and passive MAP is an effective cultivar-specific strategy for maintaining postharvest quality during 25 days of storage in green beans.

Introduction

Green beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), a member of the Fabaceae family, constitute one of the major legume crops cultivated worldwide [1, 2]. In 2024, global green bean production reached approximately 25.9 million tons, whereas Türkiye accounted for approximately 2% of this total [3]. Green beans are widely consumed vegetables owing to their vibrant colour, desirable flavour, pod thickness, and nutritional attributes [4, 5]. They also represent a valuable dietary source of minerals, vitamins, dietary fiber, and plant-based proteins, all of which play a crucial role in human nutrition and overall health [6, 7].

Green beans have a notably limited storage life due to their high respiration rate and are therefore classified as highly perishable vegetables. They are commonly marketed either in bulk or in foam trays wrapped with stretch film. Such marketing practices may accelerate postharvest quality deterioration, particularly under high ambient temperatures, leading to loss of freshness and textural quality. Respiratory metabolism and carbohydrate depletion adversely affect pod quality during storage [8]. These metabolic processes are also closely associated with postharvest quality deterioration in green bean pods during storage [4]. In addition, green beans are extremely susceptible to postharvest mechanical damage, including shrivelling, chlorophyll degradation, and increased fiber accumulation [5, 9]. Lignin and cellulose metabolism play critical roles in pod fibrousness and lignification. As cells elongate and the secondary cell wall thickens, lignin accumulates within the fiber bundle network, accompanied by intensive cellulose synthesis that leads to the formation of fiber bundles [10]. Consequently, the pod texture becomes coarse and fibrous, and significant alterations occur in total dietary fiber content as well as in the proportions of soluble and insoluble dietary fiber [9]. These changes adversely affect consumer acceptance and eating quality. Other challenges encountered during bean storage include product losses associated with improper storage conditions and microbial spoilage in marketed products [11].

The global demand for food has risen substantially in recent years, in parallel with the continuous growth of the world population [12]. Nevertheless, more than 30% of food produced each year is lost along the supply chain, with fresh fruits and vegetables accounting for over half of these losses [13, 14]. The imperative to reduce these substantial postharvest losses has therefore become increasingly critical. To this end, recent years have witnessed a growing interest in utilizing safer postharvest compounds and bioregulators as alternatives to synthetic chemicals to extend the postharvest storage life and preserve the quality of horticultural crops [15–17].

Among these postharvest bioregulators, salicylic acid has been identified as a promising candidate for sustaining the postharvest quality of stored produce [18]. Phytohormones, including auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, abscisic acid, ethylene, brassinosteroids, jasmonic acid, strigolactones, and salicylic acid, are naturally occurring, non-toxic compounds that play essential roles in plant growth, development, and responses to stress, and may be synthesized naturally by plants or produced artificially [18–22].

SA serves as an endogenous phenolic acid, phytohormone, and signaling molecule, playing a pivotal role in the regulation of stress adaptation, defense mechanisms, and secondary metabolism [23]. It has been widely used in commercial settings to enhance both local and systemic resistance in horticultural crops [24, 25]. As a naturally occurring plant hormone, SA is classified as “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) for use in fruits [26–28]. Moreover, SA is characterized by a multitude of advantages, such as cost-effectiveness, high efficacy, safety, ease of application, biodegradability, and the absence of harmful toxic residues on the produce. Owing to these merits, it is extensively utilized to enhance postharvest quality and prolong both storage and shelf life [29–32]. Previous studies have shown that SA can preserve product colour, modulate respiration and ethylene production, stimulate antioxidant system activity and the phenylpropanoid pathway, and enhance stress tolerance and overall quality in fruits and vegetables [33–37]. Additionally, phytohormones including SA have been proposed as potential alternatives to mitigate the adverse effects associated with synthetic chemical preservatives in horticultural products [38].

MAP is a postharvest technology that modifies the gas composition within the package, generally by increasing CO2 and reducing O2 levels around fresh produce. In passive MAP systems, the internal atmosphere is initially ambient air and is subsequently modified through the respiration of the produce and the gas permeability of the packaging film. The development of polymeric films with different gas permeability properties has expanded its use in the storage of horticultural commodities. Due to its cost-effectiveness and non-chemical mode of action, MAP is widely used to prolong storage life and maintain product quality during storage and transport [39–43].

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the potential of combining salicylic acid (SA) with modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) to improve postharvest quality in horticultural commodities. In nectarine and apricot [44, 45], SA + MAP treatments were reported to be more effective than either treatment alone in maintaining quality and extending storage life, while studies using active MAP systems with SA in mango [46] also demonstrated beneficial effects on storage performance. These findings indicate that the biochemical action of SA and the physical protection provided by MAP can act complementarily to enhance postharvest preservation. However, limited information is available on how the combined application of SA and MAP influences postharvest physiology, antioxidant responses, and organic acid profiles in green beans, particularly in different cultivars.

This study was structured around three primary research questions related to the storage performance of the 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean cultivars: (1) To determine the effects of different doses of SA on the storage characteristics of both cultivars and to identify the most effective dose(s); (2) To evaluate the quality performance of beans stored under control (non-packaged) and passive MAP conditions within the same cold storage environment throughout the storage period; and (3) To assess the storage performance of both cultivars under the combined application of SA and passive MAP (SA + MAP).

Materials and methods

In the study, 'Gina' standard (Phaseolus vulgaris cv. Gina) and 'Gevaş' local bean (Phaseolus vulgaris cv. Gevaş) cultivars, cultivated in the Gevaş district of Van province, were used as plant materials. Pods harvested under optimum conditions were brought to the cold storage facility as rapidly as possible on the same day. Pre-cooling was performed on 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean cultivars for 24 h (10ºC and 80 ± 5% RH). Subsequently, the pods were sorted to be uniform for standardization purposes, and damaged pods were discarded. Surface sterilization was performed by immersing the samples in 250 ppm sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min, after which they were rinsed three times with distilled water. Salicylic acid (SA) solutions (1 and 3 mM), selected based on previous postharvest studies reporting beneficial effects of salicylic acid within a comparable low-millimolar concentration range, such as Sayyari et al. [47] and El-Beltagi et al. [48], were prepared by dissolving SA powder in distilled water at 50ºC for 30 min. The pods were immersed in prepared 1 and 3 mM SA solutions, while the control group was dipped in distilled water for 15 min. Following the applications, the pods were air-dried at room temperature (20 ± 2ºC and 60 ± 5% RH) for 30 min until complete surface drying was achieved. Subsequently, 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean pods were placed into foam trays. The tops of the beans subjected to passive MAP application were covered with 8 µm thick polyvinyl chloride (PVC) stretch film, selected to simulate standard commercial practices. The experiment utilized passive modified atmosphere packaging (passive MAP) to evaluate the postharvest storage life of the pods. The initial internal atmosphere was ambient air (approx. 21% O2 and 0.03% CO2), which was subsequently modified naturally by the respiration of the 200 g samples and the gas permeability of the PVC film during the 25-day storage period at 7ºC and 80 ± 5% RH. The storage temperature of 7 °C was selected to avoid chilling injury at lower temperatures and to limit accelerated metabolic deterioration at higher temperatures. Physiological and biochemical analyses were conducted at harvest (day 0) and at 5-day intervals (days 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25) throughout the cold storage period. At each sampling date, each cultivar × treatment combination was represented by three independent biological replicates (200 g pods per replicate), and a fresh set of replicates was used for all analyses.

Respiration rate

The respiration rate was calculated as mL CO2 kg−1 h−1 by measuring the quantity of CO2 released by pods (approximately 100–150 g per container) incubated in 1 L gas-tight containers for a duration of 2 h, using a Headspace Gas Analyser GS3/L (Systech Illinois, Johnsburg, IL, USA) [49].

External ethylene production

External ethylene production was determined using a Shimadzu GC-2010 gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a capillary column (Rt-Alumina BOND/MAPD, Restek, 50 m × 0.53 mm i.d., 10 µm film thickness). Gas samples were withdrawn from 1 L gas-tight containers in which approximately 100–150 g of beans had been incubated for 2 h, and the results were expressed as µl C2H4 kg−1 h−1 [49].

Headspace gas composition

The variations occurring in the internal oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) concentrations within the packages were monitored using a Headspace Gas Analyser GS3/L (Systech Illinois, Johnsburg, IL, USA).

Organic acids

A modified version of the method described by Bevilacqua and Califano [50] was employed for the determination of organic acids in 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean cultivars throughout the storage period. The analysis of organic acids was conducted on an HPLC system (Thermo Scientific Dionex UltiMate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a DAD detector and a C18 column, with detection at 190 nm. In this study, 0.009 N H2SO4 served as the mobile phase.

Antioxidant enzymes

For the extraction of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) in bean cultivars, a 1 g sample was homogenized using a mechanical homogenizer (Daihan Scientific HG-15D, South Korea) with 5 ml of extraction solution (0.1 mM Na-phosphate:0.5 mM Na-EDTA:1 mM ascorbic acid) (pH 7.6) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Regarding the extraction of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), a 1 g sample was homogenized in an identical manner with 5 ml of cold extraction buffer (0.1 M Na-phosphate:0.5 mM Na-EDTA) (pH 7.6) and subjected to centrifugation. The resulting supernatants were preserved at −80 °C until the analyses were performed [51, 52].

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity was detected by monitoring the disappearance of H2O2 at 240 nm. Briefly, 0.2 ml of bean extract and 2.5 ml of reaction solution (0.05 M KH2PO4: 1.5 mM H2O2) (pH 7.0) were mixed, and CAT activity was calculated from the change in absorbance between 0 and 60 s at 240 nm, using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific GENESYS 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [51, 52].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity was assayed by the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) at 560 nm. Briefly, 0.1 ml of bean extract was mixed with 2.5 ml of reaction solution (50 mM Na2HPO4:33 μM NBT:0.1 mM Na-EDTA:13 mM methionine:5 μM riboflavin) (pH 7.0), incubated under 75 μmol m−2 s−1 (40 W) light at 25ºC for 10 min, and read at 560 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific GENESYS 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). SOD activity was defined as the activity that reduces 50% of NBT [51, 52].

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity

APX activity was determined by the reduction of H2O2 dependent on ascorbic acid. For the measurement, 0.1 ml of APX bean extract was mixed with 3 ml of reaction solution (50 mM KH2PO4:0.1 mM Na-EDTA:0.5 mM ascorbic acid:1.5 mM H2O2) (pH 7.0) and APX activity was determined from the change in absorbance between 0 and 60 s at 290 nm, using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific GENESYS 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [51, 52].

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined to assess lipid peroxidation in bean cultivars. A 0.5 g bean sample was homogenized using a mechanical homogenizer (Daihan Scientific HG-15D, South Korea) with 10 ml (0.1%) trichloroacetic acid (TCA), after which the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. A 1 mL aliquot of the obtained supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of a solution containing 20% TCA and 0.5% TBA, incubated in a water bath at 95ºC for 30 min, and immediately cooled in an ice bath. To determine the MDA content, the absorbance values of the samples were measured at wavelengths of 532 and 600 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific GENESYS 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [52].

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation of the obtained data was conducted based on the completely randomized design. Within the scope of the study, cultivar, treatment, and storage periods were handled as independent variables, and data were subjected to analysis of variance at a 5% significance level (p < 0.05) to determine the effects of these factors. Duncan’s multiple comparison test was applied to differentiate statistically significant variances; the SPSS package program was utilized for these fundamental analyses. On the other hand, Principal component analysis (PCA) was preferred to elucidate the patterns of change occurring in parameters during the storage process. Bivariate relationships between variables were examined using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Correlation analyses were coded using the 'corr_coef' function of the 'metan' package within the RStudio (v4.4.2) platform.

Results

Respiration rate

Fluctuations in respiration rates were observed in both 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean varieties during storage. In the 'Gina' bean variety, decreases relative to harvest were observed in most treatments by the end of storage, except for the 3 mM SA application and the control group. The 'Gevaş' variety exhibited increases relative to harvest across most treatments, except for the 1 and 3 mM SA + MAP applications at the end of storage. Although all passive MAP-based interventions (alone or combined with SA) suppressed respiratory activity in 'Gina' beans, the control group exhibited the highest rates by the end of storage; the strongest suppression was achieved with the 1 mM SA + MAP treatment, while treatment means confirmed that passive MAP, 1 mM SA + MAP, and 3 mM SA + MAP were effective in reducing respiration rates compared to the other treatments (p < 0.05). In the 'Gevaş' variety, increases relative to harvest were observed in most treatments from the 5th day of storage onwards; however, the effects of passive MAP, 1 mM SA + MAP, and 3 mM SA + MAP on respiration became more evident from the 15th day onwards. At the end of storage, the control group exhibited the highest respiration rate, whereas the lowest value was recorded in the 3 mM SA + MAP treatment. External ethylene production

External ethylene production in both 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' cultivars fluctuated during storage, but generally decreased by the end of the storage period. For the 'Gina' cultivar, marked reductions were observed on day 5 in the 1 mM SA + MAP treatment and on day 10 in the 1 mM SA treatment. Although ethylene levels in these treatments increased during subsequent storage intervals, the final values remained lower than the initial (day 0) levels. At the end of storage, the 1 and 3 mM SA + MAP applications effectively suppressed external ethylene production in 'Gina'. In particular, the control samples exhibited the highest ethylene production, whereas the lowest values were recorded in the 3 mM SA + MAP treatment, and the treatment means showed that 1 mM SA + MAP differed significantly from the control, 3 mM SA, and passive MAP treatments (p < 0.05). In the 'Gevaş' cultivar, a general decrease accompanied by fluctuations was observed across most treatments throughout storage, except for the control on day 15. From day 5 onwards, the 1 and 3 mM SA + MAP treatments suppressed external ethylene production in 'Gevaş'. Similar to the findings for 'Gina', the highest value at the end of storage was observed in the control group, whereas the lowest was found in the 3 mM SA + MAP treatment. In contrast, the initial composition of the 'Gevaş' cultivar was dominated by malic acid, followed by tartaric acid, whereas fumaric, citric, oxalic, and succinic acids were detected at lower levels. During storage, tartaric, malic, and succinic acids generally declined in the ‘Gina’ cultivar, whereas citric, fumaric, and oxalic acids increased. In recent years, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) has been widely adopted by researchers to elucidate the dynamic patterns among traits in postharvest studies. In this context, PCA was utilized to evaluate changes in external ethylene amount, respiration rate, headspace gas composition (O2 and CO2), organic acids (tartaric, malic, succinic, citric, fumaric, and oxalic acids), enzymatic activities (CAT, SOD, APX), and MDA content throughout the storage life of 'Gina' beans. The analysis indicated that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for 48.1% of the total variance, while the second (PC2) accounted for 17.2%. Cumulatively, these two components (PC1 + PC2) explained 65.3% of the total variation. Analysis of the PCA plane revealed an inverse relationship: organic acids generally declined with the progression of storage, whereas respiration rate exhibited an upward trend. Discussion

Respiration constitutes a fundamental metabolic activity in all living organisms and remains a crucial physiological process for post-harvest products [53–55]. Elevated respiration rates in fruits and vegetables deplete nutrient reserves, such as organic acids and sugars, and generate substantial metabolic heat, thereby detrimentally affecting the storage quality of fresh produce [56]. Therefore, the respiration rate is considered intrinsically linked to the storability of fresh produce [57]. MAP constitutes a widely adopted conservation strategy designed to extend the storability of horticultural commodities by manipulating the gaseous composition within the packaging headspace. Through the application of MAP, the respiration rate and metabolic activities of fruits and vegetables are suppressed, and ripening is regulated, thereby extending the shelf life of the products [49]. Ethylene exerts a substantial regulatory influence over the respiratory metabolism of horticultural crops. Specifically, an upsurge in ethylene biosynthesis can induce elevated respiratory activity, thereby precipitating the acceleration of senescence [58]. Salicylates act as inhibitors of ethylene biosynthesis and signal transduction, capable of preventing rapid respiratory spikes in post-harvest crops by slowing the decline in sugar production [26, 33]. As shown in Table 1, the 1 mM SA + MAP treatment for 'Gina' and the 3 mM SA + MAP treatment for 'Gevaş' were particularly effective in suppressing respiration rates at the end of storage period. This outcome may be associated with a synergistic effect: the metabolic deceleration induced by MAP [59] coupled with the physiological impact of salicylic acid. Specifically, SA may have contributed to the suppression of ethylene biosynthesis by reducing ACC and ACO activities, thereby limiting the conversion of ACC to ethylene [60]. This interpretation finds support in Asghari and Aghdam [58] who observed that SA-mediated negative regulation of these enzymes curbs ethylene evolution, consequently lowering respiration rates. Furthermore, the respiration dynamics observed here align closely with findings in other horticultural crops, including tomato [61], morel mushroom [62], winter jujube [25], Chinese flowering cabbage [60], and longan [57].

Ethylene serves as a pivotal regulator in the ripening and senescence of horticultural commodities. By triggering the induction of cell wall hydrolyzing enzymes, it precipitates tissue softening and elevates respiration rates, thereby accelerating the overall senescence process in fruits and vegetables [63, 64]. SA, a plant hormone known to exhibit inhibitory effects on polyphenol oxidase enzymes [65], is widely used for the postharvest preservation of fresh agricultural produce [62] and is recognized for its ability to suppress ethylene production in numerous horticultural crops [36]. Previous studies have reported that SA applications offer significant potential for delaying ripening, maintaining quality, and mitigating postharvest losses by downregulating the expression of ethylene biosynthesis genes and inhibiting cell wall-degrading enzyme activity [66, 67]. In the current investigation, the combined application of 3 mM SA + MAP was determined to effectively suppress external ethylene production in both bean cultivars compared to the control group. While MAP itself is effective in limiting ethylene production [68], Asghari and Aghdam [58] stated that SA further reduces ACC production; specifically, by lowering ACC and ACO activities, SA prevents the conversion of ACC to ethylene. The reductions in external ethylene amount observed in Table 2 are consistent with these mechanisms. Furthermore, these findings align with results obtained in studies involving salicylic acid treatments on apricot [59], tomato [61], morel mushroom [62], and broccoli [69].

Respiration serves as the fundamental metabolic process providing energy to fruits and vegetables. Depending on oxygen utilization, it is categorized into two distinct pathways: aerobic respiration, which entails the oxidative breakdown of organic substrates into simpler molecules alongside carbon dioxide, water, and energy release [70]; and anaerobic respiration, a fermentation process where pyruvate is decarboxylated into ethanol without oxygen consumption [71, 72]. Anaerobic respiration is regarded as a detrimental physiological state, as the accumulation of metabolites predominantly ethanol can result in the development of objectionable off-odors in the produce [73]. Consequently, regulating respiration rates is paramount for mitigating postharvest losses [74, 75]. One of the most effective strategies for extending shelf and storage life involves rigorously controlling the O2 and CO2 concentrations in the atmosphere surrounding the produce [76]. MAP stands out as a prevalent strategy extensively utilized for the preservation of fresh produce. It has been established that MAP is particularly effective in mitigating chilling injury by suppressing respiration rates and ethylene biosynthesis, while simultaneously curtailing water loss and the accumulation of ethanol and acetaldehyde [49, 77]. By tailoring the in-package gas composition to the specific requirements of the commodity, MAP effectively slows down ripening and is integral to the preservation, transportation, and distribution of horticultural crops [78, 79]. As noted by Gontard et al. [76], since oxygen drives decomposition reactions, many packaging strategies aim to preserve quality specifically by excluding or limiting oxygen access. An analysis of the headspace gas compositions detailed in Tables 3 and 4 reveals that both 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean varieties experienced a progressive depletion of O2 and a concurrent accumulation of CO2 by the end of the storage life. This is thought to be due to the fact that the MAP application slows respiration in the beans and the salicylic acid application suppresses ethylene synthesis, and may also be attributed to the decrease in respiratory rate resulting from stomatal closure in the beans and the delay of senescence by reducing metabolic activity [80]. Notably, these results align closely with findings from similar studies on salicylic acid-treated parsley [81] and thyme [80].

In the respiration of fruits and vegetables, organic acids play a pivotal role by acting as both a primary substrate and a critical energy reserve. Beyond their metabolic function, they function alongside sugars as key determinants of organoleptic properties, including taste, odor, and aroma. Moreover, organic acids are critically important given their specific role in fostering antioxidant capacity and antimicrobial activity [82–84]. The profile and predominance of organic acids vary substantially across different species [85]. Throughout the processes of ripening and senescence, the breakdown of organic acids occurs principally via two distinct metabolic routes. Initially, organic acid degradation is driven by their conversion into sugars via gluconeogenesis within the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) cycle [86]. A distinct pathway comprises the fermentation of these acids to yield ethanol, resulting in a significant drop in the acidity of fruits and vegetables [49, 87]. Furthermore, ethylene acts as a catalyst for ripening while simultaneously promoting the breakdown of organic acids into simple sugars to fuel respiratory processes [88, 89]. The study determined that salicylic acid applications were effective in preserving organic acids, although the extent of this effect varied depending on the application. Among the combined treatments, 1 mM SA + MAP generally showed a stronger capacity to preserve several organic acids, although the response varied depending on the cultivar and the specific acid considered. This protective capacity may be associated with a synergistic mechanism in which salicylic acid may slow TCA-related metabolism and reduce respiration rates, while also influencing enzymes involved in organic acid metabolism [26, 33, 90, 91]. Concurrently, MAP application may have contributed by suppressing the general metabolic activity of the beans. These results align with recent findings in the literature. Çelik et al. [92] reported that the application of salicylic acid at varying concentrations (0.5, 1, and 1.5 mM) resulted in higher retention of malic, citric, succinic, tartaric, and oxalic acids in plums compared to the control group after a 45 day storage period. Similarly, Akın et al. [93] reported that the application of 1 mM salicylic acid and spermidine to raspberry fruits resulted in higher concentrations of citric, malic, succinic, oxalic, and fumaric acids compared to the control group after a 15 day period. Further corroborating this trend, Kaygisiz et al. [91] reported that the treatment of 'Depar' tomatoes with 1 mM salicylic acid and melatonin resulted in a superior retention of malic, citric, oxalic, and tartaric acids, maintaining levels significantly higher than those of the control group after 24 days of storage.

When considered together, the organic acid findings of the present study indicate that SA and MAP did not exert a uniform protective effect on all acids; rather, they appear to have regulated postharvest organic acid turnover in a cultivar-dependent manner. Malate, citrate, succinate, and fumarate are closely associated with central carbon metabolism, and low-O₂ stress has been reported to reduce the synthesis of several organic acids, particularly those linked to the tricarboxylic acid cycle [94]. Likewise, storage conditions characterized by lower respiratory activity and decreased ethylene production have been associated with improved preservation of organic acids in apple fruit [95]. Exogenous salicylic acid has also been reported to maintain higher levels of certain organic acids during storage, while modulating key genes and enzyme activities involved in organic acid metabolism [96]. In addition, 1-MCP, an ethylene inhibitor, has been shown to delay the loss of malate and citrate and to contribute to the maintenance of fruit acidity by regulating the balance between malate biosynthesis and degradation [97]. In the present study, the suppression of external ethylene production by SA + MAP, together with the accompanying reduction in respiration, suggests that the preservation of specific organic acids may be partially related to a limitation of ethylene-associated metabolic consumption during storage. Therefore, the decline of some organic acids and the maintenance of others under SA + MAP should be interpreted not as a uniform preservation of all acids, but rather as a cultivar-specific reorganization of organic acid turnover and the balance of TCA-related intermediates under conditions of reduced metabolic demand.

Low-temperature stress induces the activation of antioxidant systems, accounting for the initial increase in antioxidant activity. Since antioxidants function as electron donors to stabilize free radicals, this mechanism offers a plausible explanation for the observed gradual decline in antioxidant activity [98, 99]. Oxidative processes associated with fruit ripening and senescence result in a reduction in the activity of detoxifying enzymes (e.g., CAT) and a concurrent increase in the activity of enzymes generating superoxide or H2O2, thereby leading to the accumulation of toxic Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) [100]. Consequently, the equilibrium between CAT and SOD activities plays a critical role in maintaining stable intracellular superoxide (O2⁻) and H2O2 levels. Postharvest application of salicylic acid maintains cell membrane integrity in fruits and vegetables by promoting the activation of defence mechanisms and antioxidant enzymes. When salicylic acid (SA) interacts with specific enzymes, it elicits the accumulation of H2O2; this accumulation subsequently stimulates the activation of protective enzymes and Pathogenesis-Related (PR) proteins, thereby enhancing the fruit's resistance to pathogens [28]. SA also triggers the build-up of H2O2 and other oxidative radicals; this initial increase is crucial for activating genes linked to disease resistance during the early storage period. However, these free radicals must be neutralized during the later stages of storage to prevent oxidative damage [99]. As reported by Zahedi et al. [101] plants possess evolved protective mechanisms to maintain low intracellular ROS levels, scavenging reactive and toxic ROS through antioxidant enzyme systems such as CAT, SOD, and APX as well as various antioxidant compounds. Enzymes including SOD, CAT, APX, and POD play critical roles in ROS metabolism and the scavenging of free radicals [102]. SOD functions as the primary enzyme responsible for the detoxification of superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), catalysing their disproportionation into H2O2 and O2. Subsequently, APX and CAT contribute to the maintenance of oxidative equilibrium by degrading the resulting H2O2 into H2O and O2 [99]. Furthermore, APX plays a pivotal role in regulating the levels of H2O2 and ascorbate, factors which significantly influence fruit quality during ripening and the postharvest period [103]. Bukhat et al. [104] highlighted the capacity of SA to enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, thereby mitigating both the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent membrane damage. In the present study, it was determined that the 1 mM SA + MAP application in the ‘Gina’ bean variety and the 3 mM SA + MAP application in the ‘Gevaş’ bean variety exhibited higher activity values ​​for SOD, CAT, and APX enzymes compared to the control group. This is likely attributable to the fact that salicylic acid application triggers antioxidant enzymes, partly in response to the elevated activity of lipoxygenase (LOX), an enzyme responsible for the generation of superoxide radicals [105]. These findings are corroborated by recent literature. Wang et al. [106] reported that following 20 days of storage of ‘Cuibao Seedless’ grapes treated with melatonin (100 µM), SA (100 mg L−1), and combined applications, SOD and CAT enzyme activities were higher in the salicylic acid treatment compared to the control group. Similarly, Wu et al. [107] observed that SA treatment (200 µmol L−1) yielded the highest SOD enzyme activity at the end of storage in ‘Chester’ variety blackberries, outperforming methyl jasmonate treatments (200 µmol L−1). Supporting this, Cheng et al. [108] reported that applying SA (0.02, 0.20, and 2 mM) to 'Xinli No. 7' pears effectively preserved the activity of key antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD, and APX) above control levels until the end of storage life. The cited literature parallels the data obtained at the conclusion of this study.

The differential responses of 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' to SA + MAP further suggest that the postharvest effect of this treatment combination was strongly cultivar-dependent. The stronger response of ‘Gina’ to 1 mM SA + MAP and of ‘Gevaş’ to 3 mM SA + MAP may be associated with inherent differences in their physiological and biochemical behavior during storage, since genotype-dependent variation in postharvest quality and antioxidant-related traits has been reported in horticultural crops [109, 110]. Such divergence may also reflect differences in redox regulation, as antioxidant capacity and postharvest antioxidant responses are known to vary among cultivars [109, 110]. In addition, cultivar-related variation in ethylene responsiveness may have contributed to the different optimal doses observed here, as cultivars can differ in their sensitivity to ethylene during postharvest storage [111]. Differences in surface barrier properties, including cuticle structure and permeability, may also be relevant, because fruit cuticles can influence postharvest quality and storage potential, and cultivar-related variation in cuticular composition has been reported in some fruit species [112]. Although SA uptake and metabolism were not directly measured in the present study, the cultivar-specific patterns observed in respiration, ethylene production, antioxidant enzyme activities, MDA accumulation, and organic acid dynamics support the interpretation that ‘Gina’ and ‘Gevaş’ differed in their regulatory responses to SA + MAP during storage.

Under stress conditions, cellular redox homeostasis in plants is disrupted due to the excessive accumulation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), including OH•, 1O2, O2•−, H2O2 [113, 114]. This accumulation triggers lipid peroxidation, which compromises the membrane structure of fruit; consequently, cell membrane integrity progressively declines throughout senescence. Elevated ROS levels not only activate hydrolytic enzymes but also induce damage to proteins, nucleic acids, and membrane lipids [115]. Malondialdehyde (MDA), generated as a secondary end product of lipid peroxidation, serves as a critical indicator of membrane integrity and cellular damage [106, 116, 117]. Current findings indicate that the most successful treatments for curbing MDA accumulation were 1 mM SA + MAP for the 'Gina' cultivar and 3 mM SA + MAP for 'Gevaş', respectively. This effect is attributed to the capacity of Salicylic Acid to enhance antioxidant activity and improve cell membrane integrity [60]. These findings are consistent with recent studies conducted on grapes [106], mushrooms [62], melons [18], and blackberries [107].

Taken together, the present findings indicate that the beneficial influence of SA + MAP on postharvest bean physiology did not arise from a single process, but from the interaction of respiration, ethylene, oxidative status, and organic acid profiles. Under passive MAP, the altered in-package atmosphere likely reduced metabolic demand by slowing respiration, in line with previous reports showing that modified atmospheres can suppress respiratory activity and retard senescence-related deterioration in fresh produce [118]. SA may have further strengthened this response by restricting ethylene biosynthesis and weakening ethylene-associated ripening and senescence processes [119]. The higher SOD, CAT, and APX activities observed under SA + MAP, together with lower MDA accumulation, are also consistent with enhanced ROS-detoxifying capacity and improved membrane protection, as reported in other SA-treated sand pear fruit [120]. In addition, previous studies have shown that SA can regulate organic acid metabolism, while ethylene inhibition can delay the depletion of major acids and help preserve fruit acidity during storage [96, 97]. Accordingly, the cultivar-dependent patterns observed here suggest that SA + MAP delayed senescence through a coordinated mechanism involving metabolic deceleration, attenuation of ethylene-mediated ripening, improved oxidative stress management, and a consequent reshaping of organic acid turnover during storage.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that varying doses of SA, passive MAP, and their combinations (SA + MAP) effectively regulated the postharvest physiology of 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' bean varieties, thereby improving their storage potential. Specifically, in the 'Gina' variety, the 1 mM SA + MAP treatment suppressed external ethylene production and respiration, modulated headspace gas composition, and maintained organic acids (malic, citric, fumaric, and oxalic acids). Furthermore, this treatment favourably modulated antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, SOD and APX) and MDA accumulation. Conversely, in the 'Gevaş' variety, the 3 mM SA + MAP treatment proved most effective in curtailing respiration and ethylene production, while significantly influencing oxalic acid content, antioxidative enzymes, and MDA levels. Overall, the 1 mM SA + MAP and 3 mM SA + MAP treatments provided robust protection for 'Gina' and 'Gevaş' varieties, respectively, for a duration of 25 days. These differences suggest that the response to SA + MAP was cultivar-dependent and may be associated with inherent differences in the physiological and biochemical characteristics of the ‘Gina’ and ‘Gevaş’ cultivars during storage, possibly reflecting differences in cultivar-specific metabolic responses and genetic background. These findings highlight the efficacy of combining SA with MAP as a promising postharvest strategy for bean preservation. From a practical perspective, the combined SA + MAP approach may represent a feasible postharvest strategy for green bean storage and distribution, since MAP is already widely used in commercial handling systems and SA can be applied through relatively simple postharvest treatments. However, further validation under commercial-scale conditions, including cost–benefit evaluation and integration into existing supply chain practices, is still needed before large-scale application. Future research incorporating diverse cultivars and dosage concentrations will further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these effects on postharvest physiology.
