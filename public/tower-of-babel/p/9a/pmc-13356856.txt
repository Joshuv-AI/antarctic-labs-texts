From Systemic Autoimmunity to the Local Immune Niche of the Synovium: New Insights into the Development of Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Minghao Wang, Hua Li, Zhiqiang Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is commonly regarded as a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease driven by systemic immune dysregulation. However, this view does not fully explain why inflammation preferentially localizes to the synovium, or why patients differ in local inflammatory burden, structural damage, and treatment response. Recent advances in synovial biopsy, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial omics, and multimodal imaging have shifted attention toward the tissue-level mechanisms of RA. Current evidence suggests that RA develops through a continuous interaction between systemic autoimmunity and local synovial lesion formation. Systemic autoimmune abnormalities provide the initiating background for disease development, whereas the transition to persistent clinical synovitis appears to require the establishment and stabilization of a local synovial immune niche; however, current evidence does not support this niche as a sufficient cause of RA in isolation. Within this niche, stromal cells, myeloid cells, adaptive immune populations, vascular remodeling, extracellular matrix reorganization, and metabolic adaptation interact to sustain local inflammation. As these local programs consolidate, the lesion may shift from an inflammation-maintaining state to a destructive niche that promotes bone erosion, cartilage injury, and persistent remodeling at the bone-cartilage-synovium interface. In parallel, RA research and therapeutic strategies are moving beyond inflammation control alone toward a framework centered on local lesion formation, dominant pathogenic networks, and tissue stratification. Synovial tissue profiling, lesion-driven precision stratification, and multimodal integrative assessment may offer a more direct basis for predicting treatment response and guiding precision therapy. This review summarizes the key mechanisms linking systemic autoimmunity, local synovial immune niche formation, and structural damage in RA, and discusses the implications of these advances for future research and treatment strategies.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease that primarily causes pain and swelling in multiple joints. Its central pathological hallmark is synovitis, which, when sustained, drives cartilage damage and bone erosion and may also affect extra-articular tissues, ultimately leading to long-term functional disability and substantial disease burden.1,2 RA arises through the combined influence of genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, mucosal immune dysregulation, autoantibody generation, and systemic inflammatory network disruption.3,4 This framework explains disease susceptibility and preclinical immune perturbations, particularly the early appearance of anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) and rheumatoid factor (RF) before clinically apparent arthritis develops.5,6

Even so, systemic autoimmunity alone does not fully explain why RA lesions localize predominantly to the synovium, or why patients show marked interindividual differences in local inflammation, tissue destruction, and therapeutic response. Increasing evidence suggests that RA develops through continuous interaction between systemic immune abnormalities and remodeling of the local synovial microenvironment.7 The synovium functions as a local immune compartment with spatial organization, cellular heterogeneity, and considerable plasticity.8 Single-cell RNA sequencing, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing, multimodal imaging, and spatial transcriptomics have identified an immune niche within the synovium, characterized by distinct anatomical locations, cellular composition, functional states, and intercellular communication.9,10 This niche is likely linked to persistent inflammation, lesion persistence, and progressive tissue injury.11 Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) and heterogeneous myeloid cells actively participate in initiating and sustaining local pathology, rather than acting only as downstream effectors of inflammation.12,13

Recent advances in tissue-level analyses—including single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, multimodal imaging, and synovial biopsy—support a re-examination of RA pathogenesis beyond systemic autoimmunity.14,15 Although differences remain in cell subset nomenclature, functional assignments, and causal interpretation, the field increasingly emphasizes tissue-centered mechanisms that drive local synovial lesions.16 Against this background, this review focuses on systemic autoimmune dysregulation, the establishment of the local synovial immune niche, the major cellular and molecular networks sustaining chronic synovitis, their links to osteochondral damage, and the implications of these insights for precision therapy and future investigation (Figure 1). Literature Search

A literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies related to rheumatoid arthritis development, systemic autoimmunity, and the local immune niche of the synovium. The search terms were designed according to the aim of this review and included combinations of the following keywords: “rheumatoid arthritis”, “disease development”, “pathogenesis”, “systemic autoimmunity”, “synovium”, “synovial immune niche”, “autoantibodies”, “ACPA”, “rheumatoid factor”, “fibroblast-like synoviocytes”, “macrophages”, “T cells”, “B cells”, “neutrophils”, “dendritic cells”, “cytokines”, “chemokines”, “immune microenvironment”, and “joint destruction”. Boolean operators, including “AND” and “OR”, were used to combine disease-related, immune-related, and synovium-related terms.

Peer-reviewed original articles, review articles, and relevant mechanistic studies published in English were considered. Priority was given to recent studies, highly cited publications, and articles directly related to the transition from systemic autoimmunity to local synovial inflammation and joint damage in rheumatoid arthritis. Additional references were identified by manually screening the reference lists of key articles. Studies with limited relevance to rheumatoid arthritis, synovial immune mechanisms, systemic autoimmunity, or inflammatory joint destruction were excluded.

Systemic Autoimmunity: A Prerequisite for the Development of Rheumatoid Arthritis

The development of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is usually not abrupt, but unfolds through a progressive interplay among genetic susceptibility, environmental exposure, mucosal immune dysregulation, and gradual autoantibody evolution.17 Available evidence supports systemic autoimmunity as a major precondition for RA, particularly in anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA)-positive disease.18 Even so, systemic immune abnormalities alone do not determine whether RA will progress to persistent synovitis, nor do they adequately explain the joint tropism of the disease or the marked heterogeneity of its clinical course.19 Systemic autoimmunity can therefore be viewed as the immunological basis of RA, but not as a complete explanation for its full clinical and histopathological spectrum.

Genetic Susceptibility and Environmental Triggers: The Basis of Preclinical Immune Abnormalities

The emergence of systemic autoimmunity during preclinical RA appears to depend on long-term interaction between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure. Among known genetic factors, human leukocyte antigen-DRB1 (HLA-DRB1) shared epitope (SE)-associated alleles remain the most characteristic. These variants may facilitate the expansion of autoreactive T and B cells by lowering the threshold for immune tolerance, largely through effects on the presentation of modified antigens, especially citrullinated peptides.4,17,18 Environmental influences, however, are not independent of this genetic background. Smoking and occupational inhalational exposures may promote the initiation and amplification of preclinical autoimmunity, with stronger effects observed in individuals carrying HLA-SE alleles.20,21 Taken together, systemic autoimmunity in RA is better understood as a gradual loss of immune homeostasis in a susceptible host under sustained environmental pressure, rather than the direct consequence of a single exposure.

Mucosal Immune Dysregulation: From Local Interfaces to Systemic Autoimmunity

Mucosal immune dysregulation is increasingly considered an important contributor to RA onset and progression. The mucosal origin hypothesis proposes that sites such as the lungs, oral cavity, and gut are not only interfaces for environmental exposure, but may also represent key locations where RA-related autoimmunity first emerges and expands.7 At these surfaces, persistent inflammation, barrier impairment, microbial dysbiosis, and aberrant innate immune activation may together promote the generation of modified antigens and abnormal antibody responses, allowing local immune disturbances to develop into systemic autoimmunity. Findings from gut microbiome studies are broadly consistent with this view, although a definitive causal link to RA onset remains to be established.22 Preclinical RA autoimmunity is thus more plausibly interpreted as the cumulative consequence of chronic disequilibrium across multiple mucosal sites than as a process confined to a single organ.

Evolution of Autoantibodies: Core Phenotypes of Systemic Autoimmunity

ACPAs and rheumatoid factor (RF) are the most representative features of preclinical systemic autoimmunity in RA. Longitudinal serum studies indicate that these autoantibodies may appear several years before clinically apparent arthritis. In a serial blood-donor study of 79 individuals who later developed RA, 39 patients (49.4%) were positive for IgM-RF and/or anti-CCP before symptom onset, with positivity first detected a median of 4.5 years prior, ranging from 0.1 to 13.8 years. In the same cohort, anti-CCP and IgM-RF appeared a median of 4.8 years and 2.0 years before symptom onset, respectively.23 Another pre-symptomatic cohort showed that anti-CCP antibodies and RF isotypes predated RA onset by several years, and that the predictive value of anti-CCP increased as clinical onset approached, supporting the view that citrullination and autoantibody production are early processes in RA development.24

The ACPA response also undergoes qualitative maturation before clinical RA becomes evident. In a study of pre-RA serum samples from 79 blood donors, 53 individuals were ACPA-positive before RA onset. Among 25 patients who seroconverted from ACPA negativity to positivity, 72% initially reacted to a single citrullinated peptide, whereas the number of recognized peptides increased over time. ACPAs were detectable at low levels several years before diagnosis, with titres rising more markedly approximately 2–4 years prior.25 A multiplex autoantigen-array study of 81 future RA patients demonstrated time-dependent expansion of ACPA subtypes, indicating that the preclinical phase is characterized by accumulation of multiple autoantibody specificities and epitope spreading.26 These findings indicate that autoantibody responses undergo substantial immunological maturation well before clinical arthritis.

Beyond ACPAs and RF, studies of anti-carboxymethylated protein antibodies (anti-CarP) and refined analyses of citrullinated epitopes suggest that preclinical RA autoimmunity is unlikely to be defined by a single antibody specificity.27 Instead, it involves an evolving and partially overlapping network of autoreactive responses.28,29 ACPA-negative RA should not be regarded simply as an ACPA-positive form with one marker absent; it may reflect distinct immune triggers and disease evolution patterns.30 Collectively, these observations highlight substantial heterogeneity within systemic autoimmunity in RA.

From High-Risk State to Clinical Onset: The Limits of Systemic Autoimmunity

Although systemic autoimmunity is a critical prerequisite for RA, it is not sufficient on its own to determine inevitable progression to persistent clinical arthritis.31 Evidence indicates that a single serological marker has limited predictive value, whereas integrated assessment of symptoms, immune phenotypes, gene expression, and imaging-defined subclinical inflammation is more informative for identifying individuals at high risk.32,33 The high-risk phase is also not necessarily irreversible, as immune intervention may delay progression to clinical RA.19 Systemic autoimmunity therefore provides the immunological substrate for RA development, but the transition to persistent synovitis likely depends on additional mechanisms that trigger, establish, and sustain pathology at the local tissue level. This point also provides a rationale for focusing subsequent discussion on synovial lesions.

In rheumatoid arthritis, systemic autoimmunity may gradually evolve into local synovial inflammation through coordinated vascular, humoral, and stromal mechanisms. Endothelial activation within the synovium promotes leukocyte adhesion and trafficking, thereby facilitating the recruitment of circulating immune cells into inflamed joint tissues.34 Autoreactive B-cell responses and autoantibodies, particularly anti-citrullinated protein antibodies, may further amplify inflammation through immune-complex formation and myeloid-cell activation.18 Single-cell analyses of rheumatoid arthritis synovial tissues have identified distinct inflammatory cell states involving T cells, B cells, monocytes, and fibroblasts, supporting the role of chemokine-mediated immune-cell retention and local immune organization.35 In addition, synovial fibroblasts are increasingly recognized as active organizers of the inflammatory niche rather than passive structural cells; recent evidence suggests that specific fibroblast subsets can remodel the local immune microenvironment and promote proinflammatory T-cell responses.13 These findings support a stepwise model in which systemic autoimmune priming, immune-cell recruitment, autoantibody-mediated activation, and stromal reprogramming jointly contribute to the transition from systemic immune dysregulation to persistent synovial pathology6 (Table 1). Key Stages from Systemic Autoimmunity to Clinical Onset of RA

Establishment of the Local Immune Niche in the Synovium: From Inflammation to Lesion Establishment

Systemic autoimmunity creates the conditions for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) to develop, but persistent synovitis emerges only when these circulating immune signals are captured, amplified, and spatially organized within the synovium. The key transition is therefore mediated by vascular activation, chemokine-driven immune cell recruitment, immune-complex-related myeloid activation, and stromal cell reprogramming, which together allow the synovium to convert systemic immune dysregulation into a stable local lesion.

Structural Basis and Early Pathological Stratification of the Synovium

Under physiological conditions, the synovium is a highly specialized intra-articular membrane composed mainly of a lining layer and a sublining layer. Its principal cellular constituents include synovial fibroblasts and synovial macrophages, together with an extensive microvascular network, a limited number of pericytes, and scattered immune cells.36 Unlike many barrier tissues, the synovium lacks a continuous basement membrane. This relatively open architecture supports lubrication and nutrient exchange, but also renders the tissue more susceptible to vascular activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune cell influx under inflammatory conditions.37,38 The synovium should therefore be viewed not only as a target of inflammation, but also as a tissue intrinsically capable of supporting the initiation and expansion of local inflammatory responses.

Histological and single-cell analyses further indicate that the synovium is spatially organized rather than uniform. The lining compartment is enriched in fibroblasts and macrophages linked to matrix remodeling and tissue invasion, whereas the sublining compartment more commonly shows perivascular inflammation, immune cell accumulation, and lymphoid-like organization.35,39 In RA, synovial pathology extends beyond a simple increase in inflammatory cell numbers. It also includes vasodilation, angiogenesis, lining hyperplasia, fibroblast expansion, and patterned immune cell aggregation and redistribution.40,41 Distinct pathological states, including lymphoid, myeloid, and hypoinflammatory or fibroid patterns, can be identified across patients, suggesting that synovial heterogeneity is not merely a late secondary phenomenon, but may arise earlier and shape subsequent lesion evolution.42,43

Immune Cell Recruitment and Local Inflammation Initiation: From Entry to Retention

For systemic autoimmunity to progress to persistent synovitis, immune cells must first be recruited from the circulation into the joint. In RA, synovial endothelial cells play an active part in this early phase. In response to inflammatory cues, they upregulate adhesion molecules, chemokines, and pro-angiogenic mediators, thereby facilitating the adhesion, transendothelial migration, and local retention of circulating mononuclear cells, T cells, and B cells.44,45 At the same time, microvascular dilation and angiogenesis provide the structural basis for sustained inflammatory cell influx and further amplification of local inflammation.46,47

Immune cell entry into the synovium, however, cannot be attributed to endothelial activation alone. Current evidence suggests that endothelial cells, synovial fibroblasts, and myeloid cells together create a pro-inflammatory entry network.48 Once immune cells cross the endothelium, activated synovial fibroblasts can further reshape the local microenvironment through chemokine production, extracellular matrix remodeling, and modulation of vascular-associated signaling. This helps incoming immune cells acquire a resident and functionally active state within the synovium. Local inflammation is therefore established through coordinated interactions between structural and immune cell populations, rather than through the action of any single cell type.

From Inflammatory Entry to Lesional Establishment: How Persistent Synovitis Forms

Not every episode of immune cell influx into the synovium progresses to the persistent synovitis characteristic of RA. A critical question is whether self-sustaining cellular circuits and tissue remodeling programs become established within the synovium.49,50 In this transition, changes in the state of resident synovial cells appear particularly important. Under inflammatory conditions, synovial fibroblasts can shift from maintaining tissue homeostasis to promoting inflammation, cell migration, and tissue damage. In parallel, the homeostatic barrier function of synovial macrophages is compromised in active RA. Tissue-resident and barrier-associated macrophages decline, whereas inflammatory myeloid cells and circulating monocytes increase, converting the synovium from a relatively contained homeostatic interface into a persistently inflamed and permissive one.51

This shift implies that the transition from inflammatory entry to lesional establishment depends less on transient exposure to inflammation than on whether the local tissue has been remodeled into a state capable of maintaining chronic inflammatory activity. Synovial biopsy and transcriptomic studies further suggest that RA synovium may follow distinct pathological trajectories from early in the disease course. Lymphoid, myeloid, and fibrotic features do more than describe the current inflammatory state; they are also associated with treatment response, disease progression, and the likelihood that more intensive targeted therapy will be required.52 Persistent synovitis should therefore be regarded not as a simple extension of systemic immune abnormalities into the joint, but as the outcome of coordinated local tissue remodeling, cellular interaction, and pathological stratification.

The Synovial Immune Niche from a Single-Cell and Spatiome Perspective

Advances in single-cell and spatial omics have shifted the local synovial immune niche from a conceptual model to an observable tissue framework. Studies indicate that the RA synovium contains not only broad cellular classes, such as fibroblasts, macrophages, T cells, and B cells, but also multiple subsets with distinct functional states and spatial preferences. These populations are distributed across the lining and sublining compartments, perivascular regions, and immune cell aggregates, where they form discrete microenvironments. Within each of these settings, the extracellular matrix context, cytokine exposure, and cell-cell interactions differ, which likely contributes to differences in pathogenic potential.37,53

These approaches do more than confirm that the synovium is heterogeneous. They also suggest that particular niches may correspond to different disease states. In active RA, the myeloid-fibroblast network is markedly expanded. By contrast, in treatment-responsive disease or remission, specific tissue-resident macrophage populations and their spatial relationships with blood vessels and fibroblasts may partially recover.49,51,54 Existing studies remain largely cross-sectional, however, and consensus is still lacking on cell subset nomenclature, functional assignment, and causal interpretation. At present, the main value of single-cell and spatial omics lies in offering a framework for understanding the spatial organization and pathological stratification of local lesions, rather than in defining a fully unified model of RA pathogenesis (Table 2). Causal Position of Synovial Immune Niche Formation in RA Development: Necessary, Sufficient, or Permissive

Recent single-cell, spatial omics, and synovial biopsy studies raise a key question: should the formation of a synovial immune niche be interpreted as a cause, a consequence, or merely a correlate of RA? Current evidence indicates that the niche alone is not sufficient to initiate RA without systemic autoimmune predisposition. Autoantibody maturation, mucosal immune dysregulation, genetic susceptibility, and systemic inflammatory perturbations often precede clinically apparent arthritis, indicating that the synovium is not an isolated starting point.79 At the same time, systemic autoimmunity alone is not enough to produce persistent RA, because RA-related autoantibodies may exist for years without progressing to chronic synovitis.79 Therefore, synovial immune niche formation acts as a permissive tissue-level step that enables systemic autoimmunity to manifest as persistent joint disease.59

From this perspective, the synovial immune niche is conditionally necessary for established RA synovitis. Persistent clinical arthritis requires more than circulating autoimmunity; it also depends on local endothelial activation, immune-cell recruitment, stromal reprogramming, myeloid activation, adaptive immune retention, and microenvironmental remodeling within the synovium.55 Without these local changes, systemic autoimmune signals may remain preclinical or transient. However, similar inflammatory or stromal features will not cause RA unless they occur in the appropriate systemic autoimmune and genetic context.80

A hierarchical model clarifies the causal structure of RA development. Genetic susceptibility, mucosal immune dysregulation, and autoantibody evolution provide the initiating systemic background. Endothelial activation, increased vascular permeability, and early immune-cell recruitment trigger tissue entry.80 Resident synovial fibroblasts and myeloid cells amplify inflammation by forming self-reinforcing stromal–myeloid circuits.55 Once these circuits are established, T peripheral helper cells, B cells, plasma cells, and ectopic lymphoid structures stabilize the lesion through local antigen-dependent adaptive immune feedback.65,67 Finally, vascular, extracellular matrix, and metabolic remodeling provide a structural scaffold that maintains the niche and renders chronic synovitis relatively resistant to spontaneous resolution.55,81

This hierarchical model is not strictly linear. Once established, the synovial niche is unlikely to act only as a passive amplifier of systemic immune signals. Instead, it may actively feed back onto systemic autoimmunity by sustaining antibody production, promoting epitope spreading, and recruiting additional circulating immune cells. Thus, systemic autoimmunity and synovial niche formation form a stage-dependent positive feedback loop, in which systemic immune abnormalities initiate synovial entry, and the remodeled niche reinforces autoimmune persistence.

This framework helps distinguish different levels of causality. Systemic autoimmunity initiates susceptibility but does not by itself explain synovial localization.79 Endothelial and innate immune activation mediate the earliest local transition from circulating inflammation to tissue entry.59 Fibroblast–myeloid interactions amplify and organize local inflammation.80 Tph–B-cell interactions and ectopic lymphoid structures mainly contribute to maintenance, persistence, and local immune autonomy.65,67 Therefore, synovial immune niche formation is best viewed as a necessary tissue-level checkpoint for the transition to established RA synovitis, but not as a sufficient cause of RA in isolation.

Local Cellular and Molecular Networks Driving Chronic Synovitis

Once a local lesion has formed, the persistence of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) synovitis is not sustained by continued influx of any single inflammatory cell population. It depends instead on the emergence of a mutually reinforcing network within the synovium. This network, involving fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and B cells, not only amplifies ongoing inflammatory signals but also progressively stabilizes the lesion through cellular reprogramming, local immune feedback, and consolidation of the tissue microenvironment. As a result, synovitis becomes less dependent on transient peripheral triggers and acquires greater persistence and relative autonomy.

Fibroblast–Myeloid Cell Interactions: The Major Amplification Module of Chronic Synovitis

In the proposed causal hierarchy, FLS–myeloid interactions are unlikely to represent the sole initiating event of RA. Instead, they constitute a major amplification module after systemic immune abnormalities and early synovial entry signals have reached the joint.

In RA synovium, FLS can no longer be considered merely reactive stromal cells. They are now recognized as major organizers of chronic synovial lesions. Their pathogenic role extends beyond inflammatory mediator production and matrix degradation. FLS help sustain local inflammatory programs through state transitions, spatial positioning, and persistent cellular interactions.56 Under prolonged inflammatory stimulation, they adopt pathological states with distinct functional biases. Across different synovial microenvironments, these states contribute to inflammation, matrix remodeling, invasion, and tissue expansion.55 Chronic synovitis is therefore not defined simply by the accumulation of inflammatory cells, but also by the acquisition of inflammation-sustaining capacity within the stromal compartment.

Macrophages and monocytes form the other major component of this tissue core. In active RA, synovial myeloid changes are not limited to increased cell numbers. Their functional states are also persistently shifted, with expansion of pro-inflammatory monocyte–macrophage populations and relative impairment of homeostatic protective programs.60 These cells release mediators such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which maintain FLS activation. In turn, pathogenic FLS produce interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), and other chemotactic factors that recruit and reprogram myeloid cells. This reciprocal interaction sustains inflammatory amplification, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling.61 Thus, the FLS–myeloid feedback circuit is a central mechanism by which synovitis shifts from active inflammation to persistent tissue pathology.62

This axis should not be interpreted to mean that all mesenchymal programs inevitably promote inflammation. Recent work suggests that the synovial stromal compartment retains considerable plasticity. Different fibroblast states may associate either with persistent inflammation or with inflammatory resolution. FLS are therefore not simply a collective label for pathogenic cells. Rather, they represent a regulatory hub that influences whether a local lesion becomes chronic or, at least in part, returns toward homeostasis.82

Local Adaptive Immune Feedback Loop: The Tph–B-Cell Axis as a Maintenance Mechanism

Interactions between stromal and myeloid cells alone do not fully explain why RA synovitis can persist over time, reactivate repeatedly, and in some patients show striking chronic stability. A deeper layer of this process is the gradual establishment of a relatively autonomous adaptive immune loop within the lesion, which turns the synovium from a site of inflammatory response into a tissue environment where lymphocyte clonal expansion, antigen recognition, and effector maintenance can continue locally.65,66,68 Within the causal hierarchy of RA synovitis, the Tph–B-cell axis is therefore better interpreted as a maintenance and stabilization mechanism rather than as the primary initiating event. Its key contribution is to sustain local adaptive immunity, reinforce B-cell maturation and plasma-cell differentiation, and promote persistence of antigen-dependent immune responses within the synovial lesion.

Within this context, T peripheral helper (Tph) cells have attracted particular attention. Recent studies indicate that Tph cells are not simply peripheral equivalents of follicular helper T cells, but rather B-cell helper populations adapted to inflamed tissue environments, where they can continuously support B-cell activation, differentiation, and antibody responses within ectopic inflammatory sites.65 Further work suggests that synovial Tph cells in RA are themselves heterogeneous, with subpopulations that differ in spatial localization and functional bias. Stem-like Tph cells appear more closely linked to sustained replenishment and maintenance of the response, whereas effector Tph cells are more directly involved in local inflammatory amplification and B-cell help. This pattern argues against a transient role for Tph-associated responses and instead supports their contribution to the ongoing operation of lesional adaptive immunity.66

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis combined with immune receptor profiling has identified marked clonal and functional relationships among activated B cells, NR4A1+ B cells, plasma cells, and selected T-cell populations in RA synovium. These findings suggest that B-cell maturation and antibody production are not restricted to peripheral lymphoid organs, but can continue within the synovial tissue itself.66 When these local T-B interactions are coupled with ectopic lymphoid structures (ELS), the synovium may gain an even stronger capacity for self-maintained immunity. ELS are associated not only with enhanced local B-cell responses, but also with sustained autoimmune reactions directed against autoantigens derived from the synovial matrix, with potential consequences for inflammatory persistence and treatment response.68 Maintenance of adaptive immunity in chronic RA synovitis therefore depends not on isolated expansion of T cells or B cells, but on a closed local network involving Tph cells, activated B cells, plasma cells, antigenic sources, and supporting tissue architecture.66,68

Microenvironmental Consolidation: Long-Term Support from Vascular, Matrix, and Metabolic Programs

Beyond direct cellular interactions, long-term lesion persistence also depends on pathological remodeling of the local microenvironment. In other words, chronic RA synovitis is shaped not only by which cells are present, but by the vascular, matrix, and metabolic conditions in which they operate.83 As disease progresses, synovial endothelial cells and the microvasculature function as more than entry routes for immune cells; they become active elements of lesion maintenance. Under sustained inflammatory stimulation, the endothelium can adopt a phenotype that favors cell trafficking, increased permeability, and angiogenesis, thereby continually reinforcing inflammatory cell retention and local signal exchange.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) and spatial tissue architecture are likewise not passive background features. Recent studies of the RA tissue microenvironment indicate that the composition, mechanical properties, and spatial organization of the synovial matrix provide position-dependent cues to both resident and infiltrating cells, influencing adhesion, migration, survival, and effector differentiation.61 This means that even when certain inflammatory pathways are partially suppressed by treatment, lesions may retain substantial pathological inertia as long as the local cell-vessel-matrix scaffold remains abnormal. Chronic synovitis, in this sense, reflects not only the persistence of cytokine activity but also remodeling of an integrated tissue-level functional system.

Metabolic reprogramming provides a deeper molecular basis for this scaffold. RA synovium is chronically exposed to hypoxia, nutrient competition, and sustained inflammatory stress; under these conditions, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages, and T cells undergo metabolic adaptation to support inflammatory and invasive functions. Available evidence suggests that enhanced glycolysis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered lipid and amino acid metabolism are not merely consequences of inflammation, but also contribute to stabilization of pathological cellular states and persistence of local inflammation.83 More focused studies further indicate that Syntenin-1-related pathways can simultaneously reprogram inflammatory and metabolic programs in RA FLS and endothelial cells, thereby promoting angiogenesis, cytokine release, and local inflammatory expansion.84 Work on synovial macrophage state transitions likewise suggests that metabolic programs are closely tied to myeloid polarization, inflammatory amplification, and the capacity for inflammatory resolution.62 Metabolism should therefore be regarded not as a secondary by-product, but as an integral component of chronic synovitis maintenance.60

From Maintenance Networks to Pathological Stratification: Why Different Patients Follow Distinct Chronicity Trajectories

Once matrix-myeloid interactions, adaptive immune feedback loops, and microenvironmental scaffolds are in place, RA synovial lesions no longer behave as uniform persistent inflammatory tissues. They diverge instead along distinct trajectories of chronicity. Large-scale single-cell and tissue-integrated studies have shown that RA synovium can display different cellular abundance patterns and molecular subtypes; these differences do more than reflect inflammatory intensity, and instead point to variation in which component of the local maintenance network predominates.55 Some lesions are enriched in T and B cells and are marked by active local humoral immunity, whereas others are more strongly driven by myeloid programs or matrix-dominant remodeling. Together, these differences provide a tissue-level basis for the heterogeneity of local RA lesions.85

Spatial transcriptomic analyses further suggest that, in some refractory RA tissues, fibrotic-like fibroblasts and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)-associated spatial programs are markedly enhanced. This indicates that lesion persistence does not always arise from simple extension of the classical inflammatory axis, but may also involve pathological matrix remodeling and consolidation of fibrotic programs.82 Chronic RA synovitis is therefore better viewed not as a single endpoint reached through one pathway, but as a set of distinct chronicity trajectories shaped by the interaction and competition of multiple local programs.55

Overall, chronic synovitis in RA is maintained not by any single cell population, but by coordinated processes operating at three interconnected levels. First, FLS and myeloid cells form a self-reinforcing tissue core. Second, the Tph-B-cell axis, together with structures such as the ECM, supports an antigen-dependent adaptive immune feedback loop. Third, vascular, matrix, and metabolic programs provide the environmental scaffold required for long-term lesion stability.63,64 On this basis, a local inflammatory focus gradually evolves into a relatively stable, stratified, and difficult-to-reverse chronic tissue program, thereby creating the pathological conditions for subsequent osteochondral destruction (Table 3). Synovial Pathotypes/Lesion Programs and Their Clinical Implications in Rheumatoid Arthritis

From Persistent Local Lesions to Structural Damage: The Formation and Self-Perpetuation of a Destructive Niche

Structural damage in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is not simply a passive consequence of prolonged inflammation, nor does it arise in a linear manner from the action of any single cell type. Instead, it reflects the interplay among chronic synovitis, disturbed bone metabolism, and remodeling at the osteochondral interface. Once a local lesion shifts from a maintenance-oriented inflammatory program to a tissue-destructive program, joint damage tends to become more persistent and relatively self-sustaining.

Bone Erosion: A Key Transition from Chronic Inflammation to Structural Damage

Marginal bone erosion is a defining structural lesion of RA, and osteoclasts are its principal effector cells. Inflammatory signals continuously promote differentiation of monocyte–macrophage precursors into osteoclasts through multiple pathways, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL). These pathways also enhance the resorptive activity of mature osteoclasts.71,72 Bone erosion in RA should therefore not be considered merely collateral damage from inflammation. Instead, it reflects active reshaping of the bone microenvironment by inflammatory networks that drive pathological osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption.

At the tissue level, bone erosion develops mainly at the synovium–bone interface, rather than uniformly throughout bone. Pathogenic fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) express RANKL and interact closely with myeloid precursor cells, providing sustained local cues for osteoclast differentiation.57 In parallel, inflammatory monocytes show an increased tendency to convert into osteoclasts, and this monocyte-to-osteoclast transition appears intrinsically enhanced in RA.73

Recent studies have expanded understanding of osteoclast biology beyond the classical RANKL-centered framework. Osteoclast activation in inflammatory bone loss is regulated by coordinated influences from local cellular interactions, metabolic reprogramming, and alterations in the bone marrow microenvironment.72 For example, L-arginine metabolism can suppress inflammation-associated bone loss by reprogramming osteoclast energy metabolism, demonstrating that osteoclasts are not passive responders to inflammation, but pathogenic effector cells sustained by specific metabolic programs.74 Therefore, bone erosion in RA reflects not only inflammatory intensity, but also whether the local tissue has developed a pro-resorptive metabolic niche.

Impaired Bone Repair: A Dual Imbalance of Increased Resorption and Reduced Formation

The limited reversibility of RA-associated bone erosion is explained not only by enhanced bone resorption, but also by concurrent suppression of bone formation and repair. Earlier and recent studies indicate that the inflammatory environment inhibits osteoblast differentiation, bone matrix deposition, and repair of erosive lesions. These changes make structural recovery difficult once erosion has occurred.86,87 This impaired repair is closely linked to suppression of Wnt-related osteogenic signaling. Factors such as Dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK1) and sclerostin inhibit osteogenesis. The inflammatory environment can further reinforce these inhibitory signals, thereby maintaining RA bone lesions in a state of persistent damage and restricted repair.86–88 FLS also contribute to this imbalance. They promote not only bone resorption but also impaired osteogenesis through inflammatory mediators and signals that inhibit bone metabolism.71 Therefore, RA bone erosion is best understood as a dual imbalance, characterized by sustained activation of resorptive pathways together with suppression of repair pathways, rather than as a simple reduction in bone mass.

Humoral immune abnormalities may further contribute to this imbalance. B cells participate not only in resorption-related responses, but also in impaired bone formation. Relevant studies indicate that B cells in RA can directly inhibit osteoblast differentiation and thereby compromise bone repair.89 ACPAs have likewise been associated with reduced bone mass and progressive bone loss in some patients. This suggests that autoantibody abnormalities are not merely inflammatory markers, but may also participate in the early development of skeletal fragility.90–93 Taken together, bone damage in RA is not a late secondary consequence of inflammation. Rather, it reflects bone metabolic dysregulation jointly shaped by inflammation, matrix remodeling, B cells, and autoantibodies.

Cartilage Destruction: From FLS Invasion to Active Chondrocyte Destabilization

The mechanisms driving cartilage destruction in RA are not entirely the same as those underlying bone erosion. Current evidence suggests that pathogenic FLS are major effector cells in cartilage damage. By forming invasive pannus, secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS) family members, and maintaining direct contact with the cartilage surface, they actively promote degradation of the cartilage matrix.68 Cartilage damage is therefore not simply a passive by-product of synovitis, but a direct consequence of chronic synovial lesion expansion toward the cartilage interface.

Studies have shown that chondrocytes derived from RA tissue express higher levels of pro-inflammatory proteins and matrix-degrading molecules, suggesting that pathological reprogramming also occurs within cartilage itself.75 Single-cell sequencing studies have further identified distinct chondrocyte subpopulations in weight-bearing RA cartilage, with marked heterogeneity in inflammatory activity, metabolism, and matrix regulation.76 This suggests that cartilage destruction in RA should not be viewed solely as the result of external synovial invasion, but rather as a process involving both pathogenic FLS-mediated attack from outside and disruption of chondrocyte homeostasis from within.

Cartilage damage also does not necessarily arise only in advanced disease. Studies in early RA suggest that cartilage destruction may be associated with specific B-cell subsets, implying that adaptive immune abnormalities may influence the osteochondral interface at relatively early stages, in addition to driving synovial inflammation.77 Work on MMPs likewise indicates that these enzymes do more than mediate structural degradation; by cleaving extracellular matrix, receptors, and inflammatory mediators, they may further amplify local inflammation and tissue injury.78 Cartilage destruction is thus a multilevel process shaped by the combined effects of FLS invasion, chondrocyte destabilization, B-cell abnormalities, and matrix-associated enzymatic networks.

Bone-Cartilage-Synovial Coupling: Why Structural Damage Becomes Self-Perpetuating

From a mechanistic perspective, bone erosion and cartilage destruction in RA are not independent parallel events, but components of a coupled bone-cartilage-synovium system driven by chronic synovial lesions. Once this coupling is established, the lesion acquires stronger self-perpetuating properties. Neovascularization and an abnormal microenvironment provide sustained routes for molecular exchange and cell migration between inflammatory and stromal compartments, thereby accelerating pannus expansion and destruction at the osteochondral interface.96 At the same time, matrix degradation products and a persistently activated MMP network further amplify local inflammatory responses, pushing the tissue from a chronic inflammatory state toward destructive remodeling.78,94 This may help explain why structural damage continues to progress in some patients even when systemic inflammatory markers improve: once a destructive niche is established, tissue injury shows a degree of relative pathological inertia.95

From Inflammation Control to Lesion-Driven Stratification: Reconstructing RA Treatment Strategies and Future Directions

Taken together, the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is better understood not as a passive reflection of systemic autoimmunity within the joints, but as a dynamic process shaped by continuous coupling among the systemic immune background, persistent synovial lesions, and osteochondral destruction. From this perspective, the central therapeutic question is no longer simply how to suppress inflammation more effectively. It is also necessary to determine which local mechanisms are actively sustaining disease, whether these mechanisms have already driven tissue remodeling, and how treatment can be adjusted accordingly.97,98 Current guidelines still rely on treat-to-target principles and the stepwise use of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs). Although this framework has substantially improved overall outcomes, it does not fully resolve problems related to lesion heterogeneity, variable treatment response, and therapeutic resistance.28,82 RA treatment is therefore moving beyond an inflammation-control model toward a more lesion-oriented strategy.

Why Inflammation Control Alone is No Longer Sufficient: From Treat-to-Target to Disease-Driven Thinking

The 2022 update from the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) and the 2021 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guideline continue to support a treat-to-target strategy, with methotrexate as the anchor therapy and escalation to biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs when remission or low disease activity is not achieved.98 This approach remains the foundation of current RA management because it is supported by extensive randomized trials and long-term cohort data, and has clearly reduced inflammatory burden, improved function, and slowed structural progression.97 Yet, as discussed above, synovial lesions in RA are not uniform inflammatory entities. They are local niches defined by distinct pathological programs, cellular interactions, and tissue microenvironments. Against this background, the clinical observation of similar disease activity scores but different treatment outcomes is not unexpected; rather, it reflects underlying lesion heterogeneity directly.99,100

This also helps explain why the concept of difficult-to-treat rheumatoid arthritis (D2T-RA) has become increasingly important. EULAR has proposed both a definition and management principles for D2T-RA, emphasizing that it should not be reduced to repeated drug switching alone, but instead represents a complex state that may involve persistent inflammation, amplified non-inflammatory pain, comorbidities, adherence-related problems, and true multi-mechanism drug resistance.101,102 More recent reviews further argue that D2T-RA is best regarded as a highly heterogeneous clinical endpoint. In some patients, persistent inflammation remains dominant, whereas in others, ongoing symptoms are driven mainly by non-inflammatory factors. Treatment decisions therefore cannot be equated with simply moving to the next therapeutic target.99,100 The real limitation of current treatment frameworks is not necessarily a lack of available drugs, but the fact that conventional stratification still depends largely on peripheral inflammatory markers and prior treatment history. These measures do not fully capture the tissue-level programs that are actively sustaining pathology within the synovium. Precision medicine in RA should therefore not be narrowed to the search for a single serological biomarker, but instead aim to match therapy to the current histological state of the lesion, the dominant cellular networks, and the spatial programs present within synovial tissue.

Linking Synovial Pathotypes to Therapeutic Decision-Making: A Translational Rather Than Prescriptive Framework

At present, treatment decisions in rheumatoid arthritis are not routinely determined by synovial histopathology. Current management remains primarily based on treat-to-target principles, clinical disease activity, prognostic factors, serological status, comorbidities, safety considerations, patient preference, and prior treatment response.97,103 Therefore, synovial biopsy should not be presented as a mandatory procedure or as a tool that directly assigns a fixed therapeutic regimen. Its main clinical value is more appropriately understood as a translational framework that helps explain heterogeneous treatment responses and supports future patient stratification, especially in difficult-to-treat RA, clinical trials, and selected refractory cases.42

From this perspective, different synovial pathotypes may suggest different dominant pathogenic programs rather than direct treatment prescriptions. A lymphoid or B-cell-rich synovium may indicate prominent adaptive immune activation and local humoral immune responses, providing a biological rationale for considering B-cell-targeted or adaptive immune–modulating strategies in appropriate clinical contexts.43,69 A myeloid-dominant pattern, characterized by macrophage activation and cytokine-centered inflammatory circuits, may help explain better responses to cytokine-directed or intracellular signaling-targeted therapies, such as TNF, IL-6, or JAK pathway inhibition.43,51 By contrast, fibroid, stromal-dominant, or pauci-immune patterns may suggest that persistent symptoms are not driven solely by classical leukocyte-rich inflammation, but may also involve fibroblast activation, tissue remodeling, pain amplification, or structural damage.58 In such cases, the implication is not immediate selection of a specific stromal-targeted therapy, but more cautious evaluation of whether further escalation of immunosuppression is biologically justified.

Thus, synovial pathotypes should be viewed as an intermediate layer between mechanism research and clinical practice. They do not replace current treatment algorithms, but they can help clarify why patients with similar clinical disease activity may respond differently to the same therapy. This provides the logical basis for integrating synovial biopsy, non-invasive biomarkers, imaging, and molecular profiling into future precision-medicine strategies in RA.

Synovial Biopsy and Tissue Stratification: The Precision Strategy Closest to the Lesion

Compared with peripheral blood, synovial tissue more directly reflects the cellular composition, molecular programs, and local interactions that define the lesion itself. It is therefore more likely to reveal the mechanistic differences that shape therapeutic response. Synovial biopsy analyses from the R4RA trial showed that pre-treatment humoral immune and B-cell-related molecular features within the synovium were associated with response to rituximab, whereas patients with low B-cell signatures appeared to derive greater benefit from tocilizumab. Stromal or fibroblast-associated profiles, by contrast, were linked to broader treatment resistance.70 The importance of these findings lies not simply in showing that a higher abundance of a given cell type predicts response to a matching drug. More importantly, they provide some of the first systematic evidence that the molecular state of the synovial lesion can be linked in practice to mechanism-based drug selection.70 Recent reviews likewise suggest that the most promising biomarkers for future precision stratification in RA may not come from peripheral blood, but from synovial tissue, which directly reflects lesion status.104 Peripheral blood offers an averaged readout of systemic inflammation and immunity; synovial biopsy is closer to the tissue that treatment is actually intended to modify.

From Research Technique to Clinical Tool: Feasibility and Standardization of Synovial Biopsy

Available studies indicate that ultrasound-guided synovial biopsy has gradually become a safe, well-tolerated, and minimally invasive technique capable of repeatedly obtaining high-quality samples from both large and small joints. At present, the main barriers to broader implementation appear to relate less to technical feasibility than to operator training, workflow integration, and resource allocation.104

Evidence also suggests that the resource requirements for ultrasound-guided synovial biopsy can be quantified with reasonable clarity, and that its cost and procedural burden can be evaluated in practical terms. This means that evidence-based decision-making around synovial biopsy has moved beyond conceptual discussion and into the domain of health economics and real-world implementation.105 In parallel, the standardized training model released by EULAR indicates that the learning process, procedural steps, and sampling workflow for ultrasound-guided synovial biopsy can be standardized through structured training, which is likely to be important for future multicenter implementation and research consistency.106 The key organizational question is therefore gradually shifting from whether synovial biopsy can be performed to how it can be scaled while maintaining quality.106

However, these advances do not mean that synovial biopsy is ready to be used as a routine procedure for all patients with RA. Its broader clinical application still requires careful consideration of cost-effectiveness, patient acceptance of invasive and repeated sampling, operator training, tissue-processing capacity, and reproducibility of histological or molecular classification across centers. Therefore, at the present stage, synovial biopsy may be most useful in selected settings, such as difficult-to-treat RA, unexplained treatment failure, early translational cohorts, and mechanism-based clinical trials. A more practical approach may be to use biopsy as a reference standard in selected patients, while developing blood-based, imaging-based, and synovial-fluid biomarkers as less invasive tools for wider and repeated clinical monitoring.

In patients with refractory disease or persistent symptoms, synovial biopsy may also help distinguish cases driven by ongoing inflammation from those dominated by non-inflammatory mechanisms, thereby avoiding automatic escalation of immunosuppression in every patient with clinically complex disease. Recent studies of poly-refractory and refractory RA support this view: patients who appear similarly refractory in clinical practice may still fall into distinct subgroups, including inflammatory and non-inflammatory forms, and these groups require different treatment strategies.107,108 Histological information may therefore contribute not only to drug selection, but also to identifying which patients are unlikely to benefit from blind escalation of anti-inflammatory therapy.

Non-Invasive Alternatives and Multimodal Integration: From Direct Examination to Synovial Approximation

Although synovial biopsy provides the closest view of the lesion itself, future precision stratification will not necessarily require invasive sampling in every patient. A more practical direction may be to use synovial tissue as the ground truth and then develop surrogate indicators from blood, imaging, and synovial fluid that can approximate the intrinsic pathotype and molecular state of the synovium.109 In this sense, tissue-based and non-invasive stratification are not competing models. One defines the lesion directly; the other attempts to infer it.

Recent plasma proteomic studies support this direction. These studies suggest that the plasma proteome can distinguish individuals at risk from those with established clinical RA and may also help predict disease progression and, to some extent, treatment response, indicating that peripheral protein signatures could serve as surrogate readouts of lesion state.109 Such signals do not yet replace histology, but they point to a future in which precision stratification in RA is unlikely to rely on a single marker. An integrated framework combining histology, peripheral omics, and clinical phenotype appears more plausible. Imaging-based work on synovial pathotypes is also progressing. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound studies have identified differences in perfusion and enhancement patterns across synovial pathological types, offering an early basis for non-invasive approximation of tissue classification.110 The value of this work lies less in replacing biopsy altogether than in opening the possibility of a monitoring system that is less burdensome, repeatable, and suitable for longitudinal follow-up. Precise stratification in RA is therefore unlikely to mean biopsy for all; a more realistic model would integrate hematomic data, imaging, and clinical features, anchored by histological information in a subset of patients, into a tiered multimodal framework (Table 4). New Treatment Directions: From Empirical Drug Switching to Deconstruction of Lesion Networks and Immune Reset

RA lesions are not always maintained by a single inflammatory pathway. In many cases, persistence appears to involve fibroblast programs, adaptive immune feedback loops, microenvironmental remodeling, and the inertia of established structural damage. New therapeutic strategies should therefore focus not simply on adding anti-inflammatory agents, but on identifying and dismantling the local networks that truly stabilize lesions and contribute to treatment resistance. Repeated failure across multiple therapeutic targets does not necessarily indicate more severe inflammation; in some patients, it may instead reflect a mismatch between the dominant tissue-level programs and the mechanisms targeted by available drugs.107,108 This lesion-centered interpretation, however, needs to be clinically bounded. A mismatch between dominant tissue-level programs and available drug mechanisms may explain some forms of treatment resistance, but difficult-to-treat RA in clinical practice is broader than refractory synovial pathology alone. Apparent treatment failure may also reflect inadequate drug exposure or pharmacokinetic variability, immunogenicity, poor adherence, intolerance or safety-driven underdosing, and comorbidities that amplify symptoms or disease-activity scores. Fibromyalgia, centralized pain, osteoarthritis, obesity, depression, fatigue, and other comorbid conditions may contribute to persistent pain and functional impairment even when synovial inflammation is not the dominant driver. Therefore, before persistent symptoms are attributed to a refractory synovial lesion or used to justify further escalation of immunosuppression, clinicians should reassess the diagnosis, confirm inflammatory activity using clinical, laboratory, and imaging evidence, and evaluate adherence, drug exposure, pain amplification, and comorbidity burden. The lesion-driven framework proposed here should therefore be understood as a complementary mechanistic layer within the broader assessment of difficult-to-treat RA, rather than as a replacement for treat-to-target management or holistic evaluation of non-lesional causes of poor outcomes.101,102

In practical terms, the single-cell findings that most deserve prospective validation are those already connected to treatment response or lesion persistence. These include B-cell-rich humoral niches linked to B-cell-directed therapy, macrophage- and cytokine-dominant inflammatory programs linked to myeloid or IL-6/JAK/TNF-centered pathways, and stromal or fibroblast-associated programs linked to broad treatment resistance. Rather than implying that every newly defined cell subset should become an independent therapeutic target, these findings suggest that future studies should prioritize tissue programs that are measurable before treatment, change after effective therapy, and explain resistance when clinical inflammation and targeted mechanisms are mismatched. From this perspective, the most translatable targets are not isolated cell labels, but dominant lesion-sustaining circuits, especially adaptive immune niches, myeloid inflammatory circuits, and fibroblast-mediated stromal resistance programs.

In this setting, cell therapy and immune reprogramming have begun to enter the RA treatment discussion. A case report published in 2025 described early success with fourth-generation chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy in treatment-resistant RA, suggesting that deep immune reprogramming may be feasible in a very small subset of highly refractory patients.111 Another report from the same year described the use of CD19 CAR-T in polyrefractory RA, further supporting the translational potential of this strategy.112 Consistent with these early clinical signals, a 2025 review systematically discussed the theoretical rationale, possible advantages, and practical barriers of CAR-T therapy in RA, and concluded that it is more likely to serve as a high-level immune reset strategy for the very small group of patients who have failed multimodal therapy, rather than as a near-term replacement for existing standard treatment pathways.113 At present, this approach is better viewed as a prototype for future disease-reset strategies than as a mature routine therapy. In the nearer term, the more likely shift in RA treatment will probably not be widespread adoption of CAR-T, but movement away from empirical sequential trial-and-error and toward drug selection and combination based on lesion-dominant pathways. This is precisely why a mechanistic framework centered on the formation, maintenance, and pathogenic consequences of local lesions has acquired direct therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

The pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) can no longer be fully accounted for by a single framework centered on systemic autoimmunity. Genetic susceptibility, environmental exposure, mucosal immune dysregulation, and autoantibody evolution are all important prerequisites for disease development, yet they do not adequately explain why RA predominantly targets the synovium or why patients differ so markedly in local inflammatory burden, structural damage progression, and treatment response. In light of recent advances in synovial biopsy, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial omics, and multimodal imaging, RA is more appropriately viewed as the product of sustained interaction between a systemic immune background and the evolution of local synovial lesions. The establishment, persistence, and consolidation of these lesions represent critical intermediate processes linking chronic inflammation, pathological stratification, and tissue injury.

From this perspective, the central issue in RA is not simply whether inflammation is present, but how local lesions convert inflammatory input into persistent synovitis, progress into a destructive niche, and ultimately drive irreversible damage at the bone-cartilage-synovium interface. The conceptual framework for RA research and treatment is therefore shifting away from identifying systemic immune abnormalities alone and toward clarifying how local lesions arise, persist, and mediate tissue destruction. This transition not only deepens understanding of the heterogeneity and chronicity of RA, but also provides a tissue-based rationale for lesion-driven stratification, prediction of treatment response, and precision therapy. The synovial immune niche should therefore be interpreted as a tissue-level checkpoint that is conditionally necessary for the transition from systemic autoimmunity to established chronic synovitis, but not independently sufficient to initiate RA without the appropriate systemic autoimmune background. Future progress will likely depend less on expanding lists of inflammatory mediators or cell subsets and more on translating the mechanistic framework of local synovial lesions into clinically useful stratification tools. In this regard, future approaches may integrate systemic autoimmune features, such as autoantibody status and inflammatory biomarkers, with synovial molecular pathotypes, dominant immune cell populations, cytokine and chemokine profiles, imaging features, and clinical phenotypes. Such multidimensional stratification may help distinguish patients in whom disease activity is more closely associated with systemic immune activation, persistent synovial immune niches, stromal remodeling, or destructive osteochondral interface lesions, thereby supporting more individualized therapeutic selection and treatment sequencing.

However, the clinical translation of this framework remains challenging. RA is highly heterogeneous, and the transition from systemic autoimmunity to local synovial pathology is unlikely to follow a uniform or linear course in all patients. Although synovial biopsy and spatial omics have greatly advanced our understanding of local immune organization, synovial tissue sampling is still not routinely available in many clinical settings. In addition, variation in sampling site, disease stage, prior treatment exposure, and analytical platform may affect how synovial immune features are interpreted. Many current observations are also based on cross-sectional studies or relatively limited patient cohorts, leaving the predictive value of synovial immune niches for treatment response, disease persistence, and structural progression to be confirmed in prospective studies. Future work should therefore focus on standardizing synovial assessment, integrating tissue-based findings with blood biomarkers and imaging indicators, and validating synovium-guided stratification in longitudinal clinical cohorts. Such efforts may help move the systemic-to-local framework from a pathogenic concept toward a practical basis for precision treatment in RA.
