Rethinking hypomagnesemia: diagnostic thresholds and metabolic implications in critical illness
Authors: Thanh Luan Nguyen, Van Phieu Duong, Hoang Ngoc Thao Duong, Phuc Tuong Pham, Van Hoang Nam Ho, Quang Dai Le, Cong Dang Tran
Published: 2025

The recent prospective study by Zwart et al. provides valuable new insights into magnesium homeostasis in critically ill patients [1]. They observed a precipitous decline in ionized magnesium (Mg2+) during the first 24 h after admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and proposed that this reflects redistribution into cells rather than true magnesium depletion. Magnesium (Mg), however, is not merely an electrolyte but a pivotal modulator of vascular tone, immune homeostasis, and cellular energy balance [2–4]. Its deficiency affects endothelial stability, metabolic efficiency, and inflammatory signaling, all of which are critical determinants of outcome in critical illness. These findings raise two key questions, namely through which pathways magnesium might be driven intracellularly under acute stress [3, 5], and how closely this shift is linked to augmented metabolic demand during accelerated glycolysis [6].

In this Commentary, we aim to broaden the perspective on hypomagnesemia by addressing three complementary issues. First, plausible mechanisms may link catecholamine-driven metabolic flux to intracellular magnesium sequestration [3, 7]. Second, existing evidence indicates that current serum magnesium thresholds may underestimate true deficiency [8]. Third, the metabolic importance of magnesium and its association with lactate dynamics support a more proactive, context-sensitive supplementation strategy rather than therapeutic hesitation [3, 4, 9].

We propose integrating two mechanistic threads, neuro-endocrine stress signaling and enhanced glycolytic flux, into a coherent model of magnesium redistribution. Under critical illness, sepsis, or shock, elevated catecholamines and stress hormones stimulate glycogenolysis and glycolysis in multiple tissues, enhancing aerobic lactate generation even in the presence of adequate oxygenation [6]. This metabolic surge increases the demand for magnesium-adenosine triphosphate (Mg-ATP) complexes, which serve as essential cofactors for key glycolytic enzymes such as hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase [2]. As intracellular ATP turnover accelerates, cells may sequester free Mg2+ from the extracellular space to preserve Mg-ATP homeostasis and sustain aerobic energy metabolism [3, 4], resulting in a rapid decline in circulating ionized magnesium.

The molecular framework of magnesium transport supports this hypothesis. The channel-kinase transient receptor potential melastatin 7 (TRPM7) is a key regulator of cellular magnesium permeability and responds to intracellular signaling cues, increasing Mg2+ influx during metabolic stress [5]. In addition, other transport systems such as SLC41A1 on the plasma membrane and the mitochondrial channels Mrs2 and SLC41A3 participate in maintaining intracellular and mitochondrial magnesium balance [3]. These transporters are closely linked to cellular energy status, suggesting that stress-related hormonal activation can rapidly alter Mg distribution between compartments. Hormonal and second-messenger pathways have also been shown to modulate Mg2+ flux across membranes through phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms [7, 10]. Within mitochondria, Mg activates key dehydrogenases of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and stimulates ATP synthase activity [3]. Collectively, these findings suggest that stress-induced hormonal cascades coordinate Mg transport to sustain oxidative metabolism and cellular energy homeostasis, explaining the transient decline in circulating ionized magnesium observed in critical illness.

Importantly, such redistribution may represent an adaptive rather than purely pathological response. This concept aligns with prior evidence suggesting that magnesium redistribution occurs as part of the acute metabolic stress response, enhancing glycolytic efficiency and ATP buffering during energy crisis, supporting short-term survival [3, 7]. However, prolonged or excessive intracellular sequestration could limit extracellular magnesium availability for cardiovascular, neuromuscular, and endothelial stability [2, 10]. This duality highlights the challenge of interpreting early hypomagnesemia and the need for dynamic rather than static assessment of magnesium status.

This mechanistic model generates testable hypotheses. The nadir of plasma Mg2+ may follow peaks in catecholamines and glycolytic markers such as pyruvate [6, 7], consistent with stress-related metabolic flux. Expression or phosphorylation of TRPM7 or related transporters might rise early in critical illness, and pharmacologic attenuation of adrenergic signaling could blunt the decline in ionized magnesium independent of supplementation. Parallel measurements of intracellular and plasma magnesium could confirm this inverse relationship.

While this framework helps explain the rapid fall in ionized magnesium, a second question concerns how hypomagnesemia is defined and interpreted in clinical practice. Zwart et al. emphasized redistribution rather than depletion, yet this interpretation should be viewed with caution when guiding therapy. Even when serum magnesium appears “normal,” functional magnesium deficiency may remain undetected because conventional diagnostic thresholds are set too low [8]. Historically, current lower limits of 0.70–0.75 mmol/L for total magnesium were established from population averages rather than outcome-based analyses and included symptomatic individuals [8]. As a result, these cutoffs were never validated against physiologic endpoints such as neuromuscular excitability or energy metabolism, leading to systematic underrecognition of deficiency. In a re-analysis of 3,894 symptomatic patients, Liebscher demonstrated that if a cutoff of 0.70 mmol/L is used, about 90% of truly deficient individuals are misclassified as normal, and even at 0.75 mmol/L, half remain undiagnosed [8]. These data indicate that magnesium deficiency can be clinically relevant well within the so-called normal range. A higher interpretive threshold may therefore better identify patients who would benefit from supplementation. Clinicians should integrate biochemical findings with the clinical context rather than waiting for serum magnesium to drop below arbitrary limits before initiating therapy. A normal total or ionized magnesium concentration does not guarantee physiologic sufficiency [2, 8, 10], as functional depletion may precede measurable declines in plasma levels and contribute to cardiovascular instability, neuromuscular irritability, and metabolic dysfunction. Recognizing this limitation could prevent undertreatment of a reversible condition.

Beyond diagnostic thresholds, the physiological role of magnesium in energy metabolism further supports timely supplementation. Magnesium catalyzes most enzymatic reactions involved in aerobic glycolysis and is crucial for the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which connects glycolysis with the tricarboxylic acid cycle [3, 4, 9]. Magnesium deficiency impairs the activity of thiamine pyrophosphate, which is formed through the interaction of Mg2+ and thiamine, thereby inhibiting aerobic pyruvate oxidation and diverting pyruvate toward lactate even under adequate oxygenation, creating a state of metabolic pseudohypoxia [9]. Moreover, magnesium also stabilizes adenosine triphosphate; nearly all intracellular ATP exists as Mg-ATP, the active form that powers cellular work [4]. When magnesium is depleted, oxidative phosphorylation becomes inefficient, and cells compensate through accelerated glycolysis, further increasing lactate production [3]. These mechanisms explain the association between hypomagnesemia (or may be low-normal magnesium levels) and hyperlactatemia in sepsis and other critical illnesses [11, 12]. A small clinical study has shown that magnesium repletion improves lactate clearance, shortens ICU stay, and reduces the frequency of lactic acidosis [13].

From a clinical standpoint, magnesium repletion should not rely solely on an absolute total or ionized magnesium threshold. Waiting until overt manifestations of severe hypomagnesemia, such as unexplained neuromuscular disturbances (confusion, coma, seizures, carpopedal spasm, or tremors), may represent a delayed therapeutic window [14]. Instead, treatment should be individualized and guided by the presence of biochemical or physiological disturbances plausibly linked to magnesium deficiency, including refractory hypokalemia or hypocalcemia [14], new-onset atrial fibrillation or other unexplained arrhythmias [15], and persistent hyperlactatemia despite optimization of hemodynamic factors. In such circumstances, even borderline magnesium concentrations may justify reasonable supplementation. However, when magnesium is not truly depleted, excessive administration may lead to accumulation and hypermagnesemia once magnesium is released from intracellular or bone stores, particularly in patients with impaired renal excretion [2]. Therefore, dosing should be carefully titrated to the clinical context and accompanied by close monitoring of magnesium levels and renal function.

Taken together, these observations suggest that magnesium deficiency may not simply accompany metabolic failure but may actively contribute to it. Recognizing magnesium repletion as a fundamental element of metabolic support could help bridge the gap between biochemical correction and physiological recovery. A broader understanding of magnesium physiology, encompassing redistribution, diagnostic thresholds, and metabolic function, may facilitate earlier recognition and guide more context-sensitive supplementation. Timely magnesium replacement, even in patients with low-normal concentrations, could help restore mitochondrial efficiency, stabilize lactate dynamics, and support recovery from critical illness. A renewed focus on magnesium may therefore offer a simple and physiologically grounded opportunity worthy of further investigation to improve outcomes in the critically ill.
