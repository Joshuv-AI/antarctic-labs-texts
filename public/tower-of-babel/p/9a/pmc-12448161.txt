Background

North American guidelines recommend oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) as adjunct therapy for hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) in patients whose condition may be influenced by hormonal fluctuations.1 For teens and young adults, making decisions about starting OCPs, even for noncontraceptive purposes, can be challenging given societal norms on OCP use.2 Limited data on which patients with HS are likely to benefit from OCP therapy further complicates these decisions.

In 2023, TikTok reported 150 million active monthly users in the United States, most of whom were young women aged 16 to 24.3 A large percentage of these users obtained health information from the platform, either intentionally (65%) or unintentionally (92%).4 To better understand how social media engagement may shape patient awareness and influence shared decision-making, we analyzed TikTok videos on HS care and contraceptive therapy that were accessible to US users in English.

Methods

Two naive accounts (last accessed 12/2024) were used to search TikTok for the top 20 videos on “hidradenitis suppurativa birth control.” Videos discussing both HS and contraceptive therapy were analyzed for reported benefits and side effects, along with the number of views, likes, and comments. Four reviewers categorized videos as positive, negative, or neutral. A second search was conducted using the same methodology, focusing on “hidradenitis suppurativa birth control side effects.”

Results

TikTok searches for HS and contraceptive therapy identified 10 unique videos. Of these, 3 were created by a self-identified medical professional (MP) and 7 by a non-MP. All MPs are identified as dermatologists. Video views, likes, and comments varied dramatically (Table 1). The most viewed and liked video was posted by a non-MP; however, overall, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of views, likes, and comments between MP and non-MP.

Overall, TikTok videos on HS were more likely to express negative perceptions of hormonal therapy (60%) than positive (20%) or neutral (20%) (Fig. 1). Positive videos emphasized potential benefits or detailed specific advantages of contraceptive therapy, while negative videos highlighted adverse side effects without mentioning benefits. Neutral videos presented contraceptive therapy as a treatment option without a distinct positive or negative stance. A higher percentage of non-MP videos expressed negative perceptions of hormonal therapy compared with MPs; however, this was not statistically significant (P = .067). There was no statistically significant difference in engagement metrics among videos with negative, positive, or neutral perceptions.

Discussion

The analyzed TikTok content, which discussed HS and contraceptive therapy, garnered over 2 million views and 3,100 written comments. Despite originating from a relatively small number of videos, the reach was broad. Notably, for this aspect of complex medical decision-making, videos created by dermatologists did not appear to attract more attention than those posted by nonmedical creators. While US congressional efforts to regulate TikTok have focused on national security concerns, there remains no clear guidance on how clinicians should address the growing influence of social media in shaping patients’ medical decisions and declining trust in medical professionals.5 Regardless of any single platform’s future, clinicians must adapt to navigating an increasingly complex information landscape—ensuring patients have access to reliable, scientifically rigorous information in spaces like social media.
