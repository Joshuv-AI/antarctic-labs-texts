BEAC Induction Regimen and Prior Thoracic Radiotherapy Increases the Risk of ASCT-Associated Pneumonitis
Authors: Elina Kaprio, Anna Jokimäki, Hanne Kuitunen, Joel Kontiainen, Tuomas Selander, Taina Turpeenniemi-Hujanen, Outi Kuittinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHigh-dose chemotherapy (HDT) followed by autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) has been standard of care in the treatment of relapsed and refractory lymphoma. Some severe toxicities are associated with this treatment modality. Pulmonary toxicity is one of these significant adverse effects and a potential cause of treatment-related mortality.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we report the incidence, risk factors, and outcome of treatment-induced pneumonitis in 286 lymphoma patients receiving HDT followed by ASCT.ResultsThe cumulative incidence of treatment-induced pneumonitis was 4.6% and occurred in 11/286 patients. Three months incidence rate was 2.6%. Most of the patients diagnosed with treatment-induced pneumonitis had received BEAC as HDT regimen. The risk of treatment-induced pneumonitis was higher if HDT-ASCT was given in later treatment lines. Also, a prior thoracic radiotherapy as part of first-line treatment was associated with higher risk for pneumonitis after HDT-ASCT. In 2 patients, the pneumonitis did not respond to high-dose steroid treatment.ConclusionThis study provides new information about the incidence of pneumonitis associated with HDT-ASCT and its risk factors, which might be useful to take into consideration during and after treatment with HDT-ASCT.

Introduction

High-dose chemotherapy (HDT) followed by autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) is considered as the established therapeutic modality for various hematological malignancies, particularly in the context of relapsed or refractory lymphoma. Extensive clinical evidence supports the efficacy of ASCT in enhancing both progression-free survival and overall survival (OS) across distinct lymphoma subtypes, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [1, 2], mantle cell lymphoma, primary nervous system lymphoma, as well as improving progression-free survival in follicular lymphoma, and Hodgkin’s lymphoma [3–5]. International guidelines recommend the administration of HDC followed by ASCT in either first-line or subsequent treatment lines, depending on the lymphoma type [6–8]. The data of chimeric antigen receptor T-cell treatments in the second and third line have changed the role of ASCT treatment, especially in patients with early relapses and refractory disease in the second-line treatment [9, 10].

Nevertheless, the HDT-ASCT treatment is associated with substantial acute and delayed onset toxicities, rendering it suitable only for eligible patients due to its considerable toxicity profile. Common acute toxicities associated with HDC regimens include pancytopenia, febrile neutropenia, mucositis, diarrhea, and, notably, septicemia [11–13].

A recognized and critical complication of HDT followed by ASCT is interstitial pneumonitis, which represents a significant contributor to non-relapse-related mortality post-HDT-ASCT [14, 15]. Retrospective studies have indicated an incidence range of 10–26% for non-infectious pulmonary toxicity after HDT-ASCT. In certain studies, it has been shown that the use of carmustine (BCNU)-containing regimens and the BCNU dose would be associated with elevated pneumonitis risk [16, 17].

This retrospective analysis was conducted with the primary aim of determining the incidence of pneumonitis among patients undergoing HDT-ASCT as part of their lymphoma treatment. Additionally, our investigation sought to identify risk factors associated with pneumonitis. As a secondary objective, we aimed to assess the impact of pneumonitis on patient outcomes, particularly its influence on OS.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection, Staging, and Treatment

This retrospective registry investigation enrolled patients diagnosed with lymphoma who underwent HDT followed by ASCT. The patient cohort was drawn from the medical records of Oulu University Hospital, encompassing a dataset of 286 individuals who underwent HDT-ASCT between the years 2000 and 2019. The administration of HDT followed by ASCT was not restricted to a specific treatment line.

Conducted in adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, this study strictly adhered to ethical and legal frameworks within the Finnish context. Given its retrospective nature, characterized by the non-intrusive analysis of existing medical records without direct patient interaction, the study protocol received approval from institutional authorities, ensuring compliance with national legislation.

Baseline clinical attributes, inclusive of details such as the HDT regimen, the treatment line of ASCT, and the potential utilization of radiotherapy (RT) within the thoracic region in any part of the disease track, were recorded from the hospital’s patient registry. Notably, RT was categorized as thoracic if any segment of the thorax region received irradiation.

Pneumonitis Characteristics

Routine clinical practice included monitoring of HDT-ASCT patients after the discharge from the hospital. The possible pneumonitis symptoms, like cough, dyspnea, fever, were monitored in follow-up visits. The patients were also encouraged to contact the hospital in case of any new symptoms. High-resolution computed tomography of lungs was performed for every patient presenting symptoms indicating pneumonitis. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed by a pulmonologist in all patients presenting with pulmonary symptoms to exclude infectious causes. Measurement of the diffusion capacity and spirometry parameters were also prescribed. Over the study period of almost 20 years, no major changes occurred in the diagnostic principles of pneumonitis. Diminished diffusion capacity and spirometry parameters were not included in the diagnostic criteria. They only provided additional support for the diagnosis. The therapeutic approach to pneumonitis involved in all cases the administration of high-dose oral corticosteroid prednisolone, with a median daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Those not responding to steroid were treated with other immunosuppressive agents.

Statistical Analysis

The primary objective was to evaluate the incidence of ASCT-related pneumonitis and to discover which clinical features associated with a higher risk of developing pneumonitis. The secondary objective was to evaluate if pneumonitis had an effect on patients’ OS. Categorical variables underwent analysis employing the two-sided Pearson chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Cox regression was used to investigate the effect of several clinical factors on the risk of pneumonitis. Survival analyses with corresponding p values were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test. R (R Core Team 2023) and IBM SPSS 29 were used for statistical analysis and p values <0.05 were considered to be significant.

Pneumonitis Incidence

The comprehensive cumulative incidence of pneumonitis within the initial year post-transplant was determined to be 4.6%, manifesting in 11 cases among the 286 patients included in the study cohort. The median onset of pneumonitis occurred at 68 days (range: 49–277 days) after ASCT, as illustrated in Figure 1. According to the Kaplan-Meier analysis, the risk for the pneumonitis was 2.6% in first 3 months and the incidence of pneumonitis was highest within the first month after ASCT (5/11 cases).

Risk Factors of Pneumonitis

The majority of patients received BEAM or BEAC as HDT regimen. In 10/11 pneumonitis cases, the HDT treatment was BEAC regimen, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.046). Additionally, a pronounced elevation in the risk of pneumonitis was observed when ASCT was administered in later treatment lines (p < 0.001). Conversely, age and gender were not identified as significant risk factors for pneumonitis. Analysis of potential risk factors revealed that prior RT in the thoracic area, as part of the patient’s first-line treatment, emerged as a significant risk factor for pneumonitis (p = 0.002), as detailed in Table 1.

In the univariate analysis, age (p = 0.816), gender (p = 0.306), and diagnosis (p = 0.225) were not identified as significant risk factors for pneumonitis. In contrast, ASCT administered in later treatment lines (p < 0.001), thoracic RT (p = 0.002), and the use of BEAC as a HDT regimen (p = 0.045) remained significant risk factors in the univariate analysis.

The multivariate analysis included variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis. Upon engaging in multivariate analysis, the presence of prior thoracic RT and the administration of ASCT in later treatment lines emerged as notable factors significantly associated with an increased risk of pneumonitis (Table 2). The use of BEAC as HDT regimen was a significant risk factor of pneumonitis in univariate analysis. However, the HDT regimen could not be subjected to multivariate analysis due to the near-uniform utilization of the BEAC regimen in all but one of the cases with pneumonitis. Therefore, it remains unclear whether BEAC represents an independent risk factor.

Pneumonitis Treatment and Outcome

All patients received oral corticosteroid prednisolone as the primary therapeutic intervention for pneumonitis, with a median dosage of 1 mg/kg. The duration of corticosteroid treatment was several months across all cases. In instances where pneumonitis persisted despite high-dose corticosteroid therapy, 2 patients were identified as having a steroid-refractory pneumonitis. The alternative salvage treatments employed for these refractory cases included cyclosporine, initiated at a dosage of 50 mg twice daily, subsequently escalated to 75 mg twice daily in 1 case, and azathioprine for a duration of 5 months in the other patient. Both patients with steroid-refractory pneumonitis ultimately survived.

Remarkably, no fatalities were attributed to pneumonitis, and the presence of pneumonitis did not have a statistically significant impact on either OS (p = 0.51, HR 0.84, shown in Fig. 2) or lymphoma-specific OS (p = 0.65, HR 1.6, shown in Fig. 3) within the patient cohort. OS could not be estimated for a subset of patients as their diagnosis or post-ASCT follow-up may have occurred at another institution.

Discussion

This article describes the incidence and associated risk factors of treatment-induced pneumonitis in the context of HDT-ASCT. The utilization of HDT-ASCT in relapsed and refractory lymphoma became a standard practice following the Parma trial in 1995, which demonstrated the OS benefit of ASCT in non-Hodgkin lymphoma [1]. According to the 2022 publication from the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT), over 7,800 lymphoma patients in Europe underwent ASCT [18].

HDT-ASCT is linked to a heightened risk of adverse effects, necessitating its administration exclusively to eligible patients. Acute toxicities associated with HDT-ASCT include mucositis, nausea, diarrhea, febrile neutropenia, and septicemia [11–13].

The incidence of pneumonitis related to HDT-ASCT has been reported to reach up to 26% in previous studies [16, 19]. In the current study, 11 out of 286 patients experienced pneumonitis, resulting in a cumulative incidence of 4.6%, comparatively lower than previously reported.

Literature on pulmonary toxicity caused by BCNU dates back several decades and it is a well-known complication of the treatment [20, 21]. The use and the high dose of BCNU in HDT regimens is associated with elevated pulmonary toxicity [16, 22]. A higher BCNU dose >300 mg/m2 was considered a risk factor of pulmonary toxicity in a retrospective study by Chen et al. [17] in 2015 and patients treated with lower BCNU dose (<300 mg/m2) had better outcome in DLBCL and follicular lymphoma compared to patients receiving higher BCNU dose. In our hospital, the average BCNU dose as a part of HDT regimen is 300 mg/m2.

The choice between BEAC (132 patients) and BEAM (82 patients) regimens in systemic lymphomas at Oulu University Hospital is often made based on the clinician’s judgment, toxicity profile, and patient comorbidities. During the study period, the BEAC regimen was more frequently selected at the clinician’s discretion. Following the study period, according to subsequent clinical practice reports, the use of the BEAM regimen has become more common. There are no prospective trials comparing the efficacy and toxicity of these two regimens. One retrospective analysis has pointed out that BEAM might be more toxic in terms of gastrointestinal toxicity. Otherwise, the toxicity profile and efficacy seem to be similar [11, 12].

90Ibritumomab tiuxetan (90Y-IT, Zevalin®) combined with BEAM or BEAC was also used as HDC regimen in 13 patients until a randomized trial published in 2018 by Chahoud et al. [23] demonstrated that the addition of 90Y-IT offered no survival benefit compared to BEAM alone. This combination is not in common use anymore. Common adverse effects associated with 90Ibritumomab tiuxetan include infections and cytopenias, whereas pneumonitis is not considered as common 90Ibritumomab tiuxetan-related toxicity [24].

In primary nervous system lymphoma, the HDC-regimen of choice consists of central nervous system penetrating agents, mostly thiotepa and BCNU. Busulfan-containing regimen was given to 1 patient [25].

BCNU is included in both the BEAM and BEAC regimens; the key difference lies in the final agent, with melphalan in BEAM and cyclophosphamide in BEAC. In this study, BEAC regimen was associated with a nearly 10% risk of pneumonitis, with Z-BEAM resulting in a single case of pneumonitis in a patient with DLBCL. None of the BEAM-treated patients had pneumonitis. Pulmonary toxicity is a well-known complication of BCNU treatment. Literature also describes lung toxicity associated with cyclophosphamide [26–28]. There are studies suggesting that BCNU in combination with cyclophosphamide could augment the possibility of pulmonary toxicity [19, 21]. These reports are in line with our data pointing out an association between pneumonitis and the BEAC regimen.

Thoracic RT as a part of first-line treatment emerged as a significant risk factor for pneumonitis (p = 0.002) in the univariate analysis. In the multivariate analysis, patients who underwent RT in the thoracic area as part of their first-line treatment had a 7-fold (95% CI 1.43–33.97, p = 0.016) increased risk of developing pneumonitis. Among the 11 patients diagnosed with pneumonitis, 3 patients had a history of thoracic RT. Notably, 1 of these patients received HDT-ASCT in the first line, while the remaining 2 patients underwent HDT-ASCT in the second and third treatment lines. These findings are in line with the results of a study conducted by Lane et al. [16] in 2012, which similarly identified mediastinal RT as a risk factor for the development of pneumonitis. However, as only 3 cases were observed, this finding does not allow definitive conclusions.

Also, later treatment line during ASCT was considered a risk factor of pneumonitis. The majority of pneumonitis cases occurred in patients treated with HDT-ASCT in second or later line of treatment. In multivariate analysis, the risk of pneumonitis was 4-fold (95% CI 0.47–37.10, p = 0.2) when ASCT was given in second line, 24-fold (95% CI 2.26–246.60, p = 0.009) in third line, and up to 76-fold (95% CI 5.55–1,039.10, p = 0.001) in lines 4–7. This suggests that possibly patients who have received more treatments in previous lines are more susceptible to treatment side effects.

All the patients diagnosed with treatment-induced pneumonitis received per oral prednisolone as their treatment with median dose of 1 mg/kg. In 2 of these patients, the response to steroid treatment was not sufficient, and these cases were considered as steroid refractory. These patients received azathioprine and cyclosporine as salvage therapy and both patients recovered from pneumonitis.

Corticosteroids are widely recognized as the cornerstone in the management of treatment- or drug-induced pneumonitis [20, 29]. Steroid-refractory pneumonitis cases are relatively rare, and the literature addressing optimal treatment approaches in such cases remains limited. The widespread use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in the treatment of solid malignancies has contributed to an increased incidence of drug-induced pneumonitis, thereby escalating the number of cases refractory to steroids.

In cases of steroid-refractory pneumonitis, the prevailing recommendation is to employ immunosuppressive therapies such as azathioprine and infliximab [30]. However, the optimal dosage, timing, and duration of such treatments remain unclear [31]. In general, a drug-induced pneumonitis can be treated in the same way as a pneumonitis related to immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment.

The outcome and toxicity of HDT-ASCT treatment is affected by several factors. In this study, the patients prior smoking or comorbidities were not recorded. In a study by Duque-Afonso et al. [32], the OS for patients with prior lung diseases was decreased after receiving HDT-ASCT. Also, induction treatment with pulmotoxic agents prior to HDT-ASCT may have an effect on patient’s outcome [33].

The limitations of this study are the retrospective study design and heterogenous patient population and relatively small amount of pneumonitis cases, leaving room for a chance to affect the results. This study had, however, strict criteria for pneumonitis and accurately assessed diagnosis, which is an advantage. We find that our study brings up new information about the risk factors of pneumonitis that could even have clinical significance when deciding on HDT regimen for a particular patient.

Statement of Ethics

This retrospective registry study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and its conduct conformed to the relevant Finnish ethical and legal frameworks. According to Finnish laws and legislations, Ethical Committee approval is not needed for registry studies. Instead, registry studies, which do not interfere with patients treatment and the patients are not contacted, are approved by institutions authorities. Ethical approval was not needed for this study in accordance with national guidelines. The retrospective and non-interventional character of the study negated the necessity for informed consent in compliance with national legislation.
