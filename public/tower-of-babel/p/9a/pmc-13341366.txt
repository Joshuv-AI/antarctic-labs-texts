Impact of Removing Restrictive Haemoglobin A1C Eligibility Criteria for Insulin Pump Therapy Funding for Children With Type 1 Diabetes in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Alanna Weisman, Karl Everett, Peter C Austin, Rayzel Shulman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsIn Ontario, publicly funded insulin pump therapy has been available to children with Type 1 diabetes since 2006. Before 31 March 2022, eligibility required three Haemoglobin A1c (A1C) measurements in the preceding year, with the two most recent < 10%. On 1 April 2022, this criterion was removed. We assessed whether this policy change reduced socioeconomic disparities in pump access among new applicants.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a population‐based retrospective cohort study including all children in Ontario who applied for insulin pump funding from program inception to 31 March 2024. The primary exposure was the time period (pre‐ vs. post‐policy change). The outcome was social disadvantage, measured using a five‐level neighbourhood index. Ordinal logistic regression with generalised estimating equations (accounting for neighbourhood clustering) estimated the odds of greater disadvantage post‐policy, adjusting for age, sex and diabetes duration. Segmented regression assessed changes over time in differences in application proportions between the most and least disadvantaged quintiles.ResultsA total of 7540 children applied pre‐policy and 1311 post‐policy. The mean A1C at application was similar between time periods (8.5% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.0002). Adjusted odds of greater disadvantage were higher post‐policy (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.18–1.47). The difference in applications between the most versus least disadvantaged quintile decreased by 0.13%/quarter (95% CI: −0.22 to −0.05) pre‐policy and by 1.64%/quarter (95% CI: −8.3 to 5.0) post‐policy, though the pre versus post‐policy difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.1).ConclusionsRemoval of A1C‐based eligibility may improve equity in paediatric insulin pump access, though longer follow‐up is needed.Keywords: disparities, health policy, insulin pump therapy, Type 1 diabetes

Introduction

Insulin pump therapy for children with Type 1 diabetes has been publicly funded in Ontario, Canada, since 1 December 2006. The provincial programme covers the full cost of the insulin pump and provides $2400 annually towards pump supplies, distributed as $600 quarterly. Although the quarterly funding is intended to offset the cost of pump supplies, it is not a reimbursement‐based system and does not require submission of receipts. As such, patients may incur additional out‐of‐pocket costs if supply needs exceed this amount or for adjunctive materials, unless these are covered through private insurance. Eligibility for the pump programme requires both the patient and an authorised physician or nurse practitioner to complete and sign the application form. The healthcare provider must attest that the applicant has Type 1 diabetes, has completed structured diabetes education, attends clinic regularly and demonstrates appropriate diabetes self‐management; however, there is no standardised approach to how these criteria are assessed. Once approved, the insulin pump is shipped directly to the patient. Pump training is provided either by industry representatives or by clinic‐based diabetes educators with pump expertise, depending on local practice. Public coverage for continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)—a key component of automated insulin delivery (AID)—has expanded substantially in Ontario over time, including the introduction of publicly funded intermittently scanned CGM in 2019 and broader access to real‐time CGM for children beginning in 2022.

Despite the availability of public funding, multiple studies have demonstrated persistent inequities in access to insulin pump therapy in Ontario. Children experiencing social disadvantage—including those from lower‐income households or living in more materially deprived neighbourhoods—have been less likely to initiate pump therapy and more likely to discontinue pump therapy once initiated [1, 2]. These disparities likely reflect a combination of residual financial barriers, such as remaining out‐of‐pocket costs for supplies, as well as non‐financial barriers, including physician or provider gatekeeping, lower health literacy, competing social stressors and differential access to diabetes care supports [3]. However, it is also possible that restrictive haemoglobin A1c (A1C) eligibility criteria inadvertently exacerbated these inequities: before 1 April 2022, the application required submission of the three most recent A1C values, with the most recent two required to be < 10% to qualify for funding. This criterion was intended to mitigate the perceived increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in pump users with suboptimal glycaemic management.

Socially disadvantaged children are more likely to have higher A1C levels and thus may have been disproportionately excluded from accessing pump therapy in Ontario due to the A1C eligibility criterion [4, 5]. Given that insulin pump therapy, particularly AID systems, is associated with improved glycaemic outcomes and reduced acute complications, this exclusion may have contributed to worse diabetes outcomes among socially disadvantaged children [6, 7].

Beginning in 2020, the Ontario Paediatric Diabetes Network, which comprises 35 dedicated paediatric diabetes clinics, advocated for the removal of the A1C eligibility criterion based on studies that showed that children with the highest pre‐pump A1C levels have the greatest improvement in glycaemic management following initiation of pump therapy [8, 9, 10]. Members of the Paediatric Diabetes Network also expressed concern that the restrictive A1C criterion was worsening socioeconomic disparities in access to insulin pump therapy. The A1C eligibility criterion for funding was removed as of 1 April 2022. We leveraged this policy change as a natural experiment to evaluate whether the removal of the A1C criterion on 1 April 2022 was associated with a reduction in socioeconomic disparities among children applying to the insulin pump programme. In addition, we examined glycaemic and safety outcomes following pump initiation, with particular focus on children who had an A1C ≥ 10% at the time of application (i.e., those who were previously excluded).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a population‐based retrospective cohort study including all children (≤ 18 years old) who applied to the publicly funded insulin pump programme administered through the Assistive Devices Program (ADP) between 1 December 2006 (its inception) and 31 March 2024 (date until which data were available). This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely‐collected health Data (RECORD) guidelines [11, 12].

Data Sources

The study was conducted using administrative health care data available at ICES (formerly known as the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences). ICES is an independent, non‐profit research institute funded by an annual grant from the Ontario Ministry of Health (MOH) and the Ministry of Long‐Term Care (MLTC). As a prescribed entity under Ontario's privacy legislation, ICES is authorised to collect and use health care data for the purposes of health system analysis, evaluation and decision support. Secure access to these data is governed by policies and procedures that are approved by the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario. Parts of this material are based on data and information compiled and provided by the Ontario MOH. The analyses, conclusions, opinions and statements expressed herein are solely those of the authors and do not reflect those of the funding or data sources; no endorsement is intended or should be inferred. Parts or the whole of this material are based on data and/or information compiled and provided by Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC), current to March 2024. However, the analyses, conclusions, opinions and statements expressed in the material are those of the author(s) and not necessarily those of IRCC.

Databases used in these analyses include the Discharge Abstract Database and National Ambulatory Care Reporting Services for hospital and emergency department diagnoses; the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) Claims Database for physician service claims; the Ontario Laboratories Information System (OLIS) for laboratory data; ADP for insulin pump application records; the Registered Persons Database for sociodemographic information and the Permanent Resident Database for immigration status, which records immigration to Canada since 1985. These datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analysed at ICES.

Participants

We included individuals who submitted a new application to ADP to initiate insulin pump therapy on or before 31 March 2024 and were 18 years of age or younger at the time of insulin pump application. In Ontario, public funding for insulin pump therapy through ADP is exclusively for individuals with Type 1 diabetes. We were unable to determine from administrative data whether ADP applications were not ultimately followed by pump initiation. Children were excluded if their postal code or sex was missing; their diabetes diagnosis date was missing or recorded as being subsequent to their ADP application date; or if they were not eligible for the OHIP for 1 year before or subsequent to their ADP application date.

Exposure and Covariates

The primary exposure was the time period of the ADP application categorised as pre‐policy change (on or before 31 March 2022) and post‐policy change (1 April 2022–31 March 2024). Other variables included age on the date of ADP application; age at diabetes diagnosis (defined using the validated Ontario Diabetes Database [ODD] definition) [13], duration of diabetes, sex, most recent A1C before the ADP application date within the previous 2 years, hospitalisations or emergency department visits for hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia in the year before ADP application (yes vs. no), immigration to Canada since 1985 (the date from which immigration data are available) and rural versus urban residence.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was neighbourhood‐level social disadvantage, defined by the material resources quintile of the Ontario marginalisation index (ON‐MARG), with Quintile 1 being the least disadvantaged and Quintile 5 being the most disadvantaged. The material resources domain includes indicators from census data for the attainment of basic material needs, such as the percentage of individuals who are unemployed or lack a high school education [14]. Secondary outcomes included changes in A1C and the rate of diabetes‐related emergency department visits or hospitalisations following pump initiation (codes in Table S1). For the change in A1C, the first A1C value 90 to 365 days following ADP application was compared to the baseline A1C value as described above among individuals with both A1C values available. For diabetes‐related emergency department visits or hospitalisations, rates per 100 person‐years were calculated for the year before pump initiation and the year following pump initiation, with differences in rates and rate ratios calculated for differences between the two time periods.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were described using frequencies and percentages, or medians and interquartile ranges. Differences between time periods were compared using standardised differences, where differences > 0.2 were considered potentially clinically meaningful. We used two analytical approaches to assess whether the policy change was associated with changes in applicants' material resources quintile. First, we used an ordinal logistic regression with a multinomial distribution and a cumulative logit link function to evaluate the association between time period (pre‐ vs. post‐policy change) and the odds of being in a more disadvantaged material resources quintile. To account for neighbourhood‐level clustering (6513 clusters), we estimated the model using generalised estimating equation (GEE) methods with an independent working correlation structure. Both a crude (unadjusted) model and a model that adjusted for age, sex and diabetes duration were fit. Second, we used an interrupted time‐series analysis with a segmented linear regression model to generate linear predictions and evaluate changes in trends before and after the policy change. Specifically, we estimated the quarterly change in the percentage difference between the most and least disadvantaged quintiles (Q1–Q5) during the pre‐policy period, the immediate change at the time of the policy implementation (1 April 2022) and the change in slope in the post‐policy period compared with the pre‐policy trend. Specifically, the outcome was the difference in percentages between the most and least disadvantaged quintiles among all applicants. Although an ARIMA model was specified a priori, diagnostic assessment demonstrated poor model fit. SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA) was used for all analyses. The statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethics

ICES is a prescribed entity under Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA). Section 45 of PHIPA authorises ICES to collect personal health information, without consent, for the purpose of analysis or compiling statistical information with respect to the management, evaluation or monitoring of the allocation of resources to or planning for all or part of the health system. Projects that use data collected by ICES under Section 45 of PHIPA and use no other data are exempt from REB review. The use of the data in this project is authorised under Section 45 and approved by ICES' Privacy and Legal Office.

Results

Cohort Description

In total, 8851 new applicants to ADP were included (Figure S1). Of those, 7540 applied before the policy change on 1 April 2022, and 1311 applied subsequent to the policy change. Mean age at application was 11.3 years (SD: 4.1), and 4261 (48.1%) were females; neither age nor sex distributions differed between time periods. Diabetes duration at the time of application was longer before the policy change (3.55 years [SD: 3.3] pre‐policy change vs. 2.75 years [SD: 2.9] post‐policy change). The mean A1C did not differ between time periods (8.3% [SD: 1.5] pre‐policy change vs. 8.5% [SD: 1.9] post‐policy change). The proportion of applicants in each social disadvantage quintile did not substantially differ pre‐ versus post‐policy change, though 12.1% of applications pre‐policy change were for the most disadvantaged quintile compared with 17.0% post‐policy change. Despite the eligibility criteria pre‐policy change specifying the requirement for the two most recent A1c levels to be < 10%, 626 (8.3%) applicants had a most recent A1C ≥ 10%. Following the policy change, this increased to 214 (16.3%). All characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Table S2.

Interrupted time‐series analysis showed that the percentage difference between the least (Q1) and most (Q5) disadvantaged quintiles decreased by 0.13 percentage points per quarter during the pre‐policy period (1 April 2006 to 31 March 2022; β = −0.13, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.05; Figure S2; Table S4). There was no immediate level change at the time of policy implementation on 1 April 2022 (β = −0.02; p = 1.0). Following the policy change, the slope suggested a faster rate of gap closure (β for change in slope = −1.49 percentage points per quarter); however, this difference compared with the pre‐policy trend did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.10). Post‐policy, the slope was −1.64 percentage points per quarter (95% CI: −8.30 to 5.00; p = 0.60), indicating substantial uncertainty and no statistically significant change from zero. The wide CI likely reflects the limited number of post‐intervention time points.

Diabetes‐Related Emergency Department Visits/Hospitalisations Following Pump Initiation Pre‐ and Post‐Policy Change

Before pump initiation, diabetes‐related emergency department visits/hospitalisations were most frequent among applicants with an A1C ≥ 10% in both time periods (19.7 per 100 person‐years pre‐policy change and 23.4 per 100 person‐years post‐policy change compared with 12.7 and 12.2 per 100 person‐years for those with an A1C < 7%; Table 3). Although diabetes‐related emergency department visits/hospitalisations remained most frequent among those with an A1C ≥ 10% in the year following pump initiation, rates declined substantially compared with the year before pump initiation, and this was similar for both time periods (rates 12.1 per 100 person‐years pre‐policy change and 12.6 per 100 person‐years post‐policy change compared with 3.8 and 1.2 per 100 person‐years for those with an A1C < 7%); the rate ratio for those with A1C ≥ 10% was 0.6 (95% CI: 0.5–0.8) for pre‐policy and 0.5 (95% CI: 0.4–0.8) for post‐policy.

Discussion

In this population‐based study evaluating the impact of removing A1C‐based eligibility criteria for publicly funded insulin pump therapy in Ontario, we found that the policy change may have been associated with a shift towards fewer socioeconomic disparities among children applying for pump therapy. Following the removal of the A1C threshold, applicants were more likely to come from more socially disadvantaged neighbourhoods and had higher A1C values at the time of application, suggesting that the previous eligibility criterion may have functioned as a structural barrier to access for some children. Although socioeconomic disparities in pump applications were already narrowing before the policy change, post‐policy trends suggested a greater rate of gap closure, albeit not statistically significant over the limited follow‐up period. Importantly, children with A1C ≥ 10% at application experienced substantial improvements in glycaemic control and reductions in diabetes‐related acute care use following pump initiation, supporting the clinical safety and benefits of expanding access to pump therapy among higher‐risk paediatric populations.

The observed increase in the odds of applicants from more socially disadvantaged neighbourhoods in the post‐policy period suggests that the prior A1C‐based eligibility criterion functioned as a structural barrier to access, disproportionately excluding children facing greater social and clinical complexity. Consistent with this, mean A1C at application was higher in the most disadvantaged (8.45% in Q5 vs. 8.24% in Q1), supporting the premise that higher A1C levels—more common among socially disadvantaged children—may have contributed to inequities under the prior eligibility criteria. Although the interrupted time‐series analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant slope change following policy implementation, this finding should be interpreted in context. Post‐policy follow‐up was limited to 2 years, reducing the power to detect changes in application patterns, particularly for equity‐focused outcomes that may emerge gradually. In addition, secular trends towards improved equity in diabetes technology access were already underway before the policy change, which may have attenuated the ability to attribute observed changes solely to the removal of the A1C criterion. Notably, despite the restrictive eligibility requirements in the pre‐policy period, 8% of applicants had a most recent A1C ≥ 10%. As the ADP does not independently verify clinical data and relies on provider attestation, this may reflect the use of exemptions or variability in how the criteria were interpreted and applied in practice. Such variability may have attenuated the measurable impact of removing the A1C threshold. Taken together, these findings should be interpreted as early, supportive evidence of improved equity in access rather than definitive proof of a causal policy effect.

We were unable to determine whether clinics implemented changes to staffing, onboarding processes, or waitlist prioritisation following the removal of the A1C eligibility criterion, as these decisions are made at the individual clinic level and are not captured in provincial administrative data. However, such factors likely play an important role in shaping equitable access. In the absence of coordinated system‐level efforts to prioritise individuals facing structural disadvantage, removal of restrictive eligibility criteria alone may be insufficient to fully address disparities in insulin pump uptake. Provider‐level factors, including implicit bias and structural racism within healthcare systems, may further contribute to inequitable access, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged and racialised populations who are over‐represented in more disadvantaged neighbourhoods [3]. Notably, disparities in insulin pump uptake have also been observed in settings without A1C‐based eligibility criteria, further highlighting that removal of restrictive thresholds alone is insufficient to achieve equitable access [15, 16].

The largest reductions in A1C were observed among children with baseline A1C levels ≥ 10%. Though we cannot distinguish pump therapy in manual mode from AID in our study, clinical trials have demonstrated substantial glycaemic improvements following AID initiation in adolescents with very high baseline A1C [17, 18]. These findings challenge historical concerns that high A1C predicts suboptimal pump outcomes. Notably, contemporary analyses of long‐term cohorts show that the excess risk of DKA associated with pump therapy has declined markedly since earlier decades and is now of low magnitude [19]. This may be due to lower risk with improved insulin formulations; advances in technology such as increasing use of AID; better recognition of infusion‐set failures; and enhanced patient education. Importantly, we also observed lower rates of diabetes‐related emergency department visits and hospitalisations following pump initiation among children with high baseline A1C, a group with the greatest burden of acute care utilisation before therapy. This pattern aligns with international evidence demonstrating lower admission rates and substantial healthcare cost savings with AID compared with multiple daily injections [20, 21]. Taken together, these findings support prioritising insulin pump therapy for children with higher A1C levels, rather than excluding them on the basis of poor glycaemic management.

These findings have important implications for publicly funded diabetes technology programmes. Removal of performance‐based eligibility criteria—such as A1C thresholds—may reduce socioeconomic inequities in access without compromising safety, aligning with growing international momentum to decouple technology access from glycaemic performance. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services in the United States have historically included eligibility criteria tied to performance measures, while the American Diabetes Association explicitly recommends that insulin pump therapy and AID not be restricted by age, A1C, or duration of diabetes [22, 23]. Specifically for AID users, numeracy skills have become less important, as multiple studies have demonstrated safety and improved glycaemic outcomes even without precise carbohydrate counting or, in some systems, without meal announcement [24, 25].

Our study has several strengths. We leveraged linked administrative data to capture all paediatric applicants to Ontario's publicly funded insulin pump programme over nearly two decades, minimising selection bias and enabling evaluation of a system‐level policy change using complementary analytic approaches. The long pre‐policy observation period and use of a validated neighbourhood‐level measure of material resources strengthened assessment of equity trends, while linkage to laboratory and acute care data permitted evaluation of clinical outcomes among higher‐risk applicants. However, our study also has some limitations. First, the post‐policy follow‐up duration was limited, reducing statistical power to detect changes in temporal trends and precluding assessment of longer‐term outcomes. Second, we were unable to evaluate individual social determinants of health and relied on neighbourhood‐level proxies. Third, we were unable to examine the use of CGM devices or whether insulin pump therapy was used manually or as part of an AID system. Finally, we were unable to examine insulin pump use as a proportion of the total population of children with type 1 diabetes or changes in A1C for the entire Type 1 diabetes paediatric population (pump users + non‐users) due to limitations in identifying non‐pump users within administrative data, which is a substantial limitation.

Although the A1C eligibility criterion evaluated in this study was specific to Ontario's insulin pump funding programme, the implications extend well beyond this context. Performance‐based eligibility requirements, even when well‐intentioned, may inadvertently function as gatekeeping mechanisms that restrict access to diabetes technologies for those who stand to benefit most, thereby widening existing socioeconomic disparities. As AID systems are increasingly recognised as the standard of care for Type 1 diabetes management, ensuring equitable access to these technologies is of paramount importance. Our findings underscore the need for policymakers and clinicians to critically examine eligibility criteria, recognising that restrictive thresholds may reinforce structural disadvantage rather than promote safety or effectiveness. Designing technology funding and implementation strategies that prioritise inclusion, support and equity will be essential to avoid perpetuating disparities as diabetes care continues to advance.
