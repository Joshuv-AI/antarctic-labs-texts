Structural fatigue failure analysis and lifetime reliability monitoring strategy of the lattice jib in all-terrain cranes
Authors: Jia Yao, Yuqin Fu, Changqing Li, Liwei Zhang, Wuhe Sun, Wei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During lifting operations, the boom of large-tonnage all-terrain cranes bears quasi-static loads imposed by the structure weight and wind forces, and alternating cyclic loads caused by the departure, rotation, and landing of cargo. These factors give rise to localized fatigue failure of welded joints, thereby triggering engineering accidents. Current fatigue monitoring strategies mainly rely on historical data and simplified load spectra, which are insufficient to accurately capture the randomness and complexity in crane operations. This study proposes a new fatigue monitoring strategy that integrates real-time operational data with finite element (FE) modeling. The fatigue failure of the lattice jib section (LJS) of an all-terrain crane was investigated through bench testing and FE analysis to establish a fatigue lifetime reliability monitoring strategy for the lattice jib of all-terrain cranes. First, the static loading strain at specific positions, fatigue failure positions, fatigue life and characteristics of the fracture surface were obtained through the implementation of full-scale static load tests, fatigue tests, and fractographic investigation. Then, a refined solid element FE model of the LJS containing weld elements was established, and the hot spot stresses along the wall thickness direction at the weld toes were extracted for fatigue failure analysis. At last, by utilizing the Internet of Things crane operation data and simplified FE model of the entire crane, the fatigue load spectra for components were established, enabling the prediction of cumulative damage rate and potential fatigue failure locations in the crane lattice jib. The simulation method and lifetime monitoring strategy developed in this study can serve as a valuable reference for reliability assessment of crane.

Introduction

Large-tonnage all-terrain cranes are extensively utilized in wind power equipment installation projects due to their high lifting performance, flexible mobility, and convenient operability. According to data as of the end of July 2024, China’s cumulative installed capacity of wind power has reached approximately 470 million kW, with a year-on-year growth rate of 19.8%. As the demand for wind power equipment installation continues to rise annually, the need for enhanced construction efficiency. Consequently, within a given timeframe, cranes experience a significant increase in working frequency and load ratio, thereby exacerbating concerns regarding structural fatigue safety.

As shown in Fig. 1, the construction process of a wind turbine involves frequent lifting and lowering actions, which subject the crane boom to repeated cyclic tensile and compressive loads. Under frequent alternating loads, there is the possibility of local crack initiation and crack propagation in the base metal or weld of the lattice jib, which might reduce the local effective load bearing area and increases the local stress, ultimately leading to fatigue failure of the bearing structure1–3.

Photograph of the wind power hoisting process with an all-terrain crane.

With the advancement of lightweighting processes and green manufacturing, there has been a general reduction in the overall thickness of crane structures, an increase in the number of holes in plate components, and a transition from double-sided to single-sided welding. These factors have enhanced energy utilization efficiency and operational flexibility. However, they concurrently reduce the structural stiffness. Under identical load conditions, lightweight structures exhibit amplified overall deformation and localized stress, resulting in heightened fatigue damage4–6.

On one hand, the load-bearing structure of an all-terrain crane is a typical welded structure. Weld joints exhibit geometric discontinuity and material heterogeneity, which make them prone to stress concentration and susceptible to crack initiation and propagation under cyclic loading7. Finding these cracks prior to their penetration through the wall thickness is challenging, thereby increasing the possibility of sudden fatigue failure8,9. The fatigue life evaluation of large steel welded structures is commonly conducted by combining finite element (FE) analysis with experimental measurements. The universally applicable methods for evaluating the fatigue characteristics of welded structures in international standards or manuals mainly include the nominal stress method10,11, the hot spot stress (HSS) method based on surface extrapolation12, and the equivalent structural stress method13–15. The nominal stress method calculates the fatigue life based on the average stress on the welded joint section without considering local structural details, and is not suitable for evaluating the fatigue of non-standard welded structures. Compared to the nominal stress method, both of the latter two methods consider structural details. The HSS method extrapolates from surface reference points to determine the structural stress at the weld toe position and its corresponding fatigue life, whereas the equivalent structural stress method determines the equivalent structural stress at the weld toe position by extracting nodal forces and moments in order to evaluate its fatigue life. Hectors16 developed a full-shell element model and various sub-models for a welded overhead crane runway girder and conducted simulations on crack propagation based on the actual working load spectrum. It was emphasized that the HSS method is the preferred approach for evaluating structural fatigue in cases where the defects are unknown. Zhang17 and Guo18 conducted fatigue bench tests with constant amplitude on pipe-sphere joints in a grid structure, utilizing the FE method to investigate stress distribution and identify fatigue failure locations in the weld joints under tension and proposed a unified fatigue life calculation method for different types of pipe-sphere joints based on the equivalent structural stress approach. The HSS method and the equivalent structural stress method are both applicable to structures with simple structural characteristics and constant loading directions.

Currently, the research on fatigue characteristics of load-bearing structures in all-terrain cranes is relatively limited. There are several challenges that need to be addressed. First, due to large dimensions and complex structural features, the fatigue bench experiments in batches for lattice jibs are expensive. Second, the weld size is considerably smaller than the overall structural dimensions, which leads to a substantial workload in FE modeling and analysis. Third, the close proximity of weld joints in lattice jibs leads to high local stresses, making it difficult to applicate surface extrapolation-based HSS method. Fourth, all-terrain cranes operate under various operating conditions and experience frequently changed loading directions, resulting in diverse values of unit node forces and moments during batch calculations using the equivalent structural stress method, leading to a decrease in computational efficiency.

On the other hand, due to the diverse structural forms, variable working postures, and complex load conditions of all-terrain cranes, an accurate and reliable load spectrum is essential for monitoring the fatigue performance of crane bearing structures. The analysis and compilation of fatigue load spectra are primarily achieved through methods such as design manuals, field measurements, computer simulations, and intelligent algorithms19–21. Jiang22 developed a digital twin framework to predict fatigue evolution throughout the entire life cycle of steel bridges using historical fatigue data. Shittu23 proposed a comprehensive framework for assessing fatigue damage in structural steels used for offshore wind turbine tower jacket applications, incorporating a combination of finite element simulation and artificial neural network response surface modeling techniques. Dong24 established a digital twin fatigue prediction model for bridge cranes by combining historical service data with inherent physical entity information. The aforementioned fatigue load spectrum data are primarily obtained under approximate operational conditions. They are often derived from a limited fault case database or laboratory data, followed by algorithmic fitting or extrapolation. However, the operating conditions of cranes exhibit significant randomness and complexity. The structural configuration and boom posture are determined by lifting requirements, leading to various combinations of boom posture and load weight, which introduce nonlinearity into the load path. The segmented historical data fail to accurately reflect the actual load spectrum, which readily results in conservative prediction results or delayed early warnings.

In previous research, our team analyzed the buckling failure of crane booms using experimental and FE methods5,25, and conducted FE simulation analysis and provided preventive strategies for fatigue fracture in the swivel shafts of boom26. Based on the foundation of our previous research, the fatigue failure of the lattice jib section (LJS) in the QAYX all-terrain crane was investigated through bench testing and finite element (FE) analysis in this study, aiming to establish a fatigue lifetime reliability monitoring strategy for the lattice jib of all-terrain cranes. First, the static loading strain at specific locations, fatigue failure positions, fatigue life, and characteristics of the fracture surface were obtained through the full-scale static load tests, fatigue tests, and fractographic investigations. Then, a refined solid element FE model of the LJS containing welded joints was established, and the HSS along the wall thickness direction at the weld toes were extracted for fatigue failure analysis. Finally, by utilizing the Internet of Things crane operation data and a simplified FE model of the entire crane, the fatigue load spectra for components were established, enabling an accurate prediction of the cumulative damage rate and potential fatigue failure locations in the lattice jib.

Compared to traditional methods, the lifetime reliability monitoring strategy proposed in this paper demonstrates distinct advantages in fatigue prediction for crane load-bearing structures. It shifts from relying on static historical data to utilizing a dynamic, real-time IoT data stream. It transitions from employing simplified, extrapolated spectra to generating high-fidelity component-level spectra via simulations based on parametric whole-machine models. It advances from isolated empirical formula estimations to a multi-scale, closed-loop analysis framework that fuses data and physics. This strategy aims to achieve online tracking and precise early warning of fatigue life throughout the entire individualized operational process of a crane.

Experimental structure analysis

Experimental structure

Lattice jib is a critical load-bearing structure of an all-terrain crane assembled to the end of the telescopic jib by pin shafts, which consists of several independent LJSs. LJS is a circular hollow section weld jointed steel structure composed of chords, braces, joints and accessories. Four chords form an approximate cubic space frame with braces staggered arranged in the plane created by each pair of adjacent chords.

Lattice jib intermediate sections with dimension parameters of H950 mm × W1100 mm × L6000 mm were used in the experiment, as shown in Fig. 2. Each LJS is composed of four chords (φ82.5 mm ×5 mm) and 41 braces (φ45 mm × 4 mm). The chord and brace were connected by 4 mm fillet welds with 1.2–2.0 mm weld depth, and there were 80 circular weld joints in the lattice jib. The material properties of LJS are listed in Table 1. The standard S-N curves acquired from the tensile fatigue test and the bending fatigue test of the welded joints of S890 steel are shown in Fig. 3. The bottom end of the LJS was hinged with a fixed fixture using four pin shafts, and the experimental load was applied to the top end of the LJS perpendicular to the long axis of the chord using a servo cylinder.

Fractographic analysis

Fractographic observation of fracture surface morphology is advantageous in identifying the characteristics of different stages of failure and determining the underlying causes of fractures. As the overall size of the LJS was large, disassembly and cleaning were executed before fractographic investigation of the fatigue failure regions.

Fig. 6Macroscopic morphology of fatigue fracture. (a) crack initiation region. (b) crack propagation region.

Macroscopic morphology of fatigue fracture. (a) crack initiation region. (b) crack propagation region.

The macroscopic features of fatigue crack were examined using a stereomicroscope to determine the location of fracture origin and the crack propagation path. As shown in Fig. 6a, the fatigue crack initiation region was located at the interface of the weld toe, and the fatigue crack initially propagated along the wall thickness and gradually changed to become approximately perpendicular to the wall thickness after penetrating through the chord wall. The typical fatigue crack propagation region is depicted in Fig. 6b, where a series of nearly parallel arcs, known as fatigue striations, can be observed. These striations represent the residual plastic deformation caused by cyclic stress during crack propagation.

Fig. 7Typical fatigue surface with classic fatigue striations observed under SEM.

Typical fatigue surface with classic fatigue striations observed under SEM.

The fatigue fracture surface of No. 1 specimen was examined by Hitachi Regulus 8100 scanning electron microscope (SEM) to obtain the average striation spacing values at various distances from the crack initiation region along the fatigue crack propagation path to the thickness boundary of the chord. The SEM images in Fig. 7 depict the fatigue striation features at four different distances from the crack initiation. The striations are a series of parallel and regularly spaced stripes, with their orientation perpendicular to the local crack propagation direction and protruding outward along this direction, exhibiting typical plastic fatigue characteristics28. The length values depicted in Fig. 7 denote the cumulative width of 3 successive fatigue striations.

Figure 8 provides a series of fatigue striation width values at different positions along the crack propagation path in SEM images, obtained by calculating the average width of consecutive striations. Based on the research of striation counting29,30, the total number of striations to failure can indicate the number of load cycles during the propagation stage. According to the SEM observations of striation spacing values of various positions along the crack propagation path before crack propagating the thickness, the through-thickness life can be estimated to 29,620 and 47,090 cycles through linear and polynomial fitting method, respectively. The cycle number obtained from linear fitting method closely approximates the through-thickness fatigue life in the fatigue test.

FE model and analysis

Finite element model

Finite element analysis was conducted in ANSYS 2022R1 (ANSYS, Inc.; https://www.ansys.com/), utilizing the static structural module and Ncode fatigue analysis module. Three-dimensional FE models of the experimental structure were established using quadratic solid elements, as indicated in Fig. 9a. All components in the model, including the main chords, braces and the welds, were uniformly assigned the material properties of S890 steel of Table 1. A unified, isotropic linear-elastic material model was employed. In the regions of interest at the fixed end of the LJS, mesh was refined by controlling the element size and number of layers along the thickness direction of chord or brace, and the FE model with 2 mm element refinement is shown in Fig. 9b. According to the actual welding mode between chords and braces, nodes were shared between weld toe elements and weld elements, and nodes were not shared between chords and braces. Ten FE models with different element refinement parameters in the regions of interest were established. The element size ranges from 1 to 10 mm. The mentioned element size in the following text refers to that of the regions of interest.

Based on the experimental structural characteristics, fixed constraints were applied to the rear side of the four fixed fixtures in the FE model. Both static and fatigue loads are concentrated forces applied in the negative Z-direction on the pin shaft. The load for static simulation was 160kN. In the fatigue simulation, a sine half-wave load was adopted. The peak load was 60kN, the stress ratio is 0.1, and the frequency was 1 Hz.

Fatigue simulation analysis

Figure 12 shows the fatigue life distribution of weld toe elements of 4 mm element model. The element marked in red indicates the location of the minimum simulated life and maximum damage per cycle, specifically referred as the predicted fatigue position. The location of this position is at the weld toe on the chord in region 1, with a fatigue life of 27,380 cycles and a damage per cycle of 0.00365%. The predicted fatigue positions of all the ten models exhibit excellent agreement with the locations of fatigue fracture of the three experimental structures, effectively assessing the sites of fatigue failure.

Fig. 12Fatigue life distribution of 4 mm FE model.

Fatigue life distribution of 4 mm FE model.

Considering the weld density of the LJS, the HSS sampling points of the solid element model were not located on the base metal surface. Instead, they extended from each weld toe node along the thickness direction of the base metal to establish a stress classification line. The number of value points along the SCL can be specified by NumWeldLayers (NWL), and the length of SCL can be specified by MaxWeldDepth (MWD). The selection of values for the two parameters impacts the magnitude of HSS, subsequently influencing the values of predicted fatigue life and damage per cycle at a given location. Figure 13 presents the calculated fatigue life data influenced by NWL, MWD, and element size. The range for NWL varies from 1 to 5, while the MWD values range from 1.0 to 2.5 mm, corresponding to the actual structural welding depth range of 1.2–2.0 mm. The element size of FE models ranges from 1 to 10 mm.

The results indicated that, as the NWL increased while maintaining the same MWD, the high stress zone of the weld toe was approached and HSS gradually intensified. Consequently, there was a decrease in calculated fatigue life and a reduction in model discrepancies. Notably, when NWL = 1 for each model, the calculated fatigue life significantly exceeded the experimental life by an order of magnitude with considerable deviation. Therefore, the case of NWL = 1 will not be considered further. The data enclosed by bold circular black frames in Fig. 12 represent values that fall within a 10% ARD to the experimental fatigue life (29769 cycles), and the element size of these data are mainly 4 mm, 5 mm, and 6 mm.

The calculated life of 4 mm and 5 mm FE models, influenced by dual factors, exhibit similar response characteristics, ranging from 1.9e4 to 3.1e4 cycles, with a relative deviation ranging from − 36% to 4% compared to the experimental life (29769 cycles). When the two factors are combined as MWD = 1.3 and NWL = 2 or MWD = 1.2 and NWL = 3, the calculated lifetimes align with the experimental lifetime.

The values of calculated life of the 6 mm element model are relatively higher when influenced by dual factors, ranging from 2.5e4 to 4.1e4 cycles, with a relative deviation ranging from − 16% to 38% compared to experimental life (29769 cycles). When combining the two factors as MWD = 1.5 and NWL = 4, or MWD = 1.6 and NWL = 3, the calculated lifetimes align with the experimental lifetime.

The element sizes of the 4 mm and 5 mm are close to the fillet weld length (4 mm) and the structural wall thickness (5 mm for the chord and 4 mm for the brace), thereby accurately capturing the characteristics of the stress concentration zones. Based on the results presented in this section, it can be concluded that when predicting the fatigue characteristics of components, setting the element size to be comparable to the length of the fillet weld and aligning the simulation parameters with the actual welding depth can significantly improve the accuracy of fatigue life prediction.

Research on lifetime reliability monitoring strategy

In terms of crane operations, lifting an object off the ground and subsequently lowered is considered as one load cycle, and conditions with same structural frame and working posture is considered to be one operational condition27. The load characteristics of LJSs in crane lattice jib vary significantly in one operational condition.

Utilizing vehicle-mounted Internet of Things (IoT) technology, the crane’s actual structural frame, working posture, and lifting weight information are recorded from the date of delivery to generate a comprehensive machine operational condition table. Extraction of component load spectrum

The loading characteristics of the lattice jib components exhibit significant variations under different operational conditions. By utilizing an overall beam element model of crane boom for geometrically nonlinear calculations can effectively account for the impact of large deformation factors on the force characteristics of components.

The MPC184 revolute joint two-node element was employed to establish the joint pairs between LJSs based on the characteristics of the actual hinge mode. The revolute joint element has only one primary degree of freedom, enabling one relative rotation about the revolute axis, while constraining rotations in the other two directions. Therefore, five directional reaction forces can be obtained at the hinge points of the LJS including three directional forces and two directional moments.

The FE analysis results of an operational condition of a crane equipped with a fixed lattice jib and a super-lift structure utilizing beam element models is depicted in Fig. 17. For a single LJS, the bottom end is considered as the constrained region, and the positions of four pin hinge points at the top end are regarded as loading points. The load on each loading point is extracted from the overall boom beam element model, which is comprised of forces in x, y and z direction and moments rotate along x-axis and z-axis, specifically denoted as Fx, Fy, Fz, Mx, and Mz. Since the rotation direction of the hinge joint is along Y-axis, the value of My is zero.

Fig. 17Beam element model of crane boom. (a) Mises stress in overall boom structure. (b) Mises stress in one LJS.

Beam element model of crane boom. (a) Mises stress in overall boom structure. (b) Mises stress in one LJS.

Utilizing batch calculation, static analysis is conducted for all working conditions listed in the operational conditions table to generate five directional reaction force spectra (RFS) for each loading point. The fatigue load spectrum for structural component of the LJS is formed by combining fatigue load spectra from all four loading points.

The conversion from the lifting weight in the Internet of Things to the load on the components is a mechanical solution process based on physical principles, rather than an empirical correlation driven by data. The parameterized finite element model established clarifies the complete force-transmission path from the lifting point to the target component. For each set of operating conditions defined by the Internet of Things data, the model directly yields the internal forces at the connection interface of the target component by solving the static equilibrium equations.

After obtaining the fatigue load spectrum of the component, the fatigue analysis of the component can be conducted in accordance with the fatigue analysis process outlined in Chap. 3.

The directional loading spectra of each loading point, namely Fx RFS, Fy RFS, Fz RFS, Mx RFS and Mz RFS, are incorporated into a refined solid element model that includes weld elements through the load input module. Following consolidation of load combinations, they are imported into the fatigue analysis module to conduct the fatigue analysis.

Summary and conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the LJS of all-terrain cranes by experimental investigation and FE simulation, aiming to investigate a FE fatigue failure analysis method and propose a lifetime reliability monitoring strategy for the lattice jib of all-terrain cranes. The developed fatigue failure analysis method and lifetime reliability monitoring strategy demonstrated high efficiency and accuracy in evaluating the remaining lifespan of main load-bearing components in the all-terrain crane lattice jib, and identifying susceptible locations prone to fatigue failure that require targeted monitoring. This study offers valuable insights for the comprehensive lifecycle design of large-tonnage all-terrain cranes. The following conclusions were drawn:

In three full-scale structural fatigue tests, fatigue failures all occurred at the weld toes of the main chord root under axial tensile loads, and the failure modes were identical.The quadratic solid element model with dimensions closely resembling the weld joint size and wall thickness in the LJS, along with the selection of characteristic stresses at the weld toe in the direction of the wall thickness, can effectively predict both the location and fatigue fracture life. This load spectrum serves as the basis for predicting the cumulative damage rate of components and identifying potential fatigue-prone locations.
