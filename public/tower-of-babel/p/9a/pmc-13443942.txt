One year follow-up of physical activity and health-related quality of life in youth with Cystic Fibrosis in the modulator era
Authors: Manon Kinaupenne, Ines Vandekerckhove, Stephanie Van Biervliet, Heidi Schaballie, Kristof Vandekerckhove, Heleen Demeyer, Marieke De Craemer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElexacaftor/Tezacaftor/Ivacaftor (ETI) has led to major pulmonary and nutritional benefits in youth with cystic fibrosis (YwCF), but its effects on physical activity (PA) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) remain unclear. This study investigated changes in PA and HRQoL according to modulator trajectory (ETI-naïve, ETI-initiated, ETI-throughout). YwCF aged 6–17 years completed assessments at baseline and follow-up (12–18 months). PA was measured using ActiGraph GT3X + BT accelerometers over seven days. Time in moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) and total PA were converted to sex- and age-specific z-scores calculated from a healthy reference cohort (n = 264). HRQoL was evaluated using the Cystic Fibrosis Questionnaire. Thirty-four YwCF (38% male; 13.1 ± 3.8 years) were included. Changes in MVPA (p = 0.74) and total PA (p = 0.73) did not differ across modulator trajectories. MVPA z-scores increased numerically in all groups, while total PA z-scores remained stable. YwCF who initiated or already received ETI reported lower treatment burden and fewer respiratory symptoms, whereas changes in physical functioning varied.ETI did not lead to additional improvements in PA, although improvements were observed in some HRQoL domains. Introduction

The CFTR modulators (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane conductance Regulator) are a transformative advancement in the treatment of cystic fibrosis (CF) by addressing the underlying molecular defects rather than merely managing downstream symptoms, leading to increased life expectancy1. The highly effective triple combination of Elexacaftor/Tezacaftor/Ivacaftor (ETI) is associated with improvements in pulmonary function and nutritional status1–4. However, its impact on health behaviours including physical activity (PA) in children and adolescents with CF, referred to as youth with CF (YwCF) in this paper, remains largely unknown. Despite the recognised health benefits of regular PA, research indicates that YwCF face challenges in adopting and maintaining physically active lifestyles5–7. Evidence on long-term changes in PA in the modulator era is scarce. Causer, et al. reported a case study of three adolescents showing contradictory results on time spent being physically active after initiating ETI (decreases of 32% vs. increases of 17.1%)8. In adults, some studies have reported positive effects of ETI on daily steps with an annual increase of 440 steps/day (n = 27), whereas others found no change (n = 18)7,9. At present, there are no longitudinal data published on the impact of ETI on PA levels in YwCF. Importantly, PA is a complex behaviour consisting of multiple dimensions (e.g., intensity, context) and is subject to various influences10. For example, previous research in YwCF showed that they engage in less high-intensity PA (moderate-to-vigorous PA, MVPA) compared to healthy peers5,6. In addition, in line with the healthy population, boys with CF are more active compared to girls and there is an age-related decline in PA levels that often initiates in early childhood5,11. Therefore, it is important to interpret longitudinal changes in PA according to its different intensities using a healthy reference cohort.

As modulator therapy contributes to an aging CF population, Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) is becoming an increasingly central component of CF management. HRQoL in CF is a multidimensional construct, including both generic (e.g., physical functioning) and disease-specific domains (e.g., respiratory symptoms, treatment burden)12–14. Clinical trials exploring the longitudinal effects of ETI have mostly focussed on outcomes such as weight, lung function, pulmonary exacerbation rates and the respiratory domain of HRQoL1–4. There are only a few studies looking at all HRQoL domains, reporting significant improvements post ETI initiation15–18. These aforementioned studies were predominantly conducted in adults. At present, longitudinal data on the effect of ETI on HRQoL beyond respiratory symptoms are scarce in YwCF and, to the best of our knowledge, no studies reported changes in HRQoL throughout the different age-specific versions of the Cystic Fibrosis Questionnaire (CFQ)13,14.

The aim of the present study was threefold: (1) To explore changes in PA from baseline to follow-up in YwCF according to modulator trajectory during the study period, using reference values calculated from a healthy cohort. Modulator trajectories were defined as ETI‑naïve (no ETI at baseline or follow‑up), ETI‑initiated (ETI started between baseline and follow‑up), and ETI‑throughout (ETI at both time points); (2) To compare PA levels at follow-up according to current ETI status (i.e., ETI-naïve vs. ETI); and (3) To describe changes in multiple HRQoL domains according to modulator trajectory. We hypothesise that ETI will not lead to changes in PA beyond those expected from normal age-related trends.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted an observational longitudinal study at the CF centre of Ghent University Hospital, Belgium. A cohort of YwCF were included between November 2021 and June 2023, of which baseline data were published previously5. During this period, ETI therapy became reimbursed in Belgium, which explains why some YwCF were already on ETI during baseline assessments. YwCF of the baseline cohort (i.e., larger cohort) were invited for a follow-up visit and tested between October 2023 and August 2024. The follow-up was planned 12 ± 2 months after initiation of ETI therapy (ETI-initiated) and no more than 18 months after baseline assessment for the ETI-throughout and ETI-naïve groups. YwCF aged 6–17 with a confirmed CF diagnosis were eligible to participate. They had to be in a stable clinical condition, defined as no change in medications or symptoms, one month prior to both clinical visits. A healthy reference cohort, in the same age range, was recruited between November 2021 and October 2025 using convenience sampling via the social network of researchers, flyers on social media and through primary and secondary schools. Exclusion criteria for both groups included any conditions precluding habitual PA, such as musculoskeletal disorders or cognitive impairments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ghent University Hospital (BC-10536). Written informed assent and consent were obtained from all participants and their parents/guardians.

Data collection

The following sections focus on the data collection of YwCF. Data of the healthy reference cohort (body composition and PA assessment) were collected during home visits with similar measurement protocols as the ones used for YwCF.

Patient characteristics

Data were collected during routine clinical visits at the CF centre. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated and transformed to BMI-z scores based on Flemish reference values19. Lung function was assessed in accordance with the criteria established by the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society, and expressed in relation to reference values (FEV1%pred)20. Medical history was retrieved from the electronic patient file (e.g., medication history, exacerbation history of previous year, pancreatic insufficiency and pseudomonas colonisation). Based on ETI exposure at baseline and follow‑up, YwCF were allocated to one of three following groups: ETI‑naïve (no ETI exposure at baseline or follow‑up), ETI‑initiated (no ETI exposure at baseline and ETI exposure at follow‑up), or ETI‑throughout (ETI exposure at both baseline and follow‑up).

Physical activity

PA was measured using an ActiGraph w-GT3X + BT accelerometer (Actigraph LLC, Pensacola, FL). Participants were instructed to wear the device during waking hours for 7 consecutive days on their right hip and the device could only be removed for water-based activities. Additionally, participants recorded their waking and sleeping time in a diary. Accelerometer data were downloaded with ActiLife V6.13.5 and processed using the GGIR package in R (version 3.2.6)21. Valid PA data were defined as a minimum of 4 days (including one weekend day) of 10 h wear time22. PA intensities were categorszed using age-specific prediction equations of Hildebrand and colleagues: light intensity, 63.3 mg, moderate intensity 142.6 mg and vigorous intensity 464.6 mg23,24. Based on this classification, the duration (minutes/day) of MVPA and total PA were calculated for each participant on an average day (weighted average of 5 weekdays and 2 weekend days). These mean values were then expressed as z-scores (sex- and age-specific) derived from the healthy reference cohort (n = 264) (see Supplementary). More details on the measurement protocol of PA have been published elsewhere5.

Health-related quality of life

HRQoL was measured using the Dutch version of the CFQ-R25. The CFQ-R is a validated questionnaire that includes general and CF-specific questions to assess HRQoL in people with CF14. It is based on a 2-week recall and currently exists in 4 versions according to age: one for young children (6–11 years), one for older children (12–13 years), one for adolescents-adults (14 years or older), and one for parents of youth aged 6 to 13 years (i.e. proxy reporting). All versions have 7 corresponding domains: physical functioning, emotional state, body image, eating disturbances, treatment burden, respiratory symptoms, and digestion symptoms. All domains were analysed independently and standardised on a scale of 0-100 with a higher score indicating a better HRQoL in that domain.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was performed for the primary research question of the baseline study (i.e., difference in MVPA between YwCF and healthy peers), which has been published elsewhere5. The analysis plan of this study included the following steps: First, we computed reference values for PA based on our healthy reference cohort for boys (n = 101) and girls (n = 163) separately, using generalised additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS). More details on this analysis, including percentile curves and tables, are provided in Supplementary file 1, tables S3–S6 and figures S1–S4. Second, normality of the data was analysed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and showed non-normal distribution for all PA and HRQoL outcomes. Therefore, all subsequent analysis are conducted using non-parametric tests. Third, to answer our primary research question (change in PA), we first conducted Wilcoxon-rank tests to compare the MVPA and total PA z-scores at baseline between the larger cohort (n = 49) and the subset (n = 34) to confirm whether their PA levels were lower compared to the healthy reference cohort (reference set at 0). Subsequently, we used two rank-based ANOVAs, one for MVPA and one for total PA, to assess the change in PA with modulator trajectory as the between factor (i.e. ETI-naïve, ETI-initiated, ETI-throughout) and time (i.e. baseline and follow-up) as the within factor. We chose the ANOVA-Type Statistics (ATS) for its more robust interpretation in small samples. To finalise, Wilcoxon-rank tests were computed to compare MVPA and total PA z-scores at follow-up for the subset (n = 34) to the healthy reference cohort (reference set at 0). Fourth, to answer our second research question (compare PA at follow-up), we computed Mann-Whitney U tests for MVPA and total PA to assess differences according to modulator status groups (i.e., ETI-naïve vs. ETI). Finally, to explore changes in HRQoL, data were plotted according to age and modulator trajectory and analysed descriptively. Due to the changes in report style between the various versions of the CFQ-R (i.e., self-report vs. proxy-report) for 6 YwCF, data could not be pooled for statistical analysis. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), percentages, or median (quartile 1 – quartile 3). Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.3), with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

At baseline, 49 YwCF were included, of whom 40 participated in follow-up assessments5. A subset of 34 YwCF were retained for analyses: four were excluded due to clinical instability at the time of assessment, and two due to invalid PA and HRQoL data. At baseline, this subset (n = 34) did not differ from the large cohort (n = 49) in clinical or PA outcomes (Table S1). The characteristics of the subset are presented in Table 1. Mean follow-up time was 20.6 ± 3.9 months in the ETI-naïve group, 20.5 ± 3.2 months in the ETI-initiated group, and 12.7 ± 2.4 months in the ETI-throughout group. Descriptively, the proportion of YwCF with pancreatic insufficiency is lower in the ETI-naïve group compared to the other groups and the ETI-throughout group reported lower scores on the HRQoL domain eating disturbances (i.e., more disturbances). The healthy reference cohort consisted of 264 healthy youth (38% male) aged 12.1 ± 3.4 years, more details are provided in Supplement (Table S2).

Physical Activity

At baseline, the subset of YwCF (n = 34) did not statistically differ from the reference cohort in total PA z-scores (-0.25 ± 0.97, p = 0.20), but had significantly lower MVPA z-scores (-0.50 ± 1.37, p = 0.04). This was consistent with the larger cohort (n = 49), which showed a trend towards lower total PA z-scores (-0.35 ± 1.23, p = 0.07) and significantly lower MVPA z-scores (-0.37 ± 1.01, p = 0.01) (Table S1). Changes in total PA and MVPA did not differ by modulator trajectory (p = 0.73; p = 0.74 respectively). Numerically, MVPA z-scores increased across all three groups, whereas total PA z-scores remained stable. Figure 1 displays changes in z-scores, absolute PA values are shown in Table 1. Individual changes in PA z-scores according to modulator trajectory are shown in Figures S5–S8.

Health-related Quality of Life

Changes in treatment burden varied across modulator trajectories (Fig. 3A). The ETI-naïve group showed mixed patterns across ages, with treatment burden tending to increase during adolescence (lower scores). Most of YwCF in the ETI-initiated group showed improved scores, suggesting reduced treatment burden after ETI initiation. The ETI-throughout group showed stable scores, with individual improvements mainly at younger ages.

Compared with treatment burden, physical functioning and respiratory symptoms have higher scores, with most YwCF scoring in the upper range (80–100) (Fig. 3B-C). Physical functioning remained stable, with individual improvements across modulator trajectories. However, five YwCF showed steep declines, four of which coincided with the transition from proxy- to self-report. Respiratory symptoms improved in both the ETI-initiated and ETI-throughout groups, whereas the ETI-naïve group showed mixed changes, including some declines. Discussion

Since February 2025, ETI has been reimbursed in Belgium for all people with CF aged ≥ 2 years with at least one copy of the F508del mutation, giving 73% of Belgian people with CF (1010/1379 patients) access to this therapy26. This study explored changes in PA and HRQoL among YwCF in the modulator era. The main finding was that change in PA did not differ according to modulator trajectory nor did PA differ at follow-up between YwCF receiving and not receiving ETI. Numerically, total PA z-scores remained stable and MVPA z-scores increased to levels comparable to reference values. In terms of HRQoL, YwCF who initiated or were already receiving ETI reported lower treatment burden and fewer respiratory symptoms, whereas changes in physical functioning varied more widely. As one of the first studies to investigate the effects of ETI on PA and HRQoL in YwCF, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and exploratory nature of the analysis.

The data on PA confirmed our hypothesis that changes would not differ according to modulator trajectory, however, the increase in MVPA z-scores was unexpected. These increases in z-scores reflect a relative rather than absolute improvement, suggesting that YwCF, irrespective of modulator therapy, may experience a slower age-related decline in daily MVPA (min/day) than healthy peers, leading to stable or rising MVPA z-scores over time (Figures S7 – S8). This pattern may reflect growing emphasis on healthy lifestyles within CF care within the modulator era, consistent with current recommendations27. Moreover, MVPA often occurs in structured contexts such as organised sports, (e.g. hobbies such as football, athletics, dancing…), which may be maintained even during periods of reduced well-being (e.g. pulmonary exacerbation). While these explanations may partially explain the increases in MVPA z-scores, one should acknowledge the complexity of PA, often described through the COM-B model. The model posits that PA behaviour (B) occurs as a result of fulfilling three components: Capability (C), Opportunity (O) and Motivation (M)28. Accordingly, known influencing factors of PA in YwCF, such as screen time, participation in organised sports and social support may have influenced MVPA, but these were not measured at follow up11. Additionally, selection bias may have occurred in our subset, possibly including YwCF who are motivated to do better compared to baseline assessment. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm sustained engagement in MVPA and explore underlying behavioural or physiological mechanisms, irrespective of ETI status.

HRQoL showed considerable variability within and across modulator trajectories, reflecting its subjective and multidimensional nature. HRQoL is influenced by factors beyond clinical outcomes, including CF care, family support, and occupational and social components12. Moreover, this multidimensionality in combination with its subjective nature makes it challenging to interpret, especially in youth where the measurement of HRQoL shifts from proxy- to self-report at the age of 12. For example, three YwCF already receiving ETI reported substantial declines in physical functioning (± 80 points) when transitioning from proxy- to self-report (Fig. 3B). Such discrepancies between proxy- and self-report were most evident in the physical functioning domain. While CFQ-R scores are valid and reliable to assess HRQoL, scores should ideally be compared within the same version of the CFQ-R14. Descriptively, respiratory symptoms and treatment burden improved among those who initiated or were already taking ETI. However, scores for treatment burden remained around 50, which is considered average on a 0-100 scale, consistent with previous findings in adults with CF (n = 27)18. This highlights that treatment burden remains a challenge even for YwCF benefitting from highly effective modulators. Indeed, due to the lack of short- and long-term evidence in favour of simplifying treatment, YwCF are often advised to continue their respiratory care, without changes in frequency nor modality29. Nevertheless, individual variability was substantial in all domains and could not be explained within this study. Future research using consistent CFQ‑R versions in larger cohorts of YwCF is needed to better understand changes in HRQoL in the modulator era.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the longitudinal change in PA, measured using a validated device following current methodology recommendations, and HRQoL of YwCF in the modulator era22. We strengthened our PA analysis by interpreting the PA levels using age- and sex-specific percentile curves derived from a reference cohort of 264 healthy peers, using the same methodology for PA assessment and analysis as the YwCF cohort. The use of these percentile curves enabled us to account for sex-related differences in PA levels and age-related declines associated with adolescence, both shown to be important influencing factors of PA in YwCF11. Nevertheless, the percentile curves were calculated based on cross-sectional data, but used to interpret longitudinal data. Future research would benefit from developing longitudinal PA percentile curves. This study includes real-world data reflecting spontaneous behaviour among YwCF and their families in the modulator era. However, this came with a lack of standardised follow-up and limited data on factors such as therapy adherence and ETI side effects, which could have contextualised our results. While the sample size was small, it could be considered average, arguing that CF is a rare disease. Moreover, it is a representative subset of the larger cohort in clinical and PA characteristics (Table S1). Nevertheless, differences in characteristics between groups were observed. For example, the proportion of YwCF with pancreatic insufficiency was lower in the ETI-naïve group compared with the other groups. Based on previous literature indicating that pancreatic status is not an influencing factor of PA behaviour11, and given the longitudinal design of the study with no changes in pancreatic status over time, this difference is not expected to have influenced PA levels. However, subgroups sizes restricted further in-depth analyses. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution as the study could have led to type II errors.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study suggests that ETI does not lead to additional improvements in PA of YwCF. YwCF initiating or already receiving ETI did show improvements in some HRQoL domains. Future longitudinal research is warranted with larger cohorts to explore the underlying mechanisms related to PA and HRQoL in the modulator era.
