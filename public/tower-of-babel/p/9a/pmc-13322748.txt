Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma: The Weight of History
Authors: Telma Costa Cabral, Ana Rita Ambrósio, Bruno Miguel Silva, Isménia de Oliveira, Hugo Pêgo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignant tumor arising from the epithelial cells of the bile ducts. Its incidence is increasing, and it is associated with a high mortality rate due to its aggressive behavior and frequent late diagnosis. We report the case of a 52-year-old man with a history of dyslipidemia and active smoking who was brought to the emergency department following a suicide attempt. The patient suspected he had a neoplastic disease, similar to the CCA that had affected his mother. He reported a three-month history of weight loss, asthenia, fever, and night sweats. Laboratory evaluation revealed inflammatory anemia. He also reported consumption of unpasteurized cheese at symptom onset, prompting hospital admission for etiological investigation. Computed tomography scan (CT scan) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated calcified hilar lymphadenopathy and other slightly globular, nonspecific lymph nodes. Extensive laboratory testing, such as bone marrow aspirate, bone biopsy, and gastrointestinal endoscopic studies, revealed no primary tumor. After discharge, an 18F-FDG PET-CT scan showed findings compatible with metastatic disease - hepatic uptake (single lesion), multifocal bone involvement, and intestinal uptake. Biopsy of a bone lesion confirmed metastasis from an undifferentiated carcinoma of unknown primary, and treatment was initiated. The clinical course was unfavourable. Clinical autopsy revealed a high-grade intrahepatic CCA with bone metastases. This case illustrates an atypical presentation of intrahepatic CCA manifesting as metastatic disease of an unknown primary tumor and underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with this malignancy.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignant tumor arising from the epithelial cells of the biliary ducts. It can be anatomically classified according to the tumor location as intrahepatic (iCCA), perihilar (pCCA), and distal (dCCA), being the last two also referred to as extrahepatic CCA (eCCA) [1]. These subtypes differ in embryologic origin, pathogenesis mechanisms, and clinical presentation [2].

CCA is the second most prevalent type of liver cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [3]. Perihilar CCA is the most frequent subtype (50-60% of CCA), followed by the eCCA (20-30%) and the iCCA (10-20%) [3,4]. However, the epidemiological distribution of CCA and its subtypes varies geographically as a consequence of exposure to different risk factors [5]. The incidence is higher in Southeast Asia compared to Western countries [6].

Several risk factors have been associated with CCA, with chronic biliary inflammation being the common pathogenic mechanism [6]. Primary sclerosing cholangitis is strongly associated with pCCA, whereas cirrhosis and viral hepatitis (B and C) correlate more closely with iCCA [1,5,6]. Other risk factors include liver fluke infection, inflammatory bowel disease, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, hepatolithiasis, and congenital biliary tract malformations. Environmental factors include heavy smoking, alcohol consumption, nitrosamine-contaminated food, asbestos, dioxins, vinyl chlorides, and thorotrast [5,6]. However, in Western countries, approximately half of CCA cases are sporadic with no identifiable risk factor [7].

CCA may present with jaundice, dark urine, clay-colored stool, and pruritus, occurring when extrahepatic CCA obstructs the bile duct. When the tumor involves only the intrahepatic ducts, the patient might have right upper quadrant abdominal pain, malaise, night sweats, weight loss, or cachexia [3]. CCA is frequently asymptomatic in early stages and is often diagnosed only after metastatic spread [5]. Although CCA most commonly metastasizes via the lymphatic system to regional lymph nodes and hematogenously to the liver, lungs, and peritoneum, bone metastases also occur and carry a particularly poor prognosis [8]. Bone metastases develop in a small group of iCCA patients, typically affecting the axial skeleton [8]. Patients with bone involvement have a significantly shorter median overall survival compared to those with liver-only metastases [8]. Importantly, poor performance status combined with bone metastases further worsens outcomes, raising important questions about the benefit of active chemotherapy versus best supportive care in this subgroup [8].

Diagnosis is challenging and requires a multimodal approach. Initial imaging should include multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1,3,9]. Tumor markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9), and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), may offer additional information, although none are specific. When suspecting eCCA, endoscopic ultrasound and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography are important diagnostic modalities [3].

Surgical resection remains the only curative treatment of CCA; therefore, delayed diagnosis significantly compromises outcomes. For patients where resection is feasible, adjuvant chemotherapy with capecitabine is recommended [7]. For the cases where tumor resection is not possible, chemotherapy with gemcitabine and cisplatin may be used as palliative treatment [1,5,7].

The mortality of iCCA has increased consistently over the past decade across Western countries, including Portugal. By contrast, eCCA had a slower increase in mortality rates [4].

We report a case of CCA with an atypical initial presentation of bone metastases in the absence of hepatic symptoms, highlighting the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare and aggressive malignancy.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old Portuguese man, an active smoker of 35 pack-years, with no other relevant medical history, presented to the emergency department following a suicide attempt. He expressed concern about developing CCA, the condition that had killed his mother at the age of 50. He had also experienced significant weight loss, 15% of total body weight in the last three months, generalized fatigue, fever, and hyperhidrosis for the past three months. There was a history of unpasteurized cheese consumption that coincided with the beginning of symptoms.

From December 2023 to May 2024, he underwent three admissions to Internal Medicine for etiological investigation. Blood analyses showed inflammatory anemia (hemoglobin (Hb) of 9.3 x 10 g/L, ferritin of 3,875 ng/ml with a reference interval of 30-400 ng/ml), and the search for an infectious cause did not return any positive results. The serologies for HIV (human immunodeficiency vírus), hepatitis B and C, Treponema pallidum, Cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, toxoplasmosis, brucellosis, Q fever, rickettsiosis, and borreliosis were all negative, as were the urine, bone marrow, and blood cultures, including mycobacterial media at 42 days. The tuberculosis investigation was also negative. An initial contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan showed a normal-sized liver, with regular contours and a homogeneous appearance without biliary ductal dilatation. The gallbladder was distended, with a non-thickened wall, and there was no evidence of radiopaque gallstones. There were mesenteric and posterior pericecal lymphatic nodes, the largest measuring 13 × 8 mm, with non-pathological features; however, noteworthy due to their number. On evaluation of the osseous skeleton, there was no evidence of suspicious lytic or blastic lesions. After an extensive investigation, the patient was discharged and continued the etiological investigation as an outpatient.

An 18F-FDG PET-CT scan was subsequently performed and revealed findings compatible with a metabolically active malignant disease of the liver (single lesion at the junction of segments VIII and V) and of the skeleton (multifocal, extensive). There was also suspicion of active malignant disease at the intestinal level (ascending colon and pelvic loops of the small intestine) (Figure 1).

18F-FDG PET-CT scan showing the liver lesion and the multifocal skeleton disease

At this stage of investigation, endoscopic studies, bone biopsy, and myelogram had shown no abnormalities or signs of a primary tumor. Given the high inflammatory activity of the disease, particularly ferritin levels of 7000-8000 ng/ml (reference interval of 30-400 ng/ml), an empirical course of prednisone (1 mg/kg/day) was initiated for 15 days, pending bone biopsy results, with partial stabilisation of laboratory parameters but without significant clinical improvement. Treatment was discontinued upon the availability of the second bone biopsy result, described below.

In May 2024, the patient was readmitted, and although the main suspicion was a malignancy, the etiological studies remained inconclusive. In the absence of compatible histology, the prior history of unpasteurized milk consumption warranted an empirical antibiotic course targeting brucellosis (doxycycline and rifampicin) for seven days without clinical benefit. Treatment was discontinued upon the availability of the second bone biopsy result, described later. Endoscopic studies were repeated, highlighting a subepithelial 5 mm lesion in the transition from the superior to medium third of the esophagus. A second CT scan showed a 14 mm hypovascular nodule, possibly related to metastasis, in the posterior segments of the right hepatic lobe (Figure 2). The remaining hepatic parenchyma was homogeneous. Bone evaluation revealed multiple lesions with a permeative pattern and cortical disruption: the right 11th rib, a vertebra of the thoracic spine, and several lesions in the pelvis, the largest measuring 4 cm in the right iliac bone, findings consistent with secondary (metastatic) lesions. A second bone biopsy from a rib lesion revealed infiltration of part of the bone marrow by a neoplasm composed of pleomorphic cells, some with plasmacytoid features. The immunohistochemical (IHC) study was positive for CK7 and AE1/AE3 and negative for S100, CDX2, CD38, and CK20. These findings were consistent with metastatic carcinoma of unknown primary; the differential diagnosis included poorly differentiated gastric carcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, or laryngeal carcinoma. Following a multidisciplinary oncology meeting, further investigation was advised, including evaluation for a prostatic tumour and measurement of tumour markers, all of which were within normal limits: PSA 1,36 ng/dL (reference interval: <4 ng/dL), CEA 2,6 ng/dL (reference interval: <5 ng/dL), CA19-9 <2 ng/dL (reference interval: <34 ng/dL), and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, assessed by combined positive score, was 0. Without histological identification of the primary tumor, but with lesions suggesting metastases, treatment with carboplatin and paclitaxel was started. However, the patient's general status progressively declined during treatment. After three weeks, he developed worsening orthostatic intolerance, generalized bone pain requiring symptomatic management, and deterioration of neurological status. In view of treatment futility and declining performance status, onco-directed treatment was discontinued, and palliative care was initiated.

Abdominopelvic computed tomography (axial) - 14 mm hypovascular nodule in the posterior segments of the right hepatic lobe

With worsening of the clinical status, the patient died seven months after initial admission and 10 months after the beginning of symptoms. The final diagnosis was only possible through a clinical autopsy. Macroscopic examination revealed a 1.7 cm nodular hepatic lesion and a 2 cm lytic lesion of the 11th right posterior costal arch. Microscopic examination confirmed a unifocal, high-grade (G3) pleomorphic iCCA measuring 2 cm, with bone metastases to the thoracic cavity.

Discussion

We report an atypical presentation of iCCA with extensive bone metastasis. Clinical, imagiological, and laboratory findings were insufficient to establish a diagnosis, in a disease where prognosis is heavily dependent on early diagnosis. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges inherent to iCCA, especially when presenting with atypical features. CCA has a low incidence; however, it has increased in the last decades. Despite the advances in diagnostics and treatment options, the survival rates remain poor, with a five-year survival of approximately 10% [3,5,9,10].

Our patient presented with a 15% weight loss over three months, B symptoms (fever and night sweats), and fatigue. In the early stages, CCAs are frequently asymptomatic, but, when symptomatic, they may present with nonspecific constitutional symptoms, the same way as our patient [5]. In comparison to other types of CCA, iCCA has a worse prognosis and higher mortality rate, in part, due to the nonspecific manifestations at the disease onset and consequent late diagnosis [9]. In contrast to pCCA, which may present with painless jaundice, 20-25% of iCCA diagnoses are incidental [5].

As this case presented as an inflammatory condition, during the investigation, several infections were excluded, including HBV and HCV, which are well-established risk factors [5,7]. Although chronic inflammation of the liver and biliary tree has been identified as a risk factor for CCA, most cases occur without the identification of predisposing conditions or environmental factors [5]. The patient had a positive family history for CCA; however, current evidence does not identify family history as a significant risk factor. Most mutations in CCA are somatic in origin, with germline mutations being rare [5]. The patient's 35 pack-year smoking history represents a modest risk factor for CCA [11].

There is no single diagnostic test for CCA, so diagnosis relies on a combination of imaging and laboratory findings. On CT scans, iCCA may have variable features, such as a nodule or a low-density mass, but it may also mimic other lesions or have unexpected patterns, making diagnosis difficult [5,9,12]. Contrast-enhanced CT, in iCCA, typically shows a portal or delayed phase enhancement pattern, in contrast to hepatocellular carcinoma, where an arterial enhancement pattern is seen [3,9,12]. Our patient's first CT scan showed no hepatic abnormalities; the second CT, with a five-month difference, revealed a hypovascular nodule, but lacked distinguishing features for specific diagnosis. MRI is considered the exam of choice, especially for small or diffuse lesions, and, in this case, could have provided additional information for the diagnosis [7,9,12]. PET-CT scan has an important role in the staging of CCA, but limited sensitivity for the diagnosis of the primary tumor [3,7]. In the present case, the PET-CT scan was performed without knowing the primary tumor and did not provide information regarding the primary site, but helped delineate the extent of disease. In this case, an MRI was not performed for several reasons. Initially, given the clinical picture suggestive of an inflammatory or infectious condition, a PET-CT was prioritized over an MRI to assess the extent of systemic disease. Subsequently, given the extensive bone involvement and the small hepatic nodule identified on CT, bone biopsy was favored as the most accessible and potentially conclusive diagnostic approach. In retrospect, MRI could have provided additional characterization of the hepatic lesion and potentially guided an earlier diagnosis, and its absence represents an important diagnostic limitation of this case.

The patient presented with extensive bone metastases, involving the axial and appendicular skeleton, including the ribs, thoracic spine, and pelvis. Bone metastases in CCA are uncommon, and their prevalence remains less documented [8]. Recent data suggest that bone metastases occur in approximately 17% of CCA patients [13] and are associated with a particularly poor prognosis, with a median overall survival of four months [8]. Their presentation, in the absence of an evident hepatic mass, represents a rare and diagnostically challenging scenario.

Definitive diagnosis of iCCA requires a core needle biopsy specimen and histopathological assessment [7]. Immunohistochemistry is useful for differentiating metastatic liver tumors. The immunohistochemical pattern in our patient, positive for CK7 and AE1/AE3, and negative for S100, CDX2, CD38, and CK20, tells us we are in the presence of an epithelial tumor due to the positivity of AE1/AE3. CK7 and CK20 often provide some information about the origin of the primary tumor. However, unless it is CK7- and CK20+ typical for colorectal cancer, or CK7- and CK20- typical for prostate cancer, it is mostly inconclusive due to its presence in several adenocarcinomas [11].

Given the disease progression and worsening clinical status, we assumed a tumor of unknown origin and started treatment accordingly with Carboplatin and Paclitaxel. In CCA, the treatment of choice is surgery; however, in advanced cases, chemotherapy can be a palliative option, with gemcitabine and cisplatin being the first-line option [5,7].

Although this case reflects the inherent complexity of an atypical presentation, several diagnostic limitations merit reflection. MRI of the liver - the modality of choice for characterising hepatic lesions - was not performed, and its earlier use could have identified the primary tumour before extensive metastatic spread. In addition, once an infectious etiology had been excluded and a malignant process was suspected on PET-CT, liver biopsy should have been prioritized earlier in the diagnostic algorithm rather than bone biopsy alone. Finally, a biliary primary was not considered in the initial differential diagnosis, in part due to the absence of classical features such as jaundice or biliary dilatation, underscoring the importance of maintaining a broad differential in atypical presentations with unexplained inflammatory markers.

Despite an extensive workup, the patient died without a definitive diagnosis, which was only possible through autopsy. In this case, the autopsy was an essential diagnostic tool, confirming high-grade iCCA with bone metastases and highlighting its continued importance in complex oncological cases with atypical presentations [11].

Conclusions

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges of iCCA, particularly in the setting of an atypical and nonspecific clinical presentation, highlighting the impact of late diagnosis on prognosis. It underscores the importance of recognizing established and emerging risk factors, as most patients have no identifiable predisposing conditions. Despite advances in diagnostic methods, imaging, and laboratory tests, including CT, MRI, and tumor markers, they lack sensitivity and specificity for early detection, and histopathological confirmation, although essential, may remain inconclusive. Importantly, this case demonstrates that extensive bone metastases combined with a predominant inflammatory presentation may obscure the diagnosis of iCCA even after comprehensive evaluation, and that this malignancy should be considered in cases of metastatic carcinoma of unknown primary lacking classical biliary features.

Finally, this case emphasizes the poor prognosis of metastatic iCCA and the urgent need for improved diagnostic strategies and more effective therapeutic options.
