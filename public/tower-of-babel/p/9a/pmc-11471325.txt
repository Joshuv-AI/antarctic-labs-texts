Long-term Outcomes and Prognostic Factors of Gastric MALT Lymphoma
Authors: Jae Yeon Sim, Hyun Soo Chung, Sang Gyun Kim, Soo Jeong Cho, Bo Kyung Kim, Jun Shik Hong, In Ho Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the long-term prognosis of patients with gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, including overall survival (OS), remission, and factors associated with an aggressive disease course.Materials and MethodsMedical records of 153 patients diagnosed with gastric MALT lymphoma between 2013 and 2020 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients experiencing relapse, progression, high-grade transformation, or residual diseasewere included in the aggressive group and were compared with those in the indolent group. Additionally, the endoscopic findings of Helicobacter pylori-negative patients were reviewed.ResultsPatient characteristics were as follows: mean age (56.9±11.2 years), sex (male, 51.0%), H. pylori infection (positive, 79.7%), endoscopic location (distal, 89.5%), endoscopic feature (superficial, 89.5%), clinical stage (stage I, 92.8%), invasion depth by endoscopic ultrasound (mucosa, n=115, 75.7%), and bone marrow result (no involvement, n=77, 100.0%). The median follow-up period was 59 months (mean, 61; range, 36–124) and the continuous remission period (n=149) was 51 months (mean, 50; range, 3–112). The 5-year survival rate was 97.7% while the 5-year continuous remission was 88.3%. Factors associated with the patients in the aggressive group were old age, sex(male), and clinical stage II or higher. H. pylori-negative patients’ endoscopy revealed a high incidence of atrophic gastritis in the antrum.ConclusionsThe long-term prognosis of gastric MALT lymphoma appears indolent and is indicated by the 5-year OS and continuous remission rates. Aggressive disease courses are associated with old age, sex (male), and clinical stage II or higher, but are not related to OS.Keywords: Lymphoma, B-cell; Marginal zone; Survival analysis; Helicobacter pylori

INTRODUCTION

Gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma is a B-cell lymphoma originating from the stomach, and its pathogenesis is highly associated with Helicobacter pylori infection [1]. Eradication is the first-line therapy once the existence of H. pylori is confirmed because eradication alone can lead to complete remission[2]. If there is no evidence of H. pylori infection or if the clinical stage is II or higher, patients are referred to radio-oncologists or oncologists to receive radiation therapy (RTx) or chemotherapy (CTx). Occasionally, eradication may be considered in cases of false-negative results or other Helicobacter infections.

The overall survival (OS) of gastric MALT lymphoma is known to be indolent, with a 10-year survival probability of 99.1% (stage IE) in Korea and 95% in Japan, if treated effectively [3,4]. Based on a study of patients with stage IE gastric MALT lymphoma, relapse after remission has been reported to be as low as 3% [5]. However, there is still limited information about the long-term course of gastric MALT lymphoma across all stages. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary hospital in Seoul to observe the long-term outcomes of gastric MALT lymphoma across all stages, including OS, duration of continuous remission, and prognostic factors associated with an aggressive disease course. Additionally, we aimed to assess the endoscopic findings of patients confirmed as H. pylori-negative to identify any differences between H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection

Patients diagnosed with Extranodal Marginal Zone B-cell Lymphoma of Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (C88, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] code) between 2013 and 2020 at Seoul National University Hospital were identified. Data such as age, sex, pathology report, rapid urease test (RUT), urea breath test (UBT), serologic test, H. pylori polymerase chain reaction (PCR), endoscopy results, imaging studies, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), and bone marrow (BM) biopsy were extracted and refined by researchers. The follow-up period was calculated from the day of confirmation of gastric MALT lymphoma disease by a pathologist to the last day of follow-up, whereas the time to complete remission was calculated from the day of pathological confirmation to the day when the endoscopy displayed complete remission in the endoscopic view and biopsy. Data related to death were based on medical records or acquired from the Ministry of Security and Public Administration, a division of Korea’s Central Administrative Agency. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital under number H-2305-109-1432. The research agreement was exempt from a statement and waiver of informed consent.

H. pylori infection

H. pylori infection status was assessed using various methods, including pathology report, RUT, UBT, serological test, and H. pylori PCR. Patients were categorized as H. pylori-positive if any one of these tests taken by them was positive and were categorized as H. pylori-negative if all the tests taken by them were negative. Following a positive H. pylori test result, Eradication therapy, either triple therapy (omeprazole, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin) or quadruple therapy (omeprazole, bismuth, metronidazole, and tetracycline) was initiated. The results of eradication were assessed using RUT or UBT, and if these tests indicated eradication failure, a different regimen was pursued.

Clinical staging

Abdominal computed tomography (CT) was performed to assess the initial clinical stage. Other imaging studies, such as positron emission tomography (PET), were also considered when determining the clinical stage, if available. The Lugano staging system was used to classify patients into stage I (confined to the gastrointestinal tract) or stage II or higher (extending into the perigastric lymph nodes and abdomen) [6,7].

Endoscopy and pathology

An endoscopy was conducted at our hospital to obtain biopsy specimens and determine H. pylori infection status. Endoscopic location was classified as distal (involving the antrum), proximal (involving the cardia, body and fundus), or extensive (simultaneous involvement of the proximal and distal parts). Endoscopic features were classified as ulcerative or superficial in patients without ulcerative lesions. Approximately three months after treatment initiation, a second endoscopy was performed to conduct a biopsy and evaluate disease status according to the Groupe d'Etude des Lymphomes de l'Adulte (GELA) classification, both endoscopically and pathologically [8]. If there was an improvement in the endoscopic finding and the biopsy specimen showed complete histologic response (ChR) or probable minimal residual disease (pMRD), patients were considered to have achieved complete remission clinically, not requiring further treatment. If the biopsy specimen revealed persistence or decrease of pathological features such as lymphoepithelial lesions or diffuse lymphoid infiltrates, it was classified as no change (NC) and responding residual disease (rRD), respectively. Further treatments were determined by the patient’s condition.

Assessments for atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia of the antrum and body were also performed to identify potential differences between the selected H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative patients. Endoscopic images of H. pylori-negative patients confirmed using at least 3 tests, were compared with those of H. pylori-positive patients of similar age and sex.

Treatment options

Three treatment options were available for patients diagnosed with gastric MALT lymphoma: eradication, RTx, and CTx. If H. pylori infection was positive, an eradication regimen consisting of omeprazole, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin for 14 days was considered first. If eradication failed, a second regimen consisting of omeprazole, bismuth, metronidazole, and tetracycline for 10–14 days was followed. Most H. pylori-negative patients received RTx initially, however CTx consisting of rituximab, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisolone was considered first if RTx was not indicated.

Definition of aggressive disease, indolent disease, and continuous remission

Relapse was defined as recurrence of MALT lymphoma in patients who achieved complete clinical remission as evidenced by endoscopy or biopsy specimens. Progression was defined as an increased extent of disease on endoscopy without reaching a remission state. Residual disease was defined as histological MALT lymphoma, although endoscopy showed a decreased extent of the disease. If the lesion was identified as relapse, progression, or residual disease, the clinician decided to perform a second treatment. However, in cases of residual disease, awaiting repeat endoscopy was also an option for up to 1 year after the initial treatment. Patients with relapse, transformation into high-grade lymphoma, progression, or residual disease during the follow-up period were included in the aggressive group and the others who maintained complete remission until the last follow-up were included in the indolent group.

In cases of relapse or transformation into a high-grade lymphoma, the duration of continuous remission was defined as the time interval between the date of endoscopy-confirmed remission and the date of endoscopy with relapse or transformation. In cases with no relapse or transformation after remission, the duration of continuous remission was defined as the interval between the date of endoscopy-confirmed remission and the date of the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis and identification of prognostic factor

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the OS and continuous remission duration. To compare the characteristics between the aggressive and indolent groups, either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify prognostic factors related to the aggressive group. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics at baseline

A total of 620 patients diagnosed with gastric MALT lymphoma were identified during the study period. Among these patients, 111 were excluded because the disease was diagnosed before 2013, 148 were excluded because of a short follow-up period of less than three years, and 208 were excluded because of missing medical record data. Thus, 153 patients were included in the final analysis. Their baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 57 years (range, 23–88) and 75 patients (49.0%) were female. The most commonly used diagnostic method to detect H. pylori infection was a pathology report, which showed a positive rate of approximately 60%. When combined with other methods, the H. pylori positive rate reached 79.7%. Initial endoscopy showed that the most frequently involved single site was the body (n=74, 48.4%), followed by the antrum (n=31, 20.3%) and fundus (n=3, 2.0%). Thirty-nine (25.5%) patients had extensive lesions. With respect to the morphology of the endoscopic lesions, 137 patients (89.5%) were presented with a superficial lesion, whereas 16 patients (10.5%) were presented with an ulcerative lesion on the initial endoscopy. According to the Lungao staging system, 142 patients (92.8%) were classified as having clinical stage I. Among the patients who underwent EUS (n=115), 87 (75.7%) had gastric MALT lymphoma confined to the mucosa, while 24 (20.9%) and 4 (3.5%) patients showed submucosal and proper muscle invasion, respectively. Seventy-seven patients underwent BM biopsy and none of them showed disease involvement.

Patient comparison between the aggressive and indolent group

Out of the 153 patients, 35 patients were included in the aggressive group: 13 patients who relapsed after complete remission, 1 patient who progressed, 5 patients who developed high-grade lymphoma, and 16 patients who had residual disease. The characteristics of the aggressive and indolent groups are presented in Table 2. The aggressive group showed higher prevalence of factors such as old age (≥57) (30.4%), sex (male) (29.5%), H. pylori-positive (23.0%), proximal and extensive (31.2%), ulcerative (25.0%), clinical stage II or higher (63.6%), and submucosal and proper muscle invasion (32.1%). The χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests indicated that the significant factors associated with the aggressive group were old age, sex (male), and clinical stage II or higher. There was no significant difference in the follow-up period between the 2 groups. Moreover, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses revealed that old age, sex (male), and clinical stage II or higher were significantly associated with aggression, with odds ratios of 2.50 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.15–5.74), 2.20 (95% CI, 1.02–4.95), and 7.13 (95% CI, 2.01–28.82), respectively. Through multivariate analysis similar results were obtained (Table 3).

Treatment outcomes according to H. pylori infection status

The clinical courses of the patients are summarized in Figs. 1 and 2. Among H. pylori-positive patients (n=122), 116 received eradication therapy as initial therapy. The eradication success rate was 77.6% (n=90) after the first regimen and increased to 94.8% (n=110) and 96.6% (n=112) when including the second and third regimens, respectively. After eradication, 79 patients (68.1%) showed ChR/pMRD at the second endoscopy, among which 71 (61.2%) maintained complete response, 6 (5.2%) experienced relapse, and 2 (1.7%) developed high-grade lymphoma during the follow-up period. Thirty-seven patients (31.9%) showed rRD/NC during second endoscopy after H. pylori eradication. Of these, 21 (18.1%) achieved complete response, 1 (0.9%) progressed, 1 (0.9%) developed high-grade lymphoma, and 14 (12.1%) had residual disease. Three patients (2.6%) with complete response experienced relapse during the follow-up period. Most patients experiencing relapse, transformation into high-grade lymphoma, progression, or residual disease received subsequent therapies, such as CTx or RTx, as presented in Fig. 1. Among the 6 patients who initially received RTx and subsequently exhibited ChR/pMRD during the second endoscopy, 1 showed high-grade lymphoma during follow-up, which was treated with CTx. The median follow-up period for the 122 H. pylori-positive patients was 59 months (range, 36–115).

For H. pylori-negative patients (n=31), various initial treatment options were available, including eradication, CTx, RTx, and observation. Among the 5 patients who received eradication as the initial therapy, 2 showed ChR/pMRD at the second esophagogastroduodenoscopy, however 1 patient experienced relapse post therapy and was treated with RTx. Out of the remaining three patients with rRD/NC at the second endoscopy, 2 received RTx due to residual disease. Twenty-two patients received RTx as the initial treatment, and during the entire follow-up period, except for one, all patients achieved complete remission without relapse. Two patients received CTx as initial treatment and achieved complete remission, however one patient had a relapse later. Two patients were observed without any treatment initially; 1 showed complete remission, however received RTx due to relapse, while the other patient experienced transformation of the disease into high-grade lymphoma, requiring subsequent CTx. The median follow-up period for the 31 H. pylori-negative patients was 58 months (range, 39–124).

Survival and continuous remission rate

The median follow-up period for the 153 patients was 59 months (mean, 61; range, 36–124). A total of 5 deaths were observed, due to lymphoma (n=1), liver cirrhosis (n=1), and hepatocellular carcinoma (n=1). The causes of death were unknown in 2 patients who were in complete remission at the last follow-up date. Considering all deaths as events, the estimated OS percentages at 1, 3, and 5 years were 100.0%, 100.0%, and 97.7%, respectively. There were 4 (3.36%) deaths in the indolent group and 1 (2.94%) death in the aggressive group. The log-rank test indicated no significant difference in OS between the aggressive and indolent groups (P=0.57). The Kaplan-Meier curves for the 153 patients and the indolent versus aggressive groups are shown in Fig. 3.

Among the 149 patients who achieved complete remission, the median time to complete remission was 14.8 weeks (mean, 24.2; range, 4–167). The median continuous remission period was 51 months (mean, 50; range, 3–112). During the remission period, 16 (10.7%) patients experienced relapse or transformation to high-grade lymphoma. Among these, 13 (8.7%) patients had relapses of MALT lymphoma, while 3 (2.0%) patients showed transformation to high-grade lymphoma. The median time from remission to these events was 28 months (mean, 31; range, 3–74). The continuous remission rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 97.3%, 93.1%, and 86.8%, respectively. Endoscopic findings in H. pylori-negative patients

Nineteen patients were identified as H. pylori-negative based on at least 3 tests taken by the patients. For comparison, an identical number of H. pylori-positive patients were selected based on age and sex. A lower incidence of atrophic gastritis was observed in H. pylori-negative patients than in H. pylori-positive patients (21.1% vs. 52.6%, P=0.043) (Table 4). However, no differences were observed in atrophic gastritis of the antrum or intestinal metaplasia of the body and antrum between the H. pylori-negative and H. pylori-positive patients. The most common findings on H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative endoscopies were atrophic and erosive lesions, respectively. No significant differences were observed in the incidence of these lesions. The endoscopic lesions of H. pylori-negative patients are shown in Fig. 5.

DISCUSSION

Our retrospective study indicates that patients with gastric MALT lymphoma generally experience favorable outcomes in terms of remission, remission duration, and OS. As shown in Figs. 1 and 2, most patients who were H. pylori-positive were initially treated with eradication therapy, with a remission rate of 68.1% (79/116) during the first follow-up endoscopy and 86.2% (100/116) during the follow-up later. These findings align with those of other studies conducted in Korea wherein a remission rate of 82.3%–94.4% was observed, following eradication [9,10,11,12]. Among the five H. pylori-negative patients treated with eradication as the initial approach, only 2 (40%) maintained complete remission without requiring additional treatment. With respect to the treatment of H. pylori-negative patients, a trial of eradication is recommended, however the level of evidence is weak [6,13,14]. Majority of patients who were H. pylori-negative in our study received RTx at first (n=22, 71%), although 21 patients were in clinical stage I, and 11 out of 17 patients showed invasion confined to the mucosa on EUS. Except for 1 patient who experienced relapse, all patients in this group maintained continuous remission.

Six H. pylori-positive patients received RTx as first-line therapy, among which 3 patients exhibited submucosal and proper muscle invasion, a factor associated with poor response to eradication and 1 patient had already undergone eradication before the diagnosis of gastric MALT lymphoma.4 Among the seven H. pylori-positive patients with clinical stage II or higher, 6 initially received eradication as first-line therapy. Following eradication, 3 patients did not achieve complete remission, necessitating further therapy. Submucosal invasion, t(11;18)/API2-MALT1, and regional nodal involvement are known to correlate with poor response to eradication [4,6,15]. Thus, close monitoring of these patients is recommended.

A H. pylori-negative patient with localized hyperemic erosion at the high body greater curvature was observed without any treatment and showed complete remission during the second endoscopy. However, during the 5-year follow-up endoscopy, the patient experienced a recurrence of gastric MALT lymphoma and received RTx in 2022. After treatment, there has been no evidence of disease at follow-up endoscopy until now.

Our study focused on identifying factors associated with relapse, progression, high-grade transformation, and residual disease in patients with gastric MALT lymphoma. Patients with these features were included in the aggressive group, which contained diverse clinical courses, but shared a similarity in requiring second-line treatment owing to the aggressive nature of their disease. Thirty-five patients (13 with relapse, 1 with progression, 5 with high-grade transformation, and 16 with residual disease) included in the aggressive group were compared with the other patients, who were included in the indolent group. As a result, age (≥57), sex (male), and clinical stage II or higher were significantly associated with the aggressive group, while H. pylori status, endoscopic location, and endoscopic features showed no association. These trends were consistent in both univariate and multivariate analyses, with the highest odds ratio observed for clinical stage II or higher. Among patients with EUS results (n=115), age (≥57) and clinical stage II or higher were the only factors associated with the aggressive group in multivariate analysis. This suggests that during the follow-up of patients with gastric MALT lymphoma patients, old age and high clinical stage, attention should be paid to prepare for second-line therapy. Furthermore, our study revealed no cases of BM involvement (n=77), which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [16,17].

After achieving remission, patients were typically followed up for at least 5 years in our hospital, during which 16 of 149 patients experienced relapse or transformation into high-grade lymphoma. The continuous remission rate at 5 years was 86.8%, which was higher than the finding (71%) in a large cohort study by Wündisch et al. [5]. Another study by Nakamura et al. [4], involving Japanese population, showed that the freedom from treatment failure rate at 5 years was 92%. Given the observed higher rates of lymphoma remission in Asian populations than in Western populations [18], further research focusing on ethnicity will be necessary.

The OS rate of gastric MALT lymphoma in our study was 97.7% after 5 years, which was similar to findings from other studies ranging from 90% to 99% [3,4,5]. A recent study conducted in France reported a 5-year OS of 79% in patients with gastric MALT lymphoma, attributing this to a lower remission rate and sub-optimal treatment [15]. In our study, we observed no difference in OS between the aggressive and indolent groups, suggesting that an aggressive disease course might not necessarily correlate with survival rates when patients receive adequate follow-up and treatment. The median follow-up period in our study was approximately 5 years, which was sufficiently long; however, further follow-up may be beneficial.

In previous studies, the prevalence of atrophic gastritis in patients with gastric MALT lymphoma ranged from 20% to 30% [19,20], however no study has assessed the population of H. pylori-negative patients. In our study, among the selected H. pylori-positive patients, a high proportion (approximately 50%) of atrophic gastritis was observed in the body, whereas a lower proportion was observed in the bodies of selected H. pylori-negative patients. In the antrum, a high proportion (approximately 50%) of atrophic gastritis in H. pylori-negative patients was observed, which might be due to the existence of other Helicobacter species infections [21,22]. Further studies examining the microbiome of patients with gastric MALT lymphoma might be beneficial.

However, our study has some limitations. First, many patients diagnosed with gastric MALT lymphoma were excluded due to a short follow-up period of less than 3 years or lack of important data, and there might be selection biases. Second, there was a disparity in the choice of first-line treatment between the two groups, with more RTx selected in the indolent group. This is because many H. pylori-negative patients were included in the indolent group, which may have influenced the clinical course of the disease. Third, we attempted to determine whether patients who were t(11;18) positive had a poor remission rate among those who underwent FISH analysis; however, only 16 patients underwent FISH. In contrast to previous studies [23,24,25], no difference in the rate of achieving remission between FISH-positive (80%, 4/5) and FISH-negative cases (63.6%, 7/11) by Fisher’s exact test (P=0.46) was observed. Moreover, no significant difference in the prevalence of t(11;18) between the aggressive (1/4, 25.0%) and indolent (5/14, 35.7%) groups according to Fisher’s exact test (P=1.000) was observed. The long-term prognosis of gastric MALT lymphoma concerning t(11;18) is not yet known and thus further research is necessary.

Nevertheless, our study has several strengths. It encompasses patients with various conditions and reflects real-world clinical scenarios. We aimed to identify traits associated with an aggressive disease course, demonstrating that old age, sex (male), and clinical stage II lymphoma or higher were correlated with this group. However, we did not observe a difference in OS between the aggressive and indolent groups. The continuous remission rate remained high, as in a previous study, although there was a slight decrease to 86.8% after five years. Additionally, endoscopy in H. pylori-negative patients revealed a high incidence of atrophic gastritis in the antrum.

In conclusion, the long-term prognosis of gastric MALT lymphoma appears to be indolent in terms of OS and continuous remission. When patients who experienced relapse, progression, transformation into high-grade lymphoma, or residual disease were categorized into the aggressive group, some factors were associated with the aggressive group. Moreover, no significant difference in OS was observed between the aggressive and indolent groups. Further research on the microbiota of H. pylori-negative gastric MALT lymphomas is warranted because of the high incidence of atrophic gastritis.
