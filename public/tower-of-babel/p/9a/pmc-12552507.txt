Repulsive guidance molecule A in the resolution of inflammation from ischemic stroke-associated pneumonia in mice
Authors: Jiaju Zhong, Taoying Xiong, Xiaoqin Luo, Lingcong Li, Wenwen Li, Yue Ma, Xiaohui Wu, Jing Wang, Rongrong Zhang, Xinyue Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke is an important global health concern. The most prevalent infectious complication after stroke is stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP), which severely impairs recovery of neurological function and has an adverse impact on the prognosis. However, the pathogenesis of SAP is largely unknown. We established a model of SAP by middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) and identified spontaneous pneumonia in mice with MCAO/R by staining the lung tissue with hematoxylin–eosin and measuring the bacterial load in the lungs and expression levels of inflammatory factors in plasma. We also confirmed expression of repulsive guidance molecule A (RGMa) in splenic mononuclear cells (SpMNCs) following MCAO/R surgery. Recombinant mouse RGMa protein, propranolol, and the glucocorticoid receptor blocker RU486 were administered to investigate the effects of RGMa on SAP and elucidate the underlying mechanism. Marked spontaneous pneumonia was observed in the mice that had undergone MCAO/R. RGMa expression in SpMNCs from these mice was significantly reduced at 24 and 72 h following MCAO/R surgery. Exogenous administration of recombinant mouse RGMa markedly attenuated spontaneous pneumonia in MCAO/R mice. After administration of propranolol, there was a significant increase in the expression level of RGMa in SpMNCs and amelioration of spontaneous pneumonia in these mice. Administration of RU486 did not significantly change the RGMa expression level or ameliorate spontaneous pneumonia. RGMa attenuated spontaneous pneumonia in mice that had undergone MCAO/R. The underlying mechanism may involve regulation of sympathetic excitation. Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a significant cause of mortality, causing an estimated 3.48 million deaths in 20201. Stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP) is the most prevalent complication following acute ischemic stroke, with an incidence rate ranging from 6.7% to 47%2–5. SAP is a critical determinant of neurological recovery in stroke patients and is associated with increased mortality6,7. However, the mechanism of SAP has been unclear.

Stroke-induced immunodepression syndrome is posited to be a primary contributor to the development of SAP8–10 because it increases the risk of infection11. Following a stroke, activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems can alter the composition and functionality of the immune system. This alteration may lead to atrophy of peripheral immune organs, including the spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes, a reduced number of peripheral immune cells, and functional inactivation of these cells12–14, which results in a predisposition to post-stroke infections, particularly SAP15.

Repulsive guidance molecule A (RGMa) is a 33-kDa protein that is anchored to the cell membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol16,17. RGMa is involved in various biological functions, including regulating survival of neurons, synapse formation, axon growth, angiogenesis, and immune responses17–20. RGMa has multiple negative effects on the area of the brain damaged after stroke, including facilitating disruption of the blood–brain barrier, inflammation-related injury, and proliferation of reactive astrocytes21–23. However, RGMa has a positive impact in SAP. Our clinical studies have indicated a significant reduction in the concentration of RGMa in the plasma of patients who develop SAP, and can predict the occurrence and development of SAP at an early stage24. It has been reported that activation of sympathetic nerve endings promoted an increase in RGMa expression and that RGMa facilitated resolution of inflammation and tissue repair in a mouse model of acute peritonitis25. In view of these findings, we hypothesized that peripheral immune suppression after SAP is triggered by activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the HPA axis, and that RGMA is involved in regulating SAP-related inflammation.

Given that SAP had previously been characterized in the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) model11,26,27, we established an SAP model in mice that had undergone MCAO/R. These mice developed spontaneous pneumonia postoperatively and showed a significant decrease in expression of RGMa in splenic mononuclear cells (SpMNCs). Furthermore, supplementation with exogenous rm-RGMa significantly ameliorated the spontaneous pneumonia that developed in these mice. After administration of the β-receptor blocker propranolol to inhibit the sympathetic nervous system and the glucocorticoid receptor blocker RU486 to inhibit the HPA axis, we found that inhibition of sympathetic excitation promoted expression of RGMa and facilitated the resolution of inflammation associated with spontaneous pneumonia in this model.

Methods

Creation of the SAP model

MCAO/R in C57BL/6 mice was used to produce a model for study of SAP11,26. Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 8–10 weeks) provided by the Experimental Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University were used in our experiments, all of which were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval number: 2021 − 500). The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium at a dosage of 50 mg/kg. The right common carotid artery, right external carotid artery, and right internal carotid artery were exposed. Arteriotomy was performed in the external carotid artery and a suture embolus was inserted. After 60 min of ischemia, the embolus was withdrawn to allow reperfusion. Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C on a thermostatic blanket during the surgery and postoperatively. A sham group underwent the same procedures as the MCAO/R group, except that a suture embolus was not inserted.

Ethical approval

All animal experiments strictly adhered to the guidelines established by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval number: 2021 − 500). The procedures conformed to international standards for animal ethics and welfare. All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Recombinant mouse RGMa protein (rm-RGMa) drug treatment

The mice were randomly divided into three groups. The first group was the sham group and did not receive any medication; the second group was the control group (Control + MCAO/R) and used bovine serum albumin (Genvi Science, USA) as the control protein. The administration method, time point, and volume were exactly the same as those of the rm-RGMa treatment group. The third group was the rm-RGMa treatment group (rm-RGMa + MCAO/R), where rm-RGMa (R&D, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline at a concentration of 100 µg/mL and injected via the tail vein at 4 h, 24 h, and 48 h after MCAO/R at a dose of 1 µg/mouse25.

Propranolol/RU486 drug treatment

Propranolol/RU486 drug solution preparation: Briefly, freeze-dried powders containing propranolol and RU486 (100 mg each) were dissolved in 3.3 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, containing the drug) and centrifuged to prepare 30 mg/mL DMSO working solutions. Before each injection, according to the total dosage required for one experiment, a 3 mg/mL working solution was prepared by mixing 10% DMSO (containing the drug), 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% normal saline at a specified ratio. Subsequently, the mice were randomly divided into four groups. The first group, the sham group, did not receive any medication; the second group, the control group (Control + MCAO/R), used a blank solvent as the control, with the composition being: 10% DMSO (without drugs), 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% physiological saline. The administration method, time points, and volume were exactly the same as those of the corresponding propranolol treatment group or RU486 treatment group. The third group, the propranolol treatment group (propranolol + MCAO/R), used the propranolol working solution prepared by the above method (3 mg/mL). The administration was given by intraperitoneal injection at 1 h, 4 h, and 8 h after MCAO/R surgery. The injection dose was 30 mg/kg. The fourth group, the RU486 treatment group (RU486 + MCAO/R), used the RU486 working solution prepared by the above method (3 mg/mL). The administration was given by intraperitoneal injection 24 h before MCAO/R surgery, as well as 1 h and 5 h after the surgery. The injection dose was 20 mg/kg28,29.

Neurological scoring

The Longa score was determined after MCAO/R surgery. The following scoring criteria system was used: 0, no neurological deficit; 1, left forelimb could not extend forward; 2, turning to the left; 3, tilting to the left; 4, no voluntary movement and impaired consciousness. Mice with scores ranging from 1 to 3 were included in the experiment and those with subarachnoid hemorrhage or a score of 0 or 4 were excluded.

Collection of blood and tissue samples

At 72 h after MCAO/R, the mice were euthanized by rapid cervical dislocation. Blood was collected by decapitation and placed in EDTA anticoagulant tubes, followed by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was aliquoted into 200-µL enzyme-free centrifuge tubes and stored at − 80 °C for future use. Lung and spleen tissues were collected at the same time.

Histopathological analysis of lung tissue

After the mice were fully anesthetized, the lung tissues were extracted, post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). Two investigators who were blinded to the treatment groups evaluated the lung sections.

Microbiological analysis of lung tissue

The mice were euthanized at designated time points after MCAO/R and washed with 70% ethanol under sterile conditions. The lung lobes were collected, minced, and homogenized. Next, 1 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline was added and mixed well with the lung tissue homogenate; the mixture was then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant (500 µL). The lung tissue homogenate supernatant was serially diluted 10-fold to 10−5. Aliquots (10 µL) from a series of dilutions (10−3, 10−4, and 10−5) were used to inoculate Columbia blood agar plates and cultured at 37 °C in a constant temperature incubator for 18 h. The number of colony-forming units was then counted by two investigators who were blinded to the treatment groups. The bacterial load was defined as the average number of colonies × 10² × dilution factor27,30.

Extraction and counting of SpMNCs

The spleen tissues were removed, the residual liquid on the surface was aspirated, and the spleens were weighed. SpMNCs were extracted from the spleen samples using standard procedures. SMC suspension (10 µL) was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (1000 µL), and the cells were counted using a hemocytometer.

Quantitative real-time PCR assays

Total RNA was isolated from SpMNCs using a TRIzol reagent kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Complementary DNA was synthesized using a Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The following primers were used in the real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) experiments: 5’-GACATCAAGAAGGTGGTGAAGC-3’ and 5’-GAAGGTGGAAGAGTGGGAGTT-3’ (GAPDH), and 5’-AAGTACATCGGCACCACCATC-3’ and 5’-TCCTCCACGGCGTTGACTAC-3’ (RGMa). The mRNA level of each transcript was standardized to the abundance of GAPDH mRNA obtained from the same sample. The relative mRNA level was calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

Mouse plasma and lung tissue homogenate were collected, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) were performed using a Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (Catalog no. KE10007; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), a TNF-α ELISA Kit (Catalog no. KE10002; Proteintech), and an IFN-γ ELISA Kit (Catalog no. KE10001; Proteintech) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

SpMNCs were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride protease inhibitor (MedChemExpress). The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid-based kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h, and probed with primary antibodies against the following proteins: RGMa (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and α-tubulin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, USA) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were incubated with specific secondary antibodies (1:5000) on a shaker at room temperature for 60 min and washed three times with TBST (for 10 min each time). Images were captured and quantified using a Fusion FX5 imaging system.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as the frequency (percentage) and continuous data as the mean ± standard error. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare the means of multiple samples, the log-rank test was used for survival rates, and two-way analysis of variance was used for changes in weight. All the experiments were independently repeated at least three times. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Development of spontaneous pneumonia in mice that had undergone MCAO/R

We examined the lung histopathology, bacterial load in the lung, and plasma expression levels of inflammatory factors in peripheral blood to determine whether the mice that underwent MCAO/R developed spontaneous pneumonia. HE staining of lung tissue revealed that the mice in the sham surgery group had no apparent signs of pneumonia, whereas the mice in the MCAO/R group showed typical changes of bacterial pneumonia 72 h after MCAO/R surgery, which manifested as lung consolidation, thickening of the alveolar septum, and infiltration of inflammatory cells within the alveoli (Fig. 1A). Bacterial culture of the lung tissue indicated that all the mice had bacterial loads in lung tissue 24 h and 72 h after MCAO/R, particularly at 72 h, whereas the lung tissue cultures of the mice in the sham group remained essentially sterile for 72 h (Fig. 1B and C). ELISAs revealed that the expression levels of interleukin (IL)−6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and interferon (IFN)-γ in plasma and lung tissue homogenates were significantly greater in the MCAO/R group than in the sham group (Fig. 1D and E). These findings confirmed that mice that underwent MCAO/R developed spontaneous pneumonia within 72 h after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion.

Peripheral immune suppression in MCAO/R mice and a marked decrease in the RGMa expression level in SpMNCs

We examined the spleen to determine whether the peripheral immune system was suppressed in mice in the MCAO/R group. In contrast with the sham group, mice in the MCAO/R group showed evidence of splenic atrophy, a reduced spleen weight (Fig. 2A and B), and a decreased number of SpMNCs (Fig. 2C) at 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after surgery. Next, we explored the expression of RGMa in peripheral immune cells after MCAO/R by isolating SpMNCs from the mice. The quantitative RT-PCR results indicated that the expression of RGMa mRNA in SpMNCs was significantly lower in the MCAO/R group than in the sham group at 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after surgery (Fig. 2D). Western blotting revealed that expression of RGMa in SpMNCs from the MCAO/R group was markedly lower than that in SpMNCs from the sham group, with the most pronounced differences noted at 24 h and 72 h and no significant difference observed at 7 days (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that the expression level of RGMa in SpMNCs was significantly lower on the first and third days after MCAO/R compared to the sham group.

Rm-RGMa alleviated SAP and promoted weight recovery

We administered rm-RGMa via tail vein injection to explore the role of RGMa in spontaneous pneumonia in the MCAO/R group further. Compared with the findings in mice in the control group, HE staining of lung tissue from mice in the MCAO/R group that were injected with rm-RGMa showed reductions in lung consolidation, thickening of the alveolar septum, and infiltration of inflammatory cells within the alveoli (Fig. 3A and C). Moreover, the expression levels of the inflammatory factors IL-6 and IFN-γ in plasma were significantly lower in the rm-RGMa group than in the control group (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the bacterial load int the lung was reduced relative to that in the control group (Fig. 3E). These findings suggested that exogenous rm-RGMa supplementation could improve spontaneous pneumonia in mice in the MCAO/R group. Furthermore, mice in both the rm-RGMa and control groups lost weight between days 1 and 4 post-surgery; however, mice in the rm-RGMa group showed more pronounced weight recovery between days 5 and 7 (Fig. 3F). Although there was no significant difference in the survival rate between the two groups during the 7-day observation period (Fig. 3G), the median time to death was longer in the rm-RGMa group than in the vehicle group (day 4 vs. day 2 post-surgery). This delayed mortality may be associated with the partial attenuation of spontaneous pneumonia in the MCAO/R group following exogenous RGMa supplementation. However, administration of rm-RGMa did not have a significant impact on splenic atrophy or the number of SpMNCs (Fig. 3H and I).

Inhibition of sympathetic excitation significantly increased RGMa expression in SpMNCs from MCAO/R mice and improved peripheral immunosuppression

We administered propranolol to inhibit sympathetic excitation and RU486 to inhibit activation of the HPA axis and assessed their effects on the expression level of RGMa in SpMNCs in the MCAO/R mouse model. At 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after MCAO/R surgery, the relative expression levels of RGMa mRNA in both the propranolol and RU486 groups were elevated compared with those in the control group, with the propranolol group demonstrating a more pronounced increase (Fig. 4A). At the level of protein expression, the RGMa expression level was significantly greater in mice in the propranolol group than in those in the control group at 24 h and 72 h post-surgery; however, the difference was no longer significant at 7 days. In contrast, in the RU486 group, there was no significant difference in expression of RGMa protein at 24 h, 72 h, or 7 days after MCAO/R than in the control group (Fig. 4B and C). Therefore, in the MCAO/R group, propranolol inhibited sympathetic excitation and significantly increased the transcriptional and protein expression levels of RGMa in SpMNCs. In contrast, while suppression of the HPA axis by RU486 was associated with an increase in the transcription of RGMa mRNA, this did not lead to a corresponding increase in expression of RGMa protein. Splenic atrophy and the reduced number of SpMNCs was significantly ameliorated in mice in the MCAO/R group that received propranolol in comparison with the control group; however, no significant differences were observed in mice in the MCAO/R group that received RU486 (Fig. 4D and E). These findings indicated that the reversal of peripheral immune suppression in mice in the MCAO/R group might be associated with inhibition of sympathetic excitation by propranolol.

Inhibition of sympathetic excitation improved spontaneous pneumonia after MCAO/R and enhanced the survival rate

The ability of propranolol to ameliorate spontaneous pneumonia in mice in the MCAO/R group was evaluated further at 72 h postoperatively. Compared with the findings in the control and RU486 groups, propranolol significantly ameliorated lung consolidation, reduced alveolar septal widening, and decreased alveolar inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 5A and D). Furthermore, administration of propranolol resulted in a marked reduction in the bacterial load in the lung (Fig. 5E) and decreased plasma levels of inflammatory factors, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ (Fig. 5F), thereby facilitating resolution of the inflammation associated with spontaneous pneumonia in these mice. The weight of the mice treated with propranolol rapidly returned to baseline, whereas slower recovery was observed in the RU486 and control groups (Fig. 5G). Throughout the 7-day observation period, the survival rate was significantly greater in the propranolol group than in the RU486 and control groups, with no notable difference between the RU486 and control groups (Fig. 5H). These results indicated that propranolol promoted rapid weight recovery and improved the likelihood of survival after SAP.

In summary, mice that underwent MCAO/R developed SAP, which was accompanied by a significant reduction in the expression of RGMa in SpMNCs. Exogenous administration of rm-RGMa significantly alleviated SAP-related inflammation. These findings support our hypothesis that RGMa plays a role in regulating the inflammation associated with SAP. The underlying mechanism may involve peripheral immunosuppression induced by sympathetic activation following cerebral ischemia–reperfusion (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our previous research has demonstrated that RGMa has a negative effect on regions of the brain injury that are damaged following stroke. RGMa promotes disruption of the blood–brain barrier, reactive astrocytosis and formation of glial scars, polarization of microglia toward the M1 phenotype, and release of proinflammatory cytokines21–23. However, our clinical research has shown that RGMa has a protective effect on SAP and that plasma RGMa levels in patients with arterial ischemic stroke and SAP are significantly decreased, and a reduced plasma RGMa level might assist in early identification of high-risk patients with SAP24. Prass et al.27,30 demonstrated that spontaneous pneumonia and sepsis consistently developed within 72 h in mice that had undergone MCAO/R. By examination of pulmonary histopathology, quantitative assessment of the bacterial burden in lung tissues, and systematic analysis of plasma expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in peripheral blood, we confirmed the development of SAP after MCAO/R in mice. Our findings align with the pathological manifestations documented by Prass et al.27,30. However, the precise pathogenic origin of the pulmonary infections that occur in this experimental model remains undetermined and represents a critical focus for future investigations to elucidate the mechanisms underlying infectious complications post-stroke. We also found that expression of RGMa in SpMNCs was significantly reduced in mice that had undergone MCAO/R. Exogenous administration of rm-RGMa significantly improved the histopathology of lung tissue, the bacterial load in lung tissue, and the levels of inflammatory factors in plasma, thereby ameliorating SAP in these mice. Our current research results indicate that RGMa may have a protective effect on SAP. However, our previous research has confirmed that RGMa has a negative effect on the brain injury areas after a stroke. Thus, the treatment of spontaneous pneumonia with rm-RGMa may have a dual effect on the brain and lung tissues. Ideally, rm-RGMa should directly target the inflammatory lung tissue and avoid the brain. Recently, the development of nano-targeted delivery systems sparks hope for this approach. This system employs nanoparticles to deliver drugs specifically to diseased sites, enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing side effects31. In theory, rm-RGMa can be encapsulated in a nanoparticle that targets the post-stroke inflammatory lung tissue, and the whole process does not go through the brain. We plan to develop an rm-RGMa nano-targeted delivery system for post-stroke pneumonia in future research.

It is widely known that the sympathetic nervous system is activated after cerebral ischemia, releasing substances such as adrenaline and thereby inducing peripheral immunosuppression, including atrophy of peripheral immune organs and a decrease in the number of peripheral immune cells13,32,33, resulting in post-stroke infections, particularly pneumonia34–36. Our research indicates that obvious peripheral immunosuppression and SAP occur in mice that have undergone MCAO/R and that administration of rm-RGMa ameliorates spontaneous pneumonia in these mice. Previous studies have demonstrated that peripheral immunosuppression after MCAO/R is associated with excessive sympathetic activation as a consequence of cerebral ischemia12,13,37. Furthermore, the HPA axis is activated after brain ischemia, releasing glucocorticoids and other substances to induce peripheral immune suppression12,32,33. Therefore, excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the HPA axis after ischemic stroke plays a crucial role in the onset and development of SAP. Propranolol and RU486 have been extensively investigated for their inhibitory effects on sympathetic excitation and activation of the HPA axis in MCAO/R mice25,27,30,34. Prass et al.30 used propranolol to block sympathetic excitation in these mice, which reduced the volume of cerebral infarction, promoted immune activation, and increased the splenic volume. Inhibition of activation of the HPA axis by RU486 can reduce the volume of cerebral infarction, reduce the risk of infection, and increase the likelihood of long-term survival28. Therefore, we used propranolol and RU486 to inhibit sympathetic excitation and activation of the HPA axis, respectively, so that we could explore further the underlying mechanisms via which RGMa participates in regulation of SAP. Compared with the expression level of RU486, that of RGMa significantly increased after administration of propranolol, with improvement of peripheral immunosuppression and amelioration of SAP. These findings indicate that the role of the HPA axis in SAP might not be as important as that of the sympathetic nervous system. Prass et al. reported that although inhibition of the HPA axis by RU486 had beneficial effects on lymphocyte reduction, lymphocyte apoptosis, and monocyte dysfunction, it did not reduce the bacterial load in the lung tissue of MCAO/R mice, which is in contrast with the effects observed with propranolol30. Our results are consistent with these observations. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that blocking sympathetic activation in MCAO/R mice using propranolol can significantly reduce the cerebral infarction volume in these mice, increase the antigen-presenting T-cell response in the central nervous system, inhibit splenic atrophy and apoptosis of splenocytes, and reduce the bacterial load in the lungs, thereby improving post-stroke pneumonia27,38. Clinical studies have also revealed that administration of a β-receptor blocker may reduce the mortality rate of stroke and the incidence of post-stroke pneumonia39. In summary, the sympathetic nervous system plays a crucial role in the onset and development of SAP, and the peripheral immunosuppression caused by sympathetic activation might be the main cause of SAP.

Previous studies have indicated that the role of RGMa in the inflammatory response mainly involves interaction with the receptor neogenin, inhibition of leukocyte migration, enhancement of CD4+ T-cell adhesion to intercellular adhesion molecule-1, reduction in the release of inflammatory mediators, and attenuation of the inflammatory response25,40. In a model of acute lung injury, binding of RGMa to its receptor neogenin significantly inhibited migration of leukocytes, decreased the release of proinflammatory factors, and promoted resolution of inflammation in the lungs of mice with lung injury41,42. Here, we found that both exogenous rm-RGMa and propranolol could ameliorate SAP; however, propranolol could improve peripheral immune suppression, whereas exogenous supplementation with rm-RGMa could not. These findings indicate that exogenous supplementation with rm-RGMa cannot reverse the peripheral immune suppression caused by cerebral ischemia through a “bottom-up” approach, which prompted us to contemplate further the potential mechanisms by which RGMa participates in regulating SAP. Notably, we found that the expression level of RGMa significantly increased after administration of propranolol to inhibit sympathetic excitation, suggesting an interrelationship between the sympathetic nervous system and RGMa. A recent study demonstrated a bidirectional activation effect between RGMa and adrenergic receptors located at the ends of sympathetic nerves. Specifically, activation of sympathetic nerve endings can increase the expression of RGMa. By inhibiting the activity of NF-κB and activating the rapamycin-insensitive companion of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (RICTOR) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathways, RGMa can facilitate the resolution of inflammation and tissue repair in mice with acute peritonitis25. Thus, RGMa and the sympathetic nervous system interact, and different experimental outcomes have been observed in various experimental models. The mechanism of action of RGMa in SAP may be related to peripheral immune suppression resulting from excessive sympathetic excitation after stroke.

This research has some limitations. First, flow cytometry and magnetic bead sorting techniques were not used to re-sort and culture the SpMNCs. Second, the specific mechanisms via which RGMa participates in regulating inflammation, particularly on the cell membrane or intracellularly within specific immune cells, were not examined in detail. A third limitation lies in the unresolved questions of whether rm-RGMa can traverse the blood–brain barrier to achieve intracranial biodistribution and whether it has neurobiological effects on the cerebral parenchyma. This critical knowledge gap regarding whether rm-RGMa can cross the blood–brain barrier and its potential interaction with cerebral tissue remains unaddressed in our experimental framework and should be prioritized in subsequent experimental investigations for evaluation of the neuropharmacokinetic profile of this agent in more depth.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that expression of RGMa is decreased in SpMNCs from mice that have undergone MCAO/R and that exogenous rm-RGMa supplementation can significantly ameliorate spontaneous pneumonia in these mice. The decrease in RGMa in SpMNCs from these mice is associated with peripheral immune suppression resulting from excessive sympathetic excitation after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion. RGMa is worthy of further investigation as an important effector molecule involved in the onset and development of SAP.
