Lightweight hybrid neural network with physics consistency regularization for lithium-ion battery state of health estimation
Authors: Pingan Zhang, Hui Zhang, Jiayi Zhou, Qin Zhou, Jiaqi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate State of Health (SOH) prediction for lithium-ion batteries is critical, yet conventional data-driven methods often lack interpretability and can generalize poorly under distribution shifts, while model-driven methods are computationally costly. PINN-inspired regularization provides a practical compromise by incorporating physics-consistency priors into data-driven models. This study proposes an Improved Lightweight Hybrid PINN-inspired model (IHPINN) with three core innovations. First, an efficient feature extractor replaces large fully connected layers with grouped linearization, low-rank approximation, and parameter sharing, significantly reducing weights and floating-point operations (FLOPs). Second, an adaptive activation module learnably gates sinusoidal and Swish activations, adjusting nonlinearity to mitigate gradient issues and stabilize convergence. Third, a shared-attention mechanism uses a single shared projection for Q, K, and V to reduce redundancy. The resulting model combines data fitting with PINN-inspired regularization (learned dynamics consistency and monotonicity) to achieve stable temporal representations and a better data-regularization balance with fewer parameters. Compared to a baseline PINN, IHPINN achieves a 52.8% MSE improvement on the HUST dataset with 19.3% fewer parameters and 14.4% less training time, and a 39.1% MSE improvement on the MIT dataset.

Introduction

As a cornerstone of modern energy storage technology, accurate prediction of battery State of Health (SOH) is crucial for the safe operation of Battery Management Systems (BMS). In 2023, global electric car sales neared 14 million units, accounting for about 18% of new car sales1. In parallel, lithium-ion battery pack prices dropped to a record low of $115/kWh in 20242, accelerating the deployment of batteries in both electric vehicles and stationary storage. This convergence of demand growth and cost declines compels SOH estimation algorithms to achieve a superior trade-off between accuracy, reliability, and computational cost. Mechanistically, battery degradation spans multiple physics and time scales and is highly sensitive to operating conditions3, motivating a fusion paradigm that integrates ”data + physics”. Public benchmarks for reproducible validation are also improving. The MIT dataset4, for instance, covers 124 lithium iron phosphate (LFP)/graphite 18650 cells with lifetimes from 150 to 2300 cycles, providing a foundation for standardized preprocessing and fair evaluation.

Current research methods can be categorized into three main types. First, data-driven methods, which include strategies based on deep neural networks5, convolutional neural networks6, temporal convolutional networks7, or ensemble learning8, have achieved good accuracy on public and real-world datasets9,10. However, they can be data-hungry, their ”black-box” nature leads to limited interpretability, and their generalization is often insufficient under small-sample, out-of-distribution, or varying-condition scenarios. Second, model-based methods, such as equivalent circuit models (ECM)11 or fusion models based on improved particle filters12, possess physical interpretability but suffer from complex parameter identification and calibration processes, making it difficult to balance real-time performance with computational accuracy. Third, physics-informed learning has emerged as a compromise pathway by injecting physics consistency (e.g., bounds, monotonicity, or governing-equation residuals when available) as regularization in the training objective13. In battery SOH prediction, many works adopt ”PINN-style” training where physically meaningful constraints are added to the loss function as soft penalties. Nevertheless, such frameworks are still often constrained by parameter redundancy, training instability (particularly gradient pathology problems14), limited generalization across scenarios, and constraints from on-device computational power.

To address these pain points, this paper proposes an IHPINN. Conventional physics-informed neural network

PINN (Physics-Informed Neural Network), proposed by Raissi et al. in 2019, is a novel deep learning methodology13. Its core idea is to embed physical laws (or governing equations) into the neural network’s loss function in the form of soft constraints, thereby achieving an organic integration of data-driven learning and physical knowledge. Representative extensions include fractional and Bayesian PINNs23,24. As background, the basic framework of a classical PINN is illustrated in Fig. 1, which showcases key components such as the neural network architecture, the integration of physical constraints, and the loss function design. For clarity, Fig. 1 is provided only as a background schematic of classical PINNs; the proposed method described in this manuscript is PINN-inspired and does not enforce electrochemical governing equations derived from first principles.Fig. 1Background schematic of a classical PINN.

Here, the term ”PINN-inspired” emphasizes that our regularization terms follow the PINN philosophy (adding consistency constraints into the loss), but we do not claim to solve electrochemical governing equations derived from first principles. Instead, we introduce (i) a learned dynamics-consistency regularizer and (ii) a monotonicity regularizer, both of which serve as soft physics-consistency priors for SOH trajectories. By ”physics-consistency” we refer to widely accepted structural properties of degradation trajectories (temporal consistency and a non-increasing trend under aging), rather than mechanistic electrochemical or thermal PDEs. Therefore, our method should be interpreted as a data-driven model regularized by trajectory-consistency priors, rather than a mechanistic battery model.

Methods improved lightweight hybrid neural network

Following the problem formalization and theoretical groundwork, this section introduces our proposed IHPINN method. Centered around a collaborative dual-network architecture comprising a Solution Network and a Dynamics Network, we systematically elaborate on key mechanisms such as shared attention, adaptive activation, and dynamic loss weighting. Furthermore, we present the lightweight implementation and its complexity analysis, which integrates grouped linearization and low-rank approximation. This approach is designed to address the core challenges of insufficient multi-scale representation, training instability, and limited computational resources.

Experiments

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental results, a controlled experimental design was adopted in this study. The original PINN was used as the baseline model, and experiments were conducted on four publicly available datasets (HUST, MIT, TJU, and XJTU) to validate the generalization capability of the proposed model. To ensure strict comparability across methods, we used a fixed random seed to control stochasticity throughout training and evaluation.

All experiments were conducted under a unified environment. The hardware configuration included an Intel i7-10700K CPU, an RTX 3080 GPU (10 GB), 32 GB of RAM, and a 1 TB SSD. The software environment was based on Ubuntu 20.04, Python 3.8.10, and PyTorch 1.12.1 (CUDA 11.6).

For datasets without an official predefined split, we first split the cells into 80% for training and 20% for testing, and then hold out 20% of the training cells as validation, resulting in an overall ratio of 64%/16%/20% (train/val/test) at the cell level. Datasets

This study systematically validated the performance of IHPINN on four publicly available battery lifespan datasets, covering different battery chemistries and experimental conditions to ensure comprehensive and representative evaluation. Specifically, the HUST dataset adopts LiFePO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document} batteries, with cycle counts ranging from 500 to 1500 and stable temperature conditions, featuring relatively low noise levels. It is primarily used for baseline verification.

The MIT dataset includes multiple charging strategies with moderate temperature variations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {{20}{ - }{45}^{ \circ }\textrm{C}}\right)$$\end{document}, representing high-stress scenarios with diverse operating conditions. The TJU dataset covers two chemical systems, nickel cobalt aluminum (NCA) and nickel cobalt manganese (NCM), under different charge/discharge rates (0.5C-2C), and is used to assess the model’s generalization capability. The XJTU dataset consists of multiple cylindrical battery packs subjected to long-term cycling under constant temperature conditions, providing a basis for evaluating the model’s robustness.

These four datasets encompass different chemistries, C-rates, and temperature conditions, collectively forming a broad and reliable verification platform. To ensure fair comparison, the same splitting protocol was used for datasets without an official split (64%/16%/20% at the cell level for train/valid/test), while predefined battery-level splits were followed when provided by the dataset. SOH was used as the core predictive indicator for model evaluation.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the IHPINN model, this study established a systematic evaluation framework from three dimensions: accuracy, stability, and efficiency. The primary evaluation metrics include the mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), defined as follows: MSE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= \frac{1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$\end{document}, MAE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= \frac{1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^{n}|y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$\end{document}, RMSE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= \sqrt{\textrm{MSE}}$$\end{document}, and MAPE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= \frac{1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^{n}\left| \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i}\right| \times 100\%$$\end{document}. These metrics quantify the overall accuracy and error distribution in SOH prediction, where MSE and RMSE emphasize large-error penalties, MAE reflects absolute deviation, and MAPE measures relative deviation. Auxiliary metrics include the coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$\end{document}), the maximum absolute error (MaxAE), and the Pearson correlation coefficient, which jointly evaluate model fitting, extreme deviation consistency, and correlation strength. Efficiency metrics focus on the lightweight nature and deployability of the model, including the total parameter count, training/inference time, and GPU memory usage. By integrating these multi-dimensional metrics, the comprehensive performance of IHPINN in both prediction accuracy and computational efficiency is analyzed.

To ensure strict comparability across methods, we used a fixed random seed to control stochasticity throughout training and evaluation.

Conclusion and future work

The proposed IHPINN achieves a balance among accuracy, stability, and deployability through the synergistic optimization of a lightweight structure (combining grouped linearization and low-rank approximation), shared attention, adaptive activation, and dynamic weighting. Across four datasets (HUST, MIT, TJU, and XJTU), IHPINN achieved overall reductions of approximately 36.4%, 22.0%, 20.6%, and 21.8% in MSE, MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, respectively, compared to the baseline. Simultaneously, it reduced the parameter count by approximately 19.3%, shortened training time by 17.3%, lowered inference latency by 25.2%, and decreased memory occupation by 14.4%. Furthermore, the model demonstrated smoother convergence and stronger cross-operational-condition consistency, thereby validating the effectiveness and engineering potential of the proposed method for battery SOH prediction.

Nevertheless, IHPINN still exhibits sensitivity to labeled data and weight-scheduling hyperparameters, and the current physics-consistency regularization is limited to learned dynamics consistency and monotonicity, without explicitly modeling electrochemical or thermal governing equations. Future work will proceed along three main lines: First, to deepen physical modeling by incorporating more fine-grained electrochemical/thermal priors (or reduced-order models when available) to strengthen the link between regularization and battery physics26. Second, to enhance adaptability and trustworthiness by introducing uncertainty quantification (UQ)27, online learning, and federated learning28 to improve generalization across diverse batteries and operational conditions. Third, to focus on engineering optimization for edge deployment by combining pruning, distillation, and quantization (PDQ)29 with cloud-edge collaboration, as well as exploring multi-modal fusion with Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)30 and other multi-sensor data.
