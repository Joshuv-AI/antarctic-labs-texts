Identification of anti-TIM-3 based checkpoint inhibitor combinations with activity in immunotherapy refractory melanoma models
Authors: Manali S Phadke, Jiannong Li, Sathya Sriramareddy, Paulo C Rodriguez, Brian Ruffell, Vincent C Luca, Thuy T T Tran, Yian Ann Chen, Keiran S M Smalley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA significant percentage of melanomas are refractory to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) monotherapies and combinations. As there are currently no effective second-line therapies available for ICI-resistant patients, we sought to identify novel checkpoint inhibitor combinations for future clinical evaluation.MethodsWe used responding and resistant SM1, SW1 and B16-10 melanoma models to explore the mechanism of action of anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)+lymphocyte activation gene (LAG-3), anti-PD-1+cytotoxic lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet, the anti-PD-1+T-cell immunoglobin and mucin domain 3 (TIM-3) doublet and the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet. Flow cytometry, tetramer binding assays, T-cell depletion and analysis of the tumor-draining lymph nodes were used to determine the mechanisms of action of the ICI combinations. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) data from patients with melanoma was additionally analyzed to determine immune checkpoint expression across T-cell subsets and their association with ICI resistance.ResultsThe PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 triplet combination had good efficacy in the SM1 and B16-F10 melanoma models and led to a more favorable immune microenvironment (increased CD69+CD8+ T cells and gp-100-tumor specific T cells, reduced exhausted T cells) than either doublet. The ICI-resistant SW1 model did not respond to the PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 triplet and was characterized by an accumulation of TIM-3+terminally exhausted CD8+T cells. Targeting of TIM-3 using the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet led to complete tumor regressions in the SW1 and SM1 models. The TIM-3-based triplet decreased exhausted CD8+T cells and regulatory T cells, increased numbers of gp-100 reactive CD8+T cells and increased progenitor-exhausted CD8+T cells (TCF7+Ki67+ TIM-3−) numbers in tumor-draining lymph nodes. scRNA-Seq analysis demonstrated that TIM-3 levels were higher in the CD8+T cells of patients with melanoma who were non-responsive to immunotherapy. PD-1 and TIM-3 expression was strongly correlated in the CD8+T cells from patients with melanoma, providing the rationale for co-targeting these checkpoints.ConclusionMelanoma models characterized by high levels of terminally exhausted CD8+T cells (TIM-3+) can be successfully treated with the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combination. The observation that high TIM-3 expression in CD8+T cells is associated with ICI resistance suggests the potential utility of anti-TIM-3-based combinations as a second-line treatment strategy in patients with advanced melanoma.Keywords: Melanoma, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, T cell

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

A significant percentage of patients with advanced melanoma show little or no response to standard of care anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), anti-PD-1+cytotoxic lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) or anti-PD-1+lymphocyte activation gene (LAG-3) therapy. Effective second-line immunotherapy combinations have yet to be identified.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

There has been unprecedented progress in the treatment of advanced melanoma. Much of this success has been driven by the development of therapeutic antibodies that target three immune checkpoints expressed in the tumor immune microenvironment. The first of these to be developed targeted cytotoxic lymphocyte-associated protein (CTLA)-4, a cell surface protein whose expression increases following T-cell activation.1 The second immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) to be developed, and the most successful ICI strategy to date, are antibodies that bind to and inhibit programmed cell death protein (PD)-1 and its ligand programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1).2 3 Blockade of PD-1 or PD-L1 re-invigorates exhausted CD8+cytotoxic T cells, leading to an antitumor immune response.4 The most recent ICI to show clinical success (when used in combination with anti-PD-1), are antibodies directed against lymphocyte activation gene (LAG)-3, a protein related to CD4, that binds to the major histocompatibility complex II (MHC class II).5 Among the three clinically relevant ICIs, only anti-PD-1 had induced significant single-agent activity. Anti-CTLA-4 therapy (such as ipilimumab) has low response rates as a monotherapy, but enhanced effects when combined with anti-PD-1 (an effect associated with increased immunotoxicity).1 Despite having little single-agent activity, the anti-LAG-3+PD-1 combination (relatlimab+nivolumab) was associated with increased clinical responses compared with anti-PD-1 therapy.6 Previous work from our group demonstrated that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 and the anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 combinations are both effective in mouse melanoma models, although with different mechanisms of action.7

T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (TIM-3) is a cell surface protein encoded by the HAVCR2 gene that was first identified on activated CD4+Th1 cells, as well as cytotoxic CD8+T cells.8 Use of TIM-3 blocking antibodies in vivo increased T-cell responses and autoimmunity, suggesting its role as an immune checkpoint.8,10 In cancer, expression of TIM-3 marks the most dysfunctional or terminally exhausted subset of CD8+T cells.11 There is evidence that the combination of anti-PD-1+TIM-3 enhances antitumor activity in multiple tumor models, including colorectal carcinoma and breast cancer.12 13 In breast cancer models, TIM-3 blockade also promotes CD8+T cell responses to paclitaxel, through effects on dendritic cell-mediated uptake of extracellular DNA and activation of the cyclic GMP/AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway.14 15 Despite these improvements in the treatment landscape, a significant proportion of patients with melanoma show little response to ICIs, and among responders, relapse rates are significant. Multiple mechanisms of immune escape have been identified.

There has been considerable clinical interest in combining multiple ICIs with the goal of more fully reversing T-cell exhaustion, particularly in patients who are treatment refractory or resistant. It is unclear what the best triplet ICI combination would be. Our group previously demonstrated that the efficacy of the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination was dependent on a subset of CD4+T helper cells, whereas the anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 combination was associated with a stronger CD8+T cell response.7 The observation that the different ICIs have non-overlapping mechanisms of action suggests that an ICI triplet could more effectively overcome T-cell exhaustion and deliver more durable antitumor responses. As such, several phase 1/2 clinical trials (eg, RELATIVITY-048) have been initiated to study the combination of anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 in patients with advanced melanoma, with evidence of improved long-term survival.16 In the current study, we have evaluated the triplet anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 combination against the existing US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved doublet combinations in three mouse melanoma models with varying degrees of ICI resistance. We identified refractory melanomas with high levels of terminally exhausted (TIM-3+) positive T cells that showed sensitivity to the triplet of anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) analysis of human melanoma samples identified increased TIM-3 expression in T cells as a common feature of ICI resistance. Together, our findings demonstrate that triplet ICI combinations have antitumor activity in ICI-resistant melanoma models and that the choice of triplet combination can be personalized to the mechanism of T-cell exhaustion.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

SM1 mouse BRAF-mutant melanoma cells and SW1 mouse NRAS-mutant melanoma cells were obtained from Dr Eric Lau, Moffitt Cancer Center. B16-F10 mouse melanoma cells were obtained from Dr Shari Pilon-Thomas, Moffitt Cancer Center. The cells were maintained for a maximum of 10 passages in Roswell Park Memorial Institute media (RPMI) 1640+10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cell lines were routinely tested for Mycoplasma (every 3 months).

In vivo procedures

Female 7-week-old immunocompetent C57BL/6J and C3H/HeNCrl mice were used for the experiments. All the protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of South Florida (approval #12732R). Mice were subcutaneously injected with 1.5×106 SM1, SW1 or B16-F10 cells in Matrigel (cat. #CB40234; Fisher Scientific) into the right flank. The flank tumors were allowed to grow approximately to ∼50–70 mm3 before initiation of drug dosing for SM1 and SW1 models. For the B16-F10 model, the drugs dosing was initiated the next day after the injections. Mice received intraperitoneal doses of the following antibodies in a volume of 100 µL: anti-PD-1 antibody (200 µg; clone RMP1-14; cat. #BE0146; Bio X Cell) or anti-CTLA-4 (200 µg/; clone 9D9; cat. #BE0164; Bio X Cell) or anti-LAG-3 (200 µg; clone C9B7W; cat. #BE0174; Bio X Cell) or anti-TIM-3 (200 µg; clone RMT3-23; cat. #BE0115; Bio X Cell) as a doublet or triplet combination every 5 days. For isotype control, a cocktail of IgG2a isotype control (200 µg; clone 2A3; cat. #BE0089; Bio X Cell), IgG1 isotype control (200 µg; clone HRPN; cat. #BE0088; Bio X Cell) and Syrian hamster IgG (200 µg; cat. #BE0087; Bio X Cell) were administered intraperitoneally every 5 days. The flank tumors were measured using calipers. The tumors were collected at the endpoint, weighed, and processed for flow cytometry analysis, cytokine arrays or immunohistochemistry.

GP-100 tetramer assay

The number of gp-100 T cell receptor (TCR) positive cells in tumors of the mice responding to anti-PD-1+LAG-3, anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 was determined by carrying out in vitro gp-100 tetramer assay. Prior to staining by gp-100 tetramer, a single-cell suspension of cells from the tumors of responding mice was prepared by enzymatic digestion, using an MACS tumor dissociation kit (cat. #130-095-929; Miltenyi Biotec). The number of viable cells was counted, and around 1×106 cells were blocked with purified mouse CD16/32 antibody (1:100 dilution; cat. #101301; BioLegend) for 5 min on ice. The cells were then stained with gp-100 tetramer PE (MBL #TS-M505-1 H-2Db human gp-100 tetramer KVPRNQDWL-PE) and incubated in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. Following this, the cells were also stained with Live/Dead Near IR antibody (cat. #L10119; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. Further, they were incubated with an antibody cocktail of anti-CD45-BUV395 (clone 30-F11; cat. #565967; BD Biosciences), anti-CD3-BV510 (clone 17A2; cat. #100233; BioLegend) and anti-CD8-APC (clone 53–6.7; cat. #553035; BD Biosciences) for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. Flow cytometry acquisition was performed on BD FACS LSR II. The data analysis was carried out using FlowJo software.

CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell depletion

CD8a-specific antibody (clone YTS169.4; cat. #BE0117; Bio X Cell) and CD4-specific antibody (clone YTS191; cat. #BE0003-1; Bio X Cell) were used to deplete CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells, respectively. C3H/HeNCrl mice were injected with the CD8a and CD4 antibodies (100 µg/100 µL) intraperitoneally, 3 days before the flank injections with SW1 cells. The antibody injections were continued every 4 days thereafter. The flank tumors were allowed to grow until 70 mm3 before initiation of treatment with either IgG or anti-PD-1+TIM-3 or anti-PD-1+TIM-3+LAG-3 every 5 days. The tumor volumes were measured two times a week. Tumors were collected at the endpoint around day 13. CD48+ and CD4+ T-cell depletion was verified by flow cytometry in the collected tumors.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining was carried out in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded SM1 tumor samples to check for toxicity following treatment with the triplets anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 and anti-PD-1+TIM-3+LAG-3. The sections were stained with H&E, anti-CD45 (Purple kit cat. #ab10558; Abcam) and anti-CD3 (Red kit cat. #ab16669; Abcam).

scRNA-Seq analysis

To investigate the correlation of the expression of the four immune checkpoints (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3 and CTLA-4) across the T-cell subsets and TNK cells, the scRNA-Seq data generated from 28,708 cells by the 10x Genomics platform from 43 patients with melanoma specimens (from skin, brain and leptomeningeal metastases) were curated using our ISCVA platform (Interactive Single Cell Visual Analytics) (GSE174401).17 Each checkpoint expression (in log2 normalized scale) was visualized by violin plot across subsets of T cells in melanoma specimens using the Seurat R package.2 The Spearman correlation of the percentage of T/natural killer (NK) cells with each checkpoint expression at the sample level (n=14). All statistical analyses were performed using the R V.4.2.0 software (https://www.R-project.org). To investigate if (HAVCR2) expression differed between patients with melanoma who were immunotherapy responders and non-responders, a single-cell dataset, (GSE120575), from cutaneous melanoma was downloaded from TISCH for analysis (https://tisch.comp-genomics.org/).18 Among the 45 samples, 19 were pretreatment samples, while 26 samples were post-treatment samples. The major cell lineage information, provided by TISCH, was used to identify CD8+T cells for this analysis. Single-cell expressions were processed using ISCVA after data download. A two-way analysis of variance with two main effects was used to compare if the TIM-3 (HAVCR2) expression (ie, per cent of CD8+T cells with TIM-3 expression) differed between responses (responder vs non-responder) and time points (pre-samples vs post-samples). A p value of 0.05 was used for statistical significance.

Results

The efficacy of the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet compared with the PD-1+LAG-3 and PD-1+CTLA-4 doublets

We compared the efficacy of the two ICI doublets (anti-PD-1+LAG-3 and anti-PD-1+CTLA-4) to the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet in three melanoma models, including one that was sensitive—SM1 (BRAF-mutant) and two resistant models—SW1 (NRAS-mutant) and B16-F10 (BRAF/NRAS WT). Both the SM1 and B16-F10 models were syngeneic with C57/BL6 and the SW1 was syngeneic with C3H/HeNCr mice. Flow cytometry profiling of the three models demonstrated a low level of MHC class I expression on B16-F10 and SM1 and high levels (>60% cells positive) of MHC class II expression on B16-F10 and SM1. SW1 expressed little MHC class I or II (<5% and <20%, respectively) (online supplemental figure 1). Very high expression of PD-L1 was seen in all three melanoma models.

In the ICI-sensitive SM1 model, complete tumor regression was seen to the anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 doublet, the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 doublet and the triplet. No evidence of tumor regrowth was seen (figure 1A–C). In the resistant B16-F10 model, the combination of anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 was ineffective and associated with rapid tumor growth (figure 1D,E). The combination of anti-PD-1+LAG-3 was more effective and delayed tumor growth in some mice for 14 days. The triplet delayed growth until day 21 and was associated with a significant reduction in tumor burden (figure 1D–F). However, at this point, the tumors reached endpoint on account of ulceration. The SW1 model was highly ICI resistant and showed little response to anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 (figure 1G–I). Some minor growth delay was seen to the combination of anti-PD-1+LAG-3 and the triplet, but there was little difference between the LAG-3-based doublet and the triplet. No significant reduction in tumor volume was seen in mice treated with the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 doublet or the triplet (figure 1I).

The anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet favorably remodels the tumor immune microenvironment

We next determined how the two doublets and the triplet shaped the tumor immune environment. For these analyses, samples were collected at day 6 while the tumors were still responding to therapy. In the SM1 model, treatment with the triplet and the anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 doublet led to a significantly higher accumulation of total CD4+T cells than the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination (figure 2A). Anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 treatment was associated with fewer total CD8+T cells than either the LAG-3 doublet or triplet. Tetramer binding assays demonstrated that the triplet significantly increased the levels of tumor-specific CD8+T cells recognizing gp-100 (figure 2B) compared with either doublet. The anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination, the PD-1+CTLA-4 combination and the triplet were all effective at reducing levels of PD1+ and LAG-3+exhausted CD8+ T cells, with the lowest levels of LAG-3+exhausted T cells found with the triplet (figure 2C). Unlike the PD-1+CTLA-4 combination, the triplet was not associated with increased levels of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (online supplemental figure 2). The anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 combination was associated with a significant decrease in the accumulation of cells resembling myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+) than the triplet; all three combinations led to reduced levels of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (CD45+CD11b+F4/80+) (online supplemental figure 3A,B).

For the more resistant B16-F10 model, we analyzed the immune environment at both an early response time point (ER: day 9) and after treatment failure (resistant, R: day 21). We noted that the triplet combination increased infiltration of activated CD69+CD8+ T cells (figure 2D) compared with the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination. There was an increase in exhausted (PD-1+ and LAG-3+) CD8+T cells in tumors responding to the triplet versus the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 doublet (figure 2E). No increase in TIM-3+CD8+ T cells was seen. Resistance to the anti-PD1+LAG-3 and anti-PD1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet was associated with increased levels of MDSCs (figure 2F).

In the SW1 model, the triplet was associated with the highest accumulation of activated CD8+T cells and the fewest Tregs (figure 2G–I). Significant decreases were also seen in MDSCs, total macrophages and M1 (CD45+>CD11b+F4/80+>CD11c+) and M2 (CD45+>CD11b+F4/80+>CD163+CD206+) type macrophages following treatment with the triplet (online supplemental figure 4). Negative changes in the SW1 immune environment associated with the triplet included fewer total CD4+T cells and an increase in TIM-3+exhausted CD8+ T cells (figure 2H). Levels of PD-1+ or LAG-3+CD8+ T cells were very low (~2%) and changed little in response to therapy. The anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 triplet was associated with the most striking decrease in tumor-infiltrating MDSCs and TAMs (figure 2I and online supplemental figure 4). The highest level of Tregs was seen in tumors treated with the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination (figure 2I). Cumulatively, these results demonstrate that response to immune checkpoint blockade is associated with an increase in activated CD8+T cells, while non-responsiveness is associated with an increase in T-cell exhaustion.

Anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet combinations are effective in multiple mouse melanoma models

One of the checkpoints strongly upregulated on CD8+T cells in the SW1 model was TIM-3 (figure 2H). As this typically marks the most exhausted subset of T cells,8 we evaluated the effectiveness of TIM-3-based ICI combinations in the three melanoma models. The combinations selected were the anti-PD-1+TIM-3 doublet and anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet. We chose the PD-1+LAG-3 combination as our backbone, as anti-PD-1+CTLA-4 had little efficacy against SW1 tumors. It was found that the anti-PD-1+TIM-3 doublet led to some delay in tumor growth in the SW1 model (figure 3A), with more striking results seen to the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet, with significant increases in overall survival observed. Two out of five mice achieved long-term inhibition of tumor growth and a further two out of five mice had complete tumor regression (figure 3A–C). In the ICI-sensitive SM1 model, both the PD-1+TIM-3 doublet and the PD-1+TIM-3+LAG-3 triplet combination led to a rapid inhibition of tumor growth and durable, long-term antitumor responses (figure 3D–F). The dynamics of antitumor activity were slightly quicker to the triplet than the doublet. In the B16-F10 model, both the anti-PD-1+TIM-3 and PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet showed a slight delay in tumor growth, but no changes in overall survival (figure 3G,H). We next undertook depletion studies in the SW1 model and showed that both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were required for efficacy of the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet combination (online supplemental figure 5A). Flow cytometry studies confirmed the CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell depletion (online supplemental figure 5B).

The anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet decreases T-cell exhaustion and increases CD8+ progenitor T cells

We next determined how the anti-PD-1+TIM-3 and anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combinations shaped the immune environment. In the SW1 model, the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet increased the percentage of total CD4+T cells (figure 4A) while reducing the proportion of exhausted (PD-1+ and TIM-3+) CD8+T cells (figure 4B), Tregs (online supplemental figure 6) and MDSCs (figure 4C). It is known that anti-PD-1 therapy drives the expansion of progenitor exhausted CD8+T cells in the tumor-draining lymph nodes, which then home to the tumor. An analysis of the tumor draining lymph nodes showed that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combination led to an expansion of Ki67+TCF7+TIM-3− CD8+T cells, which are characteristic of progenitor exhausted CD8+T cells (figure 4C).

In the responsive SM1 model, the TIM-3-based triplet combination led to a significant decrease in the numbers of exhausted (PD-1+ and LAG-3+) CD8+T cells and Tregs (figure 4D,E and online supplemental figure 7). The anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet also led to the expansion of tumor-infiltrating gp-100+CD8+ T cells (figure 4F). In the B16-F10 model, which was non-responsive to the PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet, there was an increase in total CD8+T cell numbers (figure 4G) but only a minor (non-significant) reduction in the numbers of exhausted PD-1+ and LAG-3+CD8+ T cells (figure 4H). In the B16-F10 model, the triplet was also associated with an inhibition of Treg accumulation (online supplemental figure 8). Overall, it seemed that the TIM-3-based ICI combinations could not overcome T-cell exhaustion in the B16-F10 model, even though Treg numbers were reduced. In responding tumors, the TIM-3-based triplet reduced numbers of exhausted CD8+T cells, and increased numbers of progenitor exhausted and tumor-reactive CD8+T cells.

PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3 and CTLA-4 are differentially expressed across cells of the melanoma immune microenvironment of mouse and humans

We interrogated our scRNA-Seq human melanoma datasets to define the expression of the four immune checkpoints (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3 and CTLA-4) across T-cell subsets that we previously defined.7 17 This first dataset (GSE174401) was generated from 43 patients with melanoma specimens, including 8 melanoma skin metastases, 14 melanoma brain metastases, and 19 melanoma specimens from the leptomeninges and thus represented melanoma from diverse anatomical sites.17 These analyses identified overlap in the expression of PD-1 (PDCD1), CTLA-4 and LAG-3 in CD4+T cells and multiple clusters of CD8+tissue resident memory T cells. LAG-3 was exclusively expressed in gamma delta γδ-T cells and a subset of CD8+T cells with an interferon-like gene signature (figure 5A). TIM-3 (HAVCR2) was expressed only in the three clusters of tissue resident memory CD8+T cells, with no expression observed in CD4+T cells or γδ-T cells. Correlation analysis of the immune checkpoints in the human melanoma specimens demonstrated that although PD-1 expression was correlated with LAG-3 and CTLA-4 expression in T and NK cells (figure 5B), the only checkpoint correlated with TIM-3 expression was LAG-3 (figure 5B,C). The finding that LAG-3 and TIM-3 were frequently co-expressed in T cells, provide a rationale for co-targeting these checkpoints in melanoma. One interesting feature of the TIM-3-based checkpoint inhibitor combinations was their activity in models with high TIM-3 expression in the T cells. To determine if this was a feature of human melanomas, we evaluated the percentage of T and NK cells with TIM-3 expression in our scRNA-Seq dataset (GSE174401) and found that 43% of all specimens had tumor-infiltrating T and NK cells that expressed TIM-3 (figure 5D). We next asked whether increased CD8+T cell TIM-3 expression was associated with ICI resistance. For this, we used a second scRNA-Seq dataset (GSE120575) which was comprised of 45 melanoma samples; 19 were pretreatment samples and 26 samples were post-immune checkpoint inhibitor samples (either anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-1+CTLA-4).18 An analysis of these data identified the significantly higher expression of TIM-3 in the T cells from patients who did not respond to ICI therapy (figure 5E).

The PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 and PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 ICI triplets were not associated with immunotoxicity

A major concern with the use of multiple ICIs is unrestrained immune activation leading to autoimmune toxicities. To investigate this, we tracked the weights of mice receiving either IgG control, doublets or the triplet. It was noted across all three models that there was little change in mouse weights for either anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 (figure 6A–C) or anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 (figure 6D–F). At necropsy, we collected multiple organs including liver, intestines and stomach and did not note any gross histological changes by H&E. Staining of the mouse organs for CD45 did not identify any increase in immune infiltrate into uninvolved organs following treatment with either of the ICI triplets (figure 6G and online supplemental figure 9).

Discussion

Resistance to ICI therapy is a major clinical problem that is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. One potential strategy to circumvent this is the inhibition of multiple immune checkpoints, with the goal of more fully reversing T-cell exhaustion. In the current study, we investigated the efficacy and mechanism of action of the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet and the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet as strategies for treating ICI-refractory melanoma. The anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 combination is currently being explored in advanced melanoma and other cancers. A preliminary report from the RELATIVITY-048 (NCT03459222), a non-randomized phase I/II trial of front-line anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4, suggested that the combination was associated with a response rate of 72% at 4 years, compared with 20% for historical controls treated with the doublet (PD-1+CTLA-4) ICI therapy.16 The triplet was well tolerated, allaying fears of the potential immunotoxicity of the combination. To our knowledge, no preclinical studies have been undertaken to define the mechanism of action of this triplet or to determine whether the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet is the optimal combination for patients with resistance to standard-of-care ICI therapy.

As our strategy, we evaluated the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet in three mouse models of melanoma, one of which (SM1) was highly sensitive to PD-1 therapy, and two (B16-F10 and SW1) that were anti-PD-1 refractory. The SM1 model was highly sensitive to all the combinations tested, resulting in complete tumor regressions in all mice. Despite these favorable responses, the anti-PD-1+CTLA-4+LAG-3 triplet offered further improvement in the tumor microenvironment compared with either doublet and was associated with an increase in tumor-infiltrating gp-100+tumor specific CD8+T cells and a decrease in the accumulation of exhausted PD1+/LAG-3+ CD8+ T cells. It therefore seemed that co-targeting three immune checkpoints offered benefit in terms of tumor-specific T-cell responses. In the B16-F10 model, the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+CTLA-4 triplet was associated with improved antitumor activity compared with the doublets. Here, the responses on the T cells were more complex, with the triplet being associated with an increased activated (CD69+) CD8+T cells, but a concurrent increase in PD-1+CD8+ T cells, fewer LAG-3+T cells and no detectable TIM-3+CD8+ T cells. In this model, the antitumor activity of the triplet was mediated in part through increased accumulation of tumor-reactive T cells that were lower in the expression of exhaustion markers. It further seemed that the addition of a third immune checkpoint inhibitor was not associated with any increase in immune-related toxicity, a finding which agrees with the interim analysis of the RELATIVITY-048 trial.16

There is already evidence that the addition of other checkpoint inhibitors to PD-1 blockade alters the immune environment, with recent studies from our group indicating that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination required CD4+T helper function to activate the CD8+T cell-mediated antitumor effects.7 Analysis of samples from a phase 2 clinical trial of anti-PD-1+LAG-3 in patients with advanced melanoma demonstrated that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3 combination increased CD8+T cell activation, even though exhaustion markers were retained.19 Increased numbers of CD8+TIM-3+ CD38+ T cells were identified in the blood of patients treated with anti-PD-1+LAG-3 therapy, but not anti-PD-1 or anti-LAG-3 alone.19 Other preclinical studies suggested that PD-1 and LAG-3 regulate different aspects of T-cell function, with LAG-3 modulating cytotoxic effector function via regulation of the TOX transcription factor, and PD-1 regulating proliferation.20

In the SW1 model, which was the most ICI resistant of the three, none of the PD-1+LAG-3 or PD-1+CTLA-4-based combinations were effective, with all of these being associated with the accumulation of an exhausted TIM-3+CD8+ T-cell population. TIM-3 is a checkpoint that is highly expressed on the most exhausted subset of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (defined by a lack of proliferation and interleukin-2 production).12 Multiple studies have demonstrated that TIM-3+CD8+ T cells are not responsive to anti-PD-1 monotherapy.12 There is, however, evidence in some cancer models, such as CT26 colorectal carcinoma and head and neck carcinoma, that the anti-PD-1+TIM-3 combination has better antitumor activity than either checkpoint inhibitor alone.12 21 Our data support this finding and demonstrated that in the severely resistant SW1 melanoma model, the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combination was superior to the ICI doublets tested and the anti-PD+CTLA-4+LAG-3 triplet. Mechanistically, TIM-3 engages with multiple ligands including galectin-9, HMGB1, CEACAM and phosphatidylserine. The binding of either galectin-9 or CEACAM1 to TIM-3 increases its phosphorylation at Y265 and Y272 (in humans) leading to the release of BAT-3, increased Src binding and the negative regulation of T-cell receptor signal transduction.22,24 In the SW1 model, the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet led to significant reduction in tumor burden and some complete tumor regressions. Use of this combination led to an increase in CD4+T cell accumulation and reduced levels of exhausted CD8+T cells and Tregs. Similar reductions in exhausted CD8+T cells and Tregs were also seen in the SM1 model, which also responded well to this triplet. Together, these data suggest that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet restored cytotoxic T-cell function in multiple melanoma models, including those that are not responsive to current FDA-approved ICI combinations. The responses to the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 triplet were dependent on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with depletion of either of these T-cell subsets abrogating the antitumor response.

TILs are heterogeneous and there is evidence that exhausted TILs can adopt multiple transcriptional/phenotypic states. Of relevance to ICI therapy, only some of these T-cell states are responsive to anti-PD-1 therapy.25 26 Although it is known that tumor-associated CD8+T cells can be re-invigorated by anti-PD-1 therapy, they tend to be short-lived and it is unlikely that they mediate the antitumor activity of PD-1 blockade.27 The major mediators of anti-PD-1 responsiveness are a population of progenitor or “stem-like” exhausted T cells that reside in the tumor-draining lymph nodes.27 28 These cells are characterized as an exhausted CD8+T cell population that are proliferative (Ki67+), have low levels of TIM-3 expression and high levels of the T-cell transcription factor TCF1 (encoded by the TCF7 gene).27 The progenitor exhausted CD8+T cells undergo a proliferative burst in response to PD-1 blockade and then home to the tumor27 29 30 where they differentiate into cytotoxic T cells.27 28 Our studies showed that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combination led to an expansion of Ki67+TCF7+TIM-3− population of CD8+T cells in the tumor draining lymph nodes and that this was associated with a concurrent expansion of tumor infiltrating gp-100+CD8+ T cells. It thus seems that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 combination could be one strategy to drive the expansion of the progenitor state in melanomas that are ICI refractory, offering the potential for future clinical translation.

The study has a number of limitations. First, we did not directly address whether the two ICI triplets were effective in models of acquired anti-PD-1 resistance, the most likely scenario requiring second-line ICI strategies. At this time, well-credentialed mouse models of melanoma that show initial sensitivity to PD-1 and then subsequent relapse are not available. Second, our study only considered the effects of TIM-3 blockade on T cells, there is evidence that TIM-3 plays a critical role in the function of dendritic cells, which was not addressed here.14 15 Third, use of combinations of ICIs in human patients with melanoma (such as PD-1+CTLA-4) are associated with serious immune-related adverse effects that are difficult to model accurately in mice. Although we did not see any evidence of toxicity to the ICI triplets in our mouse melanoma models, this does not preclude toxicity in humans. Finally, the scRNA-Seq data only gives insights into the messenger RNA expression of the immune checkpoints and does not provide information as to the expression of immune checkpoint protein expression on the surface of T cells.

In summary, we provide the evidence that the anti-PD-1+LAG-3+TIM-3 regimen is clinically relevant, with the observation that LAG-3 and TIM-3 are co-expressed in tumor-infiltrating CD8+T cells from patients with melanoma. There was also evidence that TIM-3 expression is expressed in a significant percentage of tumor-infiltrating T cells from patients with melanoma and that its expression increases in T cells from patients who are non-responsive to PD-1 therapy. Multiple studies have shown that TIM-3 expression is upregulated as an adaptation to anti-PD-1 therapy, in part through modulation of PI3K signaling.2131,33 Together, our data provide the preclinical rationale for investigating TIM-3-based ICI strategies in patients with melanoma who are refractory to standard-of-care immunotherapy as well as those whose TILs are characterized by high levels of TIM-3 expression.
