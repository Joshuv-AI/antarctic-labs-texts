Characterization of RNA using mass photometry: Method and technical perspectives
Authors: Lauren F. Barnes, Brenda Watt, Salma Jalal, Gogulan Karunanithy, Matthew J. Ranaghan, Josephine Rood, Hanliu L. Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing analytical methods for RNA-based products, such as mRNA vaccines, requires tools that support release testing, stability assessment, and characterization. Mass photometry (MP) is a label-free, single-molecule technique increasingly applied to biotherapeutics and vaccines. Here, we outline best practices and technical considerations for using MP to analyze vaccine mRNA constructs and other RNA samples. With appropriate calibration, MP provides mRNA molecular weight and quantifies heterogeneous species, including those difficult to resolve using traditional techniques. We systematically evaluated experimental parameters influencing RNA measurements, including surface chemistry and buffer composition, and show that cationic-coated sample carriers and salt-containing buffers are essential for reproducible detection and mass resolution. Low-mass species were assessed using multiple mRNA constructs, kinetic analysis, and buffer controls to distinguish genuine sample heterogeneity from method artifacts. We further examined the effects of ionic additives on oligomeric species. MP results were compared with charge detection mass spectrometry and fragment analyzer measurements, showing good agreement when accounting for differences in measurement principles. Overall, MP provides a rapid, low-sample consumption, and information-rich approach for mRNA characterization and quality assessment, either as a standalone method or as an orthogonal complement to established RNA analytical techniques.

Introduction

The production of mRNA via in vitro transcription (IVT) is one of the most promising technologies in the development for RNA-based vaccines, cancer treatments and gene therapies.1 The IVT process has important advantages including faster manufacturing development, versatility, and scalability. The unprecedented success of mRNA vaccines during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has demonstrated the potential of mRNA and IVT in swift responses to emerging pathogens. It is critical to develop robust and reliable analytical tools to characterize RNA integrity, purity, and efficacy to ensure quality and batch-to-batch consistency.

Characterization, control, and/or monitoring RNA product quality attributes such as the presence and quantity of impurities like aggregates, fragments, and double-stranded RNA require analytical techniques that can be used to detect and characterize them accurately and efficiently.2 Organizations such as the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) are working on guidelines for RNA sample characterization.3,4

Mass photometry (MP) is a recently developed analytical technique that is seeing widespread adoption across different fields. This technique uses the principles of interferometric scattering microscopy (iSCAT) to measure the mass of individual biomolecules5,6 Briefly, samples placed on a glass sample carrier slide are illuminated with a laser. The glass surface of the sample carrier reflects part of that light, while biomolecules that are in contact with the glass also scatter small amounts of the light. Mass photometers measure the interference between the reflected and scattered light. This signal—known as the interferometric contrast—is directly correlated with molecular mass.6,7

With calibration using a molecule of known mass and type, a mass photometer can be used to measure the mass of other single molecules of the same type (e.g., proteins, DNA or RNA) based on the contrast signal they produce when they interact with the sample carrier glass. In MP, binding events (where a molecule interacts with the glass) are represented by negative contrast values measured in arbitrary units, which are converted to positive mass values via calibration. Conversely, unbinding events are represented by positive contrast values, which result in apparently negative mass values after calibration and therefore not included in data analysis.

MP measurements typically run for 1 min and require low volumes (≈10 μL) of sample at nanomolar concentrations. It can measure molecular masses starting at 30 kDa up to 5 MDa with a mass error of 5% or less.6,7 These strengths make the technology a practical addition to workflows that require repeated sample characterization and low sample consumption. MP is currently being used to analyze different types of biomolecules and other particles such as proteins,8 lipid nanodiscs,9 and viral particles.10

MP can be applied to nucleic acid-based molecules using the appropriate experimental setup,11,12 and is currently being adopted to characterize large mRNA molecules in vaccine and therapeutic products under development.13,14 While MP has emerged as a promising technique in the field of nucleic acid analysis,13 its application is still relatively limited compared to its more widespread use for proteins or adeno-associated viruses (AAVs). In this study, we detail how to take MP measurements of RNA samples, beginning with calibration and show how the choice of sample carriers, buffers, and sample handling may affect results using two commercially available RNAs and three different mRNA vaccine candidates. Additionally, we performed orthogonal measurements using charge detection mass spectrometry (CD-MS) and fragment analyzer (FA) to further understand observations from MP results. The significance of this work is to provide valuable insights and guidance on the best practices for RNA measurement using MP, ultimately contributing to the standardization and optimization of MP measurement for RNA.

Results

MP measurements of RNA require cationic-coated sample carriers

MP measurements depend on particles binding nonspecifically to the glass measurement surface and scattering part of the light focused on the sample. Some biomolecules—like proteins—tend to adhere to that surface and remain bound, and so can be measured by MP using uncoated, ultraclean sample carriers. However, nucleic acids are negatively charged when in solution and tend to show only infrequent and transient binding to uncoated glass. Indeed, previous MP studies of nucleic acids have used functionalized slides to facilitate molecule binding to the glass.11,12,13,14 Therefore, measuring RNA with MP requires the use of glass that has been coated with a cationic surface to stabilize negatively charged molecules on the glass surface.

To test this, we analyzed a commercially available single-stranded RNA ladder using uncoated, ultraclean sample carriers (Figure 1A), and sample carriers with a cationic coating (Figures 1B and 1D). For the sample carriers with cationic coating, we used either commercially available positively coated slides (Figure 1B) or uncoated slides manually coated with poly-L-lysine (PLL) in house (Figure 1D). Measurements performed on the uncoated sample carriers did not match the expected mass distribution, which should contain 10 discrete ssRNA transcripts ranging from 500 to 9,000 bases. While the number of total events varied between sources and level of cleanliness of uncoated glass, similar quantities of binding and unbinding events were observed in a roughly mirrored distribution (Figure 1A). This mirrored histogram pattern, coupled with particles with a “half-white/half-dark” profile (Figure S1A), is indicative of weak binding rather than stable adsorption to the glass surface. As a result, the detected interferometric signal reflects a time-averaged contrast arising from partial surface engagement and intermittent movement during the acquisition window, leading to an apparent assignment of inaccurate lower mass and reduced mass resolution (Figure S1A). In contrast, measurements performed using sample carriers with a cationic coating clearly show the presence of the expected RNA masses (Figures 1B and 1D), where 9 of the 10 expected RNAs were sufficiently abundant to be fitted by a Gaussian curve. The largest RNA species in the ladder was detected but with poor signal-to-noise due to its low abundance (see discussion Section). Moreover, the RNA molecules show little to no unbinding (Figure 1B) and the particles show a resolved particle profile (Figure S1B). This indicates the presence of stronger and longer-lasting contacts between the RNA molecules and the coated sample carrier surface. Both types of slides with cationic coating (ready to use and manually coated in house) provide comparable profiles of this commercial single-stranded RNA ladder with high reproducibility demonstrated with two replicates on each slide.

Calibration of MP measurements of RNA samples

The interferometric contrast detected by MP when measuring RNA samples correlates directly to the mass and, consequently, the number of bases of the measured RNA molecules. Therefore, calibration using RNA molecules with a known mass or base count makes it possible to use MP to determine the mass or length of the RNA molecules in a sample based on their measured contrast.6

Standard commercial RNA ladders can be used as calibrants for MP measurements. In this study, we used the Millenium RNA Marker (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as a calibrant. This marker contains 10 RNA species (Table S1), which yielded a clear linear relationship of contrast values with RNA mass and length in both types of coated slides (R2 > 0.999, Figures 1C and 1E). All 10 RNA species could be detected, but the largest species at 9,000 bases was not present in high enough abundance to accurately fit a Gaussian curve, which would introduce larger mass errors if a fitting were forced and therefore was not used in the calibration. It is worth noting that, as MP relies upon molecule counting, the abundances reported in MP measurements are related to molar abundance (particle number), rather than to concentrations based on chromophore abundance (mg/mL).

Although the exact sequences and masses in the commercially available calibrant are not available, the mass approximation approach (Table S1) worked well. For the mRNA vaccine constructs analyzed in this study, complete sequences were available, enabling precise theoretical mass calculations. In all cases, measured masses agreed well with theoretical values, indicating that the approximated calibrant masses are sufficient for mRNA-sized molecules within the intrinsic mass accuracy limitations of MP (<5% mass error). We note, however, that for smaller RNAs or constructs in which the poly(A) tail constitutes a substantial fraction of the total mass, sequence-defined calibrants might be required for optimal accuracy.

The measurements with the most resolved peaks at the expected mass were those taken in the salt-containing buffers TE (Figure 2B) and PBS (Figure 2C), showing comparable mass distribution profiles between them, with the main population having a measured mass within 5% of the expected mass. However, measurements performed in nuclease-free water produced a broad mass distribution in addition to the expected peak (Figure 2A) and could not be calibrated as recommended because measurement of RNA ladder in nuclease-free water also produced unresolved populations (Figure S2A). Furthermore, inconsistency in peak distributions among duplicate measurements was observed when mRNA 1 was measured in water (Figure 2A), suggesting that a lack of salts precludes repeatability and optimal mass resolution of mRNA populations by MP. We hypothesize that, relative to recommended salt-containing buffers that support reproducible MP measurements, nuclease-free water introduces greater variability in either RNA adsorption or scattering behavior, leading to broadened mass distributions and reduced apparent resolution. Addition of salt to the droplet of RNA ladder in nuclease-free water resulted in regaining the resolution of the RNA ladder populations (Figure S2A), confirming that the previously recorded broad distribution of masses (Figure 2A) was not due to RNA sample degradation. Although it is not advised to measure the same droplet twice by MP, it can be a useful approach to troubleshoot possible degradation vs. buffer conditions as was done here. Additionally, although RNA instability is a known concern, the rapid MP workflow minimizes the potential risk of degradation occurring during the analysis itself.

To assess a salt concentration range appropriate for MP measurements, we diluted a commercially available mRNA encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) in nuclease-free water with increasing concentrations of NaCl (Figure S2B). Consistent with our observations of the RNA ladder and mRNA 1, measurement of EGFP mRNA in nuclease-free water showed a broad distribution of particles with contrast values far below what is expected for an mRNA of this size. However, resolution of populations was immediately achieved when salt was added, even at the lowest NaCl concentration tested (10 mM NaCl). We also observed a decrease in binding counts as the salt concentration increased, but overall, the distribution of masses and their relative abundance was most consistent among duplicate measurements between 30 and 120 mM NaCl for this mRNA sequence. As the concentration of NaCl increased >250 mM NaCl, the drop in counts was significant. Because these high concentrations of salt are not physiological, we would recommend staying within physiological salt concentrations when performing MP measurements.

Effects of buffer and concentration on mRNA low and high molecular mass species

When developing mRNA products, it is critical to confirm the correct length/mass of the desired product and assess the relative abundance of impurities, such as fragments, aggregates or other contaminating by-products. As shown in Figures 2B and 2C, while the expected mRNA product was the main species observed in either TE or PBS buffer, species of lower and higher molecular masses were also observed, indicating that MP can be applied to characterize the intended primary mRNA species and accompanying low- and high-mass species. Previous work from others12,13,14 has shown that MP measurements of mRNA often show the presence of both low and high mass species, consistent with what we observe here for both mRNA 1 and EGFP mRNA. Raw ratiometric images and processing were carefully examined to ensure the low mass species observed are not due to potential interaction issues between the intact RNA and the carrier surface. Specifically, mRNA 1 particles at low mass do not appear to have half-white half-dark profiles and there is little to no unbinding throughout the recording, unlike our observations on uncoated glass (Figures 1A and S1A), suggesting that low mass species do not reflect poor binding nor intermittent movement over the glass surface. Application of a mono-exponential association model to a recording of mRNA 1 in PBS describes cumulative counts well and reveals that the effective landing rates (k) of low mass species are similar to that of the main population (Figures S3A and S3B). This analysis also indicates that the differences in cumulative counts appear to be driven mainly by the relative abundances of each species examined (A) and not due to large differences in landing rates (k). The mono-exponential fit, together with our observation that the mass distribution pattern of low mass species is distinct between mRNA 1 and EGFP mRNA, suggests that low mass species represent sample-specific fragments or incomplete mRNA transcripts rather than artifacts of the measurement. To further test, we measured another mRNA vaccine (mRNA 2, ∼692 kDa) in PBS and in Tris-based formulation buffer (FB). As shown in Figure 3, this mRNA showed similar mass distribution in both the buffers, though the measurements in FB revealed additional low molecular mass species below 100 kDa. Measurements of FB alone suggest that some buffer components in the FB introduced the <100 kDa species independent of the sample (Figure 3, orange traces represent measurements of FB buffer without mRNA). Importantly, the mass distribution patterns between mRNA1 (Figures 2B and 2C), mRNA2 (Figures 3A and 3B), and EGFP mRNA (Figure S2B) are significantly different, making it more likely that species at low molecular mass are a true reflection of the molecule properties instead of system or method-induced artifacts and that control measurements of buffer alone can help distinguish buffer-related species.Figure 3Mass photometry measurements of RNA in PBS vs. formulation bufferRepresentative mass histograms of a ∼692 kDa RNA (mRNA 2) measured at 1 μg/mL in 1× PBS (A) or formulation buffer (B). Next, we focused on high mass species. Interestingly, similarly to what was observed for mRNA 1 and EGFP mRNA, a population of particles with a mass corresponding to a dimer of the main species was observed in both buffers for mRNA2. Taking into consideration that the FB introduces buffer interference below 100 kDa, we compared the relative abundance of species within 100–650 kDa (low mass species), monomeric mRNA and dimeric mRNA in both PBS and FB as a percentage of all molecules ≥100 kDa from triplicate measurements of mRNA 2 in each buffer (Figure 3C). While mRNA 2 in FB shows a slightly higher amount of intact RNA monomer than in PBS, the relative abundance of all three species is overall similar in both buffers.

To explore whether the formation of multimeric species is sequence-dependent and influenced by RNA concentration, we measured an RNA construct with a different sequence (mRNA 3) at two different concentrations. As shown in Figure 4A (blue trace), measurement of mRNA 3 also revealed dimers, further demonstrating that dimerization is not specific to the sequence nor source, based on collective measurement of three mRNA vaccine candidates produced in-house and a commercial mRNA. However, the impact of specific sequences on dimerization was not explored further in this study. Measurement at 10 times higher RNA concentration (Figure 4A, yellow trace) showed a similar abundance of dimers (Figure 4B). Although multimerization, while not abundant, is expected to be promoted at higher mRNA concentrations, the phenomenon is not readily detected by MP despite the 10-fold concentration difference. This is likely because the operating concentrations of both samples are still at low concentrations to enable single particle measurement. Because higher-order oligomerization structures can be modulated by the presence of metal ions such as Mg2+,16 we also measured mRNA 3 in PBS buffer, where 10 mM MgCl2 (to add Mg2+ ions) or 13 mM EDTA (to chelate residual Mg2+) was added. The presence of MgCl2 resulted in a higher relative abundance of dimers and even higher order multimeric species (trimers and tetramers could be detected, Figure S4) suggesting that Mg2+ stabilizes or promotes the oligomerization of this RNA construct (Figure 4A, green trace). Indeed, quantification of the different populations >100 kDa (Figure 4B) shows that the presence of Mg2+ ions leads to more than a 2-fold increase in the abundance of mRNA 3 dimers, with a concomitant decrease in the abundance of monomers. In contrast, adding EDTA (Figure 4A, orange trace) did not visibly affect the abundance of dimers when compared to PBS alone. This suggests that ions other than Mg2+ in the solution could contribute to dimer formation or their stability and that chelation of Mg2+ divalent cations by EDTA does not fully disrupt RNA-RNA interactions under these conditions. Additional experiments are required to further understand the impact of ions on RNA aggregation as we were unable to reliably measure RNA in the absence of ions.

Of note, while measuring at 10 times higher concentration did facilitate the identification and quantification of higher-order structures (due to an increase in counts), these measurements also show broadened monomer peaks. Therefore, MP measurements at different concentrations might be beneficial when trying to optimize buffer conditions that favor the stability of the species of interest and when looking to improve peak resolution. As shown in Figure S5, the effect of MgCl2 on oligomerization could still be evaluated when the two mRNA constructs used in Figures 3 and 4 (mRNA 2 and 3) were measured at 1 μg/mL (∼1.5 nM) in PBS, PBS + EDTA and PBS + MgCl2. Even at these lower concentrations, mRNA 2 also showed increased oligomerization in the presence of MgCl2 (shift from 3.5% to 6.9% dimer), although to a lower degree than mRNA 3 (shift from 3.2% to 8.9% dimer).

Comparison of MP to orthogonal techniques

To evaluate how MP results compare to those obtained with orthogonal techniques, and to further confirm the presence of low- and high-mass species, the mRNA 2 construct was measured by both CD-MS under native conditions and FA under denaturing conditions. CD-MS is a mass spectrometry (MS) technique that determines the mass and charge state of single ions in a sample and is particularly effective for the analysis of very large molecules >100 kDa, which is a mass range where conventional MS techniques become progressively less capable.12,17,18,19 FA is a form of capillary gel electrophoresis (CGE), separating molecules by size and charge through a polymer gel matrix inside the capillary and is used as a proxy for measuring RNA length. As shown in Figure 5A, CD-MS of mRNA-2 showed a mass of 697 kDa, comparable to MP data and to the expected mass at 692 kDa. The CD-MS spectrum also showed the presence of oligomeric species, with monomers representing 49.7% of the sample, and dimers 7.7%. In addition to dimer, masses corresponding to trimer and tetramer were also observed in CD-MS and the relative abundance of oligomers and intermediate species are higher in CD-MS compared to MP. MP measurement of mRNA 2 in ammonium acetate, the buffer in which CD-MS measurements are carried out, showed similar levels of dimer species as in PBS (data not shown), indicating that the difference in measurement buffer is unlikely to be responsible for the higher abundance of oligomers observed in our CD-MS measurements. Rather, the higher abundance of oligomers in the CD-MS measurements is likely due to the higher working concentration used (2 mg/mL in CD-MS vs. 0.001 mg/mL in MP) and potential in-source formation of aggregates, which is a common method artifact in electrospray ionization MS analysis.20 Nevertheless, CD-MS confirmed the presence of both low molecular mass species and high molecular mass species. In addition, CD-MS, as a MS-based approach, provides better resolution as reflected by narrower peak width in comparison to MP, as well as better mass accuracy in our general experience. FA measurements (Figure 5B) showed a main peak representing 92% of the sample at the expected migration time for an mRNA of this size (measured: 2,146 bases; expected: 2,000 bases), as well as other species representing 8% at lower or higher mass than the main species detected (Figure 5B). However, the abundance of high molecular mass species detected by FA was much lower than that measured by CD-MS or MP, and the sizes of the extremely low abundant high molecular mass species observed with FA are not close to the expected masses of dimer or higher oligomeric forms. Considering that the high sensitivity kit used contains denaturant and samples are exposed to heat denaturation prior to FA measurement, as recommended by the kit manufacturer, the lack of oligomers in FA suggests that the observed oligomeric forms by MP and CD-MS are non-covalently linked while they are dissociated in FA measurements. It is important to note that signals corresponding to other high molecular weight species, aside from oligomers of intact RNA, were also observed in both MP and CD-MS. These signals appear between the monomer and dimer peaks in MP, and between the monomer and dimer, as well as dimer and trimer peaks in CD-MS. These species could represent non-covalent association of intact RNA and low-mass species, or other product or process related species. However, further characterization of these species is challenging due to their indistinct peak profiles and low abundance, which fall below the instrument resolution capabilities of MP, FA, and CD-MS. High-resolution methods, such as liquid chromatography-mass spectometry (LC-MS)/MS or next-generation sequencing (NGS), would be required to elucidate their identity and detailed structure.

To test the hypothesis that oligomers in RNA samples are commonly observed and non-covalent, we measured the EGFP mRNA under non-denaturing and denaturing conditions. MP measurements of the EGFP construct under non-denaturing conditions showed the presence of monomers (60%), dimers (11%), and trimers (4%), as well as low-mass species (14%; Figure 6A). When the sample was heated for 1 minute at 75°C in the presence of urea and EDTA, most of the oligomers disassembled into monomeric species with only trace levels of dimer present (<2%) (Figure 6B). Discussion

Achieving consistent, high-quality RNA products is fundamental to ensuring their effectiveness as therapeutics or vaccines. However, while mRNA is a promising biotherapeutic, its analytical characterization remains a challenge due to its complexity and large size, and as such benefits from using a variety of analytical tools for its characterization. Our results show that MP can be used to quickly determine the mass or length of RNA molecules in native, salt-containing solutions after a straightforward calibration using RNA molecules of known mass or length, such as commercially available RNA ladders. Here, we used MP to analyze several constructs of different lengths/masses to inform on sample purity and the relative abundance of the species found. In addition, the results presented herein can provide guidance as to the experimental conditions that optimize the accurate measurement of RNA by MP.

MP has been most frequently applied to samples containing proteins or proteinaceous viral capsids,6,10 which tend to nonspecifically bind to uncoated glass and remain in place for the duration of the measurement. In contrast, nucleic acids appear to bind to uncoated glass only very transiently and when detected often show an aberrant particle profile. Consequently, MP measurements of nucleic acids performed on uncoated sample carriers can result in measurements with similar numbers of binding and unbinding events that fail to accurately estimate the mass of the RNA molecules present in the sample (Figures 1A and S1A). The use of sample carriers with a cationic coating allows for longer-lasting contacts between the RNA molecules and the measurement surface, produce a typical particle profile, and result in clear MP measurements (Figures 1B and S1B).

Although nuclease-free water is a common solution for RNA sample handling in a research lab, it is not optimal for MP measurements. Our MP measurements of three different RNA samples in nuclease-free water showed a broad distribution of signals and had poor reproducibility (Figures 2A and S2B), which prevented a clear characterization of the samples. Addition of salt immediately restored resolution to our measurements (Figure S2), indicating that salts or ions are needed for accurate MP measurements of mRNA. One explanation is that in the absence of counterions to shield their large net negative charge, RNA molecules in water bind the positively coated glass surface at higher speed than the camera can capture, and thus the MP contrast measured is lower than expected and with a broader and unresolved distribution. Alternatively, counterions are known to support compaction and folding of RNA molecules,16,21 and although MP is blind to shape and only reports on mass,6,22 we cannot dismiss the possibility that in the absence of salts, the unfolded/folded state of RNA results in variable polarizability and therefore variable scattering intensity of individual RNA molecules, resulting in broad and unresolved peaks.16,21 Importantly, our results show that MP measurements of RNA are more reproducible and accurate when samples are suspended in salt-containing buffers such as PBS or TE (Figures 2B, 2C, S2A, and S2B), similar to what has been reported recently.14 However, in that study, mRNA did not appear to bind to glass slides in nuclease-free water, while in our measurements we were able to detect molecules binding for all three samples tested (RNA ladder, mRNA 1, and EGFP mRNA). It is unclear what the differences in conditions between that study and ours are to explain the different binding behavior in water, but there is agreement that nuclease-free water is not an optimal carrier for MP measurements of mRNA.

In salt containing buffers, MP was able to detect and quantify a main species at the expected mass, as well as other lower abundance species, providing overall profiles of the samples tested. The measurements of three different mRNA vaccine candidates and two commercial RNA samples (RNA ladder and EGFP mRNA) showed different patterns of low molecular mass species, strongly suggesting that these low molecular mass species are product-related and not method artifacts. Analysis of cumulative binding over time indicates low mass species have similar binding rates to the main species, and that their lower abundance is a reflection of their concentration in the sample (Figure S3).

In addition to low mass species, we also detected mRNA oligomers and additional low abundance species with masses that do not match oligomers but are larger mass than the main species. Consistent with the notion that mRNA oligomers are common impurities in mRNA samples, previous findings have shown that the choice of RNA polymerase used in the IVT reaction,15 and the addition of polyA tails14 can alter the abundance of high mass species. Also, while we found that it is important to check potential interference species or system peaks from the FBs or dilution solutions by doing buffer measurements with no mRNA, we found that buffer-derived species did not contribute to high mass species (Figure 3) and mostly contributed to counts below 100 kDa.

In this study, we found that buffer additives and metal ions can affect the oligomeric state of mRNA (Figures 4, S4, and S5). With MP, we were able to quantify the oligomeric populations present in an mRNA sample under different conditions and at different concentrations (Figures 4, S4, and S5). Our measurements showed that, for the mRNA 2 and mRNA 3 constructs, adding MgCl2 resulted in a marked increase in the quantity of dimers both at low (1 μg/mL) and high (10 μg/mL) RNA concentrations. While MP cannot inform on conformational changes of the RNA caused by diffuse ions, we speculate that the divalent nature of Mg2+ might facilitate RNA oligomerization by screening or even chelating multiple RNA molecules depending on the number of Mg2+ bound, in similar fashion to how it promotes RNA folding and compaction.16

We further utilized orthogonal techniques, including charge CD-MS and FA, to validate observations made by MP and to compare the three orthogonal methods. All three techniques provided mass/length estimates for the main expected species that were close to theoretical values within the instrument specifications. CD-MS also confirmed the presence of both low molecular mass species and high molecular mass species, including oligomers, as observed with MP. CD-MS detected a higher abundance of dimers, as well as additional trimers and tetramers, in comparison to MP, which is likely due to the higher working concentrations used in the CD-MS measurements and potential contributions from well-known method artifacts of insource aggregate formation.17,18,20 This underscores the advantages of MP, which measures molecules in solution and requires minimal sample handling.

FA confirmed the presence of low molecular mass species but no oligomers were observed, likely due to the denaturing conditions used by this methodology. In contrast, CD-MS and MP measure RNAs under native conditions. Testing a commercially available EGFP-encoding mRNA under native and denaturing conditions supported these findings. MP under non-denaturing conditions identified monomers, dimers, and trimers of EGFP mRNA, whereas heating the sample in the presence of denaturant significantly decreased the relative abundance of oligomers and concurrently increased the abundance of monomers, aligning with Bioanalyzer data provided by the manufacturer. The value of measuring mRNA under non-denaturing conditions is underscored by a recent study by Camperi et al., where a strong correlation between the level of high mass mRNA species detected by MP and the activation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells was observed.15 Thus, it is important to utilize a variety of techniques to inform on all aspects of mRNA quality attributes to ensure quality, consistency, and safety during their development. As shown in this work, MP represents—with the appropriate methodological considerations—a powerful tool for determining the mass/length distribution of the molecules in RNA samples. The mass accuracy, fast experimental time, and low sample requirements of MP make it advantageous for quick RNA sample characterization—for example, during process optimization or when checking sample integrity.

While MP offers rapid, low-sample, and native-state analysis, there are limitations that need to be pointed out. Because MP is a single particle technique performed at low concentrations, samples must be substantially diluted prior to analysis. As a result, the observed oligomer distribution may not fully reflect the aggregation state present at the higher concentrations typical of drug substance or formulated mRNA. The operating concentration difference contributes to the differences we observed when comparing MP with CD-MS, and similar effects may arise in comparisons with size exclusion chromatography multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS). Although low-mass species can be detected by MP, they are not well-resolved in the RNA samples examined in this study. The overlapping features may reflect true molecular heterogeneity, but they may also arise from the intrinsic limits of the instrument’s mass accuracy and resolving power. Additionally, because of potential buffer interference in the low mass range, low-mass species that overlap with buffer-related signals are masked and therefore cannot be characterized by MP. Likewise, distinguishing subtle differences such as characterizing poly(A)-tail length distribution is challenging. For these reasons, orthogonal high-resolution analytical techniques remain essential for confidently characterizing species that are closely spaced in mass. Finally, trace signals observed between the monomer, dimer, and higher-order oligomers further highlight the challenge of interpreting very low-abundance features. Because these signals lack well-defined peak shapes, they cannot be confidently separated from noise or background particles, although measurements of buffer alone can help determine the abundance of stochastic events unrelated to the sample. These challenges are common to single particle techniques, including CD-MS, when resolving sparsely populated species. At the same time, single particle techniques like MP and CD-MS offer a key advantage that each molecule contributes equally as a single event, independent of its size. This provides an unbiased, number-based representation of species’ distributions, in contrast to absorbance-based methods such as UV, where signal intensity scales with chromophore content.

Our detailed examination of various conditions for RNA measurement using MP offers practical insights and serves as a reference for the development and optimization of RNA measurements. Moreover, we demonstrate that MP provides orthogonal and consistent results comparable to those obtained from CD-MS and FA measurements, after accounting for the differences in measurement conditions and detection modes among these techniques.

Materials and methods

RNA samples

Millenium RNA Marker was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and EGFP mRNA from GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). mRNA 1–3 contain N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) and were produced and provided by Pfizer via in-house IVT.

MP

MP measurements were carried out on a TwoMP instrument (Refeyn, UK) using the manufacturer provided software for acquisition, AcquireMP 2024 1.1, and analysis, DiscoverMP 2024 1.0. Measurements were carried out at room temperature, using sample carrier glass slides (MassGlass UC, Refeyn, UK) that were either manually coated with PLL or not. Commercially available cationic-coated sample carrier slides (Refeyn, UK) also were used. RNAs were diluted in various buffers and measured at 0.5–10 μg/mL as indicated. Measurements were recorded for 1 min in the large image size using default parameters.

CD-MS

CD-MS was utilized orthogonally to MP in the measurement of mRNA 2. In this work, CD-MS was performed by Megadalton Solutions, with additional data analysis performed by Pfizer. A total of 20 μL of 2 mg/mL mRNA were buffer exchanged via spin columns into an ammonium acetate salt solution before being introduced to the mass spectrometer through electrospray ionization. The instrument details are explained in previous publications.18,19 The corresponding masses are binned into a mass histogram, which is then fitted with Gaussian distributions. The number of counts for each peak in the histogram was utilized to determine the amount of monomer and dimer present as a percentage of all species greater than 100 kDa.

FA

mRNA samples were analyzed using the Agilent 5300 FAFA using the High Sensitivity RNA Kit (DNF-472). Samples were diluted in RNase-free water to final concentrations of 5 ng/μL. A final volume of 50 μL (5 μL of diluted RNA + 45 μL of diluent marker) was loaded per well into a 96-well RNase-free PCR plate. The plate was placed in an Applied Biosystems Thermal Cycler for 2 min at 70°C before being cooled on ice prior to loading onto the FA. Gel matrix, conditioning solution, and RNA ladder were prepared and used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Data were analyzed using Agilent ProSize software (v.3.0). mRNA quality was assessed based on peak size, absence of degradation products, and integrity relative to the RNA ladder. When applicable, RNA Integrity Number equivalent (RINe) values were used to quantify sample integrity. All reagents and materials were certified RNase-free, and workflows conducted under RNase-free conditions.

Data analysis

For analysis of cumulative binding events and the mono-exponential cumulative distribution fit, the number of binding events of particles within the selected mass ranges were tracked at each consecutive second and fitted to the equation y = A(1-e-kx), where y is the number of cumulative counts for that mass range, A is the plateau (asymptote maximum), k is the effective landing rate, and x is time. The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated using the equation R2 (1−SSres/SStot). SSres = sum (yexp − ymod)2 and SStot = sum (yexp − ymean)2 for each time point.

For calculations of % species in mRNA 1–3, particle counts below 100 kDa (MP and CD-MS) or signal below 300 bases (FA) were ignored to avoid counting contributions due to buffer. Low mass species were determined by the amount of counts ≥100 kDa (or ≥300 bases) and smaller than the monomer peak as a percentage of total counts (MP/CD-MS) or signal (FA) ≥ 100 kDa. Similarly, monomer and dimer abundances were calculated by the counts in each peak as a percentage of all counts or signal ≥100 kDa or 300 bases. Percentages do not add to 100% as there is a non-insignificant amount of intermediate species that do not resolve into a Gaussian peak for a specific oligomeric form.
