Endothelial Cell‐Specific Prolyl Hydroxylase‐2 Deficiency Augments Angiotensin II–Induced Arterial Stiffness and Cardiac Pericyte Recruitment in Mice
Authors: Bo Liu, Heng Zeng, Han Su, Quinesha A Williams, Jessie Besanson, Yingjie Chen, Jian‐Xiong Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndothelial prolyl hydroxylase‐2 (PHD2) is essential for pulmonary remodeling and hypertension. In the present study, we investigated the role of endothelial PHD2 in angiotensin II–mediated arterial stiffness, pericyte recruitment, and cardiac fibrosis.Methods and ResultsChondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 tracing reporter chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4– red fluorescent protein (DsRed) transgenic mice were crossed with PHD2flox/flox (PHD2f/f) mice and endothelial‐specific knockout of PHD2 (PHD2ECKO) mice. Transgenic PHD2f/f (TgPHD2f/f) mice and TgPHD2ECKO mice were infused with angiotensin II for 4 weeks. Arterial thickness, stiffness, and histological and immunofluorescence of pericytes and fibrosis were measured. Infusion of TgPHD2f/f mice with angiotensin II resulted in a time‐dependent increase in pulse‐wave velocity. Angiotensin II–induced pulse‐wave velocity was further elevated in the TgPHD2ECKO mice. TgPHD2ECKO also reduced coronary flow reserve compared with TgPHD2f/f mice infused with angiotensin II. Mechanistically, knockout of endothelial PHD2 promoted aortic arginase activity and angiotensin II–induced aortic thickness together with increased transforming growth factor‐β1 and ICAM‐1/VCAM‐1 expression in coronary arteries. TgPHD2f/f mice infused with angiotensin II for 4 weeks exhibited a significant increase in cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy, which was further developed in the TgPHD2ECKO mice. Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 pericyte was traced by DsRed+ staining and angiotensin II infusion displayed a significant increase of DsRed+ pericytes in the heart, as well as a deficiency of endothelial PHD2, which further promoted angiotensin II–induced pericyte increase. DsRed+ pericytes were costained with fibroblast‐specific protein 1 and α‐smooth muscle actin for measuring pericyte–myofibroblast cell transition. The knockout of endothelial PHD2 increased the amount of DsRed+/fibroblast‐specific protein 1+ and DsRed+/α‐smooth muscle actin+ cells induced by angiotensin II infusion.ConclusionsKnockout of endothelial PHD2 enhanced angiotensin II–induced cardiac fibrosis by mechanisms involving increasing arterial stiffness and pericyte–myofibroblast cell transitions.Keywords: angiotensin II, arterial stiffness, cardiac fibrosis, pericytes, prolyl hydroxylase‐2Subject Categories: Basic Science Research

Heart failure (HF) is a significant public health issue with high morbidity and mortality rates. 1 Hypertension, one of the major HF risk factors, results in a series of cardiac remodeling such as capillary rarefaction, cardiac fibrosis, myocyte hypertrophy, and diastolic dysfunction (DD). Among these structural changes, cardiac fibrosis is the main contributor to HF that gradually develops into HF with preserved ejection fraction. 2 , 3 Further investigation of the underlying mechanism of cardiac fibrosis is important for the development of potential treatments for HF with preserved ejection fraction caused by hypertension.

Cardiac fibrosis is generally attributed to the activation of myofibroblasts, which are responsible for the secretion of extracellular matrix. Increasingly, researchers have proposed that pericyte–myofibroblast transition could be a major source of fibrosis. 4 , 5 , 6 Pericytes localize around the basement membrane of microcirculation as mural cells, maintaining vascular homeostasis. 7 , 8 Their stem cell–like property enables the differentiation of pericytes to other cells, including fibrotic cells. The close interaction between endothelial cells and pericytes suggests that vascular endothelial cells may play a critical role in mediating the differentiation of pericytes to fibroblasts and the consequent cardiac fibrosis. Enhanced secretion of transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1) contributes to the pericyte–myofibroblast transition. 9 However, the association between pericytes, endothelial cells, and fibrosis in hypertension remains elusive.

Hypertension‐triggered cardiac remodeling may cause ischemia or hypoxia by increased energy expenditure and endothelial dysfunction, and in return, hypoxia further promotes cardiac remodeling. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF)‐prolyl 4‐hydroxylases (PHDs) play a key role in HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α, which functions imperatively in proliferation, cell survival, inflammation, and energy metabolism. 14 Among the 3 PHD isoforms identified (PHD1, PHD2, and PHD3), PHD2 is regarded as the principal cardiac isoform regulating HIF‐α, due to its substantial distribution in the heart. 15 , 16 It has been illustrated that PHD2, specifically, is essential to intracellular cAMP levels in cardiomyocytes. 17 It has been reported that deletion of PHD2 attenuated cardiac injuries and subsequent cardiac remodeling induced by myocardial ischemic injury via activation of the HIF‐α pathway. 18 , 19 , 20 Additionally, our previous study found that inhibition of PHD2 alleviated high‐fat diet–induced cardiac dysfunction. 21 These findings strongly support the critical role of the PHD2–HIF‐α pathway in cardiovascular diseases. Our recent studies found that specific knockout of PHD2 in endothelial cells (ECs; PHD2ECKO) significantly exacerbated vascular remodeling and the development of fibrosis in lung and kidney via upregulation of Notch3/TGF‐β1 signaling and excessive pericyte coverage. 22 , 23 It also simultaneously increased the deposition of adipose in the liver and impaired glucose tolerance. This led to the development of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis via the regulation of atrial natriuretic peptide and the angiotensin II/TGF‐β1 signaling pathway. 24 These data indicate endothelial PHD2 as a requisite character in the fibrosis of a myriad of organs. Despite this, the role of endothelial PHD2 in angiotensin II–induced hypertension and cardiac fibrosis is not fully understood. In the present study, using chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (NG2) tracing reporter NG2‐ red fluorescent protein (DsRed) transgenic mice, we tested a hypothesis that specific knockout of PHD2 in endothelial cells might contribute to angiotensin II–induced pericyte–fibrotic cell transition and the consequent cardiac fibrosis by enhancing the TGF‐β1 pathway.

Methods

Data Availability

All data and supporting materials have been provided with the published article.

Ethics Statement

All mouse experiments were conducted with ethical approval from the University of Mississippi Medical Center Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol ID: 1581) and in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (Publication No. 85‐23, revised 1996). All experimental animals were housed in ventilated cages with a 12‐hour light/dark cycle and controlled temperature (20 °C–22 °C) and fed normal chow and water ad libitum.

Generation of the Transgenic PHD2flox/flox and Transgenic PHD2ECKO Mice

PHD2flox/flox (PHD2f/f) mice were obtained from Dr Guo‐hua Fong at the University of Connecticut. PHD2f/f mice were used as a control mouse. Endothelial‐specific constitutive deletion of PHD2 (PHD2ECKO) mouse was generated using the Cre‐LoxP system as previously described. 22 , 23 Briefly, PHD2f/f mice were crossbred with EC‐specific constitutive Cre‐deleter mouse line B6. FVB‐Tg (Cdh5‐cre)7Mlia/J mice, which were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Strain No. 006137, Bar Harbor, ME). Cre‐mediated recombination resulted in the deletion of the flanked genome. Deletion of PHD2 in the endothelium was confirmed by Western blot analysis using cultured ECs isolated from PHD2ECKO mice. PHD2f/f mice and PHD2ECKO mice were then crossbred with NG2DsRedBAC [transgenic (Tg)(Cspg4‐DsRed.T1)1Akik/J] mice, which were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Stock No. 008241), respectively. TgPHD2f/f and TgPHD2ECKO were generated by labeled NG2 cells with red fluorescence. Genotyping was performed by tail DNA polymerase chain reaction analysis. Tail DNA was extracted using RED Extract‐N‐Amp™‐Tissue polymerase chain reaction kit (Sigma) and polymerase chain reaction was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primer sequences used for genotyping the Tg (Cspg4‐DsRed.T1)1Akik allele were as follows: Internal Positive Control Forward (IMR 7338) 5′‐CTA GGC CAC AGA ATT GAA AGA TCT‐3′, Internal Positive Control Reverse (IMR 7339) 5′‐GTA GGT GGA AAT TCT AGC ATC ATC C‐3′, Transgene Forward (IMR 8699) 5′‐TTC CTT CGC CTT ACA AGT CC‐3′, and Transgene Reverse (IMR 8700) 5′‐GAG CCG TAC TGG AAC TGG‐3′. The polymerase chain reaction products were analyzed on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide.

Osmotic Mini‐Pump Insertion

Male TgPHD2f/f and TgPHD2ECKO mice (6‐month‐old mice) were anesthetized by inhalation of 1.5% to 2% isoflurane in 100% oxygen. Osmotic mini‐pumps were implanted subcutaneously (Model 1004; Alzet, Cupertino, CA) in the midscapular region according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mini‐pumps delivered either sterile saline (vehicle) or 1.4 mg/kg per day of angiotensin II (Bachem, Americas, Inc.) for 4 weeks, as previously described. 25 , 26

Blood Pressure Measurements

Systolic blood pressure (BP), diastolic BP, mean arterial BP, and heart rate were monitored by tail‐cuff occlusion method, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Hatteras Instruments, Cary, NC). During the measurement, each mouse was first kept in restrainers heated to 37 °C while conscious. Following a 3‐day adjustment period and baseline recordings, BP and heart rate recordings were then obtained every day until 4 weeks after implantation. The results of measurements for each day were averaged for the final results. This method of BP analysis has been definitively validated. 27

Histological and Immunofluorescence Analysis

Mouse heart tissues were fixed with buffered 10% formalin solution (SF93‐20; Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA), embedded in frozen optimal‐cutting‐temperature compound (4583; Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA) and 8‐μm frozen sections prepared. Several sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Some were immunofluorescence stained with corresponding markers, including platelet‐derived growth factor receptor‐β (1:100, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and DsRed (1:200; GeneTex, Irvine, CA) antibodies for pericytes, α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA; Abcam, Cambridge, MA) for vascular smooth muscle cells (1:200), and fibroblast‐specific protein 1 (FSP‐1; GeneTex) for myofibroblasts (1:200). Other portions were immunofluorescence stained with TGF‐β1 (1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), ICAM‐1 (Invitrogen), VCAM‐1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), collagen‐1 (GeneTex) primary antibody followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate or Cy3 (1:500). Six random microscopic fields were analyzed using Image Analysis Software (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health). The calculation of pericytes was presented as DsRed+ and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor‐β+ numbers/total DAPI+ nuclei numbers. Calculations of myofibroblasts were presented as FSP‐1+ and α‐SMA+ numbers/total DAPI+ nuclei numbers. Calculation of pericyte–myofibroblast transition was presented as FSP‐1+/DsRed+ cell numbers/total DsRed+ nuclei numbers.

Other sections were stained with oxidation‐sensitive fluorescent dye dihydroethidium (Invitrogen) for evaluation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) content in the myocardium, 28 respectively. The aortic thickness (μm) was quantified by measuring 6 random microscopic fields of the aortic wall using image‐analysis software (Image J). Masson trichrome staining was conducted on coronary arteries and left ventricular sections to evaluate the degree of fibrosis in the coronary arteries and myocardium.

Echocardiography and Aortic Stiffness

After administration of angiotensin II for 4 weeks, transthoracic echocardiograms were measured by using the Vevo3100 Imaging System (Visual‐Sonics Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada). The cine loop of the left proximal coronary artery was visualized in a modified parasternal left ventricular (LV) short‐axis view by inhalation of 1% isoflurane for baseline and 2.5% isoflurane for hyperemic conditions for coronary flow reserve (CFR). The ratio of peak blood flow velocity during hyperemia to the peak blood flow velocity at baseline was used to calculate the CFR. High‐frequency Ultrasound Imaging software (VisualSonics Inc.) was used to analyze M‐mode cine loops for assessment of myocardial parameters and cardiac functions of the left ventricle including ejection fraction (%) and fractional shortening (%). 29 Arterial stiffness was measured with noninvasive Doppler ultrasound. The time difference between the peak aortic flow at 2 distinct locations of known distance in the aorta was measured to estimate pulse‐wave velocity. 29

Arginase Activity Assay

The arginase activity assay kit was acquired from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Arginase activity was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions to measure arginase activity derived from aortic tissue. 29

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean±SD and were analyzed using Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data were tested for normality by using the Shapiro–Wilk test and tested for equal variance by using the Brown–Forsythe test. For comparisons between 2 groups, the F test was used to compare the variances of the 2 groups. For equal variance, a 2‐tailed t test was used. For comparisons of ≥3 groups with 1 factor, normally distributed groups with equal variances were compared with a 1‐way ANOVA test, followed by the Tukey post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The Brown–Forsythe ANOVA test was used to compare all groups with unequal variances, followed by Dunnett's T3 test for multiple comparisons. For nonnormally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used followed by Dunn's test for multiple comparisons. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Knockout of PHD2 in EC Promotes, Angiotensin II–Induced Arterial Thickness, Stiffness, and Upregulation of Arginase Activity

Infusion of angiotensin II resulted in a significant elevation of BP, including systolic BP, diastolic BP, and mean arterial BP in TgPHD2f/f mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further enhanced the angiotensin II–induced elevation of BP (Figure 1). Angiotensin II infusion for 4 weeks led to a significant increase in the thickness of the aorta in TgPHD2f/f mice. The aortic thickness was not significantly increased in TgPHD2ECKO mice alone, whereas knockout of PHD2 in ECs exacerbated angiotensin II–induced aortic thickness (Figure 2A). Angiotensin II infusion also resulted in a significant and time‐dependent increase in pulse‐wave velocity in TgPHD2f/f mice. The pulse‐wave velocity levels were significantly elevated in TgPHD2ECKO mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further augmented the angiotensin II–induced elevation of pulse‐wave velocity in a time‐dependent manner (Figure 2B). Interestingly, infusion with angiotensin II significantly increased arginase activity in mouse aorta of PHD2f/f mice. Similarly, angiotensin II–induced arginase activity was also increased in TgPHD2ECKO mice (Figure 2C).

Knockout of PHD2 in EC Upregulates TGF‐β1 and ICAM‐1/VCAM‐1 Levels in the Coronary Artery in Response to Angiotensin II Infusion

TGF‐β1 immunostaining showed that knockout of endothelial PHD2 enhanced angiotensin II–induced TGF‐β1 expression in the mouse coronary arteries (Figure 3A). Furthermore, TGF‐β1 immunostaining was colocalized with DsRed+ cells in the coronary arteries of TgPHD2ECKO mice (Figure 3A). Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further enhanced angiotensin II–induced proinflammatory cytokines ICAM‐1 and VCAM‐1 expression in the mouse coronary arteries (Figure 3B and 3C). The fibrotic markers FSP‐1 and α‐SMA expression were also increased in the mouse arteries of TgPHD2ECKO mice infused with angiotensin II (Figure 3D and 3E). Masson trichrome staining showed that knockout of endothelial PHD2 enhanced angiotensin II–induced fibrosis in the mouse coronary arteries (Figure 3F). The coronary blood flow analysis revealed that CFR was significantly impaired in TgPHD2ECKO mice. Similarly, CFR was significantly reduced in the knockout of PHD2 mice infused with angiotensin II. Although TgPHD2ECKO mice infused with angiotensin II had a further reduction in CFR, there were no significant differences between TgPHD2ECKO mice + saline and TgPHD2ECKO mice + angiotensin II. Infusion of TgPHD2f/f mice with angiotensin II for 4 weeks did not significantly reduce CFR (Figure 3G).

EC‐Specific PHD2 Knockout Augments Pericyte Recruitment and Differentiation in the Myocardium Induced by Ang‐II Infusion

Using platelet‐derived growth factor receptor‐β as a pericyte marker, we examined the role of angiotensin II on pericyte recruitment within the heart. As shown in Figure 4A, mice infused with angiotensin II resulted in a significant increase of pericyte recruitment in the heart, and knockout of PHD2 in EC further exacerbated this recruitment. Pericyte recruitment was further validated by immunostaining with DsRed in the NG2‐DsRed mice. As shown in Figure 4B, there was a significant increase of DsRed+ cells in TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II as compared with TgPHD2f/f mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 accentuated these alterations. The expression of fibrotic markers FSP‐1 (fibroblasts) and α‐SMA (myofibroblasts) was significantly increased by angiotensin II infusion in TgPHD2ECKO mice compared with TgPHD2f/f mice or TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II (Figure 4C and 4D). By costaining DsRed with fibroblastic markers (FSP‐1 and α‐SMA), pericyte–fibrotic cell transitions were traced in the heart. As shown in Figure 4E and 4F, the numbers of FSP‐1+/DsRed+ cells were significantly increased in the hearts of TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II as compared with the TgPHD2f/f mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 also significantly increased the numbers of FSP‐1+/DsRed+ cells in the heart. The numbers of FSP‐1+/DsRed+ cells were further increased in TgPHD2ECKO mice + angiotensin II. Similarly, the numbers of α‐SMA+/DsRed+ cells were increased in the TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II compared with the TgPHD2f/f mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further increased the numbers of α‐SMA+/DsRed+ cells when infused with angiotensin II (Figure 4E).

Knockout of Endothelial PHD2 Increases Angiotensin II–Induced Collagen I Expression and Promotes Cardiac Hypertrophy and Diastolic Dysfunction in Mice

Masson trichrome staining showed that angiotensin II infusion resulting in cardiac fibrosis was exacerbated by loss of endothelial PHD2 (Figure 5C). Angiotensin II infusion significantly increased cardiac collagen‐1 expression. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further enhanced angiotensin II–induced collagen‐1 expression in the heart (Figure 5D). The ratio of heart weight/tibia length (hypertrophy) was significantly increased in TgPHD2f/f mice infused with angiotensin II. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further augmented the angiotensin II–induced cardiac hypertrophy (Figure 5E).

The echocardiographic analysis revealed that systolic function was significantly impaired in TgPHD2ECKO mice alone as evidenced by reductions of ejection fraction (%) and fractional shortening (%) compared with TgPHD2f/f mice. Knockout of PHD2 in EC also reduced ejection fraction (%) and fractional shortening (%) in angiotensin II–infused mice as compared with TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II (Figure 6A). Knockout of PHD2 in EC also increased end‐systolic diameter (s) and volume (s) in angiotensin II–infused mice as compared with TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II (Figure 6B). The levels of LV mass, end‐systolic LV anterior wall (s), and LV posterior wall (s) were significantly increased in TgPHD2ECKO mice + angiotensin II compared with TgPHD2ECKO mice (Figure 6C).

The diastolic function was significantly impaired in TgPHD2ECKO mice alone as evidenced by elevation of the isovolumic contraction time, the isovolumic relaxation time, and reduction of the early to late diastolic mitral annular tissue velocity (E′/A′ ratio) compared with TgPHD2f/f mice. TgPHD2EC KO mice infused with angiotensin II had a significant elevation of isovolumic contraction time, isovolumic relaxation time, and myocardial performance index compared with TgPHD2f/f mice with angiotensin II (Figure 6D and 6E). Discussion

Our present study demonstrated that specific knockout of PHD2 in ECs enhanced the angiotensin II–induced increase in aortic thickness and arterial stiffness. This was accompanied by a significant increase in arginase activity in the mouse aorta. Knockout of PHD2 in ECs also upregulated TGF‐β1 and inflammation in the endothelium of coronary arteries. This further led to an impairment of CFR together with significantly increased cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis. Furthermore, knockout of endothelial PHD2 augments angiotensin II–induced pericyte recruitment and pericyte–myofibroblast transition by the mechanisms involving the upregulation of the TGF‐β1 expression in the heart (Figure 6G).

Hypertension is a chronic disease that leads to hemodynamics pressure overload, which induces a series of vascular structural changes such as coronary artery remodeling and microvascular rarefaction. 30 It is well documented that endothelial cells function as hypoxia sensor. 31 HIF‐α plays an integral role due to its series of adaptive responses, which influence vascular tone, angiogenesis, metabolic balance, and cell death. 32 It has been reported that PHDs are tightly controlled by hypoxia. Under hypoxia, PHD levels were reduced, leading to the stabilization of HIF‐α. 33 , 34 PHD2 has been identified as a key regulator for HIF‐1α. 35 Although PHD2/HIF‐1α pathways are fully illustrated in pulmonary hypertension, 31 , 33 , 34 few focus on angiotensin II–induced arterial stiffness and vascular remodeling. 36 Using TgPHD2ECKO mice, we investigated the role of endothelial PHD2 in angiotensin II–induced vascular stiffness and coronary dysfunction. For the first time, we found that knockout of PHD2 in ECs augments angiotensin II–induced arterial thickness and arterial stiffness. Knockout of PHD2 in ECs also increased TGF‐β1 levels and inflammation in the endothelium of coronary arteries with an impairment of CFR, which may promote cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy in mice. Arginase, a critical urea cycle enzyme, is well known to compete with endothelial nitric oxide synthase for substrate l‐arginine to reduce nitric oxide production and increase the production of ROS. Increased arginase activity has been linked to EC dysfunction of several cardiovascular diseases including pulmonary hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis. 37 , 38 Increased arginase activity has been shown to increase arterial stiffness in an obese mouse model. 39 Similarly, elevated arginase activity contributed to angiotensin II–induced arterial thickening, fibrosis, and stiffness. 40 Consistent with these findings, our data revealed that deletion of endothelial PHD2 increased arginase activity and augmented angiotensin II–induced arterial thickness and stiffness as well as arginase activity in mice. The present study suggests that increased arginase activity may be involved in endothelial PHD2‐induced arterial fibrosis and stiffness and represents a promising therapeutic target.

Using novel specific DsRed‐PHD2ECKO transgenic mice with angiotensin II infusion, we aimed to investigate the potential role of endothelial PHD2 in hypertension‐induced cardiac fibrosis. Pericytes are mural cells surrounding microvasculature including capillaries, precapillary arterioles, postcapillary venules, and collecting venules. They can trans‐differentiate into surrounding cells due to their stem cell–like properties. 41 Accumulating studies indicate that pericyte–myofibroblast transition functions crucially to the pathological process of fibrosis. 42 , 43 In the present study, we used DsRed to specifically trace NG2+ pericytes that solely express DsRed. 44 An increased number of DsRed cells was observed in TgPHD2f/f mice + angiotensin II, whereas knockout of PHD2 in ECs exacerbated the recruitment of NG2+ pericyte in mouse hearts. Several lines of evidence reveal a pericyte–myofibroblast transition in kidneys and tumors. 45 , 46 Angiotensin II infusion has been shown to promote pericyte–fibrotic cell transition and cardiac fibrosis in mouse hearts. 44 Costaining with DsRed and FSP‐1/α‐SMA (a biomarker of fibroblast/myofibroblast) allowed measurement of the pericyte–fibroblast/myofibroblast transition. Within the present study, we observed that numbers of DsRed+/FSP‐1+ and DsRed+/α‐SMA+ cells triggered by angiotensin II were further increased by knockout of PHD2 in ECs. This indicates a regulatory role of PHD2 in pericyte–fibrotic cell transition induced by angiotensin II. Nonetheless, the specific mechanisms of how endothelial PHD2 influences pericyte activities need to be further investigated. Interactions between endothelial cells and pericytes, which are the mural cells surrounding ECs in microcirculation, are significant to both cell types. 41 Paracrine secretion functions imperatively to the interaction. Previous studies report TGF‐β, VEGF, platelet‐derived growth factor receptor‐β, angiotensin I/II and S1P as classic pathways, with the TGF‐β pathway being the principal one among these signaling. 41 TGF‐β secreted from endothelial cells is beneficial for pericyte activities such as proliferation and differentiation. 41 In this study, the absence of endothelial PHD2 enhanced TGF‐β1 expression caused by Ang‐II infusion, suggesting TGF‐β as the crucial mediator for accentuated cardiac fibrosis and pericyte transition by loss of PHD2 in hypertension. Previous studies reveal that the ROS–TGF‐β1 axis is essential to the pathological process of fibrosis. 47 , 48 In addition, ROS appears to accumulate when hypoxia chronically develops. 49 HIF‐1α, as the key regulator in hypoxia, was shown to mediate ROS formation in endothelial and epithelial cells, 49 , 50 and alteration in HIF‐1α levels was consistent with ROS formation. On the other hand, elevated ROS formation can also suppress PHD2 expression, 51 exacerbating the adverse effects of a lack of endothelial PHD2. Consistent with these findings, angiotensin II–induced ROS formation was magnified by a lack of endothelial PHD2. Our data support the notion that PHD2 potentially mediates pericyte‐fibrotic cell transition in the hypertensive heart via the ROS‐TGF‐β1 axis. We conclude that endothelial PHD2 has a critical function in angiotensin II–induced cardiac fibrosis via pericyte–fibrotic cell transition associated with the ROS–TGF‐β1 pathway.

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is clinically diagnosed by the presence of reduced CFR (a ratio of maximal hyperemic coronary blood flow to resting coronary flow). 52 Recent studies have highlighted CMD as an underlying pathology of ischemic HF as well as DD. 53 , 54 , 55 CMD is the cause of one third of angina pectoris in patients with stable symptoms. 53 , 56 There is a high prevalence of CMD in patients with DD. 57 , 58 Although the clinic is important, the contributions of CMD and endothelial dysfunction to DD remain an underappreciated area without proven treatments due to the lack of mechanistic studies. Endothelial dysfunction and CMD have been well documented in patients with diabetes. 59 , 60 , 61 Our previous studies showed that diabetes disrupts hypoxic signaling, thus leading to cardiac fibrosis and microvascular rarefaction. 62 These abnormalities may result in a fall in CFR and impaired capillary perfusion that renders the heart vulnerable to hypoxia and ultimately causes DD. To date, the direct contribution of endothelial hypoxia signaling to DD remains elusive. Our present study for the first time revealed that the knockout of endothelial PHD2 resulted in significant impairments of CFR and diastolic function in mice. These impairments are further enhanced or deteriorated when infused with angiotensin II. In addition, the loss of myocardial capillaries due to pericyte detachment and differentiation into myofibroblast may also be a contributor to CMD and DD in endothelial PHD2‐deficiency mice. Taken together, our study provided a piece of new evidence that endothelial hypoxic signaling is one of the key factors for the development of HF with preserved ejection fraction or DD.

Conditional knockout of PHD2 has been shown to reduce TNFα and ICAM‐1 expression and attenuate macrophage infiltration in obese mice. This was accompanied by a significant improvement in cardiac function. 21 In the present study, we showed that knockout of endothelial PHD2 promoted angiotensin II–induced ICAM‐1/VCAM‐1 expression in coronary arteries. Interestingly, the expression of ICAM‐1/VCAM‐1 was not increased in the PHD2EC KO mice without angiotensin II infusion (Q.A. Williams, MS, unpublished data, 2023). Those discrepancies may be due to the different times or different cell types of activation of the HIF‐α signal pathway by PHD2 knockout. We proposed that short‐term activation of HIF‐α is beneficial and protective while long‐term activation of HIF‐α is detrimental. These notions were supported by the studies from our group and other investigators. 21 , 36 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 For example, conditional knockout myeloid PHD2 reduces vein graft disease and ameliorates vein graft lesion stability by improving intraplaque angiogenesis. 63 Global conditional knockout of PHD2 increased HIF‐2α/Notch3 expression, improved pericyte/EC coverage, and reduced the mortality rate in the LPS‐treated mice. 66 In contrast, long‐term activation of endothelial HIF‐α by knockout of endothelial PHD2 resulted in pulmonary hypertension. 65 Taken together, these studies suggested cell‐specific and time‐dependent effects of PHD2 on the cardiovascular system.

Although we provided important evidence of endothelial PHD2 in CMD, there are some limitations. First, angiotensin II–induced elevation of BP was measured by tail cuff, which is not the gold standard method, and the BP may be interfered with due to the mouse being restricted. Second, the expression of proteins is solely dependent on immunostains or coimmunostains, which cannot quantify protein levels. Finally, we could not determine if endothelial PHD2 impacts coronary blood flow and perivascular fibrosis by autocrine or paracrine signaling. Further studies are warranted to address these questions.

In summary, this is the first study to our knowledge to reveal that the absence of PHD2 in ECs enhanced the angiotensin II–induced aortic thickness, arginase activity, and arterial stiffness. Knockout of PHD2 in ECs upregulated TGF‐β1 and inflammation in the coronary artery with a reduced CFR in mice. Knockout of endothelial PHD2 further augments angiotensin II–induced cardiac pericyte recruitment and pericyte–fibroblast/myofibroblast transition. These alterations may promote cardiac fibrosis and cardiac hypertrophy, which contribute to DD and hypertensive HF in mice (Figure 6G).
