Ecological change may be a common initiator of evolutionary pollinator shifts
Authors: Agnes S. Dellinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlthough evolutionary pollinator shifts are accepted as major sources of floral and species diversity in angiosperms, the mechanisms triggering pollinator shifts remain largely unexplored. In 1970, Stebbins proposed that ecological change may be a common initiator of evolutionary pollinator shifts, but empirical tests of Stebbins' hypothesis remain scarce. The lack of studies exploring the drivers of pollinator shifts likely stem from the lack of a clear definition of ecological change, and the inherent challenges of studying past evolutionary events empirically. I here propose a definition of ecological change as any abiotic or biotic macro‐ or microecological, extrinsic or intrinsic change that significantly alters the effectiveness of a taxon's ancestral pollinator(s). Because such ecological change may happen at different scales, I argue that taking an integrative macro–micro‐evolutionary approach will allow us to test where and when which type of ecological change is a common driver of pollinator shifts. I propose macroevolutionary tests of abiotic macroecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts as the easiest and most effective method, allowing for the comparison of a large number of independent shifts to establish whether there are broad generalities in evolutionary pollinator shifts, and determine when and where there are not.

Introduction

Evolutionary pollinator shifts, commonly defined as shifts among different functional groups of pollinators (sensu Fenster et al., 2004), are accepted as major sources of floral and species diversity in flowering plants. Per definition, different functional pollinator groups impose different selective pressures on floral traits (Smith & Kriebel, 2018; Dellinger et al., 2019), optimizing pollen transfer with the different vectors. Traditionally, optimized pollen transfer by just the single most effective pollinator (group) has been proposed as a driver of divergent floral phenotypes (‘most effective pollinator principle’, Stebbins, 1970, Medel et al., 2018), while more modern concepts envision flowers as ‘adaptive landscapes’ shaped by whichever combination of pollinator groups results in the largest fitness contribution (Ohashi et al., 2021; Kay & Anderson, 2025). Either process may lead to the evolution of strikingly divergent floral phenotypes even among closely related plant species, and provide reproductive isolation through differential pollinator attraction or fit even across few generations (Ramsey et al., 2003; Hoballah et al., 2007; Hermann et al., 2013; Carrió & Güemes, 2014; Gervasi & Schiestl, 2017; Binaghi et al., 2023; Wessinger, 2024). Changes in pollination effectiveness or in the cumulative fitness contribution of one or multiple pollinator guilds (sensu Kay & Anderson, 2025) are commonly regarded as triggers of evolutionary pollinator shifts (Stebbins, 1970; Rocca & Sazima, 2013; Dellinger et al., 2021). Such changes in pollination effectiveness may occur through independent or cumulative changes in pollinator quantity and quality. Pollinator quantity commonly refers to the abundance and flower visitation rates of pollinators while pollinator quality relates to behavioral or morphological differences of pollinators and measures aspects, such as the amount of pollen transferred per visit, or offspring quality (e.g. Rodríguez‐Rodríguez et al., 2013). However, despite the prominent roles that pollinators play in the evolution and diversification of flowering plants, the mechanisms which lead to changes in pollination effectiveness and hence drive evolutionary pollinator shifts remain largely unexplored (Kay et al., 2019; Kay & Anderson, 2025). Thus, it remains unclear to date whether there are broad ecological generalities driving evolutionary pollinator shifts.

More than 55 years ago, Stebbins (1970) proposed ‘ecological change’ as a likely large‐scale initiator of evolutionary pollinator shifts through changes in pollination effectiveness, but his appealing, intuitive hypothesis has remained largely untested until today (Thomson & Wilson, 2008, but see Hamilton & Wessinger, 2022, Kopper et al., 2025). Stebbins (1970) envisioned a scenario where ‘The evolutionary shift from one vector to another is probably triggered by the entrance of the plant into a habitat where the original vector is scarce and the new vector is abundant’. Largely influenced by the observation that hummingbird‐pollinated species in North America often occur in moist, forested ravines while their bee‐pollinated relatives occur in open, sunny environments (Grant, 1949; Stebbins, 1989), Stebbins proposed that reduced bee‐pollinator effectiveness in the newly colonized ravine habitats would facilitate shifts to more effective hummingbird pollinators (Fig. 1; also see Thomson & Wilson, 2008). Although Stebbins' idea that some type of ecological change may trigger pollinator shifts through changes in pollination effectiveness seems to have become generally accepted, it remains largely untested. The lack of tests of Stebbins' ecological change hypothesis is likely due to the lack of a clear definition of what ecological change means in the context of pollinator shifts, and a framework for testing it. As it stands, ecological change refers to some change in a plant's environment, and may encompass various aspects of more obvious macroecological changes, such as range expansions into novel ecosystems (Fig. 1), less obvious microecological changes in microhabitat as well as alterations in biotic interactions, which remain difficult to quantify and compare across study systems.

Hypothesized scenario for how ecological change (sensu Stebbins, 1970) may initiate evolutionary pollinator shifts, using colonization of novel environments with altered pollinator communities (2) as stand‐in for ecological change, and shifts from bee to hummingbird pollination (3) as example for evolutionary pollinator shifts. (1) In its ancestral habitat, a plant species is pollinated by its ancestral bee pollinator; the flower may show adaptations to this ‘most effective pollinator’, which is highly abundant and transfers pollen effectively (indicated by same coloration). (2) Over time, the plant species expands its range uphill and colonizes higher elevation habitats which feature different bee species (green) as pollinators. Gradual adaptation to the high‐elevation bee pollinator community, potentially together with isolation by distance or environment may lead to reproductive isolation and speciation, resulting in two distinct bee‐pollinated species (compare Grant, 1949 on pollinator‐mediated speciation, and Johnson, 2025). (3) Compared with the abundant low‐elevation bee pollinators, montane bee pollinators are relatively infrequent as flower visits because their activity is heavily restricted by cold and rainy mountain weather (Cruden, 1972), leading to overall low reproductive success of the montane bee‐pollinated species. Since our hypothetical montane bee‐pollinated flower also offers nectar as a reward, hummingbirds, which are abundant in the montane environment, start visiting the flowers. (4) In montane areas or microhabitats where the fitness contribution of montane bee pollinators is particularly low, hummingbirds may become more important pollinators, especially so when flowers start adapting to the novel pollinators. Such adaptations may occur through major mutations or strong pollinator‐mediated selection increasing visitation and pollen transfer rates of the novel hummingbird pollinators relative to the montane bee pollinators. Such scenarios may lead to the evolution of three distinct species, two of which are bee‐pollinated, one of which is pollinated by novel hummingbird pollinators. Note that adaptive evolution of traits increasing pollination effectiveness of bees in the montane bee‐pollinated species (green), such as increased display or rewards (Kopper et al., 2025), may be essential to enable the bee‐pollinated species to persist in the novel environment and retain its ancestral pollination system.

Another reason for the lack of broad‐scale tests of Stebbins' ecological change hypothesis lies in the difficulty of choosing the right scale of study. Macroevolutionary studies on pollinator shifts commonly focus on completed shifts, hence lacking information on the early stages of when shifts happen, critical for identifying underlying mechanisms (Kay et al., 2019; Johnson, 2025). Ecological as well as population‐level studies capable of documenting recent or ongoing divergence in pollinator interactions, by contrast, are often limited to documenting variation in traits, but rarely expand to exploring the role of environmental divergence in causing this variation (Wenzell et al., 2023). Furthermore, these ecological studies commonly lack a macroevolutionary perspective (i.e. evolutionary replication of events) required to discern randomness from potential causal relationships and hence critical for deducing potential ecological generalities of pollinator shifts (Fig. 2). With the increasing availability of well‐resolved molecular phylogenies and ongoing digitization efforts of botanical collections, I argue that now is the time to start closing the gap between the ecological documentation of plant–pollinator interactions and their macroevolutionary dynamics to explicitly test Stebbins' ecological change hypothesis as a driver of pollinator shifts. In the following, I will propose a definition of ecological change, lay out an approach for better integrating macro‐ and microevolutionary data, and explore the major conceptual advances we may gain from such an integrated analysis of the evolutionary dynamics of the ecological change–pollinator shift associations.

Ecological patterns (i) of pollinator–environment associations may stem from singular evolutionary origins (ii) or repeated evolutionary events (iii), as exemplified here for hypothetical bee‐ and hummingbird‐pollinated species across an elevational gradient. If pollinator–environment associations appear repeatedly across a clade's evolutionary history (iii; and even if singular events (ii) are repeated across multiple plant lineages), we may explore questions around the relative timing of environment and pollinator shifts to test whether environment shifts (abiotic ‘macroecological change’) may indeed be initiators of evolutionary pollinator shifts (compare Fig. 1).

Defining ‘ecological change’

Ecological change likely invokes some image in each reader, with the number of different images perhaps matching the number of readers. If our aim was to think about ecological change as a potential driver of pollinator shifts, then our question should always be: ‘Does the ecological change have an effect on pollination effectiveness (or the cumulative fitness function of all flower visitors)?’ By making fitness, or derivatives of it, such as pollination performance (Opedal et al., 2023) or pollinator functional group (Fenster et al., 2004) the response variable, we are able to perform hypothesis tests to explore whether whichever aspect of ecological change has a significant effect on our response, thereby facilitating synthesis across both macro‐ and microevolutionary scales. But while the response variable is clear, what is the explanatory variable, ecological change?

Perhaps the most easily imaginable and quantifiable ecological change over evolutionary timescales is abiotic and macroecological (Box 1), such as environmental niche shifts when a plant lineage expands its range. If range expansions include the colonization of environments with significantly different pollination effectiveness compared with a lineage's ancestral environments, we may expect the change in pollination effectiveness to result in pollinator shifts (Fig. 1). Theoretically, negative effects of reductions in pollination effectiveness in novel environments may become particularly pronounced when multiple taxa compete for the same (ancestral) pollinators, thereby accelerating shifts or character displacement (Beans, 2014; Cozzolino et al., 2022). Empirical support for abiotic macroecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts comes from studies on bee‐hummingbird shifts in North American Penstemon (Hamilton & Wessinger, 2022), bee–vertebrate shifts in tropical Melastomataceae (Dellinger et al., 2021; Kopper et al., 2025), insect–bird shifts in South African Lachenalia (Duncan et al., 2024), and has also been suggested for butterfly–hummingbird shifts in North American Lilium (Givnish et al., 2020a). In all cases, bee‐pollinated relatives of pollinator‐shifted species or clades occur in significantly different macroenvironmental niches. Sister species comparisons, for example, in Clarkia also suggest that ecogeographic habitat differentiation goes along with pollinator shifts (Miller et al., 2014; Goff et al., 2021), and so do community‐level studies documenting different habitat associations between abiotic and biotic reproductive strategies (Givnish et al., 2020b).

Prominent ecogeographic patterns in the global distribution of major functional pollinator groups (Schemske et al., 2009; Dellinger et al., 2023) further point toward abiotic macroecological change as a key driver of evolutionary pollinator shifts. For example, tropical ecosystems boast a broad diversity of functional pollinator groups, such as bats, nectar‐feeding birds, reptiles, and mammals, absent from most temperate regions (with the exception of hummingbirds in the temperate Americas; Ollerton, 2017). Similarly, elevational gradients commonly exhibit major turnovers in functional pollinator groups, from insect‐dominated pollinator communities in lowlands featuring many different hymenoptera, coleoptera, and lepidoptera to bumblebee‐ and fly‐dominated pollinator communities in the high‐elevation Alpine zone (Arroyo et al., 1982; Perillo et al., 2017; Lefebvre et al., 2018; McCabe & Cobb, 2021). In Neotropical mountains, diverse bee‐pollinator communities in lowlands are replaced by hummingbird‐dominated communities in high‐elevation cloud forests (Cruden, 1972; Dellinger et al., 2023). Differences in the physiological properties and dietary requirements of different pollinator groups are commonly regarded as explanations for these nonrandom distributions of pollinator groups across the globe and may directly feedback to differences in pollination effectiveness and hence plant fitness. For example, endothermic pollinators like vertebrates, bumblebees, or cold‐tolerant flies may fare better and hence represent more effective pollinators under low temperature conditions common in mountains than ectothermic pollinators like many bees, butterflies, beetles, and reptiles (Cruden, 1972; McCallum et al., 2013; Classen et al., 2015; Oyen & Dillon, 2018; Givnish et al., 2020a; Dellinger et al., 2021; Herrera et al., 2023). Abiotic macroecological change does not have to be restricted to the spatial scale but may also be temporal – divergence in flowering phenology between dry and wet season or summer and winter may expose species to markedly different pollinator communities. In South Africa and South‐East Asia, for example, several studies have shown that bird‐pollinated species flower in the cooler, rainy months when insect pollinators are less abundant, while insect‐pollinated relatives flower in the warmer and drier seasons (Funamoto, 2019; Duncan et al., 2024).

Although the existing studies on ecogeographic differentiation in pollination strategies suggest a major role of macroecological change in pollinator shifts, there are prominent model systems (e.g. Costus) where pollinator shifts happen in the absence of macroclimatic niche differentiation (Kay & Grossenbacher, 2022). This does not mean, however, that ecological change needs to be absent. If less easily detectible abiotic microecological change such as microclimatic niche differentiation (Box 1) leads to changes in the effectiveness of a species' ancestral pollinators, these subtle changes may suffice to shift floral fitness landscapes and hence pollination systems (Kay & Anderson, 2025). In sympatric sites of two Costus species, for example, microhabitat differentiation results in different flower visitor spectra (Juárez & Kay, 2025): Costus wilsonii, restricted to topographically more exposed, more seasonal ridges, is pollinated by hummingbirds, while bee‐pollinated Costus kuntzei occurs in warmer sites down the slope in the same forest. Similarly, single‐species (e.g. Dongzhou et al., 2025) as well as community‐level studies (e.g. Ohler et al., 2020) in Alpine habitats have demonstrated that subtle microhabitat differences in soil type or soil temperature can impact pollinator type and pollinator species richness, with the potential to act as ecological change strong enough to cause long‐term changes in pollinator effectiveness. Such small‐scale microenvironmental differences may have particularly strong effects on ectothermic pollinators, such as many insect groups. For example, increased heat or cold through differences in microhabitat shadowing (McCabe et al., 2019) may shorten foraging opportunities for insect pollinators (Herrera et al., 2023), leading to fewer but perhaps longer visits per flower and hence smaller or genetically less diverse pollen loads. Similarly, changes in anthesis patterns in the course of the day may expose species to different pollinator communities, especially if daily temperature fluctuations differentially impact pollinator activity (e.g. Cruden, 1972; Dellinger et al., 2025).

Ecological change is not restricted to abiotic environmental factors, however. We may envision at least two broad categories of ecological change that are biotic in nature. Extrinsic biotic changes (Box 1) may refer to changes in a focal plant species' biotic environment, that is in its interactions with all other living organisms. With biotic interactions generally being more challenging to capture and quantify than macroecological abiotic conditions, such changes may go largely unseen. However, evidence for how biotic ecological change may impact plant–pollinator interactions and, if persisting long term, could lead to pollinator shifts is gradually aggregating. For example, new species coming into flower across the season or successional changes in the co‐flowering community may greatly shift the relative and cumulative fitness contributions of pollinators through changes in the pollinator visitor spectrum, their behavior or visitation rate (Kay & Schemske, 2003; Lázaro et al., 2009; Albor et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2020; Kemp et al., 2022). Such changes in visitation rates induced by other co‐flowering members in the community might lead to floral convergence if a species benefits from looking similar to and sharing pollinators with other co‐flowering species (facilitation; Guerrero et al., 2025). Alternatively, if competition for pollinators is strong, a species should benefit from looking different from and attracting different pollinators than other co‐flowering species, driving floral divergence and, potentially, shifts in pollination systems in the absence of abiotic ecological change (Muchhala et al., 2014; Temeles et al., 2016; Kay & Anderson, 2025). Strong competition among pollinators, coupled with thermal restrictions by the environment, has also been suggested to explain why moth‐pollinated species flower at nighttime in the tropics but at daytime in temperate zones (Cruden et al., 1976). Specifically, tropical nights are warm and come with the benefit that nectar‐foraging hummingbirds, major competitors of moths, are inactive, while in North American mountains, nights are too cold for moth activity but because competition by hummingbirds is generally lower, moths commonly forage during daytime (Cruden et al., 1976). Furthermore, the appearance of a novel pollinator, such as the supergeneralist honeybee Apis mellifera, or introduced plant species may also signify biotic ecological change with the capacity of altering pollination effectiveness (Johnson & Ashman, 2019). Many other biotic interactions, such as with herbivores and pathogens, mycorrhizal fungi or nectar microbes can further impact plant–pollinator interactions, but whether changes in those interactions may be strong or permanent enough to affect changes in pollination effectiveness and ultimately pollinator shifts remains unknown (Barber & Gorden, 2015; Kessler & Chautá, 2020; Quevedo‐Caraballo et al., 2025).

The last category of biotic ecological change which may technically alter pollination effectiveness are intrinsic changes, so changes inherent to a specific plant lineage or species (Box 1). Shifts in life form such as from tree to herb could expose species to different pollinator communities and microclimates in the canopy vs the forest floor (Neves et al., 2023). Mutations in floral traits relevant for pollinator attraction like color, scent and reward have also been proposed as potential triggers of pollinator shifts. Experimental work with artificial flowers and in hybrid zones has indeed confirmed that changes in one, and especially multiple attraction traits can lead to the differential attraction of bee and hummingbird pollinators (e.g. Gegear et al., 2017). Although such strong differential attraction may facilitate the rapid spread of floral trait changes in a population, there is general agreement that intrinsic ecological change through trait mutations is unlikely to trigger pollinator shifts in isolation (Thomson & Wilson, 2008; Wessinger, 2024; Kay & Anderson, 2025). Instead, shifts in growth form often occur in parallel with shifts in abiotic environmental niches (Neves et al., 2023) and spontaneous mutations of large effect on floral traits are more likely to become fixed in a population when accompanied by some extrinsic ecological change (Kay & Anderson, 2025). Thus, while partitioning ecological change into abiotic and biotic components is helpful conceptually, they likely often interact and are best considered in an integrative manner (Dellinger et al., 2024).

Choosing the right scale to test the role of ecological change in pollinator shifts

Whether abiotic or biotic factors drive diversification and biodiversity patterns on our planet remains a central and controversial question of evolutionary biology (Benton, 2009; Condamine et al., 2020), and has rarely been investigated in the context of pollinator shifts. The ‘Court Jester’ scenario broadly states that extrinsic abiotic factors explain diversity dynamics, and indeed, abiotic macroecological changes are emerging as the major moderators of biodiversity patterns across large spatial (i.e. continental) and temporal (> 1 million years (Myr)) scales (Benton, 2009, Condamine et al., 2020). By contrast, the ‘Red Queen’ scenario posits that species‐intrinsic biotic factors explain diversity patterns, and those biotic factors are commonly assumed to affect more localized diversity dynamics across short (< 1 Myr) evolutionary time scales (Benton, 2009, Condamine et al., 2020). Clearly, abiotic and biotic processes are often interlinked so that abiotic ecological changes such as climate change may be the initiators of biotic ecological changes, such as changes in the co‐flowering community (Faust & Iler, 2022; Zoller et al., 2023). Similarly, biotic interactions such as competition among co‐flowering plants may drive divergence in flowering times, thereby exposing species to different abiotic conditions and pollinator communities (Cruden et al., 1976; Skeels et al., 2021). Although immediately relevant for understanding whether and, if so, which combination of ecological change is a common driver of evolutionary pollinator shifts, studies which explicitly test for the role of abiotic and/or biotic factors as triggers of pollinator shifts remain scarce (Skeels et al., 2021; Hamilton & Wessinger, 2022; Dellinger et al., 2024). Thus, it remains unclear what type of ecological change is the most likely to drive evolutionary pollinator shifts at macro‐ and microevolutionary scales, and how strong ecological change needs to be to trigger pollinator shifts.

Because ecological change with the potential of triggering pollinator shifts may be abiotic or biotic in nature, and because we may expect the two to operate on different spatial and temporal scales, tests of Stebbins' ecological change‐pollinator–shift hypothesis require tests at different levels, that is macro‐ and microevolutionary. Microevolutionary population‐level studies assessing geographic variation in pollinators across a species' distribution range have been proposed as the ideal level of study for understanding how pollinator shifts happen through the formation of pollination ecotypes (Johnson, 2025). Especially when such population‐level studies represent mixed pollination systems encompassing different functional pollinator groups (see Moir et al., 2025), they allow not only for documenting under which abiotic environmental conditions which pollinators fare better or worse, but also for assessing how variation in biotic components such as the co‐flowering community affects the performance of different pollinators and floral adaptive landscapes. Furthermore, such microevolutionary studies are amenable to manipulative experiments, where researchers may artificially change the co‐flowering community, put species in artificial sympatry, or through transplants test for the effects of microhabitat differentiation on pollination performance (e.g. Dorey & Schiestl, 2024; Juarez & Kay, 2025). Experimental manipulations of flowers, such as changes in floral orientation, color or scent (Hoballah et al., 2007; Armbruster & Muchhala, 2020) further allow for testing for the effects of intrinsic biotic ecological change on population‐level pollination performance.

While population‐level studies are particularly powerful for exploring the effects of biotic ecological change on pollination performance, I argue that we should, in addition, start leveraging macroevolutionary data to explore the role of (abiotic) ecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts. The limitation of population‐level studies stems, in part, from the fact that they are usually restricted to the comparison of just a few populations and hence may often only reveal insights on singular, isolated shifts (e.g. in a single population). Establishing ecological generalities of environment‐shift‐pollinator–shift associations, however, requires independent evolutionary replication across a lineage's phylogeny (Fig. 2). Furthermore, although population‐level studies are highly relevant for capturing the effects of realized, momentary plant–pollinator interactions such as annual fluctuations in pollinator or plant communities (Mayfield et al., 2001) and the early stages of potential pollinator shifts (Kay et al., 2019), they may not always accurately capture the long‐term effects of pollinator‐mediated selection required for completing evolutionary pollinator shifts. That said, if population‐level studies of plant–pollinator interactions are paired with population‐level molecular assessments to reconstruct the relative relatedness among populations, and repeated ecological change–pollinator shift associations can be documented across populations with limited gene flow, population‐level studies may effectively bridge the gap between ecological pattern and evolutionary outcome. In the following, I will lay out expectations for how ecological change and pollinator shifts may appear in a phylogenetic context, how we may test for abiotic macroecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts, and when assessments should be paired by microevolutionary assessments.

Taking a comparative phylogenetic (macroevolutionary) approach allows us to trace the evolutionary dynamics of pollinator shifts across a large number of taxa and ecological conditions, facilitating tests of which type and strength of ecological change may result in pollinator shifts. Specifically, by employing methods from comparative phylogenetics, we may explore the relative temporal sequence of ecological change and pollinator shifts, thereby understanding which one likely came first, and how often that is the case (Fig. 3). Specifically, we may envision at least four different scenarios for how ecological change and pollinator shifts may or may not be related, with clear expectations for the patterns and relative timing such associations would exhibit on plant phylogenies (Fig. 3). For simplicity, I will use environment shifts as a stand‐in for ecological change in my examples. First, if shifts into novel environments with altered abiotic conditions are a common driver of evolutionary pollinator shifts, we may expect: a clear linkage between environment shifts and pollinator shifts in that the two occur in the same clades on phylogenetic trees; and environment shifts to occur on deeper (older) branches than pollinator shifts (Fig. 3a). Consequently, this would mean that ancestors with the ancestral pollination system first successfully colonized novel environments, where they then experienced reductions in the effectiveness of their ancestral pollinators, followed by shifts to novel pollinators. If such scenarios apply, we may expect closely related species with the ancestral as well as the novel pollination system to occur in substantially different environments than more distantly related relatives which retained the ancestral pollinators. Conversely, it is possible to imagine a scenario where pollinator shifts preceded environment shifts, reflected by pollinator shifts occurring along deeper (older) branches of the phylogeny than environment shifts (Fig. 3b). Such a finding would indicate that evolutionary pollinator shifts occur independently of environment shifts and are likely driven by other factors. Pollinator shifts would, in turn, facilitate subsequent range expansions, for instance into areas where the novel pollinators are more abundant or diverse. Under such scenarios, we may expect most extant species which shifted pollinators to occur in different environments than their close and distant relatives which retained ancestral pollinators. It is also plausible to imagine that pollinator shifts and environment shifts occur as a gradual process and go hand‐in‐hand, reflected through strongly correlated evolution and changes in both traits along the same branches of the phylogeny (Fig. 3c). Such scenarios may be particularly plausible when studying the effect of major floral trait mutations on pollinator shifts, where finding the derived state (trait mutation) both among a taxon with the novel and the ancestral pollination system is unlikely. Finally, pollinator shifts and environment shifts may be unrelated and occur on different branches of the phylogeny, with no association in terms of relative timing (Fig. 3d).

Four scenarios for how abiotic environment shifts (as stand‐ins for ecological change) and pollinator shifts may be linked over deep time, represented through ancestral character estimation on plant phylogenies, pollination on the left side phylogeny, and environment on the right side phylogeny in each panel. Ancestral states are represented by blue colors; novel states are represented by orange/yellow colors for both pollination and environment; dots with one color represent 100% certainty of a specific tip or node exhibiting the respective state, while dots with two colors at nodes represent 50% probability of having either state (note that in actual analyses, many different probabilities may exist). (a) According to Stebbins' (1970) hypothesis, ecological change, represented as shifts into novel environments here, may act as important initiator of evolutionary pollinator shifts. If environment shifts do indeed precede pollinator shifts, we may expect a higher probability of having shifted environment than of having shifted pollinators at deeper nodes. Comparing tip states, we may further expect closely related species both with the ancestral and novel pollination system to occur in novel environments, supporting the idea that first, novel environments were colonized, where then, pollinator shifts happened. (b) Alternatively, finding that pollinator shifts consistently precede environment shifts would indicate that shifts in biotic interactions may be a critical first step rather than a result of range expansions into novel environments. Under such scenarios, we would expect a higher probability at ancestral nodes of having shifted to novel pollinators than to novel environments. Among tips, we would expect to find novel pollinators even among species occurring in the ancestral environment, assuming that not all ancestors dispersed to novel environments and that species with novel pollinators did not go extinct in the novel environment. (c) Another alternative could be correlated evolution of both pollinator and environment shifts in that the two go hand‐in‐hand. Under such scenarios, we would expect similar elevated probabilities of having shifted pollinator and environment at the same ancestral nodes, and matching tips exhibiting both novel pollinators and occurring in novel environments. (d) Finally, environment and pollinator shifts could be unrelated, meaning that tips with novel pollinators may or may not occur in novel environments, and that ancestral probabilities of having shifted or not are unlinked.

Explicitly testing the expectations for the evolutionary sequence of ecological change and pollinator shifts in a phylogenetic comparative setting (Fig. 3) has several advantages. First, besides giving the required evolutionary replication of shifts, it also allows for assessing how strong ecological change has to be in order to trigger a pollinator shift. When ecological change is recorded as a quantitative (multivariate) variable, one can quantify the difference in niche attributes among closely related taxa which shifted pollinators and which retained their ancestral pollinators, as well as compare those with more distantly related species which retained ancestral pollinators. Such comparisons and associated significance tests allow for differentiating the strength of (abiotic) ecological change inherent to a lineage's speciation process (i.e. niche differentiation among species independent of pollinator shifts) from that associated with pollinator shifts (Kopper et al., 2025). Second, comparative tests of niche differentiation may help determine whether different pollinator shifts (e.g. bee‐bird vs bee‐fly) underlie different thresholds (strength) or types (e.g. divergence in temperature or precipitation) of ecological change. Such pollinator‐group specific thresholds may very well exist, exemplified by studies documenting that fly pollination increases in importance globally at c. 5°C mean annual temperature (McCabe & Cobb, 2021). Similarly, Kopper et al. (2025) found shifts from bee to vertebrate pollination in tropical Melastomataceae to occur at high elevations (generally above 1500 m above sea level), while shifts from bee to generalist insect pollination occurred at lower elevations and hence may underlie different drivers.

The fact that some types of ecological change are more easily explored in a phylogenetic comparative setting than others (Box 1) need not be a limitation but rather a strength of the approach. The arguably most easily testable types of ecological change are macroecological abiotic factors such as macroenvironmental, biogeographic or seasonal differentiation, and some intrinsic biotic factors such as changes in growth form. For both of those aspects, information may be retrieved relatively easily and quickly from global databases or species descriptions for a large number of species. Certainly, focusing hypothesis tests only on these easy‐to‐collect data means that important aspects of ecological change as drivers of pollinator shifts may be left out. However, because studies investigating the drivers of pollinator shifts across different plant clades are currently largely lacking, leveraging the easiest‐to‐collect data for testing for recurring associations (Fig. 3a–c) with pollinators shifts is warranted for the time being. Furthermore, detecting a lack of association (Fig. 3d) would be equally important and should not be dismissed as an uninteresting negative result. Instead, studies finding no association between macroenvironmental or intrinsic biotic ecological change and pollinator shifts may wish to parallel their macroevolutionary study with population‐level assessments to explore the potential importance of the harder to test microenvironmental and extrinsic biotic ecological change in explaining differences in pollination strategies. Over time, the explicit macroevolutionary testing for macroenvironmental or intrinsic biotic ecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts will allow for synthesizing across multiple independent plant clades to determine whether distinct plant clades follow distinct ecological rules, or whether pollinator shifts are generally driven through the same mechanisms across the globe. Such cross‐clade comparisons may also allow for investigating whether periods or areas of pronounced climatic perturbations (e.g. Miocene cooling, mountain uplift, Pleistocene climatic fluctuations) were particularly likely drivers of pollinator shifts, either directly or by initiating extrinsic biotic ecological change, particularly relevant in light of ongoing climate change (Condamine et al., 2020; Faust & Iler, 2022).

A macroevolutionary approach to testing ecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts

With their power in capturing evolutionary replication, I propose macroevolutionary comparative assessments as a fruitful avenue for testing the importance of ecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts (Fig. 3). Such phylogenetic comparative approaches require well‐resolved and well‐sampled molecular phylogenies, ideally representing taxa across a clade's distribution range, covering all relevant lineages within the study clade, and covering the known diversity of flower morphologies or pollination systems. Besides robust molecular phylogenies, a solid understanding of plant–pollinator interactions in the respective study group is essential. Especially for large clades, it is commonly intangible to obtain empirical pollinator observations for all study species. If empirical pollinator observations are available for a representative subset of species, covering both the phylogenetic, biogeographic, and flower morphological breadth of the study group, tools such as pollination syndromes may be employed to predict pollinators for species lacking empirical observations (Dellinger, 2020). Despite the criticism pollination syndromes have received (and rightfully so, Ollerton et al., 2009), they may be highly useful in identifying the long‐term agents of selection and hence long‐term most efficient pollinators. Care should be taken that pollination system inference through syndromes is based on floral functional traits relevant for capturing plant–pollinator interactions in the specific study system. I recommend employing statistical classification techniques, such as Random Forest analyses, which allow for training and validating prediction models on species with empirical observations first, and only when models yield high prediction accuracy, using them for predicting pollinators for species lacking observations (Dellinger et al., 2019; Dellinger, 2020; Kay & Grossenbacher, 2022; Kopper et al., 2024). Macroevolutionary studies may most commonly work with pollinators grouped as (system specific) functional pollinator groups (Dellinger, 2020). However, when empirical observations are available for a large number of species, and especially when multiple pollinator groups visit plant species, relative pollination performance or visitation rates may also be used as response variables (Smith et al., 2008).

By choosing a macroevolutionary approach to testing ecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts, abiotic macroenvironmental as well as intrinsic biotic factors become most tangible for testing as explanatory variables (Box 1). The simplest macroenvironmental variables are mere geographic locations obtained from herbarium specimens or databases, such as the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, (Flemons et al., 2007). These occurrences should be screened carefully to remove erroneous occurrences or misidentified taxa, and may then be used to determine species' biogeographic areas or extract macroenvironmental niche attributes, such as climatic, soil, and habitat types from global raster stacks (e.g. using CaNDI, available at https://github.com/abbyj‐g/candi). Intrinsic biotic factors may be easy to record from species descriptions or herbarium specimens if they relate to major categorical differences, such as differences in growth form, floral reward type or color categories. If the role of floral traits in explaining pollinator shifts is tested, however, care has to be taken that those traits had not previously been used in assigning pollinator groups through pollination syndromes (to avoid circularity). Microenvironmental ecological change may sometimes be amenable to testing in a macroevolutionary setting if its variation is contained at the interspecific (rather than intraspecific) level and when it can be quantified for a large number of species. Such scenarios may apply if microhabitat differentiation is consistent and well documented at the species level. More commonly, however, microenvironmental variation is not well known and below the resolution of publicly available environmental raster layers, such as Worldclim climate data (finest resolution of c. 1 km, Fick & Hijmans, 2017). In such cases, laborious manual documentation through fieldwork may be required, often limiting the number of species for which such information can be obtained (Li et al., 2018). Extrinsic biotic ecological change is likely the most challenging to test in a macroevolutionary framework because of its variability across a species' distribution range, and more suitable for testing at a population level (Johnson, 2025, to be described later).

Reconstructing the evolutionary history both of pollination systems and of ecological change using approaches such as stochastic character mapping or ‐SSE models allows for comparatively evaluating the distribution and relative timing of evolutionary dynamics of both variables (see Smith, 2010 and Holland et al., 2020 for overview on methods). Both methods are flexible in that they allow for binary as well as multi‐state characters (e.g. pollinator data: bee, fly, moth, and hummingbird; environment data: lowland forest, montane forest, lowland savannah, and montane savannah), and certain ‐SSE models (e.g. QUASSE, Fitzjohn, 2010) allow for the analysis of quantitative traits. With either approach, care should be taken that response and predictor variable are reconstructed the same way to assure comparability. Specifically, state changes should be either mapped along branches (i.e. in stochastic character mapping) or reconstructed at nodes. Given that both pollinator shifts and ecological change may have major impacts on diversification dynamics, ‐SSE models, which can model the potential influence of a trait on diversification dynamics, may be particularly appropriate. In addition, the newly developed HISSE models allow for the inclusion of additional, unobserved traits which may also impact speciation and extinction rates (Beaulieu & O'Meara, 2016). Furthermore, new models have recently been published that allow for the joint assessment of discrete and continuous traits at the same time (Boyko et al., 2023; Tribble et al., 2023), hence making it possible to directly link pollinator data (usually discrete) and environmental data (often continuous). Finally, using tests of correlated trait evolution, researchers may explicitly test whether pollinator shifts and ecological change occurred in a correlated manner, or whether data are better explained by assuming no correlation in pollinator shifts and ecological change (e.g. corrHMM, Beaulieu et al., 2013). Especially when researchers are interested in understanding the role of intrinsic biotic changes such as flower trait changes in relation to speciation events and pollinator shifts, models such as ClaSSE, which allow for testing for cladogenetic and anagenetic evolution of both characters, offer a power tool (Goldberg & Igić, 2012).

Results of ancestral character estimations may be analyzed in multiple ways. Visual inspection of plots showing the reconstructed evolutionary histories allows for assessing the relative timing of ecological change and pollinator shifts (Fig. 3). One may also formulate specific questions around the relative timing of shift probabilities and trace back shift probabilities for pollinator and environment shifts across nodes. Thus, while it is impossible to study the actual pollinator shifts as they happened, deducing from ancestral state reconstructions may give us insights into, for instance, how likely it was for nodes reconstructed with a high probability as having an ancestral pollination system to occur in a novel environment. Clearly, reconstructions will only be as good as the input data (i.e. well‐resolved, well‐sampled molecular phylogeny, sound trait data), and they always have to be regarded as model results rather than the true evolutionary history. Evolutionary replication across the phylogeny, that is a large number of independent pollinator shifts and ecological changes. is critical since it may help buffer erroneous reconstructions stemming from more sparsely sampled lineages in the tree. Evolutionary replication further allows for evaluating the robustness of results, such as whether the same ecological change–pollinator shift associations are detected repeatedly (Vasconcelos, 2023). Overall, reconstructions of pollinator shifts and ecological change may be most trustworthy at relatively shallow nodes toward the tips of the phylogeny, while the possibility of erroneous reconstructions increases with node depth (Gascuel & Steel, 2014; Holland et al., 2020). Similarly, high transition rates toward derived states, high extinction of species with the ancestral state, as well as heterogeneity in transition rates may increase error rates in ancestral character estimation (King & Lee, 2015; Holland et al., 2020). It is also important to note that well‐resolved phylogenies may not be available for young clades still undergoing rapid radiation. Such clades still provide invaluable examples of early stages of pollinator shifts and divergence (Kay et al., 2019), and may call for in‐depth population‐level comparisons of pollinator–environment associations (Musker et al., 2024). If signatures of reticulate evolution and hybridization are discovered, such young radiations may offer additional insights into the role of different reproductive barriers in providing reproductive isolation (Uckele et al., 2024).

Substantial evidence of ecological change‐pollinator–shift relationships may also be deduced from comparative analyses of extant taxa. For example, comparing environmental variables among the most closely related taxa which shifted to new and which retained ancestral pollinators (i.e. through phylogenetic ANOVAs), and contrasting these most closely related taxa with more distantly related relatives which retained ancestral pollinators will inform on the likelihood of ecological change preceding and hence triggering pollinator shifts (e.g. see Hamilton & Wessinger, 2022; Kopper et al., 2025). Specifically, if shifts into novel environments are a common initiator of pollinator shifts, we would expect to find closely related shifted‐ and nonshifted taxa to share more similar environments that are substantially different from those inhabited by more distantly related taxa with the ancestral pollination system. Additional comparative analyses across all nonshifted taxa may further inform on the role of, for instance, adaptive evolution of floral traits as strategies allowing for retaining the ancestral pollination system even in novel environments where the fitness contribution of ancestral pollinators is lower (Kopper et al., 2025).

A potential caveat to the proposed macroevolutionary approach is a scenario where all sister taxa of shifted species either went extinct after colonizing the new environment, or also adopted the pollinator shift. Such a scenario likely precludes differentiating the true sequence of pollinator and environment shifts (Fig. 3). However, comparative analyses would still provide valuable insights into whether shifted pollination systems generally dominate in different environments than ancestral pollination systems. As outlined above, macroevolutionary approaches are also likely inappropriate for detecting extrinsic biotic ecological change as a trigger of pollinator shifts. Indirect tests of the impact of extrinsic biotic interactions exist through diversity‐dependent diversification modelling (Drury et al., 2016) and may inform on aspects such as clade competition as a driver of pollinator shifts (also see Skeels et al., 2021). Ultimately, however, establishing whether abiotic macroecological change is a common driver of evolutionary pollinator shifts, and determining when it is not, forms the basis for then pairing these macroevolutionary assessments with experimental approaches that further explore how biotic ecological change modulates species interactions. Linking these macro‐ and microecological and evolutionary scales will provide a fruitful way forward for establishing ecological generalities underpinning evolutionary pollinator shifts (Harmon et al., 2019).

Pollination represents only one of many interactions with animals that plants engage in, but whether shifts in other mutualistic (e.g. ant–plant, seed–disperser) and antagonistic (e.g. plant–herbivore) plant–animal interactions follow general ecological rules remains untested. Like plant–pollinator interactions, all of these interactions affect plant (and animal) fitness, diversification dynamics and biodiversity more broadly (Bronstein, 2021), and show nonrandom ecogeographic patterns. Seed disperser diversity, for example, shows marked differences between tropical and temperate habitats, with the first featuring primates, frugivorous bats and reptiles as dispersers besides birds and nonflying mammals (Sinnott‐Armstrong et al., 2018). Similarly, ant–plant mutualisms seem to be particularly prevalent in tropical ecosystems, while mite–plant mutualisms are more common in temperate environments (Myers et al., 2024). Among insect herbivores, increased host specificity has been reported from the tropics (Forister et al., 2015). These ecogeographic patterns point toward an overwhelming role of the abiotic environment in modulating plant–animal interactions, and explicitly testing whether and which type of ecological change drives shifts in plant–animal interactions beyond pollination has the potential of revealing broad ecological generalities on the evolution and assembly of global biodiversity.

Conclusions

Despite the important role pollinator shifts play in the evolutionary history of angiosperms, it remains unclear whether they are triggered and driven by consistent ecological factors. Exploring Stebbins' (1970) hypothesis that ecological change may be a critical driver of evolutionary pollinator shifts is particularly interesting because it has the capacity of revealing major ecological generalities underlying pollinator shifts, and has important implications in light of ongoing climate change. The relative importance of abiotic and biotic factors in driving the evolution of organismal diversity has long been debated (Voje et al., 2015), and demonstrating that ecological change through abiotic environment shifts is a major driver of pollinator shifts would confirm the classic idea that changes in the physical environment are necessary to trigger shifts in biotic interactions (‘Court Jester’ scenario, Barnosky, 2001). Although the macroevolutionary approach I outlined here is particularly apt for testing exactly this scenario, finding that macroenvironmental change is an unlikely driver of pollinator shifts would be equally interesting, since it would strengthen the view that biotic interactions themselves are more important drivers of plant macroevolution and pollinator shifts (‘Red Queen’ scenario, Van Valen, 1973). Thus, systematically applying macroevolutionary tests of the role of abiotic macroecological change as a driver of pollinator shifts has the capacity to change our perspective on the mechanisms underpinning pollinator shifts.
