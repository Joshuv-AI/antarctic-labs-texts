Comparative analysis of the transcriptome and methylome in distinct trophoblast populations at the time of embryo implantation in mares
Authors: Joanna Jaworska, Dawid Tobolski, Shebl E Salem, Katarzyna Ropka-Molik, Tomasz Ząbek, Tomasz Szmatoła, Anne Kahler, Izabela Wocławek-Potocka, Katarzyna Piórkowska, Amanda M de Mestre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring equine implantation, two distinct trophoblast subpopulations are identified: the invasive chorionic girdle (CG) and the non-invasive allantochorion. The invasive CG trophoblast is believed to modulate the maternal immune response and facilitates embryo attachment to the endometrium, while the allantochorion contributes to placental development without breaching the endometrium. However, the mechanisms underlying differential adhesion and immune modulation by these two lineages remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the regulation of biological processes underlying trophoblast attachment, development and maintenance of immune tolerance by comparing the transcriptomes and methylomes of CG and allantochorion trophoblasts before and after implantation, that is, between gestational days 33 and 42.ResultsIrrespective of gestational day, allantochorion (n = 4) was enriched in TGFB and its receptors TGFBR1 and TGFBR2. Across the implantation window, expression of regulatory macrophage markers such as IL33, PLG-RKT, and CD163 increased in the allantochorion, along with upregulation of TFEB and SERPINB9. The invasive CG (CG33, n = 4) showed higher expression of NLRC5, a transcriptional regulator of MHC I, compared to the non-invasive allantochorion (ALC33, n = 4). Notably, NLRC5 expression declined in the allantochorion from day 33 to 42. Furthermore, Beta-2 microglobulin (B2M), which encodes the MHC I light chain, was hypermethylated in the allantochorion. Several genes, including CAVIN1, LHB, and INSR, were both differentially expressed and methylated between the two trophoblast types, while others, such as IFNGR1, CD177, showed differential regulation across the implantation window. Gene Ontology and pathway analyses revealed that differentially expressed and methylated genes were enriched in biological processes and pathways critical for implantation, including cell migration and adhesion, TGFB signaling, fibrosis, wound healing, and leukocyte extravasation.ConclusionsThe increased expression of IL33, and CD163 suggests that the allantochorion may promote the recruitment or polarization of macrophages toward a regulatory phenotype, thereby modulating the local immune environment. TFEB may contribute to the steroidogenic activity and functional maturation of the allantochorion. Spatial and temporal differences in gene methylation between CG and allantochorion suggest involvement of epigenetic regulation in trophoblast invasion and attachment to the endometrium. Finally, combined B2M hypermethylation and differential expression of NLRC5 point to epigenetic and transcriptional control of MHC I expression in the equine trophoblasts during implantation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12822-8.Keywords: Horse, Pregnancy, Implantation, Allantochorion, Chorionic girdle, RNA-seq, Methylation, Placenta

Background

Trophoblast differentiation and expansion are essential for establishing and maintaining pregnancy. During implantation, trophoblast cells migrate toward the endometrium and form tightly regulated interactions that support placental development [1]. The extent of trophoblast invasion varies widely across mammals [2]. In species with epitheliochorial placentation, such as horses, most trophoblast cells establish only a superficial connection with the endometrial epithelium and do not penetrate deeper maternal tissue layers. In contrast, species with hemochorial placentation, such as humans and rodents, exhibit deep trophoblast invasion through multiple maternal tissue layers [2]. These differences arise from precise molecular control of pathways that regulate trophoblast identity, invasiveness, immune interactions, and overall placental function [3].

Equine trophoblast differentiation begins on day 6 of development, and by day 14 the trophectoderm fuses with the avascular mesoderm to form the chorion, which later gives rise to the invasive chorionic girdle (CG) and the non-invasive allantoamnion [4]. Around day 27, CG trophoblast cells proliferate and differentiate from small mononuclear precursors into large binucleate endometrial cup cells that secrete equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) for approximately three months [4]. Key regulators of this process include bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), which promotes differentiation of eCG-secreting CG cells [5], the transcription factor ELF5, which controls eCG subunit expression, and FGF-ERK1/2 signaling, which influences both invasion and differentiation [6]. In contrast to CG, the allantochorion retains a non-invasive epithelial phenotype throughout gestation, replacing the yolk sac to form the primary placental membrane [4]. The two trophoblast populations are immunologically distinct: CG cells express fetal MHC class I during differentiation and functional capacity to modulate maternal immune responses. The allantochorion lacks MHC I expression and more broadly has been considered immunologically inert [7, 8]. These contrasting phenotypic profiles suggest distinct trophoblast strategies for maintaining maternal–fetal tolerance, though the underlying mechanisms remain largely uncharacterized.

In many species, trophoblast differentiation and attachment depend on hormonal signaling, growth factor–mediated transcriptional control, environmental factors such as oxygen tension, and epigenetic mechanisms including non-coding RNAs and DNA methylation [9]. DNA methylation, carried out by DNA methyltransferases, DNMT1, DNMT2, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B, modulates gene expression typically silencing affected loci [10]. While DNA methylation has been characterized in the early equine conceptus (days 14 and 19) and in mid- to late-gestation placental tissues [11, 12], the methylation landscape of the CG and allantochorion during the implantation window remains unknown. This gap limits understanding of how spatially and temporally regulated methylation directs invasive versus non-invasive trophoblast fates.

We hypothesized that invasive and non-invasive equine trophoblasts have distinct transcriptional and epigenetic profiles that shape their divergent phenotypes and contribute to trophoblast differentiation, invasion, and maternal immune tolerance. To test this hypothesis, we compared: (i) the allantochorion transcriptome between days 33 and 42 of gestation; (ii) the transcriptome profiles of allantochorion and CG trophoblast populations; and (iii) the methylome of each of these trophoblast populations during the same gestational period.

Materials and methods

Animal procedures and sample collection

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 (as revised) and the EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. The study was approved by the Royal Veterinary College Ethics and Welfare Committee, specifically its Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body (AWERB) under UK Home Office project license PL70/8577. Horses used in this study were owned by the Royal Veterinary College for the duration of the study.

Equine pregnancies were established following previously published procedures [13]. Conceptuses were retrieved non-surgically via uterine lavage using sterile saline solution on days 33 and 42 post-ovulation. The mares were restrained in a stock and sedated with detomidine hydrochloride (Dormosedan, 10 mg/mL; Pfizer, UK) at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, administered intravenously. Immediately following collection, conceptus materials were dissected under a stereomicroscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany), allowing for precise separation of anatomical components: chorion, allantochorion, CG (collected exclusively from day 33 specimens), yolk sac, and embryonic tissue. All samples were immediately cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Bulk RNA next generation sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from allantochorion samples collected at gestational day 33 (n = 4), adjacent CG (n = 4), and allantochorion at gestational day 42 (n = 4) using the PureLink RNAmini kit (Invitrogen, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequent sample preparation followed previously described protocols [14]. Briefly, 300 ng of total RNA was used to construct cDNA libraries using the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Library quality and concentration were assessed using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Qubit™ dsDNA BR Assay Kit, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and the TapeStation 2200 system (D100 ScreenTape, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were normalized to 10nM and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end 150 bp reads.

Raw sequencing data quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.7. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with Flexbar software v3.5.0 [15]. Filtered reads were aligned to the EquCab3.0 reference genome (GTF version 110) using STAR software v2.7.5c [16], with coordinate-sorted BAM output specified. Gene-level read counts were generated using HTSeq-count software v1.99.2 [17].

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package [18]. Genes were considered differentially expressed (DEGs) if they met the criteria of an absolute log2 fold change > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Identified DEGs (Supplementary Table 1) were submitted to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen Digital Insights, Redwood City, CA, USA) to identify canonical pathways, upstream regulators, and major biological functions. Pathways were considered statistically significant if they had an absolute z-score > 2 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Raw p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) [19]. A total of 12 RNA-seq libraries were sequenced, yielding an average of 28.9 million high-quality filtered reads per sample (Supplementary Table 2). Alignment to the reference genome resulted in a mean unique mapping rate of 80.3% across all samples (range 72%– 89.8%). A smaller fraction of reads mapped to multiple loci (range 1.65–6.38 million reads). Unaligned reads were categorized as either too short (range 0.50–4.6 million reads) or unmapped for other reasons (range 5,000–77,000 reads). Across all samples, 14.8–21.4 million reads per sample were assigned to genes, corresponding to 54.0%–70.6% of the total filtered reads. The overall mean proportion of reads assigned to genes was 61.8%. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that samples clustered according to tissue type and gestational day (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Reduced Representation Bisulfate Sequencing (RRBS)

Genomic DNA was isolated from the same samples used for total RNA extraction using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RRBS libraries were prepared using the Ovation® RRBS Methyl-Seq System 1e16 (NuGEN Technologies, San Carlos, CA, United States), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, 100 ng of genomic DNA was digested with the MspI restriction enzyme (recognizing site: CCGG). The digested DNA fragments were ligated to adapters, followed by bisulfite conversion using the EpiTect Bisulfite kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR, 13 cycles), and the resulting libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, United States). Library quality and concentration were assessed using the TapeStation 2200 System (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) and the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, UK). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq500 system using the High Output kit v2.5 and 75 sequencing cycles.

Computational analysis of DNA methylation

Raw sequencing data quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.7 (Babraham Bioinformatics, Cambridge, UK), evaluating sequence quality metrics, base composition, and potential technical artifacts. Preprocessing, including adapter trimming and quality filtering optimized for RRBS data, was performed using Trim Galore (Babraham Bioinformatics, Cambridge, UK). Quality-filtered reads were aligned to the EquCab3.0 reference genome (GTF version 110) using Bismark v0.24.2 (Babraham Bioinformatics, Cambridge, UK), with parameters optimized for paired-end RRBS data. Stringent quality control was applied by retaining only CpG sites covered by at least five sequencing reads and showed statistically significant methylation levels, determined using a one-tailed binomial test with a FDR-adjusted p-value ≤ 0.01.

CpG methylation sites were annotated relative to the EquCab3.0 reference transcriptome (GTF version 110). Regulatory regions were defined as 2 kb upstream of transcriptional start sites (TSS) and 2 kb downstream of transcriptional termination sites (TTS) [20–22]. Methylation density distributions were analyzed across functional genomic elements (CpG islands, shores, and shelves) as well as gene structural features including 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTR), promoters, exons, introns, and 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTR).

Fetal sex

Fetal sex was determined for six of the eight samples (day 33: 2 colts, 2 fillies; day 42: 2 colts, 2 unknown) using DNA isolated from placental tissue biopsies. PCR was performed using primers for Sex determining region of Y (SRY) and X linked androgen receptor following published methods [23]. Although day 33 samples clustered together despite a balanced sex ratio, the possibility that placental/fetal sex contributed to differences between day 33 and day 42 cannot be completely excluded.

Statistical analysis of methylation patterns

The normality of methylation data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data were not normally distributed differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between conditions were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test and identified using thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Linear regression analysis was performed to examine the relationship between methylation density and chromosomal structure. To compare methylation levels across different genomic features and conditions, one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc tests was applied.

To investigate potential regulatory relationships between DNA methylation and gene expression, correlation analyses were performed between methylation levels at specific gene features (5′ UTR, promoters, exons, introns, 3′ UTR) and corresponding gene expression values. Genes exhibiting inverse correlations were identified as candidates for methylation-mediated transcriptional repression. Gene enrichment analysis of these methylation regulated candidate genes was then performed using DAVID v2024q4 [24]. RRBS yielded an average of 12.4 million reads per sample, with a high bisulfite conversion efficiency of 99.6% across all samples (Supplementary Table 3). Sequence alignment to the EquCab3.0 reference genome achieved a mapping rate of 84%, enabling reliable analysis of genome-wide methylation patterns. PCA of methylation profiles demonstrated clear segregation of samples by both tissue type and gestational age, indicating distinct methylation signatures across experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, 301 pathways were activated in ALC33 samples. Changes across implantation, comparison of ALC42 to ALC33

DEGs in the allantochorion pre (D33) and post (D42) implantation were found to be associated with 69 canonical pathways (IPA). Of these, 30/69 pathways were activated at day 33, many of which were related to immune signaling. Innate immune pathways included leukocyte extravasation, fMLP signaling in neutrophils, natural killer cell signaling, and eicosanoid signaling, while adaptive responses were represented by T cell receptor signaling (z-score > 2; Fig. 2C). Additional activated pathways reflected hormonal and vascular activity, such as estrogen receptor signaling, corticotropin-releasing hormone signaling, and GP6 signaling. Upstream regulators of pathways active at day 33 included IL12 family cytokines, BMP2, and Zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1).

A total of 39 pathways were activated in ALC42. Most were involved in lipid, cholesterol, and steroid metabolism, including multiple cholesterol biosynthesis routes, glycerophospholipid biosynthesis, sphingolipid metabolism, linolenate and stearate biosynthesis, as well as steroid hormone metabolism. Several pathways related to protein processing and targeting, such as N-linked glycosylation, post-translational phosphorylation, and SRP-dependent protein targeting were also activated, indicating a shift toward metabolic and protein-processing functions (Fig. 2D). In addition, genes involved in focal adhesion, integrins alpha such as ITGA3, ITGA6, ITGA7, and beta: ITGB4, ITGB5, tetraspanins CD9, CD82, CD151, as well as plasminogen receptor with a C-terminal lysine (PLG-RKT), SERPINE1, and clade B serpins (SERPINB6, SERPINB9) had greater expression in ALC42 than ALC33. Additionally, ALC42 displayed enhanced expression of epithelial-associated genes, including claudins CLDN4, CLDN7, CLDN8, cadherin 1 (CDH1), and keratin 8 (KRT8). Activated upstream regulators at day 42 included transcription factor EB (TFEB), INSR, progesterone receptor (PGR), and the LH complex.

The impact of methylation on genes’ expression

To directly link DNA methylation to alterations in gene expression, transcriptomic and methylome data were directly compared. It was assumed that genes exhibiting inverse relationships, i.e., higher methylation accompanied by lower expression, or vice versa, were likely regulated by the alterations in methylation observed.

In the comparison of CG33 to ALC33, 70 genes had correlated higher methylation and lower expression in the CG. Among them were key developmental regulators such as HOXA9, HOXA10, and HOXA13; transcription factors TBX2 and TBX4; and the tumor suppressor CAVIN1. In the same comparison 95 genes had lower methylation and higher expression in the CG, including Luteinizing Hormone Subunit Beta (LHB, ENSECAG00000007548) and INSR.

In the comparison of ALC42 to ALC33, 64 genes showed higher methylation and lower expression, while 28 genes exhibited lower methylation and higher expression at day 42. Notably, ENSECAG00000016325 (CD177) and interferon gamma receptor 1 (IFNGR1), were among the genes whose expression was higher while methylation lower from gestational day 33 to day 42, suggesting potential epigenetic activation.

Of particular interest, the gene B2M, which encodes the MHC class I light chain, showed significant methylation in both ALC33 and ALC42 relative to CG33, indicating a potential epigenetic mechanism for spatially regulated MHC Class I expression in subpopulations of trophoblasts. Discussion

Implantation is a complex and tightly regulated process that requires coordinated interactions between fetal and maternal tissues. Historically, the CG has been considered the principal driver of maternal immune modulation during early equine pregnancy, owing to its invasive nature and expression of antigenic molecules such as MHC class I [8, 25, 26]. In contrast, the non-invasive allantochorion has been viewed primarily as a structural and metabolic tissue, responsible for establishing the nutrient exchange, and by day 45, initiating steroidogenesis [4, 27]. However, the present study challenges this dichotomy by providing transcriptomic and epigenomic evidence that the allantochorion may actively contribute to the immunological and structural events of implantation. Before firm endometrial attachment, the allantochorion already exhibits transcriptional signatures indicative of immune signaling, epithelial remodeling, and steroidogenic activity. Notably, genes involved in NK and T cells signaling, cytokine response, and focal adhesion were differentially expressed between gestational days 33 and 42, suggesting that the allantochorion undergoes dynamic functional changes during implantation. These findings may imply that the allantochorion is not merely a passive scaffold but participates in multiple processes shaping the maternal-fetal interface.

Allantochorion may be actively involved in implantation

On day 33 of gestation, the allantochorion showed activation of inflammatory and immune pathways. It uniquely expressed IL33, a cytokine that promote type 2 immune response and recruits NK and CD8⁺ T cells [28]. IL33 is also associated with anti-tumor immunity and increased infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), consistent with the higher expression of CD163 (one of the TAMs’ marker) in ALC33 and with the NK- and T-cell–related pathways identified by IPA [29]. Although IL-33 is typically described as a paracrine regulator of placental growth, its expression within the allantochorion at the onset of rapid expansion suggests that autocrine IL-33 signaling may also contribute to growth at this critical stage [30].

Across both gestational days, the non-invasive allantochorion showed consistent enrichment of TGFB1 and its receptors TGFBR1 and TGFBR2. TGFB1 is highly expressed in the endometrium during implantation and plays central roles in immune modulation, tissue remodeling, and control of trophoblast invasion [31, 32]. In this study, TGFB1 emerged as a key upstream regulator in the allantochorion and was also previously identified in endometrial non-immune cells at day 33, aligning with the onset of implantation in the horse [13]. Further supporting this, increased expression of TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 has been observed at this gestational age in equine endometrial macrophages and endothelial cells, respectively [13]. In humans, TGFB signaling promotes maternal immune tolerance by driving Tregs, decidual NK cells, and macrophage differentiation and modulating cytokine secretion and extravillous trophoblast (EVT) invasion [31, 32]. Similar functions are reported in pigs, where TGF-β family members are upregulated during implantation [33, 34]. The profibrotic actions of TGFB1 are consistent with the enrichment of ECM components, multiple collagens, fibronectin 1 (FN1), and actin alpha 2 (ACTA2) in the day-33 allantochorion [35]. By day 42, after completion of chorionic girdle invasion, the allantochorion showed increased expression of PLG-RKT, a plasminogen receptor that promotes fibrinolysis, mononuclear cell recruitment, and clearance of apoptotic cells [36]. PLG-RKT also supports macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype, and its expression in the day-42 allantochorion may facilitate both immune tolerance and resolution of inflammation at the maternal–fetal interface [36].

Interestingly, both invasive and non-invasive trophoblast express SERPINB9 which is a potent intracellular inhibitor of granzyme B (GZMB) [37]. GZMB is strongly expressed by eNK2/3/4 cells at both days 33 and 42 [13]. SERPINB9 may be expressed in GZMB-secreting lymphocytes to prevent self-apoptosis [37], but its elevated expression was also detected in immune privileged sites as tumors where it protects the tumor cells against GZMB + NK or GZMB + T cells mediated killing [38]. Thus, its elevated expression in the trophoblast may similarly protect invasive and non-invasive trophoblast cells during implantation by limiting granzyme-dependent cytotoxicity.

Regulation of fetal MHC I expression

Equine trophoblast subpopulations appear to interact differently with the maternal immune system, in part through dynamic regulation of fetal MHC I expression during early pregnancy [8, 39, 40]. The CG, but not the allantochorion, expresses MHC I during its differentiation and invasion into the endometrium [7]. However, this expression diminishes following the transformation of the CG into endometrial cups around gestational days 42–45 [7]. The mechanisms governing MHC I expression and its functional significance during equine implantation remain poorly understood [4].

As expected, both MHC I (ENSECAG00000035532) and B2M (encoding the MHC I light chain) were upregulated in CG33 compared to ALC33. Additionally, CG33 samples were enriched in NLRC5 (log2FC = 6), a transcriptional activator of MHC I genes [41]. In contrast, NLRC5 expression decreased significantly in the allantochorion across implantation (log2FC = − 3.3). In human EVTs and the choriocarcinoma cell line JEG3, both of which exhibit restricted Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) expression and NLRC5 is not expressed [42]. These cells also lack classical HLA-A and HLA-B expression, instead expressing NLRP2, another NOD family member that promotes expression of non-classical HLA-C, -G, and -E [42]. In tumors, loss of NLRC5 is associated with reduced MHC I expression [43]. Similarly, the equine allantochorion, which does not express MHC I, showed reduced NLRC5 expression compared to CG33 and across implantation. Furthermore, B2M, encoding the light chain of MHC I [44], was hypermethylated in ALC33 (log2FC = 1.5, p-value = 0.07) and ALC42 (log2FC = 0.95, p-value = 0.07) relative to CG33. These findings suggest that both tissue-specific methylation of B2M and differential expression of NLRC5 may contribute to the regulation of fetal MHC I expression in distinct trophoblast subpopulations during implantation.

Impact of gene methylation on trophoblast function during implantation

The number of differentially methylated regions (DMRs) was greater between invasive (CG33) and non-invasive (ALC33) trophoblasts (2,126 DMRs) than between allantochorion samples across gestational days 33 and 42 (856 DMRs), highlighting the distinct regulatory mechanisms in these two trophoblast populations [45]. A comparable number of DMRs has been reported in the allantochorion at later gestational stages (months 4, 6, and 10) [12]. Most DMRs were located in exons and introns. Approximately 24% of DMRs were located in gene promoters, substantially higher than the ~ 10% observed in mature allantochorion from the fourth gestational month onward [12] suggesting methylation may play a more prominent regulatory role in early placental development compared with mid to late gestation [45].

Trophoblast cells, including non-invasive types, undergo dynamic epigenetic remodeling during implantation. In both human and murine models, methylation of tumor suppressor genes has been implicated in regulating trophoblast invasion [46]. In this study, the tumor suppressor gene, CAVIN1, was hypermethylated in CG33. Genes associated with DMRs between CG33 and ALC33 were enriched in TGF-β and BMP signaling pathways, as well as pathways related to cell movement and adhesion. Similar methylation patterns in TGF-β-related genes have been observed in cancers [46], where TGF-β signaling can induce methylation changes that affect cell migration, morphology, and survival [47]. Furthermore, differential methylation of adhesion-related genes has been reported in preeclamptic versus normal human placentas [48], supporting a role for DNA methylation in modulating trophoblast invasiveness and ultimately placental growth and function.

Among the genes that exhibited lower methylation and thus higher expression in the ALC at day 42 in comparison to day 33 were IFNGR1 and CD177. Less IFNGR1 expression at day 33 may reflect lower sensitivity to IFN-γ, which is produced by innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) during early implantation [13]. Interestingly, expression of IFNG in ILCs decreased from log2FC = 2.47 at day 33 to log2FC = 1.59 at day 42. This suggests that transient epigenetic downregulation of IFNGR1 may serve to limit.

IFN-γ signaling to specific cell types at very specific developmental window or protect the trophoblast during periods of heightened IFN-γ expression. In pigs, IFN-γ is produced by the trophoblast and acts on the endometrium to support tight junction formation during blastocyst attachment, with receptor expression largely restricted to the endometrium [49]. The CD177, whose methylation also changed across implantation, encodes a GPI-anchored surface glycoprotein involved in the proliferation and differentiation of myeloid cells and in neutrophil activation [50]. It also functions as an IgG Fc receptor supporting antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) [51]. Temporally regulated expression of CD177 in the allantochorion may protect tissues during CG invasion, when MHC I expression is high. Additionally,CD177 interacts with β2 integrins to immobilize neutrophils, potentially modulating their interactions with trophoblasts or other immune cells during implantation [50]. Supporting this, the neutrophil degranulation pathway exhibited the highest z-score in ALC42 compared to ALC33. In addition, two of the hypermethylated genes, PEG3 in CG33 and INSR in ALC33, were previously characterized as imprinted genes in the equine trophoblast [51, 52].

Development/maturation of allantochorion

By gestational day 45, the allantoic placenta replaces the yolk sac and acquires the capacity to synthesize and metabolize steroids. Just a few days later, it begins forming villi [4]. This developmental transition was accompanied by transcriptomic changes that reflect the emerging functions of the allantochorion. By day 42, enriched pathways included cholesterol biosynthesis, transport of vitamins and nucleosides, post-translational protein phosphorylation, and steroid metabolism. However, the specific genes driving these changes in the equine trophoblast remain poorly characterized [4, 27]. Compared to the CG, and across the implantation days, the allantochorion showed increased expression of TFEB, a transcription factor that binds the E-box sequence within the Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation (CLEAR) motif located in the promoter region of lysosomal genes. In the human placenta, TFEB is essential for syncytiotrophoblast development, vasculogenesis and steroidogenesis, in part by regulating the expression of CYP19A1 [53]. However, functional experiments are required and additional TFEB targets in the equine trophoblast remain to be identified.

The formation of microvilli in the allantochorion supports nutrient and oxygen exchange. During implantation, the allantochorion upregulated expression of tetraspanins CD9, CD81, CD82, CD151, and laminin binding integrins (ITGA3, ITGA6, ITGA7, ITGB4, ITGB5) [54]. These molecules may assemble into tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs) with partners such as CLDN1, HBEGF and EGFR, all of which are expressed in the allantochorion. TEMs are localized to microvilli, where they organize membrane proteins, facilitate signaling and adhesion, and support the release of cytokines and exosomes [55]. Additionally, TEMs and CDH1 are thought to stabilize cell-cell junctions by reducing RhoA small GTPase activity [56].

This study has several limitations. First, the RNA-seq data were generated from bulk tissue and therefore lack single-cell resolution, preventing confident attribution of gene expression to specific cell types. For example, TGFB1 may be expressed by macrophages, other leukocytes, or trophoblast cells, but this cannot be distinguished in the current dataset. Second, the principal component analysis (PCA) showed one ALC42 sample positioned apart from the others. Since sequencing quality metrics showed no technical issues, this sample was retained in the analysis as the variation may represent natural biological differences. The potential influence of placental fetal sex on this variation remains uncertain, as fetal sex was not determined for two day 42 samples, including the outlier (Supplementary Fig. 1B). It should be noted that the day 33 samples did not show an influence of sex at the PCA level. Third, the sample size was limited (n = 4 per gestational stage), which reduces the ability to detect more subtle transcriptional differences and alterations in expression of genes that show significant underlying biological variation. Finally, this study is descriptive in nature. Potential functions of the identified genes and pathways are discussed; however, functional studies are required to confirm the roles of these candidates in implantation and early placental development.

Conclusions

These results challenge the traditional view that the chorionic girdle is the sole immunologically active trophoblast subpopulation during early equine pregnancy. The allantochorion at day 33 shows expression of specific cytokines such as TGFB1, CCL28, and IL33, and activation of leukocyte extravasation, as well as NK and T cell signaling and cytokine signaling (IL-4, IL-8, IL-33, IL-2) pathways. This important membrane therefore plays a key role in promoting alterations in maternal immunity as well as attachment and autocrine regulation of growth. The expression of CD163 and PLG-RKT by the allantochorion, combined with previous characterization of macrophages at the maternal-fetal interface [13] suggests the maternal macrophages are important regulatory cells during equine implantation. DNA methylation of pro-migratory genes expressed in the CG and allantochorion indicates tight regulation of trophoblast invasion. We propose TFEB as a one of the transcriptional coordinators of allantochorion maturation, given its known roles in lysosomal function, steroidogenesis, and placental development. Finally, we suggest that methylation of B2M, along with differential expression of NLRC5, a key transcription factor that regulates classical MHC I genes expression, may be involved in modulating MHC I expression spatially on equine trophoblast across the implantation period.
