Silk fibroin from production wastes, a new raw material for biomaterial applications: physicochemical properties and biological performance
Authors: Ana M. Gaviria Castrillón, Natalia Jaramillo-Quiceno, Maritza Londoño-Berrio, Juliana Builes, Luis Javier Cruz, Antonella Motta, David L. Kaplan, Valeria E. Bosio, Adriana Restrepo-Osorio, Marlon Osorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silk textile production generates considerable amounts of waste, increasing environmental impact. However, this waste holds significant potential as a sustainable source of regenerated silk fibroin (SF) for biomedical applications. This study investigated the physicochemical properties and cellular compatibility of SF extracted from silk cocoons (SFC) and silk waste (SFW). Both materials demonstrated comparable morphology, thermal stability, and wettability. Structural analysis and amino acid composition revealed that while SFC presented a higher molecular weight, lower polydispersity, and a greater presence of charged amino acid residues (Asp, Arg, Lys, and His), the regeneration process effectively compensates for these variations. Specifically, the transition from random coil to β-sheet during film casting homogenizes the matrix, ensuring that the final biological properties remain comparable. Quantitative metabolic assays confirmed that both SFC and SFW maintain cell viability above 70%, meeting ISO 10993-5 standards for non-cytotoxicity. Furthermore, qualitative cell adhesion studies demonstrated effective fibroblast spreading and attachment to both film surfaces, indicating that the initial molecular variations in the waste source do not compromise the resulting matrix’s biological performance. These findings highlight that SFW is a functionally equivalent and sustainable alternative to SFC, suitable for the development of advanced biomaterials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50517-y.

Introduction

Silk is a natural protein composed of fibroin fibers (SF) coated with silk sericin (SS). The sericin is removed through a degumming process to isolate the fibroin and optimize the textile spinning process. The process to produce silk textiles generates significant amounts of waste, with up to 50% of raw material loss during production1. Globally, this represents c.a 11 million tons per year2. Fibrous waste includes second-grade, double, or non-reelable cocoons with stains or holes, as well as short threads known as borra. Due to their shorter fiber length, these fibrous wastes (SFW) are unsuitable for conventional silk textile production. Up to 15% of the SFW partially reused is used for low-cost handmade textiles production1. The remaining waste (45%) generates water, soil, air, vegetation, and crop contamination, primarily due to residual chemicals and inorganic salts introduced during degumming2.

Despite its environmental challenges, SFW offers significant opportunities in terms of sustainability, since it contains a high silk fibroin content comparable to silk cocoons (SFC). SFW can be easily processed into high-performance silk biomaterials with promising potential for biomedical applications3,4. Numerous studies have demonstrated the versatility of SF derived from SFC for biomedical applications, including scaffolds such as sponges, films, electrospun fibers, nanoparticles, and hydrogels5. These SF materials exhibit remarkable mechanical properties, biocompatibility, controlled degradation rates, and the ability to form crystalline structures such as β-turns (silk I) and β-sheets (silk II)6. These structures promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and viability while diminishing cytotoxic effects7–9. Moreover, SF concentration tailor directly the elasticity and biodegradability. Higher SF concentrations enhance the formation of β-sheet structures10,11, resulting in shorter gelation times, smaller pore sizes, and reduced water content. These structural features correlate with the biodegradability and enzymatic degradation behavior of SF materials, influencing its biological properties12.

Previous studies, such as those conducted by Yang et al. (2007), Tang et al. (2009), and Wray et al. (2009), have evaluated the biocompatibility of SF materials derived from Bombyx mori silk cocoons13–15. Similarly, researchers like H. Mollahosseini (2019) and C. Yang et al. (2021) have explored the biocompatibility of silk waste materials16,17. However, comparative analyses of fibroin obtained from both SFC and SFW have not been reported in the literature yet.

Accordingly, this study aims to fill this gap by comparing the structural properties of fibroin derived from both silk cocoons and silk waste. Special emphasis is placed on evaluating the potential of silk waste as a raw material for biomedical applications. Molecular features of SFC and SFW were analyzed and correlated with the physicochemical properties of SF films. Furthermore, cell viability using 3T3 fibroblast adhesion was assessed in order to explore their potential as suitable biomaterials for biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

For degumming and SF extraction, SFC and SFW were kindly donated by the Corporation for the Development of Sericulture in Cauca-CORSEDA (Popayán, Colombia). Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) from Merck, and lithium bromide (LiBr) were sourced by Sigma-Aldrich.

Preparation of silk fibroin samples

Regenerated silk fibroin (SF) was obtained from two distinct sources: silk cocoons (SFC) and production wastes (SFW) from Bombyx mori silkworms. The degumming process was performed using a 0.5% w/v sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution at boiling temperature for 1 h to ensure the total removal of sericin. The resulting fibroin fibers were dissolved in a 9.3 M LiBr solution and subsequently dialyzed against distilled water for 72 h at room temperature to eliminate salts18. From the resulting 5% w/v aqueous SF solution, the material was processed into different physical states according to specific analytical requirements. SF solution was cast into plastic molds and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h to obtain solid-state films with uniform thickness and structural integrity. These films served as the primary material for amino acid composition, molecular weight determination (GPC), microscopic (SEM), thermal (TGA), infrared (FTIR), contact angle, and cell adhesion characterizations. For cytotoxicity evaluation (MTT) the SF solution was lyophilized to ensured rapid and complete re-dissolution in the culture medium at the required concentrations. Finally, the films dedicated to cell adhesion were treated with methanol to induce β-sheet formation, thereby enhancing their water stability and structural resistance during incubation.

Amino acid content.

Structural characterization

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR) was performed to analyze secondary structure of SF films. Spectra were collected in the range of 4000–400 cm− 1, with 64 scans and a resolution of 1 cm− 1. Data were processed using OMNIC® software and peaks within the amide I region were fitted to twelve components. Percentage of crystalline structures was determined using the methodology described by Qiang Lu et al.21. Briefly, Fourier self-deconvolution was applied to the protein amide I band in FTIR spectra, followed by curve fitting with twelve Gaussian components to determine the percentage of crystalline structures. Three replicates were analyzed for each sample, and results are reported as means ± standard deviations.

X-ray diffraction (XRD).

X-ray diffraction (XRD).

X-ray diffraction (XRD).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of SF films were obtained using an XPert PANalytical diffractometer operating at 30 kV and 20 mA for SF films. The primary focus was to identify the characteristic diffraction angles associated with the crystalline forms of Silk I and Silk II, enabling differentiation between the less-ordered Silk I and the highly stable, crystalline Silk II22. Data acquisition was performed at a scan rate of 4°/min over a 2θ range of 5–60°. Collected data was analyzed using Origin software, employing Savitzky-Golay smoothing with a five-point window and baseline subtraction. This enabled the detection of peaks corresponding to Silk I and Silk II structures, providing insights into the molecular organization of the material. Crystallinity indices were calculated by totalizing the contributions of Silk I peaks and dividing by the total spectral area, using Fourier self-deconvolution in Origin software. Peak positions determined via second derivative analysis, were fixed during deconvolution. The software automatically adjusted the band width23.

Scanning Electron Microscopy.

Scanning Electron Microscopy.

Scanning Electron Microscopy.

Surface morphology of the SF films was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JEOL JSM-6490 LV SEM equipped with an E&T detector, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 15 kV. Prior to imaging, samples were coated with a thin gold layer to ensure conductivity.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of SF films was carried out using a Mettler-Toledo TGA/SDTA85 instrument. C.a. 10 mg of SF film was placed in a ceramic crucible, and the temperature was ramped from 30 to 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation. TGA was used to measure the sample’s weight loss as a function of temperature, providing insights into its thermal stability, degradation profile, and composition.

Contact angle measurement

Wettability of SF films was evaluated using sessile drop contact angle measurements. A 10 µL droplet of distilled water was carefully dispensed onto the surface of each film, and contact angle was measured using a Dataphysics OCA 15 instrument. This method provides insights into either the hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of the material, which is crucial for determining its potential interactions with biological fluids. The contact angle measurements between the distilled water droplet and the SF films were recorded at intervals from 0 to 60 s after the droplet was dispensed. Each sample was analyzed by triplicate to ensure reliability, and results are presented as means ± standard deviations.

Cell adhesion.

Cell adhesion.

Cell adhesion.

Silk fibroin films were immersed in methanol for one hour, followed by a thirty-minute rinse (twice) with agitation to ensure structural stability. The films were sterilized by exposing each side to sterilizing conditions for 30 min. Subsequently, 3T3 fibroblasts were seeded onto the films for two and seven days to assess cell adhesion.

After culturing period, the films were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 3.7% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. The films were then washed with PBS at 4 °C and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 3 min. Subsequent washing steps were performed using the same PBS solution. To block non-specific background staining, the films were incubated with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in PBS (PBSA) for 3 min.

Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (diamidino-2-phenylindole) at a concentration of 4 µg/mL in PBS containing 1% BSA. The films were incubated with the staining solution for 15 min at room temperature, followed by two washes with PBSA (5 min each) and a final wash with PBS for 5 min. Samples were stored in PBS at 4 °C until imaging.

Data acquisition was performed in the dark using inverted fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Axiovert®) at 10X and 40X magnifications. Cell morphology was visualized by phase-contrast mode, while nuclei were imaged using a DAPI filter cube (excitation: 358 nm; emission: 463 nm).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For FTIR, XRD, TGA, and contact angle measurements, analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3) to determine means, standard deviations, and statistical differences. Cytotoxicity assays were conducted in quintuplicate (n = 5) across three independent experimental runs to ensure reproducibility. Data normality was first assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while homogeneity of variance was evaluated through Levene’s test. For normally distributed data, an independent samples Student’s t-test was applied when equal variances were assumed. In cases of normal distribution with unequal variances, Welch’s t-test was employed. For data that did not meet the normality assumption, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was utilized. Statistical significance was defined at (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Chemical characterization

Amino acids content.

Amino acids content.

Amino acids content.

Table 1 summarizes the amino acid composition of SF derived from SFC and SFW. Both samples exhibit —primarily glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), and tyrosine (Tyr)—that form repeating glycine-alanine-glycine-alanine-glycine-serine (GAGAGAS) sequences, known for their self-assembling into antiparallel β-sheet structures, which give the mechanical and structural properties of silk fibroin24. Results indicate similar proportions of Glycine (Gly), Alanine (Ala), and Tyrosine (Tyr) for both SFC and SFW. Glycine content was found to be 49.61% in SFC and 53.48% in SFW. Alanine content was 31.2% in SFC and 32.79% in SFW. Tyrosine content was 4.74% in SFC and 4.63% in SFW.

However, SFC showed higher contents of serine (Ser, 4.91%) and aspartic acid (Asp, 0.91%) compared to SFW. These higher levels of Ser and Asp in SFC are due to the presence of SS, in native cocoons19,25.

The higher presence of charged amino acids (Asp, Arg, Lys, and His) in the SFC sample promotes greater molecular organization due to the electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding26. In contrast, the SFW sample contains a higher proportion of non-bulky amino acids, which may contribute to its comparative lower molecular weight (See Table 1)27. This structural profile is primarily attributed to the material history of the silk production wastes; specifically, they exposure to temperatures (60–100 °C) during initial processing phases. This thermal exposure, along with alkaline degumming steps and mechanical strength applied during reeling, leads to the fragmentation of the silk filaments. Such degradation mechanisms facilitate the cleavage of peptide bonds, particularly in less sterically hindered regions, effectively revealing the underlying reasons for the observed molecular weight distribution in waste-derived fibroin28.

Apparent molecular weight distribution of SF solutions derived from SFC and SFW is shown in Fig. 1. The SFC sample exhibited higher average molecular weight (Mn) and weight-average molecular weight (Mw) compared to the SFW sample, with Mw and Mn values of 1950 kDa and 618.7 kDa for SFC, respectively, and 1444.4 kDa and 270.5 kDa for SFW. Native silk fibroin comprises three protein components with significant molecular weights: the heavy chain (approximately 350 kDa), the light chain (approximately 26 kDa), and the glycoprotein P-25 (approximately 30 kDa)29. The significant reduction in MW observed in SFW compared to SFC is primarily due to the inherent nature of the source material. SFW consists of fibers that may have undergone partial degradation or mechanical stress during industrial handling, leading to a more fragmented protein structure. This is further supported by the higher polydispersity index (PDI) of SFW (5.3) versus SFC (3.3), indicating a more heterogeneous mix of protein fragments in the waste source.

Furthermore, it has been reported that a higher molecular weight is essential for preserving the structural integrity of fibroin chains, which directly enhances cell-material interactions. This effect promotes superior cell adhesion and spreading by providing a more stable substrate with a higher density of intact cell-binding motifs. As demonstrated by Kim et al. (2016), a reduction Mn leads to a significantly diminishes metabolic activity and prevents proper cytoskeletal organization, as evidenced by the transition from extended to rounded cell morphologies30.

Structural characterization

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR-ATR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR-ATR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR-ATR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD).

Two primary crystalline forms exist for Bombyx mori SF: silk I and silk II. Silk II exhibits a stable antiparallel β-sheet structure. Silk I, another major crystalline form, is generally considered metastable, originally observed within the silk gland. During silk extrusion, this metastable silk I undergoes a transition to the more stable silk II structure31.

FTIR spectra of both SFC and SFW samples of the amide I and II regions (Fig. 2A), show the presence of silk I (1652 cm− 1 and 1538 cm− 1) and silk II (1616 cm− 1 and 1515 cm− 1) structures in both samples. Quantitative analysis of the IR spectra (Fig. 2B) revealed significant differences in β-sheet content (p = 0.01) and amorphous side-chain structures (p = 0.039). Despite these differences, no statistically significant variation was observed in the total amorphous and crystalline secondary structures content between the samples. Specifically, crystalline structure content, quantified as the sum of β-sheet and β-turn percentages, was determined to be 41.6 ± 2.17% for SFC and 44.55 ± 7.73% for SFW. This results are consistent with literature reports, which indicate a crystalline structure content ranging from 40 to 50% for reference silk32,33. These findings suggest that structural differences lie in the reorganization of crystalline (β-sheets and turns) and amorphous (α-helix and random coil) regions rather than overall crystalline content.

XRD deconvolution for SFC (Fig. 2C) and SFW (Fig. 2D) corroborated the FTIR hypothesis of secondary structure reorganization. Silk I primary structural components are ordered α-helices and coils, characterized by diffraction angles at approximately 12.0°, 19.7°, 22.1°, 23.6°, 24.7°, and 28.2°. In contrast, Silk II dominant structure is β-sheet, exhibiting diffraction peaks around 18.9, 20.7°, 24.7°, 29.5°, and 30.931,34–38. In SFC compared to SFW, a decrease in the peak at 12.2° (Silk I)38 was observed. Moreover, in SFC sample, peaks associated with Silk II appeared at 18.9° and 29.5°, and the intensity of the peak at 30.9° (Silk II)39 was higher than that observed for SFW films. This observation is further supported by crystallinity index, which showed statistically significant differences (p = 0.002) in Silk II (β-sheet) content between SFW (48.3 ± 2.79) and SDC (62.3 ± 1.4). The higher prevalence of Silk II in SFC is directly linked to its greater concentration of charged amino acids (aspartic acid, arginine, lysine, and histidine). These residues promote molecular organization through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, generating a more compact and stable structure. This enhanced molecular packing is consistent with the higher molecular weight observed in SFC, whereas the structural fragmentation and reduced charge in SFW likely hinder the protein chains ability to transition into stable β-sheet configurations26.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of SFC and SFW samples are shown in Fig. 4. An initial thermal event around 100 °C was observed in both samples, corresponding to moisture loss. This phenomenon was more pronounced in SFW sample, where the weight loss at 250 °C was 7.94% ± 0.02% compared 5.10% ± 0.05% in SFC films. The onset degradation temperatures (Ti) were similar for both samples, at approximately 263.1 ± 1.11 °C for SFC and 263.2 ± 2.56 °C for SFW. Although the maximum degradation temperature (Tmax) was slightly higher for SFC (306.2 ± 0.74 °C) compared to SFW (295.6 ± 7.65 °C), these differences were not statistically significant. This could be attributed to the observed deviation in the SFW sample, which is directly related to the intrinsic heterogeneity of this raw material.

Contact angle measurement

Contact angle measurements, presented in Fig. 5, releved significant differences between SFC and SFW films at the initial time point (t = 0 s). The contact angle of SFC was significantly higher that of SFW (p < 0.05), suggesting a more hydrophobic character compared to the waste-derived silk. However, no significant statistical differences were observed at the 10 s and 60 s time points, as the contact angle of SFC decreased over time42. This behavior indicates that the SFC film exhibits a pronounced initial hydrophobic-like effect, which decreases as the surface hydrates. On the other hand, SFW possesses a higher proportion of amorphous regions, resulting in greater availability of lateral hydrophilic functional groups on its surface; this characteristic increases the interaction with water molecules and, therefore, its wettability43. Despite these initial differences, no statistically significant differences were observed at the 10 s and 60 s time points, as the surfaces of both films demonstrated comparable wettability after hydration, a favorable feature for their use in biomedical applications44.

In vitro performance of the biomaterial

Results of the MTT metabolic activity assay exposed to varying concentrations (1% to 25%) of SF solutions derived from SFW and SFC are shown in Fig. 6A. At concentrations below 5% w/v, none SFW nor SFC presented cytotoxic effect. However, concentrations above 9% w/v induced cytotoxic effects (p < 0.05), particularly in SFW. This finding is likely attributed to the lower molecular weight and higher PDI of SFW compared to SFC. As established by Kim et al. (2016), a reduction in molecular weight is directly associated with diminished biological performance and a significant decline in cell metabolic activity30.

Despite the observed effects, both SFC and SFW consistently maintained cell viability above 70% across all tested concentrations. This finding, in accordance with the international standard ISO 10993-5, considers both biomaterials as non-cytotoxic since cell viability remains above this threshold. This confirms that, in terms of cytotoxicity, both materials are safe for biomedical applications. Although this study evaluated SF at its maximum dissolution concentrations, both SFC and SFW showed 100% cell viability up to 5% (w/w) (Fig. 6B,C). This concentration, consistent with standard extraction procedures, suggests that both materials are viable candidates for biomedical applications at this base concentration. Consequently, subsequent cell adhesion studies were performed at 5% (w/v).

The safety profile observed in our SF solutions is consistent with recent advancements in the repurposing of silk nonwovens for internal tissue repair. As demonstrated in the study of robust bioadhesives derived from natural silk waste16, the transformation of these materials does not compromise their biocompatibility, exhibiting minimal inflammatory response and no toxicity during in vivo tissue amendment. Our results at the standard concentration for regenerated SF (approximately 5% w/v) align with this evidence, as both SFC and SFW maintained 100% cell viability. This reinforces the finding that silk-derived residues, despite possessing distinct characteristics due to their origin from shorter fibers and exposure to prior thermal processing, exhibit a performance comparable to standard silk sources in medical application scenarios.

Cell adhesion.

Once the safety of the SF was verified, the evaluation of the films for cell adhesion followed. 3T3 fibroblasts were seeded onto SFC and SFW films after 7 days of incubation, and the presence of cells adhered to the surface was evaluated (Fig. 1S) (Fig. 7). Initial attachment was observed through DAPI staining after two days of incubation (Fig. 1S-A, B), confirming that both surfaces support early cell-material interaction. Phase-contrast images (Fig. 7A, B) demonstrate effective cellular adhesion to the material, suggesting favorable surface properties that support cell attachment of fibroblasts on both films. Additionally, DAPI-stained images (Fig. 7C, D) provide a detailed view of nuclear organization within the cells, revealing clearly defined and uniformly distributed nuclei, further confirming cell viability. Merged images (Fig. 7E, F), combining phase contrast and DAPI staining, offer a comprehensive perspective by simultaneously highlighting the film’s surface topography, cell morphology, and nuclear localization. In these composite images, the surface structure is visible alongside the spatial arrangement of adhered cells, illustrating how they populate and integrate on the material. The distinct visualization of DAPI-stained nuclei within the cellular framework confirms the consistent presence of healthy and viable cells on both SFC and SFW films.

The cell adhesion mechanism in this context is based on the interaction between proteins (in this case, silk fibroin) and cell surface receptors, primarily integrins. Integrins are transmembrane glycoproteins that possess positive charges in their extracellular domain, allowing them to interact with proteins that possess negative charges, such as aspartic acid and glutamic acid. Thus, the material acts as an anchor for the integrins, which connect the cellular cytoskeleton to the surface of the biomaterial45.

Despite these differences in solution, the functional equivalence in cell adhesion on the resulting films suggests a structural compensation during the regeneration process. As demonstrated in studies on the recycling of silk waste fibers, the dissolution and subsequent film casting allow for the precise modulation of the fibroin’s secondary structure, specifically the transition from random coil to β-sheet17. In our study, the processing of SFW residues likely homogenized these structural characteristics, resulting in a stable matrix with surface properties comparable to those of SFC. This indicates that initial variations in fiber length or molecular weight within the waste material do not compromise the formation of a stable surface, providing similar microenvironments for 3T3 fibroblast attachment and growth regardless of the silk’s origin.

The findings of this study demonstrate that silk fibroin derived from production waste (SFW) serves as a technically viable and sustainable biomass for biomedical applications. Although SFC exhibited a higher molecular weight and lower polydispersity related to a greater presence of charged amino acid residues (Asp, Arg, Lys, and His) that promote molecular organization, the SFW showed comparable thermal stability (TGA) and wettability whit SFC. Furthermore, XRD and FTIR analyses confirmed that both materials successfully reorganize into β-sheet conformations, which are essential for structural integrity. Regarding biological performance, both SFC and SFW maintained cell viability above the 70% threshold and demonstrated excellent biocompatibility. The effective fibroblast spreading, observed on SFW films confirm that the material supports cell attachment comparable to SFC.

These biological results are further supported by the mechanical and stability profiles established in previous reports by the authors. Gaviria et al. (2023) conducted comparative analyses between SFW and SFC fibers, confirming that mechanical properties such as the modulus of elasticity and elongation at break, showed no statistically significant differences, this performance related to removal of sericin during industrial pre-treatments46. Additionally, Gaviria et al. (2025) demonstrated that scaffolds developed from this SFW exhibit structural stability, retaining 92% of their mass in hydrolytic studies and 55% under accelerated enzymatic degradation with Protease XIV47.Furthermore, following similar in vitro degradation protocols, Zhou et al. (2010) established a correlation with in vivo performance, confirming full degradation and vascularization within eight weeks of implantation in a rat mode48. Collectively, these findings validate the use of waste-derived fibroin as a sustainable alternative that meets the mechanical and biological requirements for advanced biomedical applications.

Conclusion

This study characterized silk fibroin (SF) extracted from silk cocoons (SFC) and silk waste (SFW) for biomedical applications, emphasizing sustainable sourcing. While SFC exhibited a higher molecular weight, lower polydispersity and higher presence of charged amino acid residues, the regeneration process effectively homogenized the structural properties of both materials. Structural analyses through SEM, XRD, and FTIR confirmed that despite these initial molecular variations, both SFC and SFW successfully transitioned to stable β-sheet conformations. Furthermore, TGA and contact angle measurements revealed comparable thermal stability and wettability, which are essential for surface-cell interactions. Biologically, both materials met the ISO 10993-5 standards for non-cytotoxicity, maintaining cell viability above 70% and supporting robust fibroblast adhesion with normal morphology. These findings demonstrate that the structural reorganization occurring during processing compensates for the inherent differences in waste-derived fibers. Consequently, SFW is established as a functionally equivalent, sustainable, and effective resource for the development of advanced biomaterials, offering a viable alternative to traditional silk source.
