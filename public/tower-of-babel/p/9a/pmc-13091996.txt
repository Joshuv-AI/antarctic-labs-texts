The combined effect of task-oriented treadmill and high-intensity interval training on balance, gait parameters, and ADL in patients with chronic stroke: A randomized controlled trial with short-term follow-up
Authors: KyuRyeong Kim, Nam-Jin Jung, Min-Bong Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to determine the effect of task-oriented treadmill training combined with high-intensity interval training (TO-HIIT) on the balance, gait parameters, and ADL in patients with chronic stroke compared to high-intensity interval treadmill training (HIIT).MethodsThis parallel-group randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio enrolled 30 chronic stroke patients who were randomly assigned using computer-generated randomization to either an TO- HIIT group (n = 15) or HIIT group (n = 15). Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Both groups trained 30 min per session, three times a week for four weeks. Balance ability was evaluated using the Zebris FDM-T, Gait parameters were measured by the GAITRite, and the activities of daily living (ADL) was assessed using modified barthel index (MBI). Data were analyzed via two-factor repeated measures ANOVA.FindingsSignificant interaction effects between time and group were observed for the balance (COP 95% area, COP path length, COP average velocity), gait parameters (step length, stride length, cadence, double limb support, and single limb support), and ADL (p < .05). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the TO-HIIT group showed greater improvements compared to the HIIT group (p < .025); however, no significant between-group differences were observed in cadence and MBI (p > .025). Both groups demonstrated significant improvements over time (p < .017).InterpretationThis study suggests that TO-HIIT is more effective than HIIT alone for chronic stroke patients. High-intensity, repetitive, meaningful task-oriented exercises positively impact balance, gait, and ADL demonstrating notable transfer effects. Therefore, TO-HIIT should be considered in rehabilitation programs.Keywords: Stroke, High-intensity interval training, Task-oriented training, Walking rehabilitation

Introduction

The capacity for ambulation serves as a barometer for an individual's present level of functionality and their general quality of life, encompassing their involvement within their community.1 However, survivors of stroke frequently encounter difficulties in ambulation due to persistent unilateral motor deficits, which limits their capacity to engage in daily activities.2 The correlation between walking ability and quality of life is well-documented, and the identification of effective interventions to improve walking ability is of paramount importance.3

The most prevalent disability resulting from stroke is motor impairment, which predominantly affects the motor control of the face, arms, and legs on one side of the body.4 The abnormalities in motor control exhibited by patients vary depending on the affected cerebral hemisphere, the cause of the lesion, and the time since onset. However, they generally demonstrate a predominance of a flexor synergy pattern in the upper limb and an extensor synergy pattern in the lower limb, resulting in a hemiplegic gait pattern characterised by asymmetry.5,6 Sheffler and Chase7 reported that the effects of hemiplegia on spatiotemporal gait parameters include a decrease in walking speed, stride length, step length, cadence, and single-limb support, and an increase in double-limb support. These changes are explained as an effort to compensate for the increased energy expenditure and instability during gait caused by hemiplegia.

In recent years, a range of treatment approaches have been proposed with the aim of improving gait function after stroke 8, 9, 10. In particular, high-intensity interval treadmill training (HIIT) has been identified as a promising intervention for enhancing gait function in stroke patients.11,12 As posited by Marzolini et al.,13 high-intensity interval training has been demonstrated to be more efficacious in enhancing not only gait ability but also cardiorespiratory fitness when compared with moderate-intensity continuous training. Furthermore, Mah et al.14 reported that a systematic literature review had shown that high-intensity treadmill training had positive effects on functional outcomes such as gait speed and balance without adverse effects when compared to low-intensity exercise.

However, high-intensity interval treadmill training primarily focuses on improving physiological strength and cardiopulmonary function, and thus has limitations in adequately reflecting the complex gait tasks required in daily life.15 Community walking in stroke patients necessitates not only the execution of basic straight-line walking but also the concurrent performance of various cognitive-motor tasks, including obstacle avoidance, turning, and dual-task performance.16 Consequently, interventions that emphasise exercise intensity alone, in conjunction with the performance of various tasks, may prove efficacious in terms of transferring functional walking ability.

The training method known as task-oriented training (TOT) is founded upon the principles of motor learning theory and the concept of neuroplasticity. The programme has been shown to enhance motor function and facilitate its integration into daily living activities through the repeated execution of practical functional tasks.17 This approach has been demonstrated to enhance patients' active participation and problem-solving abilities by providing tasks with specific goals and context,18 and has been reported to be effective in improving functional performance in real-world settings.19 In particular, previous studies applying TOT to gait training have reported significant improvements in walking speed, balance, and the ability to perform activities of daily living 20, 21, 22.

While previous research has established the benefits of cognitive-motor dual-task training in stroke survivors,23,24 studies focusing on motor-motor dual-task interference remain sparse. Consequently, there is a lack of evidence directly comparing high-intensity training protocols with and without task-oriented components that demand complex motor interference. The integration of HIIT with a task-oriented approach is therefore hypothesised to yield superior outcomes by simultaneously addressing physiological and motor demands, ultimately contributing to improved community participation and quality of life. To address this gap, the present study aimed to examine the effects of TO-HIIT — which integrates HIIT with task-oriented obstacle crossing mimicking the complex physical demands of community ambulation — on spatiotemporal gait parameters, static balance, and quality of life compared to HIIT alone. We hypothesised that this integrated motor-motor dual-task approach would yield superior clinical outcomes.

Methods

Trial design

This was a parallel-group, assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The study was conducted at the inpatient rehabilitation ward of K Hospital in Daegu, Korea, from October 14, 2025 to November 25, 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyungwoon University (approval number: KW-2025-B-9) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This clinical trial was prospectively registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), a primary registry in the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (registration number: KCT0011107).

Participants

The sample size was calculated using G∗Power 3.1.9.7 based on the primary outcome variable, gait parameters. A 2 × 3 mixed ANOVA (group × time point) with a medium effect size (f = .25), significance level .05, power .80, repeated measures correlation coefficient .5, and sphericity correction coefficient 1 resulted in a total of 28 subjects being needed. In consideration of the attrition rate, a total of 36 subjects (18 per group) were recruited from the inpatient rehabilitation ward of K Hospital in Korea. Following the subject eligibility assessment, 36 patients were selected for participation, but 6 were excluded due to personal reasons, such as refusal to participate, leaving 30 subjects (15 in the TO-HIIT group and 15 in the HIIT group). The inclusion criteria were as follows: The onset of the stroke must have occurred between 6 and 24 months prior to the assessment, A score of at least 24 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) must have been achieved, The subject must have the ability to walk a distance of at least 10 m, with or without assistance, The subject's gait speed must be less than .8 m per second (m/s). Exclusion criteria encompassed severe visual or cognitive impairment, cardiovascular or circulatory disease, or medical instability affecting lower extremity mobility. All participants provided written consent for their involvement in the study.

Randomization and allocation concealment

A total of 36 stroke patients who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were enrolled and randomly allocated to either the experimental group (n = 18) or the control group (n = 18). Randomization was conducted using Random Allocation Software 2.0 (University of Medical Sciences, Iran, 2008) by a researcher not involved in the intervention or assessment to minimize selection bias. Random allocation was performed by a graduate student with no clinical involvement in the trial, and for allocation concealment, sealed envelopes sequentially numbered and opaque were used. The envelopes were kept in a location distinct from the assessment place and were not available to the assessor or the data analyst. The envelope was signed, dated, and opened by the allocation examiner immediately before the intervention, and only in the absence of the assessor and the data analyst.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention type and process, it was not possible to blind participants and therapists. However, the outcome assessor and data analyst were unaware of the group assignment and were not involved in the delivery of the intervention.

Study protocol

The study protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1. A total of 36 subjects were selected and randomly assigned to either the TO-HIIT or HIIT group; however, 6 subjects (3 from each group) withdrew during the study period, resulting in 30 subjects (15 in each group) completing the study. The reasons for dropout were as follows: discharge due to personal reasons (n = 2) and occurrence of other medical problems (n = 1) in the experimental group; and discharge due to personal reasons (n = 2) and refusal to participate in the study (n = 1) in the control group.

CONSORT flow diagram illustrating the study protocol, participant enrollment, randomization, allocation, follow-up, and analysis for the TO-HIIT and HIIT groups.

Over the course of four weeks, both groups participated in 12 training sessions, each lasting 30 min, conducted three times per week. The experimental group received task-oriented treadmill training combined with high-intensity interval training (TO-HIIT), while the control group received high-intensity interval treadmill training (HIIT) without task-oriented components. The post-test data were collected from 30 participants (15 in each group). A follow-up assessment was conducted two weeks after the post-test to evaluate retention and learning effects.

To ensure participant safety during treadmill training, a body-weight support harness system (Walking Exercise System, NEO-TECH, Korea) was used with a rehabilitation treadmill (Fitex 6080, Fitex, Korea). All training sessions were supervised by a licensed physical therapist trained in emergency response protocols.

Intervention

Task oriented treadmill training combined with high-intensity interval training (TO-HIIT)

The task-oriented treadmill training combined with high-intensity interval training protocol was developed based on the methods described by Amatachaya et al.25 This intervention was designed to simulate real-life walking environments by incorporating obstacle-crossing tasks typically encountered in home or community settings.

Obstacles of two types (Type A: 55 mm × 90 mm; Type B: 55 mm × 90 mm × 20 mm) were manually placed on the treadmill belt by the therapist (Fig. 2). The obstacles were arranged in a predetermined sequence, and participants were instructed to maintain a central walking position while crossing the obstacles (Fig. 2). The obstacle-task program was delivered over 4 weeks, with the stepping-over direction (affected vs. non-affected side) and obstacle size systematically progressed by week. During weeks 1 and 2, participants stepped over the obstacle toward the affected side; obstacle size A (55 mm × 90 mm) was used in week 1, and obstacle size B (55 mm × 90 mm × 20 mm) was used in week 2. In week 3, participants stepped over the obstacle toward the non-affected side, and obstacle size was presented in a random manner. In week 4, both the stepping-over direction and obstacle size were randomized during task performance.

During the 30-min training session, high-intensity interval training principles were applied as described in Section 2.6.2.

High-intensity interval treadmill training (HIIT)

High-intensity interval treadmill training was conducted following a modified version of the protocols used by Askim et al.,26 Cleland and Madhavan,27 and Munari et al.28 (Fig. 3). Exercise intensity was prescribed according to the Karvonen formula by calculating the target heart rate (THR) using heart rate reserve (HRR), and maximal heart rate (HRmax) was estimated using equation (206.9) − (.67 × age):

Representative photograph of the high-intensity interval treadmill training (HIIT) protocol. Participants performed treadmill walking at target heart rate intensity using a body-weight support harness system.

Participants aimed to reach 70–80% of their HRR during high-intensity intervals. Each training session lasted 30 min and included a warm-up, main training, and cool-down. The warm-up consisted of 5 min of low-intensity walking at 30–40% HRR. The main training lasted 20 min and consisted of repeated cycles of 2-min high-intensity walking (70–80% HRR) and 2-min active recovery (30–40% HRR). The cool-down consisted of 5 min of low-intensity walking.

Heart rate was continuously monitored using a Polar H7 heart rate sensor (Polar Electro, USA), which transmitted data via Bluetooth to a mobile device for real-time tracking. Treadmill speed was titrated in real time based on continuously monitored heart rate to achieve and maintain the prescribed HRR zones as promptly and safely as possible (i.e., increasing speed when HR was below target and reducing speed when HR exceeded the target or when symptoms occurred). Given the known delay in heart rate kinetics during rapid transitions in exercise intensity, the target intensity was evaluated across each 2-min high-intensity bout (allowing HR to rise after bout onset) rather than requiring immediate attainment of 70–80% HRR at the first seconds of the interval. The 5-min warm-up (30–40% HRR) was included to elevate baseline HR and facilitate earlier attainment of the target zone within each work bout. Borg rating of perceived exertion (RPE) and symptom reports were used as adjuncts to heart rate to guide workload adjustments, particularly when heart rate responses were delayed or blunted.

If participants experienced any signs of distress (e.g., shortness of breath, chest pain, or muscle discomfort), the session was immediately stopped, and vital signs—including blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation—were monitored. Further training was withheld until medical clearance was obtained from the attending physician.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The GAITRite (GAITRite, CIR system Inc, USA) was used to measure the gait parameters in chronic stroke patients. The GAITRite consists of an electronic walking plate measuring 5 m in length, 61 cm in width, and .6 cm in height. It is equipped with 16,128 sensors, each 1 cm in diameter, vertically arranged along the walking plate at intervals of 1.27 cm to collect temporal and spatial gait variables. The collected data were processed using computerized software. During measurement, participants stood in front of the walking plate and were instructed verbally by the examiner to walk across the plate at their most comfortable walking speed until they stepped off. Its measurable variables include step length, stride length, cadence, double limb support, and single limb support on the affected and nonaffected sides. Measurements were performed three times, and the average values were recorded. The instrument demonstrated a high inter-rater reliability with r = .90 and an intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of .96 or higher.29

Secondary outcomes

The Zebris FDM-T (FDM-T AP1171. Zebris Medical GmbH, Germany) was used to measure the balance ability in chronic stroke patients. The Zebris FDM-T consists of an electronic mat equipped with 3432 pressure sensors arranged in an 88 × 39 matrix, which is placed beneath the treadmill belt. Data extraction occurs by measuring plantar pressure while the participant is walking or standing on the treadmill, with a high sampling frequency of 120 Hz. The collected data are transmitted to a computer via a USB cable and analyzed using Zebris FDM software (version 1.18.44). The input pressure signals are displayed as 2D/3D spatiotemporal graphs, including the center of pressure (COP), during standing or walking. For the measurement, participants were instructed to stand still on the treadmill while facing forward for 30 s. The variables measured to assess static balance ability in this study included the COP confidence ellipse area, COP path length, and COP average velocity. Measurements were performed three times by a single physical therapist, and the average values were reported. The measuring equipment has demonstrated high reliability.30

The modified barthel index (MBI) was used to assess the activities of daily living (ADL) in chronic stroke patients. The MBI evaluates functional disability based on ADL independence and consists of 10 items scored on a 5-point scale, with a total score of 100. Scores ranging from 0 to 24 indicate complete dependence, 25 to 49 severe dependence, 50 to 74 moderate dependence, 75 to 90 mild dependence, and 91 to 99 minimal dependence. The tool shows high reliability, with test-retest and inter-rater reliability of .89 and .95, respectively.31 Assessments were conducted by an experienced physical therapist.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were used to summarize the participants’ general characteristics. Between-group comparisons at baseline were performed using the chi-square (X2) test for categorical variables and the independent t-test for continuous variables to confirm successful randomization.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted to assess normality. To compare groups over time, a 2 × 3 two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed with group (TO-HIIT vs HIIT) as the between-subject factor and time (pre-test, post-test, follow-up) as the within-subject factor. The statistical significance level was set at α = .05.

When significant time × group interactions were observed, post-hoc analyses were conducted as follows:

(1)Within-group comparisons: One-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by pairwise comparisons across three time points (pre-test vs post-test, pre-test vs follow-up, post-test vs follow-up) using Bonferroni correction (3 comparisons per group).

Within-group comparisons: One-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by pairwise comparisons across three time points (pre-test vs post-test, pre-test vs follow-up, post-test vs follow-up) using Bonferroni correction (3 comparisons per group).

(2)Between-group comparisons: Independent t-tests at post-test and follow-up (2 comparisons) using Bonferroni correction.

Between-group comparisons: Independent t-tests at post-test and follow-up (2 comparisons) using Bonferroni correction.

All reported p-values are Bonferroni-corrected, with statistical significance set at p < .05.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the impact of TO-HIIT on the ambulatory capacity of chronic stroke patients and the subsequent temporal transfer effects. The findings of this study demonstrated significant interactions between measurement time points and groups. Consequently, the transfer effects could be confirmed through post-hoc analyses between groups at each time point and within groups over time.

Subsequent post-hoc analysis of spatiotemporal parameters of gait and the ratio of both limbs and single-leg support phases of the gait cycle demonstrated that both groups exhibited favourable changes, as indicated by the effects of hemiplegia on spatiotemporal gait parameters, as proposed by Sheffler & Chae.7 These alterations indicate a potential recovery of ambulatory function. In this study, the gait parameters of the participants were re-measured a fortnight after the intervention, in addition to pre- and post-intervention measurements. The purpose of this was to confirm the transfer effect. Consequently, substantial within-group disparities and transfer effects were substantiated for all spatiotemporal gait parameters in the TO-HIIT group. The enhanced outcomes observed in the TO-HIIT group are hypothesised to be attributable to the central pattern generator (CPG) principle 32, 33, 34, also referred to as the treadmill training effect, and the facilitation of adaptive mechanisms that are augmented by the task.35,36 CPGs in gait are neural groups that activate motor patterns that occur during gait.37 In this study, the treadmill walking task, which was applied, and the additional obstacle crossing task, applied to the TO-HIIT group, are hypothesised to have activated the movement pattern through CPG. The high-intensity repeated intervention is hypothesised to have led to a learning effect through repetition of the task, which could have shown a significant transfer effect.38 Moreover, the substantial enhancement observed in the TO-HIIT group can be interpreted from the perspective of experience-dependent neuroplasticity. High-intensity aerobic exercise, such as the HIIT protocol utilised in this study, has been documented to elicit a substantial increase in biomarkers associated with neuroplasticity in stroke patients, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), lactate, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).39 These physiological responses create a "primed" neural environment that enhances the brain's readiness for recovery. Within this optimal environment, task-oriented elements, in particular obstacle hopping, act as goal-directed motor constraints, thereby placing greater demands on motor planning, execution, and real-time error correction. The synergistic effect of high-intensity physiological stimulation and task-specific neural activation is likely to promote more efficient neural circuit reorganization than simple repetitive training. This phenomenon is further substantiated by the activation of central pattern generator (CPG) principles and the promotion of task-enhanced adaptation mechanisms.

The findings of this study demonstrated that the TO-HIIT group exhibited significant positive pre- and post-intervention differences and transfer effects for both leg support and single-leg support, which represent the ratio of the gait cycle. It was evident that single-leg support exhibited a substantial transfer effect, with a significant discrepancy between the measurements taken prior to and two weeks following the intervention. This finding suggests that the effects of the TO-HIIT intervention were not only sustained but also augmented following the intervention. The results of this study indicate that the increased instability caused by asymmetric gait was successfully mitigated. The obstacle-task, which was added only to the TO-HIIT group is known to require a weigh shifting and dynamic balance control during performance. Previous studies have reported that obstacle clearance may be accompanied by altered stance-phase control of the supporting limb and increased functional demands on the hip and knee extensors.40 In addition, Said et al.41 reported that an increase in the swing phase time and the activation of the hip and ankle flexor muscles are necessary for the successful clearance of the obstacle. In consideration of the findings from preceding research, it is hypothesised that the protracted loss of equilibrium and elevated demands for stepping and swinging during the obstacle-climbing task in the present study engendered favourable outcomes on the bilateral and single-leg support ratios, signifying weight redistribution. The findings of this study indicate that the implementation of repetitive obstacle-climbing interventions for patients suffering from chronic stroke may result in favourable alterations to their gait patterns.

In this study, static balance ability was assessed by measuring the COP 95% area, COP path length, and COP average velocity. The interactions between groups and time were examined. Subsequent post-tests revealed that the TO-HIIT group demonstrated more significant positive improvements in all between-group comparisons, with the exception of the pre-test. Furthermore, within-group comparisons revealed significant pre-test and post-test differences and transfer effects in all static balance variables. Of particular note, the mean velocity of the COP exhibited a more substantial enhancement in the evaluation conducted two weeks following the conclusion of the intervention. The findings indicate that the TO-HIIT group's supplementary repetition of the hurdle jumping task resulted in a learning effect, enhancing their static balance proficiency.

Shumway-Cook & Woollacott42 reported that balance and postural control are influenced by the interaction between three factors: the individual, the environment, and the postural task. As reported in the extant literature, including the study by Choi et al.,43 a significant decrease in postural sway was observed during dual-task training with ring transfers in 19 stroke patients during treadmill training. Similarly, Kim et al.44 reported a decrease in postural sway velocity in 20 stroke patients during treadmill training with virtual reality, which was performed three times a week for four weeks with environmental changes. According to the classification of dual-task protocols, our intervention corresponds to Type 1 (Simultaneous motor-motor execution). Unlike protocols using cognitive interference (Type 2), such as walking while performing calculations, the TO-HIIT protocol emphasizes task-specific motor demands physically coupled with the gait cycle. From a motor control perspective, performing complex walking tasks under challenging conditions has been shown to increase activation in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), supplementary motor area (SMA), and basal ganglia.45 Specifically, Fujimoto et al.46 emphasized that cortical changes, particularly in the SMA, are crucial for recovering balance and responding to external perturbations in hemiplegic stroke patients. This suggests that the simultaneous motor-motor demands in our study fostered greater neural engagement in pathways responsible for anticipatory postural control and environmental navigation, leading to superior functional transfer compared to traditional dual-task training. Anticipatory postural control, in essence, entails the proactive recalibration of sensory and motor systems to postural demands, drawing upon prior experience and learning. Concurrently, reactive postural control facilitates the swift restoration of stability following an unanticipated disturbance, thus instigating an adaptive process that culminates in a reduction of postural sway.35,36 Furthermore, it is hypothesised that the continuous environmental changes that occur during obstacle crossing induce greater loss and recovery of balance by requiring alterations in the base, movement of the centre of mass, and changes in the speed of movement of the centre of mass.47

In the context of motor skill learning, some sports science studies highlight that dual-task interventions must be highly task-specific to enhance performance in real-world scenarios.48 Unlike traditional cognitive dual-task protocols (e.g., walking while performing mental arithmetic) that lack visual attention and movement variability, the TO-HIIT protocol integrates visual-motor coupling through obstacle avoidance. This approach is grounded in the Dynamic Systems Theory, where the obstacles act as environmental constraints that drive the central nervous system (CNS) toward self-organization.49 Instead of relying on a rigid, pre-programmed motor command, the TO-HIIT group was forced to solve real-time motor problems, increasing movement variability. This variability allows patients to explore and stabilize functional coordination patterns that are more resilient to external perturbations.

The enhancements in gait variables and static balance ability in the TO-HIIT group appear to result in variations in the scores on the ADL performance assessment. In this study, an interaction between group and measurement time was also identified in the assessment of ADL performance. In the post-hoc test, significant within-group differences and transfer effects were observed in both groups; however, the between-group comparison demonstrated that the transfer effect was significantly more positive in the TO-HIIT group. The findings of this study indicate that both groups demonstrated an enhancement in their quality of life, attributable to an improvement in ADL performance ability.1 In accordance with the findings of preceding studies that demonstrated a strong correlation between gait ability and quality of life, it is hypothesised that the TO-HIIT group, which exhibited a more pronounced positive effect on gait ability, also demonstrated a significant positive transfer effect on ADL performance ability in the present study.3

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our sample size (n = 30) was determined through a priori power analysis, the assumptions used (e.g., correlation coefficient of .5, medium effect size f = .25) may have been optimistic. However, the observed effect sizes for time × group interactions demonstrate substantial and clinically meaningful intervention effects. As shown in Table 2, partial η2 values for primary outcomes ranged from .510 to .804, all substantially exceeding Cohen's criterion for large effects (η2 ≥ .14). These large effect sizes provide strong evidence that our intervention produced robust and clinically significant improvements. Second, the relatively homogeneous sample (chronic stroke patients with specific ambulatory capabilities: walking speed <.8 m/s, FAC ≥3, MMSE-K ≥ 24) may limit generalizability to stroke populations with different functional or cognitive levels. Third, the relatively short intervention duration of four weeks and follow-up period of two weeks may not fully capture the long-term sustainability and transfer effects of the intervention. Future research should consider extending the intervention period and diversifying measurement time points to examine intermediate and long-term effects more comprehensively.

The findings of this study demonstrated that TO-HIIT exhibited superior efficacy in comparison to HIIT administered in isolation for chronic stroke patients. The findings of the present study demonstrate that the performance of repeated high-intensity exercise in conjunction with meaningful tasks has a positive impact on gait parameters, static balance, and activities of daily living. This outcome is indicative of significant transfer effects. Consequently, TO-HIIT should be considered as a component of stroke rehabilitation programmes. It is recommended that future research explore the specific effects and transfer effects of different tasks through diverse task modifications and measurement time points.
