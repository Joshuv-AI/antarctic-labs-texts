Determinants of hospitalization among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims in Saudi Arabia: a nationwide retrospective cohort study
Authors: Masdalina Pane, Raharni Raharni, Asep Hermawan, Iin Nurlinawati, Made Suratri, Sarah Humaira, Aulia Rahman, Mugi Wahidin, Ekowati Rahajeng, Maria Herawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: The Hajj pilgrimage is a major global gathering that poses health challenges, particularly for those with pre-existing health conditions that require hospitalization.OBJECTIVES: To examine the determinants of hospitalization among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims and quantify their relative contributions to the burden of hospitalization.DESIGNS: Retrospective cohort studySETTING: Indonesian emergency clinics and referral hospitals for Hajj in Saudi Arabia during the 2016–2019 and 2022 Hajj seasons.PATIENTS AND METHODS: A nationwide retrospective cohort study used data from the Integrated Hajj Computerized System for Health (Siskohatkes). Variables included demographic information, clinical profiles, and hospitalization outcomes. Multivariate logistic regression to estimate the adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, and population-attributable fractions quantified significant determinants.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Hospitalization among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims during the Hajj period.SAMPLE SIZE: 876 700 Indonesian Hajj pilgrims were included in this study.RESULTS: Overall, 8978 pilgrims (1.02%) were hospitalized. Age was the strongest predictor of hospitalization, with pilgrims aged ≥65 years having more than eightfold higher odds of hospitalization than those aged <45 years (aOR=8.31; 95% CI: 7.39–9.34; P<.001), accounting for 49.8% of the attributable risk. Male sex was also independently associated with hospitalization (aOR=1.47; 95% CI: 1.39–1.56; P<.001), contributing 11.6% of the attributable risk. Among comorbidities, diabetes (aOR=2.34; 95% CI: 2.19–2.51; P<.001), diabetes with hypertension (aOR=2.23; 95% CI: 2.07–2.39; P<.001), and anemia (aOR=1.56; 95% CI: 1.48–1.63; P<.001) were significant independent predictors of hospitalization. The most common primary diagnoses among hospitalized pilgrims were pneumonia (36.7%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (21.4%), and myocardial infarction/coronary heart disease (12.4%).CONCLUSION: Older age and cardiometabolic comorbidities were the major predictors of hospitalization among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims. Strengthening pre-departure screening, chronic disease management, and risk-based interventions may reduce preventable hospitalizations and improve pilgrim safety.LIMITATIONS: Potential for information bias inherent in routinely collected administrative data and limited availability of clinical and behavioral covariates.

INTRODUCTION

Hajj represents one of the central pillars of Islam and constitutes a major annual mass gathering, drawing approximately 2.5–3 million pilgrims from over 150 countries each year, except during the COVID-19 pandemic. The pilgrimage poses substantial public health challenges due to extreme environmental conditions, high population density, and the convergence of individuals with diverse health profiles. These factors increase susceptibility to a wide spectrum of communicable and non-communicable diseases, elevating the risk of morbidity during the pilgrimage.1 A considerable proportion of Indonesian Hajj Pilgrims require inpatient care, which can interrupt the completion of pilgrimage rituals. The Indonesian government has established a tiered healthcare system. Primary care services are delivered at the flight-group, while secondary referral care is provided through Indonesian Hajj Health Clinics. Cases requiring advanced management are referred to designated hospitals under the Saudi Ministry of Health.2,3 The most common causes of hospitalization among pilgrims in Saudi Arabia include pneumonia (36.7%; 32.8 per 10 000 pilgrims), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (21.4%; 19.2 per 100 000), cardiovascular conditions such as acute myocardial infarction and coronary heart disease (12.4%; 11.1 per 100 000), diabetes (10.2%, 9.1 per 100 000), heart failure (10.1%, 9.1 per 100 000), dyspepsia (4.4%, 3.9 per 100 000), stroke (2.6%, 2.4 per 100 000), and dyspnea (2.2%, 3.9 per 100 000). Several of these conditions identified as the leading causes of mortality among pilgrims.4

Previous studies identified advanced age, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and respiratory infections as key risk factors associated with hospitalization during Hajj.5–7 However, evidence specifically on Indonesian pilgrims remains limited, particularly studies using longitudinal data and retrospective cohort design. Existing research largely emphasized disease prevalence and clinical complications rather than elucidating the determinants of hospitalization.5–7 A more comprehensive of these determinants is essential to inform targeted prevention strategies and optimize healthcare planning during the Hajj pilgrimage.

This study aimed to identify the determinants of hospitalization among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims and quantify their relative contributions to the burden of hospitalization using retrospective cohort data from the 2016-2019 and 2022 Hajj seasons. The findings are expected to inform morbidity prevention programs and guide the strategic planning of logistics, medicine, and human resources within the Hajj health system, ultimately contributing to safer pilgrimage in the future.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study used routinely collected administrative data on Indonesian Hajj pilgrims seasons from 2016-2019 and 2022. The 2020 and 2021 Hajj seasons were excluded due to international travel restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic.8 The study population included all registered Indonesian pilgrims traveling to Saudi Arabia (Mecca, Madinah, Jeddah, and other sites such as Arafah, Muzdalifah, and Mina), totalling 876 700 individuals, of whom 8978 were hospitalized during the Hajj period. We estimated the association between baseline characteristics and hospitalization using multivariable logistic regression, modelling hospitalization as binary outcome (yes/no) during the Hajj period. Each year, pilgrims were organized into approximately 480–500 flight groups, with 355–455 individuals per group, supported by one physician and two nurses.

The Saudi Arabian and Indonesian health systems supported the healthcare services for the pilgrims. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia has established first-aid stations, primary healthcare centers, and hospitals to meet the needs of pilgrims. In Makkah, six hospitals were dedicated specifically to Hajj services, whereas in Madinah, pilgrims received care through existing healthcare facilities. Complementing these services, Indonesian authorities operated Indonesian health clinics in Makkah and Madinah to provide culturally and linguistically appropriate care for Indonesian pilgrims.9,10

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN) (approval number 040/KE.03/SK/02/2024.

Study outcomes and variables

Hospitalization data were obtained from the Integrated Hajj Computerized System for Health (Siskohatkes), maintained by the Center for Hajj Health in Indonesia. This system captures health assessments and interventions related to Hajj, with the primary objective monitoring the health status of Indonesian pilgrims throughout the pre-departure and pilgrimage periods.

Clinical data were obtained through a structured multistage medical screening process. Initial health assessments are conducted at primary healthcare facilities 3–6 months before departure, and secondary evaluations at designated referral hospitals 1–2 months before departure. A final pre-departure screening was performed within 24 h before embarkation at the port health authorities, in coordination with national referral hospitals when necessary. All diagnoses were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD).

Given the heterogeneity of clinical presentations and ICD-coded diagnoses among pilgrims, hospitalization status was coded as a binary outcome (yes/no) in this study. Demographic and clinical variables included age group, sex, educational attainment, occupation, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin level, and smoking status. These variables were extracted from standardized medical records and defined in accordance with established clinical and administrative guidelines within healthcare facilities. All variables were considered potential confounders based on prior epidemiological evidence linking to pre-existing conditions and hospitalization outcomes.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions, were used to characterize the demographic and clinical profiles of the study population. Associations between independent variables and hospitalization were initially assessed using logistic regression. Temporal consistency across the study years was evaluated by comparing the variable distributions and rates. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent determinants of hospitalization, adjusted for relevant covariates. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value of <.05.

Subsequent analysis quantified the effects of several variables on hospitalization after controlling for potential confounders, with results expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aORs). The primary objective analysis was to determine the relative contribution of each factor to the ihospitalization during Hajj pilgrimage, informing the prioritization and scaling of targeted interventions in Hajj health management for the future.

The potential impact was assessed using attributable fraction measures, which describe the proportion of an outcome attributable to specific exposure or risk factor. Two types of attributable fractions were applied: (1) the Attributable Fraction among the exposed (AFE), which estimates the proportion of cases among the exposed group attributable to the exposure, and (2) the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), which estimates the proportion of cases in total population attributable to the exposure. The cohort also exhibited a substantial burden of cardiometabolic risk factors. Diabetes mellitus was the most common pre-existing condition (26.5%), while 7.1% of pilgrims had both diabetes and hypertension, and 5.7% had hypertension alone; 60.7% reported neither condition. Excess body weight was highly prevalent, with 36.6% classified as overweight and 13.1% as obese. Most pilgrims had normal hemoglobin levels (74.8%), whereas anemia and polycythemia were observed in 19.9% and 5.4%, respectively. Smoking was reported by 13.6% of participants. Collectively, these findings indicate an aging pilgrim population with a considerable burden of chronic cardiometabolic conditions that may increase vulnerability to adverse health events during Hajj.

DISCUSSION

Using nationwide data from the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Indonesia covering all provinces during the study period, this study provides a comprehensive representation of the demographic and health profiles of Indonesian Hajj pilgrims. In contrast to reports from countries such as South Africa, where Hajj pilgrims are predominantly male and younger, with poorer health status,11–14 the Indonesian Hajj population is characterized by a higher proportion of female pilgrims and an average age of approximately 50 years old. However, studies from other countries have documented older Hajj cohorts, with mean ages ranging from 57–66 years.15–17

Systematic morbidity surveillance among Indonesian Hajj pilgrims has been implemented since 1998 through the Siskohatkes (Integrated Hajj Health Computerized System), which applies standardized ICD-10 diagnostic codes. Health assessments are conducted during pre-departure medical examinations 3–6 months before the Hajj season and again within 24 hours before departure to comply with the International Air Transport Association (IATA) requirements.18 Over the most recent five Hajj seasons (2016–2022), the most reported conditions among high-risk pilgrims were endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic disorders (E00–E89), followed by diseases of the circulatory system (I00–I99). Type 2 diabetes mellitus accounted for the largest proportion within the former category, whereas hypertension predominated among circulatory diseases. These findings agree with previous studies indicating that diabetes and hypertension are the most prevalent conditions among Hajj pilgrims.19

Globally, between 27% and 58% of Hajj pilgrims have at least one underlying medical condition.11–14 The prevalence of hypertension among pilgrims ranges from 10% to 47%, while diabetes affects approximately 10%–33% of pilgrims.7,16,19–21 In Indonesia, the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus estimated at 34.1% and 10.5%, respectively, both of which shown increasing trends over time.20

In this study, approximately 1% of pilgrims (n=8978) required hospitalization in Indonesian health facilities (0.8%) or Saudi Arabian government hospitals (0.5%). Additionally, 2200 pilgrims (0.3%) were referred from Indonesian clinics to Saudi Arabian hospitals. Retrospective data on pilgrims' health status are critical for informing targeted health promotion strategies to mitigate individual risk factors during Hajj. Evidence suggests that pilgrims who receive tailored health education more likely to maintain better health and reduce their need for medical care.7 This particularly important given that high-risk pilgrims account for substantial proportion of hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and face an elevated risk of mortality during the pilgrimage.15,17,21 Consistent with prior studies, factors associated with an increased hospitalization risk included male sex, age ≥50 years, diabetes mellitus, grade II–III hypertension, overweight, and obesity. Furthermore, male, diabetes, and being overweight have been linked to an increased mortality risk. Cardiovascular diseases the leading cause of death among pilgrims (38.2%), and 13.1% of hospitalized patients were initially diagnosed with cardiovascular conditions.22

The findings indicate that Hajj pilgrims with diabetes mellitus had a 1.77 higher likelihood of receiving treatment than those without diabetes, while the coexistence of diabetes and hypertension further increased the risk (OR=1.99). Additional risk factors among Indonesian pilgrims included smoking (11.2%), overweight status (BMI 23–24.99; 46.41%), and grade II obesity (BMI ≥30; 16.57%). Notably, underweight individuals (BMI <18.5) exhibited a 1.68-fold increased risk of treatment failure. These results consistent with previous studies demonstrating, in addition to hypertension and diabetes, obesity (BMI ≥30) and underweight status (BMI <18.5) associated with higher hospitalization rates compared with normal BMI.23 Other determinants of morbidity included age, sex, education, and occupation. Male pilgrims had higher risk of hospitalization than female pilgrims (OR=1.47). Lower educational attainment (illiteracy) associated with modestly increased risk (OR=1.15) compared to higher education levels. Occupational groups such as housewives, farmers, and retirees also demonstrated elevated risk (OR range: 1.13–1.17). Consistent with evidence from other countries, hospitalization risk increased progressively with advancing age.15,23

Pre-travel medical consultation is strongly recommended for individuals with DM. Pilgrims with poor health status should seek professional medical advice to assess in the Hajj poses excessive risk and consider potential religious exemptions, where appropriate.24 In this study, approximately 1% of pilgrims required hospitalization, either in Indonesian clinics (0.8%) or Saudi Arabian government hospitals (0.5%), with an additional 0.3% referred from Indonesian facilities to Saudi Arabian hospitals. The most common diagnoses among hospitalized patients were stroke and dyspnea.

The study reports a hospitalization rate of 1%, comprising 0.8% at Indonesian clinics and 0.5% at Saudi Arabian government hospitals. Stroke and dyspnea were the leading causes of hospitalization, highlighting that noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) become the primary health concern for Hajj pilgrims, overtaking infectious diseases.26,27

For pilgrims with diabetes mellitus, pre-travel medical consultations are essential since intense physical activities like Tawaf and Sa'i can cause significant blood glucose variations.24 The survey found that 0.3% of pilgrims were referred from Indonesian healthcare facilities to specialized hospitals in Saudi Arabia due to incapacity to treat their chronic diseases. The cooperation between Indonesian clinics and the Saudi Arabian government's healthcare system highlights how crucial global health resilience is to handling of large-scale events. To avoid deadly outcomes, medical decisions to counsel pilgrims in poor health to request religious exemptions must be founded on thorough risk assessment.25

The Hajj pilgrimage involves substantial physical exertion, including Tawaf (circumambulation of the Kaaba), Sa'i (walking seven times between Safa and Marwa, totaling approximately 3.15 km), and travel between key sites such as Arafah and Mina, for estimated total of 58 km of walking over five days. While these activities are generally tolerated by healthy individuals, the interplay between pre-existing health conditions, individual risk factors, and overcrowding significantly contributes to morbidity and hospitalization among pilgrims.15,28–30

This study has several limitations: First, routinely collected health information has potential for information bias because of the variability in data quality across regions. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as genetic susceptibility or other unidentified risk determinants, may have influenced the observed associations. The generalizability of these findings are limited to the Indonesian Hajj pilgrim population, as differences in demographic characteristics, health systems, and pilgrimage practices across countries. While the large sample size enhances the statistical power, caution is warranted when extrapolating the results beyond this population.

CONCLUSION

Hospitalization among Hajj pilgrims is concentrated among high-risk individuals. Advanced age is the most significant predictor. Pre-existing diseases, particularly diabetes mellitus and hypertension, increase the likelihood of hospitalization, especially when they coexist. Males, lower educational attainment, and certain occupational groups further contribute to vulnerability. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive pre-departure screening, targeted risk stratification, and chronic disease management to reduce preventable morbidity and improve the safety of Hajj pilgrims.
