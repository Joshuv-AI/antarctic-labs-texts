Development and validation of a trans-ancestry polygenic risk score for type 1 diabetes
Authors: Basile Jumentier, Hui-Qi Qu, Tianyuan Lu, Kai Liu, Erica L. Kleinbrink, Kathleen Klein, Wiame Belbellaj, Isabel Gamache, Lauric Ferrat, Guillaume Butler-Laporte, Yangxi Li, Hakon Hakonarson, Wei Wu, Constantin Polychronakos, Celia M. T. Greenwood, Despoina Manousaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims/hypothesisThe high heritability of type 1 diabetes has enabled the development of polygenic risk scores (PRSs) as disease risk screening tools. PRSs can identify individuals at the highest genetic risk in a population, who can benefit from autoantibody and metabolic surveillance, to avoid ketoacidosis at diagnosis and to access preventive therapies. However, PRSs for type 1 diabetes developed from European data perform less well in non-European ancestries. We aimed to develop a PRS with comparable performance among different ancestries.MethodsUsing the PRS-CSx method, and data from large European, East Asian, African American and Hispanic type 1 diabetes genome-wide association studies (Ntotal_cases=29,469), we developed a trans-ancestry PRS (TA-PS), combining a non-HLA component incorporating over a million variants with the HLA component of a published European PRS (GRS2x). We tested the performance of the PRS using area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC), sensitivity and specificity in a multi-ancestry type 1 diabetes case–control cohort (Ntotal=4657; Nnon-European=556) from Montreal, Canada. We validated our results in two independent type 1 diabetes case–control cohorts (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Center for Applied Genomics [CHOP-CAG] and Genetic Risk Assessment for Chinese Eaglet-T1D [GRACE]) and two population-based cohorts (All of Us and UK Biobank).ResultsIn the multi-ancestry Montreal-based cohort, TA-PS showed an AUROC of 0.89 which was significantly higher than the respective measure obtained with GRS2x in the same population (AUROC of 0.85). We obtained better overall sensitivity at the 90th percentile cut-off using TA-PS (0.71 in Europeans, 0.77 in South Asians), compared with sensitivity of 0.32 in African Americans and 0.56 in Europeans using GRS2x. The specificity obtained using TA-PS was slightly lower than that of GRS2x, albeit still acceptable (≥0.83 across all ancestries). These results were validated in the four independent cohorts.Conclusions/interpretationWe developed a trans-ancestry PRS that outperformed the European-based GRS2x. Importantly, TA-PS provides a comparable prediction in various ancestries, which supports its use in population-wide screening programmes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains peer-reviewed but unedited supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00125-026-06706-5.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes is a chronic disease caused by the autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, which are responsible for insulin production. The prevalence of type 1 diabetes is strongly influenced by ancestry. Notably, its prevalence is four in 1000 in White Europeans but is generally lower in other ancestries [1]. Family studies have suggested that 50% of type 1 diabetes risk is explained by genetics [2]. The majority of this genetic risk can be explained by locus variation in the HLA class 2 region (DR/DQ) combined with about 100 non-HLA single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), including SNPs in the INS and PTPN22 genes [3]. This has allowed the development of powerful screening tools for type 1 diabetes based on polygenic risk scores (PRSs) [4–7]. Among these, a European score named GRS2 [8], comprising 67 SNPs, including 32 non-HLA SNPs and HLA interactions, presents the highest predictive performance with an area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC) of 0.92 (European case–control cohort, N=15,728, cases=6481). Within the highest 10% of the GRS2 distribution in a general European population, up to 84% of incident type 1 diabetes cases can be identified, which makes GRS2 a useful tool for type 1 diabetes population-wide screening programmes [9]. However, GRS2’s performance varies widely depending on ancestry. For instance, in a cohort of African Americans (N=1534, cases=168), the AUROC was 0.81 [10]. Furthermore, GRS2 distributions differ depending on the ancestry [1, 11], which complicates the choice of a threshold in screening programmes [7, 9]. Some screening programmes opted to use different thresholds of GRS2 depending on ancestry [11, 12], but this approach does not address the problem of mixed-ancestry individuals and requires additional bioinformatic analyses to define ancestry in order to make the choice of an optimal threshold. Against this backdrop, the aim of this study was, first, to develop a PRS for type 1 diabetes with comparable performance across ancestries and, second, to identify a uniform threshold of this PRS to define high risk, with the ultimate aim to facilitate application of PRSs in future population screening programmes for type 1 diabetes.

To do this, we focused on optimising the non-HLA component of GRS2. Leveraging large and recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS), on European [13], East Asian [14], African American and Hispanic [15, 16] populations, and machine learning methods for trans-ancestry PRS development [17–19], we built a series of trans-ancestry PRSs. The model selection was done in a multi-ancestry cohort (Ncases=2416, Ncontrols=2241), consisting of existing case–control collections and newly genotyped samples. Our best-performing trans-ancestry PRS (named TA-PS) was then validated in population-based or case–control cohorts (3230 cases from multiple ancestries). For our second aim, the same datasets were used to choose a threshold of the TA-PS with the optimal combination of sensitivity and specificity.

Methods

Training of ancestry-specific and trans-ancestral PRSs

To develop the trans-ancestry scores, we optimised a previous European-based score, GRS2x, that consists of three components: an HLA interaction (GRS2xInt) containing 14 HLA DR-DQ SNPs as well as interaction terms, an HLA part (GRS2xHLA) containing 21 non-DR/DQ HLA SNPs and a component (GRS2xNonHLA) containing 32 non-HLA SNPs. GRS2x [20] is an extension of the 67 SNP score GRS2 [8], which is adapted to data imputed with TOPMed (R2 and R3) (https://topmed.nhlbi.nih.gov) or whole genome sequencing (WGS) data. For the trans-ancestry optimisation, we modified GRS2x’s non-HLA part using the published methods PRS-CSx [18] and JointPRS [17]; for comparison purposes, we also used LDpred2 to optimise for Europeans [19]. To ensure absence of HLA overlap in the non-HLA trans-ancestry PRS, we excluded the MHC region (hg19: chr6:28,477,797–33,448,354) and a 1 Mb flanking LD window [21]. Both PRS-CSx and JointPRS algorithms produced a range of ancestry-specific and trans-ancestral (TA) scores with a varying shrinkage parameter (phi) based on GWAS summary statistics (electronic supplementary material [ESM] Table 1) (from large European, smaller ancestry-specific GWAS and ancestry-matched LD reference panels). We used the largest published European GWAS [13] (NEUR_cases/controls=18,942/501,638) and East Asian GWAS [14] (NEAS_cases/controls=1219/132,032). For African American and Hispanic ancestries, we meta-analysed two recent GWAS (NAFR_Michalek_cases/controls=409/482, NAFR_Verma_cases/controls=6451/115,861; NAMR_Michalek_cases/controls=153/158, NAMR_Verma_cases/controls=2295/55,134) [15, 16] using METAL [22]. These methods generated 35 PRSs for type 1 diabetes (ESM Methods): using PRS-CSx, we built six ancestry-specific PRSs per ancestry (six phi’s tested for AFR, AMR, EAS and EUR) and six TA PRSs (six phi’s tested); using JointPRS, we generated one ancestry-specific PRS per ancestry (for AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR).

Description of test cohorts

To test the trans-ancestry PRS against GRS2x, we assembled a multi-ancestry cohort of type 1 diabetes case and control participants. This cohort includes previously published Montreal-based cohorts such as: CaCo (ncases=514; ncontrols=2027), MTL (ncases=482) [23] and newly genotyped type 1 diabetes cases as part of this project (ncases=1086), and from the Montreal Children’s Hospital T1D Biobank (ncases=334). Using the R package 'PCAmatchR' (version 0.3.3), we paired based on principal components case participants from the four cohorts to ancestrally matched control participants from CaCo for European cases (Ncontrols=1908) and from CaCo (Ncontrols=117) and the 1000 Genomes Project [24] (1KGP) database (ncontrols=214) for the non-European cases (ESM Table 2). In total, we assembled 2416 case participants and 2241 control participants from five different populations: African (N=139), Hispanic (N=236), East Asian (N=56), European (N=4101) and South Asian (N=125). Type 1 diabetes diagnosis was defined according to clinical criteria in all cohorts, except in the Montreal Children’s T1D Biobank, where confirmation with autoantibodies was available. All genotypes were imputed using TOPMed (R3) [25] except for a limited number of control participants from 1000 Genomes (N=214), where WGS data were available. As part of the post-imputation quality control, we applied filters for minor allele frequency (MAF >0.001) and imputation quality (info-score >0.4). Additionally, we performed a pseudo-GWAS excluding the HLA using ancestry-specific combined datasets of type 1 diabetes case and control participants and calculated the genomic inflation factor (see ESM Methods). As shown in ESM Fig. 1, the genomic inflation factor (λ) of the ancestry-specific GWAS ranged from 1.01 to 1.17, which eliminates significant bias due to residual population stratification among case and control participants.

Computation of the PRSs

To compute all 35 newly developed PRSs and the GRS2x (GRS2xHLA and GRS2xNonHLA terms) from our imputed data, we used plink 1.9 [26] and its '--score' command. For GRS2xInt, we used the toolkit developed by Sharp et al [8] available on GitHub (USF-HII/HLA-prs-toolkit). This toolkit allows generation of scores based on SNPs (here the 14 DR/DQ SNPs) for type 1 diabetes where the epistasis of the HLA genes (gene vs gene interaction) is modelled using SNPs in strong linkage disequilibrium with the HLA haplotypes.

In order to combine the 35 new non-HLA PRSs with the HLA and the HLA interaction part of the GRS2x, we applied different approaches. First, since the 35 non-HLA scores did not have the same mean and standard deviation as the GRS2x, we scaled each of the HLA and non-HLA components to have zero mean and unit variance (scale function in R [27]). For instance, a TA non-HLA PRS generated with PRS-CSx represented the sum of scale(TA) + scale(GRS2xInt + GRS2xHLA). Second, to further optimise the combination of the different scores, we estimated coefficients for the three terms of our models (non-HLA, interaction and HLA). These coefficients were estimated only from European data (Ncases=2193; Ncontrols=1908) using logistic regression: (type 1 diabetescase_status ~ TA + GRS2xInt + GRS2xHLA). The coefficients were then extracted from this regression model (‘a’ for non-HLA, ‘b’ for GRS2xInt and ‘c’ for GRS2HLA) and applied as follows: fit = a × non-HLA + b × GRS2xInt + c × GRS2xHLA. The coefficients a, b and c were estimated using two transformations of each term with either scale or MinMax (ESM Table 3). The MinMax transformation scales data to a specified range, here between 0 and 1, by subtracting the minimum value and dividing by the range of the data.

Testing of the PRSs

To test the prediction performance across various ancestries of all generated PRSs, and of the GRS2x, we calculated the AUROCs (AUROC + 95% confidence interval [CI]). We used the Delong test to compare the AUROC between two PRSs within the same ancestry. This led to the selection of the best-performing PRS for each ancestry, and across all ancestries.

To evaluate the optimal threshold of the best-performing PRS, we calculated, across all ancestral datasets and in our multi-ancestry cohort, sensitivity and specificity metrics at different thresholds based on European control data. We considered the following thresholds: top 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% of the distribution (corresponding to the 95th, 90th, 85th, 80th and 75th centiles). For each threshold, we also calculated the F1-score metric, which corresponds to the harmonic mean of precision and recall. CIs for these different metrics were estimated by bootstrap (niter=1000) and the comparison of these values was carried out with a Z test.

Validation in independent cohorts

We then tested the performance of the various PRSs in independent case–control or population-based cohorts of various ancestries (ESM Table 4). Specifically, in the multi-ancestry All of Us Biobank (AoU, N=89,699; Ncases=1084; Ncontrols=88,635), in a case–control multi-ancestry cohort from the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Center for Applied Genomics (CHOP-CAG, N=6498; Ncases=1094; Ncontrols=5404), in the UK Biobank (UKB, N=427,847; Ncases=778; Ncontrols=427,069) and in a Chinese case–control cohort across multiple paediatric centres named Genetic Risk Assessment for Chinese Eaglet-T1D (GRACE, N=597; Ncases=294; Ncontrols=303). AoU policy prohibits conducting stratified analyses on ancestry if the number of cases is less than 20, and we applied this approach to all cohorts. The definition of case and control participants for each cohort is available in the ESM Methods. Ethics approval for use of samples of all the above studies was obtained by the respective ethics committees.

For each cohort, we estimated the AUROCs of the different PRSs and the other three metrics at the following thresholds: 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25%. The thresholds were estimated only on the control participants for the case–control cohorts while for the population cohorts the entire datasets were used. Subgroup analyses by sex and age at diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (<18 years vs ≥18 years) were performed in the entire UKB, and in its European and non-European subsets.

Results

Testing of the PRSs

First, we tested the performance of 35 generated TA PRSs (combined with the HLA part of GRS2x) by ancestry (Fig. 2, ESM Table 5). For PRS-CSx, selection of phi values is presented in the ESM (ESM Methods, ESM Fig. 2, ESM Table 6). As expected, both GRS2x and the newly built European ancestry-specific PRS using LDpred2 achieved higher AUROC in the European compared with non-European populations. With multi-ancestral data incorporated, the TA scores consistently outperformed these two European-based PRSs in the European (AUROC 0.88 for TA PRS vs 0.86 for GRS2x), African (AUROC 0.86 for TA PRS vs 0.83 for GRS2x), Hispanic (AUROC 0.86 for TA PRS vs 0.81 for GRS2x), East Asian (AUROC 0.82 for TA PRS vs 0.81 for GRS2x) and South Asian (AUROC 0.9 for TA PRS vs 0.87 for GRS2x) populations. Furthermore, the TA PRSs outperformed or were comparable to the best-performing ancestry-specific PRSs built using PRS-CSx or JointPRS. Selection of the best weighting approach for the PRS

We then focused on optimising the TA scores with the HLA part of the GRS2x (GRS2xInt and GRS2xHLA). Figure 3 and ESM Table 8 show the performance of the combined PRSs using different weighting approaches and scaling methods (scale and MinMax). The methods were named: TA-PS for the additive model, TA-PS (S) for the weighted model with scale transformation and TA-PS (M) for the weighted model with MinMax transformation. The performance of the two versions of the weighted TA-PS in the European dataset (AUROC=0.90) and of the TA-PS (S) in the Hispanic dataset (AUROC=0.86) was better than that of the unweighted version of TA-PS and of GRS2x. In the South Asian population, we found that only the crude TA-PS (AUROC=0.90) performed better than GRS2x. For other ancestries, the small number of cases did not allow us to observe significant differences between the TA-PS in its weighted or crude versions and GRS2x (ESM Fig. 3). To increase the statistical power of our analysis, we conducted analyses on the entire multi-ancestry dataset (N=4657) and on the non-European dataset (N=556) (ESM Table 8). GRS2x showed an AUROC of 0.85 while the TA-PS (S) obtained an AUROC of 0.89 (pDelong=4.67×10−31, ESM Table 9) in the multi-ancestry dataset. In the non-European dataset, GRS2x performed slightly less well with an AUROC of 0.83 vs 0.87 with the TA-PS (S) (pDelong=0.0007, ESM Table 9). Overall, these results suggest that weighting of the TA-PS can further improve type 1 diabetes prediction across ancestries on top of the crude TA-PS. Choice of the optimal threshold

We then compared the distributions of the TA-PS (in its crude or weighted versions) and GRS2x by ancestry. Specifically, we set a threshold at the 90th centile in European control participants for both scores and performed cross comparisons across ancestries (Fig. 4a). For GRS2x, the 90th centile threshold for the European dataset corresponds to the 86th for the Hispanic dataset, to the 90th for the East Asian and South Asian datasets, and to the 93rd for the African dataset. For TA-PS (S), the 90th centile threshold in the European population corresponds to the 91st for the Hispanic populations, to the 87th for the East Asian and the 85th for the South Asian, and to the 83rd for the African datasets. We calculated, for each threshold determined in control participants of European ancestry (95th, 90th, 85th, 80th and 75th centiles), three metrics: sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE) and F1 (Fig. 4b, ESM Table 10). At the 90th centile threshold, the average sensitivity across ancestries of the GRS2x was 0.53 (SENmin_AFR=0.32 and SENmax_SAS=0.66) while for TA-PS (S) it was 0.74 (SENmin_EUR=0.71 and SENmax_SAS=0.77). For crude TA-PS, the metrics were overall better than those of GRS2x but slightly worse than those of its weighted version.

Within AoU, the AUROC was higher for TA-PS (S) compared with GRS2x (0.75 vs 0.73, pDelong=3.65×10−11), with comparable sensitivity and specificity at the 90th percentile. In the African subset, TA-PS (S) showed a better AUROC than GRS2x (0.65 vs 0.62, pDelong=1.46×10−7) and a better sensitivity (0.34 vs 0.28, pZ test=0.02), while maintaining an acceptable specificity (>0.90).

Additionally, subgroup analyses based on sex and age of diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (<18 years vs ≥18 years) did not show significant differences in the performance of TA-PS (S) across sexes and ages at diagnosis, and these results were similar in European and non-European UKB participants (ESM Fig. 5).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to create an equitable genetic prediction tool for screening for type 1 diabetes in populations of diverse ancestral backgrounds. Building on previous developments in the field of type 1 diabetes genetic prediction in European populations (GRS2 and its updated version, GRS2x) [8, 20] and recently available ancestry-specific GWAS datasets, totalling almost 30 thousand cases from various ancestries, we constructed our trans-ancestry PRS, named TA-PS. While this score did not focus on the optimisation of the HLA component of the European scores, the non-HLA component was enriched with over a million SNPs, present in GWAS of four ancestries. The overall performance of TA-PS was better than that of GRS2x in our multi-ancestry testing cohort (N=4657) based on metrics such as AUROC, sensitivity and specificity. Based on the AUROC, we observed a gain of 0.035 with the weighted and scaled TA-PS, compared with the GRS2x, in our entire multi-ancestry cohort. The 90th centile of the distribution of this score showed, as a threshold to define high risk, an optimised sensitivity across all ancestries compared with the GRS2x, and a comparable specificity. Similar results were found in the European and Hispanic populations.

Our work enhances the use of type 1 diabetes PRSs in population-wide screening programmes. The importance of establishing a common threshold using the same PRS, independently from the ancestral background, comes from the fact that a significant portion of the population in many countries is admixed [1, 11, 12]. Adjustment for ancestry can be challenging, since reported ancestry can be inaccurate and participants in screening programmes may refuse to disclose it [28]. Ancestry determined based on genetic data could be applied to select an ancestry-specific PRS, or an ancestry-specific threshold of a unique PRS, but clustering of admixed individuals within an ancestral group can still be challenging.

In our Montreal-based cohort, GRS2x did not achieve comparable sensitivity across ancestries at the 90th percentile, particularly for samples of African ancestry, where the sensitivity was 0.31, while it was almost twice as high in participants of European ancestry (0.56). Contrarily, with TA-PS (S), we obtained higher sensitivities than GRS2x, and, importantly, this metric was more comparable among ancestries, ranging from 0.63 in East Asian to 0.79 in African populations, while maintaining specificities comparable to those of GRS2x, and greater than 0.84. When considering all non-European samples, at the 10% threshold, the sensitivity of TA-PS (S) was 0.72, which was significantly higher than that of GRS2x (0.53) while maintaining a specificity of 0.88, largely acceptable for a screening tool.

The validation in independent datasets confirmed the improved performance of the TA-PS over GRS2x: the multi-ancestry CHOP-CAG cohort and the East Asian GRACE case–control studies demonstrated a gain in sensitivity of TA-PS (S) compared with GRS2x using as threshold the top 10% of the distribution. The AoU population cohort also demonstrated a significant gain in terms of AUROC using the TA-PS (S) across its entire multi-ancestry dataset but also specifically for African Americans. Furthermore, within this African subset, a gain in terms of sensitivity at the 90th percentile threshold was observed. Finally, in UKB, we confirmed that TA-PS (S) outperformed GRS2x in terms of both AUROC and sensitivity (at the 90th percentile) while maintaining a specificity equal to 0.95. All these results support that TA-PS (S) allows us to increase the prediction performance compared with GRS2x but also allows us to increase the sensitivity while maintaining an acceptable specificity, at the top 10% of the distribution threshold used in previous studies.

Regarding the limitations of our study, the most important is absence of specific GWAS data for certain ancestries, notably South Asians or Indigenous peoples, limiting optimisation of the genetic predictors in these ancestries. The fact that we optimised only the non-HLA part of the score is another limitation, as the HLA loci can explain at least 50% of the heritability of type 1 diabetes. In order to optimise the HLA component using a similar approach to that of the GRS2 or GRS2x [8, 20], access to large, ancestry-specific WGS data is necessary. Interestingly, a recent study [29] focused on constructing PRSs containing only HLA SNPs or HLA alleles. The authors showed that ancestry-specific HLA PRSs performed as well as ancestry-combined HLA PRSs within their cohort. These HLA PRSs can be integrated into the TA-PS in future steps of trans-ancestry PRS development for type 1 diabetes.

Also, it is notable that previous studies reported ancestry-specific PRSs with higher performance metrics than TA-PS (S). Specifically, an African-specific PRS developed and tested in the CHOP-CAG cohort obtained an AUROC of 0.83 which is higher than that of our TA score [10], but the testing cohort was not exactly the same as our CHOP-CAG validation dataset, and external validation was lacking. Also, a recent Chinese-specific PRS showed better performance in an East Asian population [30]. In ESM Fig. 6, we compared the performance of TA-PS (S) with the two aforementioned scores in our ancestry-specific and multi-ancestry datasets. While the ancestry-specific scores showed comparable performance with TA-PS (S) in the same ancestry, they performed less well in a multi-ancestry population or a non-European dataset combining multiple ancestries. Additionally, while the enhanced results obtained by TA-PS (S) in the European dataset of our testing cohort may reflect some degree of overfitting, given that the weights were estimated on the same samples, TA-PS nonetheless showed consistent performance in the independent, predominantly European UKB. The AUROCs obtained with GRS2x and with TA-PS in the UKB and AoU were lower than those reported using the GRS2x or GRS2 in their respective publications. This discrepancy can be explained by differences in the definitions of case and control individuals in these biobanks. For instance, under previous definitions requiring diagnosis before a certain age threshold, the proportion of cases in UKB was 0.11% (408 cases and 365,622 controls) [20], while with our definition, the proportion was 0.18% (778 cases and 427,069 controls). In AoU, using previous definitions, the proportion of cases was 0.04% (147 cases and 317,817 controls), while we obtained a proportion of 1.12% (1064 cases and 88,635 controls) following Szczerbinski et al, who applied stricter criteria for healthy control individuals and excluded individuals who did not clearly meet the case or control definitions [31]. Importantly, when evaluated on the same population of case and control participants, TA-PS outperformed or was at least comparable to GRS2x. Notably, our validation cohorts were either case–control or population-based biobanks (AoU or UKB) requiring voluntary participation, which are not necessarily representative of the general population. Another limitation is the increased cost of genome-wide genotyping and imputation required to retrieve information on ~1 million SNPs included in TA-PS compared with the lower cost of including a limited number of SNPs (for example, 67 in GRS2) in a commercialised array. Given the decreasing costs of genotyping and the simplified pipelines for imputation, this limitation will be less of a consideration in the near future. Also, array genotyping combined with imputation offers the possibility to calculate many PRSs from a single DNA sample in order to perform simultaneous and cost-effective screening for many diseases. Finally, data from prospective population-wide screening programmes, such as the Canadian CanScreenT1D research consortium (https://canscreent1d.ca/), will be necessary to validate TA-PS in future studies.

To conclude, we constructed a trans-ancestry score for type 1 diabetes, named TA-PS, based on the largest and most recent GWAS datasets and employing the widely used PRS-CSx method for TA PRS construction. TA-PS in its scaled version, when applied in five independent case–control or population-based cohorts, yielded more comparable results among ancestries in terms of prediction accuracy and sensitivity than the European-based GRS2x. Future efforts, leveraging large-scale sequencing data from individuals with type 1 diabetes and control individuals from various ancestries, should focus on the optimisation of the HLA part of this score.
