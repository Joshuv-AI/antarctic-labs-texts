Pleuroparenchymal sarcoidosis: A rare manifestation mimicking metastatic lung cancer
Authors: Eugene Gan, Chee Kiang Tay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 66‐year‐old male from Myanmar presented with 3 months of cough and constitutional symptoms. He was an ex‐tobacco user with no significant medical or exposure history. Chest x‐ray showed ill‐defined bilateral opacities and a left pleural effusion. Chest CT revealed two right lower lobe masses, and a moderate‐sized left pleural effusion. PET‐CT demonstrated hypermetabolic uptake in the thickened nodular pleura, pericardium, and hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes. EBUS‐TBNA of the right lower paratracheal node and TBLB of the right lower lobe mass yielded epithelioid granulomas comprising multinucleated giant cells, epithelioid histiocytes and lymphoplasmacytic cells. Thoracoscopy revealed hard, whitish mass‐like parietal pleural plaques, and pleural biopsy revealed identical histopathologic results. His symptoms resolved quickly after commencing prednisolone 25 mg daily. Chest CT at 6 months demonstrated near complete resolution of the parenchymal masses and pleural effusion. We highlight this unique case of pleuroparenchymal sarcoidosis mimicking metastatic lung cancer in a tuberculosis‐endemic region.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcoidosis is an idiopathic, multisystem inflammatory disease, and manifestations range from asymptomatic to rapidly progressing disease. According to the 2020 American Thoracic Society guidelines, the diagnosis hinges on: a compatible clinical presentation, presence of non‐necrotizing granulomas in ≥1 tissue sample(s), and exclusion of other causes of granulomatous inflammation. 1 Sarcoidosis with pleural involvement is uncommon with reported case reports/series. Pleuroparenchymal disease is an even rarer entity and has yet to be reported in Asian patients.

CASE REPORT

A 66‐year‐old man from Myanmar with previous tobacco use of 12 pack‐years presented with 3 months of cough, exertional dyspnea, anorexia and weight loss. He denied hemoptysis or any occupational dust exposure. He ran a trading business in Myanmar. He was given multiple antibiotic courses by his family physician for presumed respiratory tract infections, and eventually referred to the local hospital due to unabating symptoms.

The index chest x‐ray (CXR) showed right multifocal discrete opacities and a left pleural effusion (Figure 1). Chest computed tomography (CT) showed a right upper lobe mass (2.3 × 2.2 cm) with intralesional calcification, and two other right lower lobe (RLL) masses (4.3 × 3 cm and 4.2 × 2.2 cm). There were also multiple left pleural‐based masses (the largest measuring 3.5 × 1.2 cm) and a massive left pleural effusion with collapse‐consolidation of the left lower lobe. Fibrosis and pneumonitis were noted in the apico‐posterior segment of the left upper division bronchus. No hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy was discerned. Diagnostic thoracentesis yielded a clear yellowish aspirate with the following pleural fluid analysis—differential count: 70% polymorphonuclear, 30% mononuclear cells; protein: 43 g/L; glucose: 6.7 mmol/L; cytology: neutrophils, lymphocytes, histiocytes and reactive mesothelial cells with no malignant cells.

Index CXR at presentation showing multifocal discrete opacities with a left pleural effusion and axial cut of PET‐CT performed before diagnosis of sarcoidosis demonstrating FDG‐avidity in right lower lobe mass and pleural/pericardial nodules.

He was started on standard anti‐tuberculosis (TB) 4‐drug regimen despite negative sputum TB tests, but he discontinued it after 3 weeks due to lethargy and pruritus. Thereafter, he sought a second opinion at our institution.

Physical examination did not reveal malnutrition, clubbing or cervical lymphadenopathy. Reduced breath sounds and stony dull percussion were appreciated in the left lower chest. Blood investigations were unremarkable—haemoglobin: 12.8 g/dL, WBC count: 4.84 × 109/L; albumin: 37 g/L; total protein: 68 g/L; and creatinine: 81 μmol/L. Positron emission tomography‐CT scan showed FDG‐avidity of right lung masses and pericardial/left pleural nodules (Figure 1). Hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes were borderline enlarged.

He underwent endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) of the right lower paratracheal lymph node (4R) that revealed adequate lymphoid yield with multinucleated cells (Figure 2). Transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) of the postero‐basal segment of the RLL demonstrated epithelioid granulomas with multinucleated giant cells, epithelioid histiocytes, and lymphoplasmacytic cells without evidence of malignancy (Figure 2). Ziehl‐Neelsen stain was negative for mycobacteria. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae sensitive to amoxicillin and clavulanic acid, and ciprofloxacin. Acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) smear/mycobacterial culture/TB nuclei acid amplification (NAA) tests were negative in sputum, BAL and TBLB samples.

(From left to right): Right lower paratracheal lymph node (4R) EBUS‐TBNA specimen revealing epithelioid granulomas, followed by right lower lobe TBLB histology slide demonstrating non‐necrotizing granulomatous inflammation with epithelioid histiocytes and multinucleated giant cells surrounded by lymphocytes and fibrous stroma; followed by thoracoscopic image showing whitish mass‐like nodular plaques on the parietal pleura and diaphragm with corresponding pleural cryobiopsy specimen yielding similar histopathological features to the TBLB specimen.

Thoracic tumour board discussion suggested further evaluation with medical thoracoscopy. During thoracoscopy, whitish mass‐like nodular plaques were seen on the parietal pleura and diaphragm (Figure 2). Pleural biopsy via conventional biopsy forceps was unsatisfactory due to marked lesional hardness. Therefore, pleural cryobiopsy was performed using a 1.7 mm cryoprobe without significant haemorrhage.

Pleural fluid analysis revealed a predominantly neutrophilic exudate—LDH: 1351 (serum 402) U/L; protein: 40 (serum 63) g/L; glucose: 3.7 (serum 6.0) mmol/L; differential count: 79% neutrophils, 11% lymphocytes, 7% eosinophils and 3% monocytes; AFB smear and TB NAA test: negative; adenosine deaminase: 30 U/L (reference <40), and CD4/CD8 ratio: 0.45. Left pleural biopsies demonstrated non necrotizing granulomatous inflammation comprising epithelioid histiocytes, occasional multinucleated giant cells, and few lymphoplasmacytic cells focally surrounded by concentric layers of fibrosis. No mycobacterial/fungal organisms were identified (Figure 2). In view of consistent histopathologic findings from multiple biopsy sites, the patient was diagnosed with pleuroparenchymal sarcoidosis and commenced on oral prednisolone 25 mg daily with trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis.

One month after treatment initiation, his symptoms resolved. Prednisolone was reduced from a daily dose of 25 to 20 mg, and then 15 mg over 3 months. Subsequently, it was tailed to 5 mg daily over the next 6 months. Interval chest CT at 6 months post‐treatment demonstrated near‐complete resolution of the pleuroparenchymal lesions and left pleural effusion. Prednisolone dose was reduced to 2.5 mg daily with clinical and radiologic stability before care was handed back to doctors in Myanmar.

DISCUSSION

Sarcoidosis‐associated pleural effusions occur in only 1% of patients with sarcoidosis. 2 , 3 They are observed in <5% of patients on CXR, and may be due to other coexisting conditions unless there is histological confirmation. 4 Proposed pathophysiological mechanisms include pleural granulomatous involvement or blockage of septal lymphatic channels by granulomas. 2 , 4 A literature review revealed only one other case of pleuroparenchymal sarcoidosis in a Caucasian female with bilateral hilar/mediastinal nodal calcification (without significant lymphadenopathy), peri‐lymphatic nodularity and pleural effusions. 5 Our case is unique as the radiologic asymmetry (right‐sided FDG‐avid lung masses, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and contralateral pleural effusion/nodules) mimicked lung malignancy/TB. Notably, the diagnosis was clinched via thorough histologic sampling of multiple sites via TBLB, EBUS‐TBNA, and pleural cryobiopsy that consistently demonstrated non‐necrotizing granulomas. This case is a reminder of the protean clinical mimicry of sarcoidosis to lung cancer in a TB‐endemic region.
