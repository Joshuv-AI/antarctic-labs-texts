A nationwide survey of radiographic exposure factor selection practices in small animal veterinary clinics across Ireland
Authors: Shauna Daly, Michelle O’Connor, Cliona Skelly, Rachel Toomey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiographic exposure factor selection impacts image quality and radiation dose. This study examined the professionals responsible for conducting X-ray examinations and their training in Ireland. An online survey was distributed to all practices registered with the Veterinary Council of Ireland. Data were collected on demographics, X-ray equipment, methods of exposure factor selection, confidence in adjustments, and knowledge of detector dose indicators. A total of 120 responses were received. Most clinics use digital systems and rely on technique charts. Many respondents showed limited understanding of detector dose indicators, with 40% reporting poor knowledge and nearly half unaware of their system’s capabilities. Exposure factors were rarely adjusted, raising concerns about dose creep. Confidence was higher among less experienced staff. No strong association was found between post-qualification training and confidence. Findings highlight the need for improved training, continuous professional development, and guidelines to optimize practice.

Introduction

Radiography plays a vital role in the diagnosis and management of pathologies in veterinary practice. Radiographic imaging is the most commonly performed imaging modality in veterinary clinics [1]. It is important for veterinary staff to acquire images of sufficient quality to ensure accurate interpretation and diagnosis. The quality of the radiographic images is dependent on the selection of appropriate exposure factors for the specific animal patient [2]. Research on exposure factor use in veterinary practice is limited.

The transition from film-screen radiography (FSR) to digital radiographic systems—computed radiography (CR) and direct digital radiography (DR)—has significantly advanced veterinary imaging through faster image acquisition, enhanced post-processing, wider exposure latitude, and the elimination of chemical processing [3, 4]. Adoption has increased rapidly in Ireland and the UK, with surveys indicating a shift from mainly FSR to digital systems in small animal practice by the end of the 2010s [5, 6]. Cost was identified as the primary barrier to digital uptake. No more recent data on the radiography systems used in Irish veterinary practice were found.

Optimizing radiographic exposure requires consideration of patient size and anatomical region to produce diagnostic images while minimizing radiation dose [2, 7]. Exposure factors such as kilovoltage peak, milliampere-seconds, source-to-image distance, grid usage, and focal spot size must be tailored to each patient and examination [7, 8]. Nuth et al. [8] demonstrated that 38% of radiographs submitted by veterinary practitioners contained exposure errors which significantly reduced image quality. While technique charts provide useful starting points, manual adjustment remains essential to ensure accurate imaging for individual animals [2, 8, 9]. In Ireland, small animals are rarely held during radiography in line with radiation safety regulations [10], but where holding does occur, staff occupational doses may be impacted.

In human radiography, the principle of keeping radiation doses ‘As Low As Reasonably Achievable’ (ALARA) is a core principle of radiation protection [11] and has been the subject of a variety of research, as demonstrated in a review by Steffensen et al. [12]. Historically, this principle has received less emphasis in veterinary radiography, where shorter lifespans led to the assumption that radiation exposure posed minimal risk [13, 14]. A survey of veterinary specialists found that over half believed radiographic doses posed no increased cancer risk to animals [15], suggesting that awareness of radiation risks remains limited. However, ALARA is increasingly recognized as important in veterinary imaging, both to reduce animal exposure and to minimize occupational doses to staff. International guidance, including the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) Task Group 107 and Publication 153, now recommends applying the principles of justification, optimization, and dose limits to veterinary practice [16, 17].

The Irish Code of Practice on the Application of the Ionising Radiation Regulations (IRR19) in Veterinary Medicine [10] does not explicitly address dose optimization in radiographic examinations of animals. However, it requires that all X-ray procedures have appropriate clinical justification and encourages measures to avoid unnecessary repeat exposures, supporting the goal of minimizing radiation doses.

Digital radiography systems offer wider exposure latitude and advanced post-processing, allowing diagnostically acceptable images even when overexposed [18]. However, this flexibility can mask exposure errors, contributing to ‘dose creep,’ where operators gradually increase exposure to avoid noise or perceived underexposure [19, 20]. Detector dose indicators, such as exposure index (EI) and deviation index (DI), provide feedback on detector dose and deviations from target exposures [21, 22]. However, inconsistent implementation across manufacturers limits standardization. Research on EI and DI use in veterinary radiography remains limited, highlighting a gap in optimizing exposure monitoring for animal imaging.

Despite the importance of optimizing exposure factors, research on their use in veterinary practice is limited. This study investigated small animal radiographic practices in Ireland, focusing on the professionals performing X-rays, their training, the equipment used, and methods for selecting and adapting exposure factors. Understanding these practices can help improve image quality while minimizing radiation doses, addressing an area of growing significance in veterinary imaging and radiation safety.

Materials and methods

This study was approved as a low-risk study by the UCD School of Medicine Undergraduate and Taught Masters Research Ethics Committee (UTMREC-SM-E-24-391-Daly-Toomey).

A cross-sectional study of Irish veterinary practices performing small animal radiography was conducted using an anonymous electronic survey, targeting veterinary professionals in Ireland involved in small animal radiography. Practices listing at least 20% of their practice as small animal work were identified from the Veterinary Council of Ireland [23] website. A total of 476 practices were contacted using the publicly available email addresses listed in the VCI’s ‘List of Registered Premises’.

The survey, hosted on SurveyMonkey, included both open and closed ended questions. It was drafted by radiographers with both human and veterinary experience and reviewed by a veterinary radiologist and an experienced veterinary radiographer prior to piloting.

A pilot study was carried out (10–27 May 2024) to assess the technical functionality, question clarity, and response rates. The pilot survey was emailed to 10% (n = 47) of the population and received 15 responses. Based on these results, adjustments to the number, layout, and phrasing of questions were made before launching the main survey. Pilot responses were excluded from the final analysis.

The amended main survey ran from 17 June to 15 July 2024 and was distributed to 429 practices via email, with a reminder email sent out between 9th and 10th of July to encourage more responses. The email invited all staff involved in radiography in the clinic to fill out the survey in order to maximize the number of responses. It included 21 questions, followed by a section which requested participants to input the exposure factors they use for various X-ray examinations. The survey gathered information on participant demographics, X-ray systems used, exposure factor selection methods, and use of automatic exposure control (AEC), and detector dose indicators. Likert-scale questions assessed confidence in exposure factor adaptation, understanding of dose indicators, and frequency of image repetition.

Survey data were cleaned, and the responses to open-ended questions (n = 4) were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis [24, 25]. Ordinal regression analysis was conducted in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) [26] to assess the relationship between various factors—participant role, years of experience, frequency of undertaking radiographic examinations, exposure factor adaptation, and post-qualification training—and confidence in exposure factor adaptation.

Results

This study aimed to gather insights from veterinary professionals on their use of X-ray systems and exposure factor selection. One hundred and twenty-nine responses were received in total; however, nine respondents did not report that they had access to an X-ray system and were excluded from analysis. The overall response rate of the survey cannot be determined due to the fact that the emails were sent out to veterinary clinics rather than to veterinary staff individually, and multiple staff members from the same clinic may have responded. The survey received responses from 120 participants, of which 66 did not complete the survey in full. These participants were included in the analysis for the questions they answered. Some questions allowed participants to select multiple responses, so percentages may exceed 100%.

Demographics and training of small animal radiographic practitioners

All 120 respondents provided demographic information. Most were veterinary nurses (62%, n = 74), followed by veterinary practitioners (35%, n = 42). The remaining 3% (n = 4) held other roles such as administrator, clinic manager, animal care assistant, or head veterinary nurse. In routine clinical practice, the selection and adjustment of exposure factors are primarily undertaken by veterinary practitioners and veterinary nurses, who represent the vast majority of respondents in this study. Experience levels varied, with 37% (n = 44) having 0 to 5 y, 33% (n = 39) having 6 to 15 y, 16% (n = 19) having 16 to 25 y, 10% (n = 12) having 26 to 35 y, and 5% (n = 6) having 36 to 45 y of experience. All respondents reported that small animals comprised more than 20% of their clinic’s caseload, confirming that the target population was reached.

Twenty-seven percent (n = 31) of participants reported undertaking additional training in radiography or diagnostic imaging post-qualification. Of these, 45% (n = 14) stated that this additional training took the form of continuous professional development (CPD), 10% (n = 3) obtained a Certificate in Veterinary Radiology, and 6% (n = 2) completed radiation safety training. Ordinal regression analysis indicated that post-qualification training was not significantly associated with higher confidence in adapting exposure factors (P = .194), suggesting that additional formal training alone did not predict greater confidence.

Radiographic equipment and technology

In terms of the X-ray systems utilized, CR and DR were equally common, each used by 41% (n = 45) of respondents (see Fig. 1).

Types of X-ray systems used.

Of the 110 participants who answered the question, 31% (n = 34) reported having preprogrammed exposure factors in their X-ray system, 58% (n = 64) did not, and 11% (n = 12) were unsure. Of the 31% with preprogrammed exposure settings in place, 59% (n = 20) reported updates to their preset exposure parameters within the last 3 y, while 21% (n = 7) were uncertain as to when these were last updated. The remaining 21% (n = 7) stated that their settings had not been updated since installation. Of these seven, four had systems installed within the last 3 y, two between 3 and 5 y ago, and one between 6 and 10 y ago.

When asked whether their X-ray system provided users with a detector dose indicator, 99 participants responded, with the majority (48%, n = 48), unsure whether or not their X-ray system provided a detector dose indicator (see Fig. 2).

Provision of detector dose indicator on participants’ systems.

In an optional open-text response where participants were invited to elaborate on how detector dose indicator values influence their choice of exposure factors, respondents expressed varying degrees of reliance on detector dose indicator values when determining exposure factors. Nine respondents indicated that detector dose indicator values do not significantly impact their practice, either because they do not actively refer to them or because exposures typically fall within acceptable limits without adjustment. For instance, one participant commented, ‘It does not. Generally falls in the appropriate range’, while another noted, ‘It doesn't as they always come back as within normal limits’. Ten participants reported using detector dose indicators to some extent to guide exposure adjustments, with comments such as ‘Adjustments of subsequent exposures to correct under/over exposure’ and ‘I would lower exposures if DI value was high’. One participant also noted limitations, including unfamiliar systems, equipment constraints, or challenges in interpreting values for small anatomical regions. These responses highlight variability in the perceived usefulness of dose indicators across clinical contexts.

Only one of 94 respondents reported an excellent understanding of detector dose indicators, while 40% (n = 38) indicated a poor understanding (see Fig. 3).

Respondents’ understanding of detector dose indicators.

Compared with those performing fewer or more than 20 to 29 radiographs per week, respondents who performed between 20 and 29 exposures (n = 7) reported higher self-rated knowledge of detector dose indicators. Two-thirds (66%, n = 4) rated their understanding of detector dose indicators as good to excellent, and none reported a poor understanding. In contrast, the majority of other respondents rated their knowledge as fair to poor (76%, n = 67), with 43% (n = 38) reporting poor understanding.

The majority of participants (62%, n = 62) were also unsure of whether their system had an AEC setting. An additional 30% (n = 30) reported that their system did not have AEC, while only eight confirmed its presence.

Exposure factor selection and adaptation methods

One hundred and one respondents reported the method by which they usually select their exposure factors. The most frequent response, at 72% (n = 73), was through using a technique chart (see Fig. 4).

Methods of exposure factor selection reportedly used by participants.

Of the 101 respondents who reported the frequency with which they adapt exposure factors, 46% (n = 46) reported ‘sometimes’ changing from those recommended by their chosen method of selection, while 24% (n = 24) ‘often’ or ‘very often’ changed them. Thirty percent rarely, if ever, changed exposure factors from those recommended (see Fig. 5).

Frequency with which respondents adapt exposure factors from standard.

Practitioner confidence in radiographic technique optimization

When asked about their confidence in adjusting exposure factors, 101 participants responded, with ‘Somewhat confident’ being the most common answer at 44% (n = 44) (see Fig. 6).

Confidence levels of participants when adapting exposure factors.

Ordinal regression analysis identified several significant predictors of higher confidence in exposure factor adaptation. Participants with 0–5 y of experience reported greater confidence than those with more experience (P = .017). The most consistent predictor was the frequency of exposure factor adaptation, with those in the ‘often’ or ‘very often’ categories showing significantly higher confidence (P < .001). Additionally, participants performing 20–29 X-ray exposures per week reported greater confidence than those performing fewer than 20 X-rays per week (P = .003). No significant relationships were found for participants' roles (P = .070) or post-qualification training (P = .194).

When asked how often they would repeat an image due to a reduction in image quality caused by inappropriate exposure factor selection, 60% (n = 61) of respondents indicated that they would rarely do so (see Fig. 7).

Frequency of repeated exposures due to inadequate image quality.

Sixty-three participants provided details in an optional open text response on the circumstances in which they would deviate from their standard method for selecting exposure factors and how they would do so. The majority of responses (62%, n = 39) related to patient factors, such as patient size, weight, and clinical condition. For example, one respondent reported the following as reasons that they would adapt their exposure factors: ‘Obesity, suspicion of fluid or air within pleural space, suspicion of proliferative bony neoplasia, juvenile vs adult’. A further 27% (n = 17) of responses were related to technique factors, which included references to the use of technique charts, preset exposure settings, and adjustments made to address image quality issues. Finally, lack of knowledge was identified as a theme in 6% of responses (n = 4), where participants expressed uncertainty about when or how to modify exposure factors.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into current small animal radiographic practices in Ireland. The 82% prevalence of digital radiography marks a dramatic increase since 2000, when these systems were not used in any Irish veterinary practices [5]. This widespread adoption reflects the well-established benefits of digital radiography, including faster image acquisition, post-processing capabilities, and wider exposure latitude [27]. However, despite this progress, a significant knowledge gap remains concerning critical optimization tools, such as AEC and detector dose indicators, and varied responses regarding how exposure factors are selected or modified for individual cases.

Study design

The survey was designed to include both closed and open-ended questions. Closed questions ensured efficiency and reliability [28, 29], while open-ended questions allowed participant to provide more detailed insights. To ensure relevance and clarity, the survey was reviewed by a veterinary radiologist and an experienced veterinary radiographer, who assessed the appropriateness of the terminology used, the clarity of the question wording, and the alignment of each item with the study objectives. Following this review, a pilot study was conducted to confirm that participants understood the questions as intended and could complete the survey without difficulty.

Lack of knowledge and availability of optimization tools

Detector dose indicators are extremely valuable for the minimization of radiation doses in digital radiographic systems [30]. The American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) advocates for their use in radiography due to their numerous benefits as an optimization tool [31]. Use of detector dose indicators assists in ensuring that diagnostic image quality is achieved by providing immediate feedback on the suitability of the exposure factors used, enabling operators to achieve optimal images at the lowest dose possible [21, 32]. It is important to note that high or low detector dose indicator values do not necessarily indicate that an image is of insufficient quality or that a repeat exposure is required. They are intended to help guide adjustments for subsequent examinations. Detector dose indicators provide useful feedback for optimization and dose management in digital radiography.

The survey results revealed a widespread lack of understanding and underutilization of detector dose indicators among respondents. While it is acknowledged that not all systems may have an exposure indicator (e.g. film screen systems will not), it was notable that nearly half of the respondents (46%) were unaware of whether their system had an exposure indicator or not, and 40% lacked understanding of their function. This underutilization may stem from limited training and awareness, leading to a lack of knowledge on how to interpret detector dose indicators, made more difficult by the variability in how detector dose indicators are implemented across different X-ray systems. It is also possible that some participants did not fully understand the question regarding detector dose indicators if they had no prior knowledge of detector dose indicators, which could have contributed to the high proportion of uncertain responses. The majority of respondents (62%) were also unsure whether their X-ray system was equipped with AEC technology, indicating a lack of knowledge on this topic. These findings highlight the importance of standardization in both the design and labelling of dose indicators, as well as the need for consistent education to ensure that all practitioners can accurately interpret and apply this information in clinical practice.

The variability in perceived usefulness of detector dose indicators across clinical contexts highlights an important issue for veterinary radiography. While DDIs can be a useful tool and guide on whether an exposure was appropriate, they should always be used in the context of clinical judgement rather than as the sole basis for accepting or rejecting an exposure [31]. Differences in equipment type, system age, and practitioner familiarity can influence how DDIs are interpreted and applied, which could lead to inconsistent adjustments of exposure factors. This variability underscores the need for standardized education and training, as well as clearer guidance on the interpretation and use of DDIs. Improving understanding and consistent application of dose indicators has the potential to enhance image quality, support optimization of radiation dose, and reduce unnecessary repeat exposures, ultimately benefiting both patients and veterinary staff.

The optimization of radiographic systems and exposure factors has multiple important benefits in veterinary practice. Appropriate optimization can improve image quality, which can reduce the need for repeat imaging [2]. This not only saves time and resources but also minimizes the radiation exposure to both patients and staff. By reducing the number of unnecessary repeats, optimization contributes to efficient clinical workflows while supporting the principles of radiation protection [33]. This highlights the practical value of systematic optimization and the importance of training and awareness among veterinary practitioners to ensure consistent and effective application in clinical practice.

The One Health concept emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, advocating for a collaborative approach to shared challenges [34]. In radiography, advancements in human medicine, such as the ALARA principle and dose-optimization technologies, like AEC systems and detector dose indicators, have significantly improved patient safety while maintaining image quality [32, 35]. Adopting these technologies in veterinary radiography could similarly reduce radiation exposure without compromising diagnostic accuracy. However, the results of this survey show that their use in veterinary practice remains limited. Expanding their implementation through interdisciplinary collaboration could enhance dose optimization and imaging standards in veterinary radiography.

How veterinary professionals select exposure factors

The majority of respondents (72%) reported using technique charts to select exposure factors. The British Small Animal Veterinary Association’s (BSAVA) ‘Manual of Canine and Feline Radiography and Radiology’ [2] recommends that practices should develop their own exposure charts tailored to their specific equipment and imaging protocols to be used as guidelines, in order to promote consistent, high-quality images while minimizing the radiation dose to the animal. The BSAVA recommends that exposure charts are updated over time, by recording parameters which provide good image quality and adjusting those which can be improved. This survey did not investigate whether the exposure charts in use were updated or specific to the equipment in the practice; this would be an interesting area for follow-up research.

A further 31% (n = 34) of respondents used a system with preset exposure factors. Of these, 21% (n = 7) did not know when their preset exposure factors were last updated. This finding indicates a potential concern that staff could be using preset exposure factors which are outdated. Observational studies in human radiographic practice found that the lack of regular updates to preset factors could exacerbate the risk of administering outdated and potentially excessively high doses to patients, as the settings may not reflect newer advances in equipment capabilities or advances in radiation safety [11].

Only 25% of respondents reported frequently adjusting exposure factors, suggesting that most veterinary staff rely on standard settings. This may be due to lack of confidence, knowledge, or time constraints. Similar patterns have been observed in human radiography, where time pressures lead to reliance on preset exposures [36].

Participants were asked how often they would generally be required to repeat an image due to inadequate image quality resulting in a non-diagnostic image. Sixty percent (n = 61) indicated that this rarely occurs, suggesting that exposure factor errors infrequently lead to images deemed non-diagnostic in clinical practice. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the wide exposure latitude and post-processing capabilities of digital radiography may allow diagnostically acceptable images to be produced despite suboptimal exposure. In addition, variability in practitioner understanding of optimal image quality and of the radiographic appearances of under and over-exposure may influence whether an image is judged to be diagnostic or not.

In human radiography, reject analysis is routinely used as a quality assurance tool to monitor image quality, repeat rates, and reasons for rejection, supporting dose optimization [37]. Human studies show that exposure-related errors account for a relatively small proportion of rejected images compared with other factors, such as positioning errors [38, 39]. Reject analysis is not currently widely implemented in veterinary radiographic practice, limiting the ability to objectively quantify repeat rates or identify the underlying causes of image rejection. Wider usage of reject analysis in veterinary practice could provide valuable insight into the frequency and causes of image repetition, allowing for targeted training interventions, and improved exposure optimization and radiation protection for both patients and staff.

Factors related to confidence in adapting exposure factors

A positive correlation (P < .001) was identified between the frequency of exposure factor adaptation and confidence in doing so. However, it is not possible to establish causality in either direction with certainty—e.g. if those who are more confident adapt their exposures more often for this reason, or if adapting exposure regularly makes practitioners more confident. The suggestion that regular engagement in radiographic practice plays a key role in building confidence is further supported by the finding that respondents performing between 20 and 29 exposures per week reported significantly higher confidence levels (P = .003) than those with lighter workloads. Veterinary staff who frequently perform radiographic examinations are more likely to gain consistent, practical experience, which may allow them to refine their radiographic decision-making, improve their ability to appraise image quality, and develop a deeper understanding of their X-ray systems’ capabilities. Further exploration of this relationship could examine whether additional training aimed at building confidence in exposure factor adaptation increases the frequency of adaptations, or if frequent adaptations naturally lead to greater confidence over time.

The finding that respondents performing ~20 to 29 radiographic exposures per week reported higher self-rated knowledge of detector dose indicators than those with lower or higher workloads may suggest that a moderate, regular radiographic workload supports familiarity with system feedback and exposure optimization. However, the small number of respondents in the 20 to 29 exposures per week group (n = 7) limits the strength of this finding, and it should be interpreted cautiously.

Interestingly, a significant negative correlation (r = −0.319, P = .001) was identified between the participants’ years of experience and their confidence levels in exposure factor adaptation. Less experienced respondents reported higher levels of confidence than their more experienced counterparts. This likely reflects recent graduates’ greater familiarity with digital systems and exposure factor adaptation, acquired through contemporary veterinary education. This finding aligns with Skelly's [5] observation that digital radiography was absent in Irish veterinary practices in 2000. The subsequent widespread adoption of these systems prompted significant curriculum revisions, equipping recent graduates with knowledge and skills not previously available to more experienced staff. These curriculum updates were required by the accreditation standards of the Veterinary Council of Ireland, which requires that graduates ‘understand the contribution that imaging and other diagnostic techniques can make in achieving a diagnosis’ and ‘use basic imaging equipment and carry out an examination effectively as appropriate to the case, in accordance with good health and safety practice and current regulations’ [40].

CPD courses were the most common form of post-qualification veterinary radiography training identified, yet no significant correlation was found between additional training and confidence in exposure factor adaptation. Given the small number of participants who had undergone training (n = 23), this finding should be interpreted cautiously. However, the lack of impact suggests that existing CPD courses may not adequately cover exposure factor adaptation and dose optimization. The low participation rate also indicates a potential gap in accessible and relevant radiographic training for veterinary staff.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. A key limitation was the recruitment method, as the use of email invitations sent to practice-registered addresses made it impossible to determine exact response rates. Multiple staff members from the same clinics were invited to participate, in order to recruit as large and representative a population as possible. However, this may have introduced unintentional bias or duplication. Incomplete responses from some participants limited the depth of analysis for certain questions, reducing the comprehensiveness of the findings. As the survey was conducted anonymously, it was not possible to prevent an individual from submitting multiple responses.

Another limitation relates to the differences between radiographic systems. Detector dose indicators vary substantially between digital systems, and are not applicable in FSR systems and therefore the interpretation of the results relating to their application should take account of the variety of systems in use across the country. Similarly, optimal exposure factors are not the same across systems. For example, FSR and CR typically require higher radiation doses to achieve the same image quality as DR due to the higher detective quantum efficiency of DR systems. These differences mean that comparisons of exposure factor usage across different system types should be interpreted cautiously.

Furthermore, while this study provides valuable insights into the practice of small animal radiography in Ireland, it did not establish the impact of variations in exposure factors on image quality and radiation dose. Although the study reveals significant trends in how veterinary staff adapt exposure factors, it is unclear, from this study, how these practices impact outcomes in terms of image quality and dose reduction. More research is needed to explore the relationship between exposure factor variations and diagnostic image quality in the veterinary field. The authors are currently further investigating these aspects in a follow-up study. Despite these limitations, the study contributes valuable data that may inform future improvements in veterinary radiography practices.

Conclusion

This study provided insight into small animal radiographic practices in Ireland. The findings highlight the widespread adoption of digital radiography in recent decades. Despite the advantages of digital imaging, this study identifies a significant knowledge gap in the understanding and utilization of optimization tools such as detector dose indicators and AEC.

The results indicate a reliance on preestablished technique charts. While this aligns with best practice recommendations, a lack of regular updates to these charts could lead to suboptimal exposures. Furthermore, the limited frequency of exposure factor adaptation reported by respondents raises concerns about potential dose creep and unnecessary radiation exposure.

Future research should explore the impact of structured educational interventions on confidence and competence in exposure optimization. By adopting best practices from human radiography and fostering a culture of dose awareness, the veterinary profession can enhance image quality while minimizing radiation exposure, ultimately benefiting both veterinary patients and staff.
