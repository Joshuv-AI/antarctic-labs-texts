Thai pharmacogenomics database −2 (TPGxD‐2) sequel to TPGxD‐1, analyzing genetic variants in 26 non‐VIPGx genes within the Thai population
Authors: Shobana John, Sommon Klumsathian, Paravee Own‐eium, Angkana Charoenyingwattana, Jakris Eu‐ahsunthornwattana, Thanyachai Sura, Donniphat Dejsuphong, Piyamitr Sritara, Prin Vathesatogkit, Nartthawee Thongchompoo, Wiphaporn Thabthimthong, Nuttinee Teerakulkittipong, Wasun Chantratita, Chonlaphat Sukasem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNext‐generation sequencing (NGS) has transformed pharmacogenomics (PGx), enabling thorough profiling of pharmacogenes using computational methods and advancing personalized medicine. The Thai Pharmacogenomic Database‐2 (TPGxD‐2) analyzed 948 whole genome sequences, primarily from the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT) cohort. This study is an extension of the previous Thai Pharmacogenomic Database (TPGxD‐1) and specifically focused on 26 non‐very important pharmacogenes (VIPGx) genes. Variant calling was conducted using Sentieon (version 201808.08) following GATK's best workflow practices. We then annotated variant call format (VCF) files using Golden Helix VarSeq 2.5.0. Star allele analysis was performed with Stargazer v2.0.2, which called star alleles for 22 of 26 non‐VIPGx genes. The variant analysis revealed a total of 14,529 variants in 26 non‐VIPGx genes, with TBXAS1 had the highest number of variants (27%). Among the 14,529 variants, 2328 were novel (without rsID), with 87 identified as clinically relevant. We also found 56 known PGx variants among the known variants (n = 12,201), with UGT2B7 (19.64%), CYP1B1 (8.9%), SLCO2B1 (8.9%), and POR (8.9%) being the most common. We reported a high frequency of intermediate metabolizers (IMs) in CYP2F1 (34.6%) and CYP4A11 (8.6%), and a high frequency of decreased functional alleles in POR (53.9%) and SLCO1B3 (34.9%) genes. This study enhances our understanding of pharmacogenomic profiling of 26 non‐VIPGx genes of notable clinical importance in the Thai population. However, further validation with additional computational and reference genotyping methods is necessary, and novel alleles identified in this study should undergo further orthogonal validation.

INTRODUCTION

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) has reshaped pharmacogenomics; extending beyond targeted sequencing to encompass whole‐exome sequencing (WES) and whole‐genome sequencing (WGS). This advancement facilitates comprehensive profiling of pharmacogenes, offering insights into rare variants and novel drug‐gene associations, thereby enhancing personalized medicine approaches in drug therapy. 1 While it has not become standard practice in clinical settings, advancements in computational techniques, and bioinformatics algorithms have made the adoption of sequencing methods more accessible. 2 Many PGx‐specific bioinformatics tools have significantly improved the calling of star alleles; however, some tools are better with certain genes. For example, in the case of CYP2D6, among Astrolabe v0.8.7.0, Stargazer v1.07, and Aldy v2.2.3, researchers found Aldy v2.2.3 to be better at calling structural variants. 3 Stargazer outperforms the other tools in rare homozygous allele phasing due to its in‐built supplementary algorithm and the diplotype calling was most often correct with PharmVar for Stargazer. 4 Likewise, some tools can call star alleles for a large number of genes (e.g., Stargazer for 58 genes), whereas others are specific to a single gene (e.g., Cyrius for CYP2D6).

PharmGKB serves as a comprehensive database, compiling clinically relevant pharmacogenes, including 69 VIPGx, over 100 gene–drug interactions, and more than 799 annotated drugs. It consolidates data from multiple pharmacogenomics (PGx) references, such as the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG) and the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC). PGx variants are predominantly characterized using the star (*) allele nomenclature. 5 Our previous research (TPGxD‐1), the analysis of 63 very important pharmacogenes (VIPGx) revealed 85,566 variants, including 13,532 novel discoveries. Notably, we identified 464 known PGx variants and 275 clinically relevant novel variants. The phenotypic and genotype predictions of 25 VIPGx demonstrated a varied metabolic profile for the Thai population. 6

This follow‐up study analyzed star alleles and variants in 26 non‐VIPGx genes (VIPGx) that are known to have associations with drug responses. These genes harbor at least one variant known to be associated with drug metabolism, efficacy, or toxicity. Genes such as NAT1, POR, SLCO2B1, UGT2B7, and IFNL3 have been associated with various drug classes, including antimetabolites, anticancer drugs, statins, benzodiazepines, fexofenadine, HMG‐CoA reductase inhibitors, and interferon. Thailand, at the forefront of pharmacogenomics research in Southeast Asia, has made significant strides in this field, supported by initiatives such as the Thailand Center of Excellence for Life Sciences (TCELS) and Genomics Thailand Initiatives (GTI). Leveraging these resources, the TPGxD‐2 study seeks to enhance our understanding of pharmacogenomic variability in the Thai population.

METHODOLOGY

WGS data retrieval

The pilot project for the Thailand Reference Genome Panel (Protocol No. ID11‐60‐12) collected samples from 948 individuals across various regions of Thailand. The study participants are the employees of the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT) from various socioeconomic backgrounds, regions, and health profiles in Thailand. The study participants represented a wide range of occupations, reflecting the diversity within the EGAT workforce. This study included participants who decided to volunteer their DNA for WGS analysis.

Variant calling and filtering in 26 non‐VIP PGx genes

The 948 samples were sequenced using Illumina and HiSeq X technologies. The analysis commenced by aligning WGS reads to the reference genome sequence GRCh38, utilizing the Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Identification and marking of duplicate reads were executed using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) 4.0.0's “Mark Duplicates” tool. Subsequently, base quality score recalibration (BQSR) was performed by generating a recalibration data table with the “BaseRecalibrator” tool. 7 For variant calling, Sentieon (version 201808.08) was employed, resulting in the generation of raw variant call format (VCF) files. During this process, variants with a missing rate >5%, samples with a missing rate >10%, or duplicate samples were removed.

The raw VCF files obtained were used for PGx‐specific analysis. Our focus was on extracting variants of interest from specific genes, particularly 26 non‐VIPGx for which we can call alleles and diplotypes using Stargazer v2.0.2, including CYP17A1, CYP19A1, CYP1A1, CYP1B1, CYP26A1, CYP2F1, CYP2R1, CYP2S1, CYP2W1, CYP3A43, CYP4A11, CYP4A22, CYP4B1, GSTM1, IFNL3, NAT1, POR, SLC15A2, SLC22A2, SLCO1B3, SLCO2B1, TBXAS1, UGT2B15, UGT2B17, UGT2B7, XPC. A BED file containing the genomic coordinates of these 26 non‐VIPGx pharmacogenes was downloaded from the UCSC genome database. 8 Subsequently, the BCFtool (version 1.18) facilitated the extraction of these non‐VIPGx genes from raw VCF files, resulting in files named “non‐VIPGx‐VCF files.” These files underwent comprehensive annotation and cataloging through Golden Helix VarSeq 2.5.0 (build 14706‐608a25a286).

Star allele analysis

The Stargazer v2.0.2 (https://stargazer.gs.washington.edu/stargazerweb/index.html) was used for the precise calling of star alleles from a dataset comprising 948 WGS. Usually, the Stargazer v2.0.2 pipeline retrieves variant information from VCF files, then performs statistical haplotype phasing using Beagle, and thereafter assigns genes' (58 genes) diplotypes based on haplotype matches with a reference star allele table. Utilizing the “non‐VIPGx‐VCF files” extracted in the previous step as the input, Stargazer v2.0.2 was used to call star from 24 non‐VIPGx genes, including CYP17A1, CYP19A1, CYP1A1, CYP1B1, CYP2F1, CYP2R1, CYP2S1, CYP2W1, CYP3A43, CYP4A11, CYP4A22, CYP4B1, GSTM1, IFNL3, NAT1, POR, SLC15A2, SLC22A2, SLCO1B3, SLCO2B1, TBXAS1, UGT2B15, UGT2B7, and XPC. The output genotype‐calls.tsv files, which include alleles, diplotypes, phenotypes, activity scores, and variant information, were used for further data analysis. The information for each allele identified in Stargazer v2.0.2 was cross‐checked with data from PharmGKB. Stargazer v2.0.2 did not include rsID details, but these were retrieved from the PharmGKB database using other variant information. The alternative algorithm Aldy 4.0 was used to call star alleles for genes CYP2R1 and IFNL3 for which Stargazer v2.0.2 did not call star alleles. Aldy 4.0 (https://github.com/0xTCG/aldy) is another star allele caller that calls genotypes of many highly polymorphic pharmacogenes (35 genes, 7 pending tests in real dataset) and reports them in a phased star allele nomenclature. It can also call the copy number of a given pharmacogenes and genotype of each copy present in the sample. In this study, a BAM file was used as input, and hg38 (GRCh38) was used as the reference genome to run Aldy 4.0. The output file included a sample, gene, major and minor copy, allele location, coverage effect, and dbSNP code, but it did not predict phenotypes.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ramathibodi Hospital Mahidol University faculty review board and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report, CIOMS guidelines, and ICH‐GCP (COA. MURA2017/759/Ref.646).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

The study population primarily consisted of the EGAT cohort, a long‐term cohort study of employees from the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (71.6%). The remaining samples were from the Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University (28.2%). Age and sex information were missing for 38.9% and 38.6% of the study population, respectively. Based on the available data, the average age was 57.1 ± 7.4 years, with a male preponderance.

Variant analysis

Number of variants identified in 26 non‐VIPGx genes

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of genetic variation across 26 non‐VIPGx genes. A total of 14,529 variants were identified, of which 12,201 were known variants (with rsID) and 2328 (without rsID) were novel variants. Among the 14,529 variants, TBXAS1 had the highest number of variants, with 3802, followed by POR (n = 1473) and SLC15A2 (n = 1027) (Figure 1). The most variants were observed in the intronic region (n = 12,968), and 381 missense variants were identified (Table 2). These variants were listed gene‐wise along with their allele frequency (AF) in Thai (this study) and other populations in Table S2. Among 2, 328 novel variants, 87 were clinically relevant and their effects on coding and protein sequences are listed in Table S3.

Table 2 lists genes and their associated drugs, highlighting how mutations in these genes can significantly impact cancer treatment responses or lead to various levels of toxicity. For instance, variants in CYP17A1 can influence the metabolism of abiraterone, affecting its effectiveness or toxicity in prostate cancer. Similarly, genetic variations in genes, such as CYP19A1 (fluorouracil, statins), CYP1B1 (antimetabolites), CYP2F1 (imatinib), CYP2S1 (oxaliplatin), CYP2W1 (colorectal cancer), NAT1 (mesalazine, thioguanine), CYP4B1 (docetaxel), and XPC (cisplatin) can also impact drug responses. Furthermore, variations in CYP4A11, SLC15A2, TBXAS1, and CYP3A43 affect cardiovascular drugs. For instance, CYP4A11 mutations may alter the metabolism of amiloride and spironolactone used for hypertension and heart failure, potentially changing their therapeutic effects or toxicity. Variations in SLC15A2 (warfarin), TBXAS1 (aspirin and other antiplatelet agents), and CYP3A43 (ticagrelor) can influence drug efficacy, toxicity, and treatment outcomes.

Some genetic variations influence multiple drug classes; for example, variations in POR can alter the metabolism of benzodiazepines, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, statins, and immunosuppressants, impacting treatments for anxiety disorders, cancer, and cardiovascular disease. Additionally, variations in SLCO1B3 (statins, anticancer agents), SLCO2B1 (antihistamines, antifungals, and statins), UGT2B15 (thrombopoietin receptor agonists, antidiabetic agents, and benzodiazepines), UGT2B17 (ibuprofen), and UGT2B7 (statins, opioids, antiretrovirals) may influence the responses to multiple classes of drugs.

Number of known PGx variants identified in 19 non‐VIPGx genes

Over all, 56 known PGx variants were identified across all genes. Among these, UGT2B7 stands out with 11 (22%) identified variants, followed by CYP1B1 and POR with 10% variants each. Regarding the sequence ontology, missense variants are the most prevalent, constituting 32% of all variants, followed by intron variants at 25% and synonymous variants at 18%. Additionally, 11% of the variants were annotated as 3_prime_UTR and 5_prime_UTR. In terms of clinical annotation (CA), 70% of the variants have clinical annotation data, while all the variants have variant annotation (VA) data available in the PharmGKB database. Analyzing the variants based on their phenotypic implications, 46% were associated with efficacy alone, 27% with metabolism/PK alone, and 13% with toxicity alone. Notably, 14% of the variants are associated with multiple phenotypic outcomes simultaneously, indicating potential multifaceted clinical implications (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of 948 WGS datasets to explore the genetic variants, frequencies, phenotypes, and functional alleles of 26 non‐VIPGx genes, providing valuable insights into pharmacogenomics in the Thai population. By including participants from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, regions, and health profiles within Thailand (specifically from the EGAT workforce), this study effectively reflects the diversity of the Thai population.

Diplotype calling

Stargazerv2.0.2 systematically identifies genetic variations in a wide range of genes, up to 58 in total, including the 26 non‐VIP genes, outperforming its contemporaries (Cyrius = 1 gene (https://github.com/Illumina/Cyrius?tab=readme‐ov‐file), Aldy = 35 genes (https://github.com/0xTCG/aldy?tab=readme‐ov‐file#gene‐support), and StellarPGx = 21 genes, https://github.com/SBIMB/StellarPGx), and employs statistical haplotype phasing for enhanced accuracy in variant calling. Additionally, Stargazer v2.0.2 provides output that includes phenotypic profiles, a feature not commonly found in other contemporary bioinformatic tools. Furthermore, it accepts VCF files as input for analyses. 9 Hence, we have chosen Stargazer as our bioinformatics platform for our study. However, Stargazer v2.0.2 did not call star alleles for four genes in this study, so instead of leaving them uncalled, we used another tool, Aldy4.0, which called star alleles for 2 of these 4 genes (CYP2R1 and IFNL3).

Genes associated with cancer treatment responses

We have identified known PGx variants for many genes involved in cancer treatment. In the case of POR, previous studies have linked the genotype TT of rs1057868C>T, which we identified in 33% of the study population, to improved survival rates of sunitinib 10 and reduced dose‐adjusted trough concentrations of rivaroxaban, 11 tacrolimus, 12 and cyclosporine 13 in individuals with gastrointestinal stromal tumors, atrial fibrillation, and kidney transplantation, respectively. Our current study's variant analysis did not report any known PGx variants for the CYP19A1 gene. However, star analysis found three different alleles, such as *2 (rs2236722A>G), *3 (rs28757184G>A), and *4 (rs700519G>A) for this gene. The PharmGKB database reported the rs2236722 and rs700519 variants. Previous studies have found that allele *2, G (rs2236722) in Neoplasm Metastasis patients has a decreased response to capecitabine and fluorouracil, 14 while variant rs700519, prevalent in 21% of the population in this study, does not increase the risk of all‐cause mortality when treated with antiandrogens. 15 We found one variant, rs305968G>A, in the CYP2F1 variant analysis. An earlier study found that the AA genotype of this variant increases the imatinib toxicity risk in gastrointestinal stromal tumor patients. 16 Star allele analysis of this gene revealed multiple variants, including *2 (rs3833221G>GC) and *4 (rs58285195T>C and rs305974G>A), but none were found in the PharmGKB database. Therefore, further evidence is required to validate their correlation with PGx.

In the case of SLCO1B3, none of the known PGx variants were reported in variant analysis, but the star allele analysis reported the *S1 (rs7311358G>A) haplotype of this gene at a frequency of 75.3%. Allele A of this variant is linked to reduced docetaxel clearance in individuals with nasopharyngeal neoplasms, 17 decreased carboplatin and paclitaxel toxicity in ovarian neoplasms, 18 increased mycophenolic acid AUC6–12 in kidney transplant patients when treated with mycophenolate mofetil, 19 and increased overall survival likelihood in lung transplant patients compared with genotypes AG and GG. 20 This study identified rs743572A>G as a known PGx variant for CYP17A1. However, star allele analysis did not call this variant; instead, it called rs367833709, a missense variant in one individual, heterozygous. Previous studies found that genotypes of AG and GG in rs743572 are not associated with a response to abiraterone in prostatic neoplasm. 21 The frequency of this variant is higher than that of EAs and this frequency was similar to that of the earlier studies. 22 In the case of CYP4B1, allele T of rs4646487C>T increases the risk of docetaxel and thalidomide toxicity. 23 Star allele analysis of this study found three different alleles with *3 (rs4646487 C>T) and *2 (rs1329419166GAT>G) at a higher frequency of 25.8% and 19.8%, respectively. However, in EAs, rs4646487 C>T was found to be less than 18% Thais.

Concerning SLC15A2, rs2257212C>T, rs1143671C>T, and rs1143672G>A, known PGx variants were identified in the variant analysis of our study. In star allele analysis, *2 is the high‐frequency allele, occurring in 59.2% of individuals. It is associated with all the above SNPs. Earlier studies reported these variants' association with PGx. For example, decreased progression‐free survival treated with sorafenib for the genotype CC of rs2257212C>T, 24 decreased dose of warfarin for the genotype TT of variant rs1143671C>T 25 and genotype AA of rs1143672G>A GG. 26 As we compare the frequency of these variants with other EAs, the frequency is lower in Thai (59%) than in EAs (71%). Regarding SLC22A2, only one known PGx variant (rs316019A>C) was reported. It has implications for the metabolic/pharmacokinetic (Meta/PK) of many drugs. For instance, genotype AC decreases the risk of nephrotoxicity caused by cisplatin. 27 However, allele C is not associated with the likelihood of cisplatin‐induced ototoxicity. Star allele analysis revealed higher frequencies of alleles *2, *3, and *4 in 13.4%, 22.2%, and 6% of individuals linked to SNPs rs624249C>A, rs316003T>C, and rs8177518A>G, respectively. Genotypes CT + TT of rs316003T>C show increased survival when treated with platinum compounds. 28

Three different variants, such as rs2228000G>A, rs2228001G>T, and rs1870134G/C were reported for XPC. In star allele analysis, *S1 (rs2228001G>T) and *S2 (rs2228000G>A) alleles were reported. Genotypes AA + AG of rs2228000G>A are not associated with a risk of anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia when treated with platinum compounds in people with carcinoma non‐small cell lung cancer as compared with genotype GG. 29 Genotypes GG and GT of rs2228001 G>T are associated with an increased risk of drug toxicity when treated with cisplatin in people with urinary bladder neoplasms as compared with genotype TT. 30 The rs2228001 G>T variant (69%) was identified at a higher frequency than other EAs (61%).

Genes associated with cardiovascular treatment responses

Among five PGx variants identified in the SLCO2B1 gene, the *S464F (rs2306168C>T) haplotype was reported in 17.35% of the population, which has an association with decreased rosuvastatin uptake in HEK293T cells 31 and decreased plasma concentration‐time curve area in healthy individuals treated with fexofenadine. 32 Overall, our findings highlight the importance of these genetic variants for pharmacogenomic outcomes and contribute to a deeper understanding of pharmacogenomics in the Thai population. Genotypes of AG and GG of the rs1126742A>G variant of CYP4A11 are linked to lower HDL‐C levels in women compared with genotype AA. 33 Genotype GG is associated with a decreased response to amiloride or spironolactone in people with hypertension as compared with genotypes AA and AG. 34 This variant frequency was higher in the Thai population (31%) than the other EAs (22%). The *S1 allele (rs1126742A>G) was reported in 31% of the study population, however, Stargazer categorized only *S1/*S1 as IMs.

Genes associated with multiple drug classes responses

Allele *2 of the CYP1B1 A (rs1056827C>A) variant that was identified in this study in 15% of the population has an association with reducing the risperidone response in schizophrenic patients as compared with allele C. 17 Likewise, genotype CC of this variant is linked to reduced cyclophosphamide, epirubicin, and fluorouracil responses in breast cancer patients compared with genotypes CG and GG. 35 In the case of CYP1A1, two different variants were annotated in variant analysis, such as rs1048943T>C (*2C) and rs2606345C>A. Literature has revealed that allele C is associated with an increased likelihood of leukopenia and mucositis when treated with sunitinib 36 and an increased response to nilotinib. 37 However, in the case of cardiovascular drugs, allele C is not associated with an increased dose of warfarin or an increased risk of clopidogrel resistance as compared with allele T. 38 For the variant rs2606345C>A, previous studies showed that allele A is associated with an increased risk of seizures when treated with aromatic AEDs. 39 Genotype CC of this variant is associated with a decreased risk of iron overload when treated with deferasirox in children with Thalassemia. 40

Genes associated with antipsychotics/AEDs drug responses

Our variant analysis revealed several genes with a notable number of known pharmacogenomic (PGx) variants that are related to antipsychotic drugs. For instance, this study identified 11 UGT2B7 variants, and two alleles such as *2 and *3 in the star allele analysis. The *2 allele (rs7439366C>T) was reported in 30% of the study population and earlier studies reported its association with many drug responses and toxicities. For example, genotype TT of this variant is linked to increased lamotrigine concentrations in epilepsy patients 41 and an increased risk of CNS depression or death in breastfeeding neonates. Variant analysis of CYP3A43 revealed two variants, such as rs472660G>A and rs12535293G>A. Earlier studies showed that AA and AG of rs472660G>A were associated with increased clearance of olanzapine and response to antipsychotics in schizophrenia patients. 42 However, allele A of rs12535293G>A was not associated with olanzapine clearance in schizophrenia patients. 16 Star allele analysis revealed numerous variants, with *2B (rs680055TA>T, rs60416653C>G) and *1B haplotypes being the most frequent, but none were found in the pharmGKB database or other PGx databases.

Genes were reported with other drug classes

In the case of CYP2S1, no known PGx variant was reported in the variant analysis. However, in star allele analysis, *3 allele (rs34971233C>T) was reported. However, for this gene, there is no clinical or VA available in the PharmGKB database. Likewise, for the CYP2W1 variant analysis, the study did not report any known PGx variants. However, Start analysis found three different alleles, such as *2 (rs3735684G>A), *4 (rs78873069G>A), and *6 (rs3808348C>T), which were reported. None of these variants have been reported in the PharmGKB database and have any known PGx‐related associations. Regarding IFNL3, the variant analysis of this study identified three different variants, such as rs11881222A>G, rs28416813C>G, and rs8103142T>C. Literature has reported that allele G of rs11881222, 43 rs28416813, 44 and allele C of rs8103142 23 variants are associated with increased responses to peginterferon alfa‐2a and ribavirin in people with hepatitis C. 45 Star allele analysis by Stargazer v2.0 did not call any alleles for this gene in this study; however, Aldy4.0 called *C/*C at a higher frequency. Genotypes AG and GG of the rs13253389 variant of NAT1 are associated with increased concentrations of cotinine in children. 46 Star allele analysis of this gene showed that *3 (rs15561A>C) and *10 (rs1057126A>T) represent 1.58% and 46.8%, respectively, of the population studied. A study showed that NAT1 *10 is associated with decreased concentrations of mesalazine and thioguanine in children with colitis, ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease, and inflammatory bowel diseases as compared with NAT1 *4 + *3. 26

In the case of TBXAS1, variant analysis reported one variant, rs6962291T>A; in addition, star allele analysis reported *2, rs6138G>A, *3, rs5768C>A, *7 rs8192868G>A, and *9 rs41311778G>A, each at a frequency of 0.1%. Genotype AA of rs6962291T>A is associated with a decreased risk of intolerance when treated with aspirin in people with asthma as compared with genotype TT. 47 Three different known PGx variants were reported in variant analysis, such as rs1902023A>C, rs3100G>A, and rs4148269T>G for UGT2B15. In star allele analysis, *2 (rs1902023A>C), *4 (rs4148269T>G), and *5 have frequencies of 29.7%, 11.8% and 17.0%, respectively. Allele G of rs4148269T>G is associated with an increased response to glibenclamide in people, as compared with allele T. 48 The frequency of these two variants, such as rs3100G>A and rs4148269T>G, is higher in Thai than the EAs at 17%. Regarding UGT2B17, rs6817882C>T was reported in variant analysis. Allele C is associated with increased expression of UGT2B17 in human livers, compared with allele T for this variant.

Although this study effectively reflects the diversity of the Thai population, the participants were not specifically recruited for the pharmacogenomics research. Additionally, the data should be validated using other computational and reference genotyping methods, and any novel alleles identified in this study need further orthogonal confirmation.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of pharmacogenomic variability in the Thai population, focusing on 26 non‐VIPGx genes. While 19 genes were found to harbor 56 known PGx variants, 87 clinically relevant novel variants were identified. The variant analysis revealed a wide range of PGx‐related mutations in genes, such as UGT2B7, CYP1B1, SLCO2B1, and POR can significantly impact drug responses, particularly in cancer treatment. Additionally, certain genes, such as POR, exhibit variations that affect multiple drug classes. Star allele analyses provided insights into allele frequencies and diplotypes, highlighting the prevalence of certain functional variants. Overall, this study contributes valuable information to the field of pharmacogenomics, emphasizing the need for continued research to fully elucidate the pharmacogenomic implications of non‐VIPGx genes in the Thai population.
