Eco-epidemiology of Hepatozoon sp. in wild rodents in protected and rural areas in north-central Chile
Authors: Patricio D Carrera-Játiva, Camilo Tomckowiack, Constanza Mena, Claudio Verdugo, Gerardo Acosta-Jamett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman-induced environmental changes can modify the transmission patterns of arthropod-borne infections across multiple ecological scales. This study evaluated the influence of habitat alteration on Hepatozoon sp. infection in wild rodents (Phyllotis darwini Waterhouse, 1837) inhabiting the semi-arid Mediterranean Coquimbo region of north-central Chile. Rodents were captured and sampled at two sites with contrasting levels of anthropogenic disturbance–a protected area (Bosque Fray Jorge National Park) and an altered rural site (El Tangue Farm)–across seven consecutive seasons from austral spring 2021 to autumn 2023. Hepatozoon sp. infection was assessed in rodent blood samples using conventional PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene and phylogenetic analyses. Statistical models were conducted to evaluate environmental and host-related factors. Overall, 144 of 446 (32.3%) blood samples were positive for Hepatozoon sp. by PCR. The likelihood of Hepatozoon sp. infection varied seasonally and was higher in rodents from the altered site and in individuals with higher body mass index. These findings highlight the potential effects of anthropogenic disturbance and seasonality on the Hepatozoon infection status on wildlife populations. By characterizing patterns of Hepatozoon sp. infection in small mammals inhabiting anthropogenically altered environments, this study contributes to a better understanding of arthropod-borne infections under human-induced pressures, with implications for biodiversity conservation.Keywords: Hepatozoon, Rodentia, Parasitism, Arthropod-borne, Apicomplexa, Mammals

Introduction

Human-induced environmental changes are recognized as major drivers of infectious diseases and pathogen emergence (Daszak et al., 2000; Fèvre et al., 2006). Given the impact of human activities (e.g. climate change, urbanization, deforestation, and husbandry) on natural habitats, arthropod-borne diseases represent significant global threats to animal and human health, as local transmission patterns can be altered across multiple ecological and epidemiological scales in altered environments (Kock, 2005).

The genus Hepatoozoon Miller, 1908 (Eucoccidiorida: Hepatozoidae) comprises apicomplexan parasites reported from a wide range of host species (Miller, 1908; Smith, 1996). Hepatozoon spp. exhibit heteroxenous life cycles, in which hematophagous ectoparasitic arthropods–such as ticks, fleas, mites, and sandflies–may act as definitive hosts, where the sexual reproduction and sporogony occur (Taylor et al., 2016; Watkins et al., 2006). Vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles, and birds, serve as intermediate hosts, in which merogony and gametogony take place (Smith, 1996). Vertebrate intermediate hosts become infected primarily through ingestion of infected ectoparasitic arthropods, or by predation on prey species harboring tissue cysts (meronts or cystozoites) that act as transport hosts (Johnson et al., 2008; Smith, 1996). In addition, vertical (transplacental) transmission of Hepatozoon canis has been documented in dogs (Schäfer et al., 2022). Infection with Hepatozoon spp. can result in a broad range of clinical manifestations, including anemia, diarrhea, and pulmonary and hematological alterations. Disease severity depends on parasitaemia level, the extent of parasite multiplication in tissues, and presence of coinfections (e.g., bacterial or viral agents) (Mundim et al., 2008).

The dynamics of Hepatozoon spp. infection in mammals may vary according to intrinsic host traits and environmental drivers that modify host-parasite interactions. Evidence suggests that individual-level factors, such as sex and body weight, play a pivotal role in patterns of parasite infection (Morand, 2015). Likewise, differences in physiological stress, hormones, and foraging behaviors can result in sex-biased prevalence or intensity (Biard et al., 2015; Morand, 2015; Zuk and McKean, 1996). Additionally, the spatial context of the infection is critical, as the type of site, in particular those characterized by anthropogenic alteration, can lead to increased arthropod density (i.e., definitive hosts) and therefore higher Hepatozoon sp. transmission rates compared to more natural habitats (Bhaskar and Gupta, 2020; Carrera-Játiva and Acosta Jamett, 2023). These variations may also be modulated by seasonality, which can regulate arthropod density and/or host immune competence, leading to seasonal fluctuations in Hepatozoon sp. prevalence (Vezzani et al., 2017).

The north-central Chile exhibits high level of endemism and is recognized as a global biodiversity hotspot (Armesto et al., 2007; Mardones and Scherson, 2023; Morlon et al., 2011). In recent decades, however, human activities such as agriculture, forestry, and urbanization, alongside global climate change, have increasingly fragmented the landscape, compromising its ecological integrity and biodiversity (Armesto et al., 2010; Braun et al., 2021; Pavez et al., 2010). Darwin's leaf-eared mouse Phyllotis darwini (Watkins et al., 2006) (Rodentia: Cricetidae), is an endemic, diet-generalist species inhabiting the semi-arid Mediterranean landscape of the Coquimbo region, Chile (Meserve et al., 2016). Other small mammals found in the semi-arid Coquimbo region include Octodon degus (Molina, 1782), Abrothrix olivacea (Watkins et al., 2006), Chelemys megalonyx (Watkins et al., 2006), Octodon lunatus (Osgood, 1943), Abrocoma bennetti (Watkins et al., 2006), Abrothrix longipilis (Watkins et al., 2006), and Oligoryzomys longicaudatus (Bennett, 1832; Beltrami, 2021). Cycling population densities of P. darwini have been documented in protected areas, such as the Bosque Fray Jorge National Park, and rural altered areas in the Coquimbo region (Beltrami, 2021; Meserve et al., 2016). Several pathogens have been reported in P. darwini in Chile, including Hepatozoon sp. (Santodomingo et al., 2022), Cryptosporidium parvum and other gastrointestinal parasites (Carrera-Játiva et al., 2024, 2025), along with potential rodent Hepatozoon sp. definitive arthropod hosts such as ticks (Ixodidae), and trombiculid and macronyssid mites (Muñoz-Leal et al., 2019; Thomas et al., 2026).

The present study aimed to assess the effects of habitat quality (i.e., protected vs. rural, season), and individual-level factors (i.e., body condition) on the prevalence of Hepatozoon sp. infection in populations of P. darwini inhabiting the semiarid Coquimbo region in Chile. We hypothesized a higher likelihood of Hepatozoon spp. infection in P. darwini from rural areas, due to the potential deleterious effects of habitat alteration on host diversity and behavior (Keesing et al., 2006). We also expected a higher infection prevalence in individuals with greater body mass, given the increased feeding rates of arthropods on larger hosts (Morand, 2015). Finally, we predicted higher infection prevalence during summer, reflecting increased contact rates between definitive and intermediate hosts during warmer months (Vezzani et al., 2017). By characterizing patterns of Hepatozoon sp. infection in small mammals inhabiting anthropogenically altered environments, this study contributes to a better understanding of arthropod-borne infections under human-induced pressures, with implications for biodiversity conservation.

Materials and methods

Study site and design

A longitudinal cohort study was carried out to study Hepatozoon sp. infection in P. darwini populations inhabiting two sites with different levels of human-induced impact (i.e., rural altered and protected natural) in the Coquimbo Region, north-central Chile, in seven consecutive seasons from spring 2021 to autumn 2023 (Fig. 1). The protected site was located within the Bosque Fray Jorge National Park (BFJNP) (−30.642157°, −71.654061°), which was established in 1941 and provide natural habitats for a variety of endemic vegetation and animals (Jaksic et al., 2004; Squeo et al., 2016). The rural altered site consisted of a private agricultural land “El Tangue Farm” (ETF) (−30.357992°, −71.527046°), in which several economic activities have been carried out since 1920, including livestock production, and agriculture (Sociedad Agrícola y Ganadera El Tangue Ltda, 2022). The semi-arid Mediterranean climate in the Coquimbo Region is featured by an average annual temperature of 14.7 °C with an average daily variation of 6 °C (Novoa and López, 2001). The wetter seasons comprise autumn (March – June) and winter (June – September), and dry climate is often found during spring (September – November) and summer (December – March) (Armesto et al., 2007; Novoa and López, 2001).

Map of the Coquimbo region in Chile showing the two sites with different kinds of anthropogenic impacts (i.e., Bosque Fray Jorge National Park - BFPNP [in green]; El Tangue Farm [in blue]) in which Darwin's leaf-eared mice (Phyllotis darwini) were sampled. In each site, 4 grids were allocated, and 200 capture points were studied per grid. (UTM projection. Datum WGS84, Zone 19J).

During each season at each site, four trapping grids (150 × 135 m; 2 ha) were established, each containing 200 capture points arranged following established methodologies (Fig. 1) (Beltrami, 2021; Romairone et al., 2018; Royle et al., 2018). The distance between grids within each site exceeded 1 km to minimize recaptures, consistent with home range of P. darwini (∼1 km2) (Muñoz-Pedreros and Gill, 2009).

Rodent capture and sampling

Phyllotis darwini were trapped using Sherman-like live traps (300 x 100 × 110 mm) placed at the capture points. Traps were baited with oat flakes and vanilla extract, set under vegetation before sunset (18:00), and checked the following morning (07:00) during four nights. Total trapping effort ranged between 600 and 800 trap-night per grid and sampling season. P. darwini individuals were identified using taxonomic keys (Muñoz-Pedreros and Gill, 2009). Captured rodents were sedated (Ketamine 0.044 mg/g bodyweight + Xylazine 0.006 mg/g bodyweight; IM), ear-tagged, morphologically measured (body length, anogenital distance; Uberman® digital caliper, precision 0.01 mm), and weighed (Pesamatic Newton Series®, Model EJ1500, A&D Weighing, San Jose, CA, USA; ±0.1 g). Blood samples were obtained by caudal vein puncture, collected using heparinized capillary tubes, and stored in EDTA-containing microtubes until further analyses. Following full recovery from sedation, rodents were released at the respective capture points.

Body weight and measurements were used to classify rodents by sex (males and females) and age class (adults and juveniles). Males were identified by a longer anogenital distance, and adults were defined based on body weight thresholds (males ≥40 g; females ≥35 g) (Lima et al., 2001; Muñoz-Pedreros and Gill, 2009). In cases where sex was not distinguished, categorization was not added. Body condition was assessed using the scaled mass index (SMI), calculated from body length and weight and estimated separately for each age class (Peig and Green, 2009).

Molecular analyses of blood samples

DNA was isolated from rodent blood samples using either TRIzol™ reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, 2016) or gSYNC DNA Extraction kit (Geneaid, New Taipei, Taiwan) according to the manufacturers' instructions. To verify successful DNA extraction, all samples were screened using an internal control PCR targeting the gapdh (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) gene (Birkenheuer et al., 2003). Samples yielding amplicons of the expected size (i.e., ∼400 bp) were screened for Hepatozoon spp. by amplifying a partial fragment of the 18S ribosomal RNA gene using conventional PCR protocols (Ujvari et al., 2004). Subsequently, samples with an amplified product of the expected size were subjected to further PCR to amplify a different region of 18SrRNA of Hepatozoon spp., following published protocols (Perkins and Keller, 2001) (ESM 1: Table S1).

Each PCR reaction was performed using the GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega, Madison, USA), using a final volume of 25 μl, that contained 12.5 μl of the master mix, 0.5 μl of each primer (10 μM each), 3 μl of cDNA template, and 8.5 μl of ultra-pure water. PCR amplification consisted of 40 cycles and were performed using an Axygen® MaxyGene II Thermal Cycler (Corning Incorporated Life Sciences, NY 14831 USA). Positive (Hepatozoon sp. from a naturally infected lizard Liolaemus sp.; Accession number PX257451) and negative controls (nuclease-free water) were included in each PCR run. DNA fragments of the expected size were excised and purified using an E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Georgia 30071, U.S.A.) and submitted for bi-directional sequencing in an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) at the AUSTRAL-omics Institute, Universidad Austral de Chile (Valdivia, Chile).

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences were edited, assembled, and concatenated using Unipro UGENE v49.1 software (Okonechnikov et al., 2012), obtaining a final 18S rRNA sequence of ∼1300 bp for sample, as carried out elsewhere (de Souza et al., 2017). Genus-level identity of consensus sequences were confirmed in the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool BLAST (Altschul, S.F. et al., 1990). A dataset was built using relevant reference sequences retrieved from GenBank and aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004) implemented in MEGA 12 V.11.0.13 (Kumar et al., 2024). The best fitting model for the DNA phylogeny was selected for the alignment based on the Bayesian information criterion. The phylogenetic relationships among sequences were inferred using the maximum likelihood (ML) method under the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano model with discrete Gamma distribution and invariant sites (Hasegawa et al., 1985), as implemented in MEGA 12. Eimeria sevilletensis was used as the outgroup.

Statistical analyses

Hepatozoon infection status was defined as rodent samples that showed amplification of the 18S segment during the first round of PCR screening. The prevalence of Hepatoozoon spp. infection in P. darwini was calculated as the proportion of individuals tested positive by the total number of individuals examined (Bush et al., 1997). Prevalence estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated following the Clopper-Pearson method (Clopper and Pearson, 1934).

To assess factors associated with Hepatozoon infection status, generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were initially evaluated, with rodent ID included as a random effect to account for repeated captures across inter-seasonal sampling periods. However, generalized linear models (GLMs) showed a better fit, as indicated by lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values compared with GLMMs (GLM: 510.6 < GLMM: 512.6) (Burnham and Anderson, 2022), so they were selected for further analyses. Accordingly, GLMs with binomial error distribution and logit link function were built to model Hepatozoon infection status as the response variable. Predictor variables included site (protected vs rural), season, and body condition (SMI). No interaction terms were considered for this study. Intra-seasonal recaptures were not considered in the analyses. To avoid collinearity, age class was excluded as a predictor. Sex was also not included because of the high number of subadults, which compromises sex identification. All possible model combinations including the four predictor variables were evaluated (ESM 1: Table S2). Model selection was based on AIC corrected for small sample size (AICc), retaining models with ΔAICc ≤2 (Hurvich and Tsai, 1989). Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke's pseudo R2 (Nagelkerke, 1991) and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (Paul et al., 2013). Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for significant variables in the most strongly supported model.

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (R Development CoreTeam, 2013) using RStudio (POSIT, 2024). The packages lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), randomforest (Garge et al., 2013), and MASS (Ripley et al., 2013) were used to build the models. Model selection was performed using the dredge() function from the MuMIn package (Barton, 2018), which generated and ranked all possible combinations of predictor variables in the global models based on their AICc values. The fmsb (Nakazawa and Nakazawa, 2024) and lme4 were used to estimate Nagelkerke's pseudo-R2. Odds ratios, incidence rate ratios, along with their corresponding 95% CI were estimated using the broom.mixed library (Bolker et al., 2024). The map of the study area was constructed using QGIS 3.20 (QGIS, 2024).

Hepatozoon sp. detection and molecular characterization

A total of 144 out of 446 P. darwini blood samples tested positive for Hepatozoon sp, showing an overall prevalence of 32.3% (95% CI = 27.9 – 36.8%). The prevalence of Hepatozoon sp. infection in Phyllotis darwini according to host- and environment-related factors is shown in Table 1 (ESM 1: Table S2).

Phylogenetic analysis included three amplicon samples that exhibited the highest band intensity in the agarose gel. Maximum likelihood analysis showed that sequences of Hepatozoon sp. detected in P. darwini clustered together within the Clade I of Hepatozoon spp. from rodents, reptiles, and birds from various regions worldwide (Fig. 2). Particularly, sequences from P. darwini in the present study clustered in a monophyletic group with isolates of Hepatozoon sp. from native rodents and ticks from Chile and Brazil (Fig. 2). Nucleotide sequences from P. darwini in this study were submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers PX107354, PX107225, and PX113220.

Eco-epidemiological associations

The only best-fitting model for Hepatozoon sp. infection status (i.e., PCR positive or negative for Hepatozoon spp.) in P. darwini (ΔAICc ≤2) included season, SMI, and site as predictor variables [Nagelkerke's pseudo R2 = 0.20; Hosmer-Lemeshow test ─ X2 = 12.2, p = 0.14] (ESM 1: Table S2). A higher likelihood of Hepatozoon sp. infection was observed in winter 2022 (β = 1.15, OR = 1.24 – 8.36, p = 0.01), summer 2023 (β = 1.43, OR = 1.77 – 10.4, p < 0.01), and autumn 2023 (β = 1.50, OR = 2.19 – 10.1, p < 0.01), compared with spring 2022. In addition, the likelihood of Hepatozoon sp. infection increased significantly with SMI (β = 0.02, OR = 1.01 – 1.04, p < 0.05). Rodents inhabiting the protected site showed significantly lower likelihood of infection (β = −0.56, OR = 0.35–0.91, p < 0.05) (ESM 1: Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, Hepatozoon sp. infection was assessed in P. darwini inhabiting protected and rural areas in north-central Chile during 2021–2023, and the effect of host-related factors, site type, and seasonality were evaluated. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first two-year longitudinal study on Hepatozoon spp. infection in wild endemic rodents in the region.

The overall prevalence of Hepatozoon sp. in P. darwini (32.3%) was lower than that reported in wild and synanthropic rodents (82.4%; 61/74) in Valdivia, in southern Chile (Alabí et al., 2021), and in wild rodents (55.2%; 37/67) in southeastern Brazil (Demoner et al., 2016). Conversely, it was higher than the prevalence reported in small mammals in midwestern (2.06%; 3/145) and in southern and southeastern Brazil (24.91%; 72/289) (dos Anjos Pacheco et al., 2025; Freitas et al., 2025). Variations in Hepatozoon sp. prevalence may be associated with particular biotic and abiotic conditions in the semi-arid region in north central Chile.

Nucleotide sequences of Hepatozoon sp. obtained from P. darwini were clustered with Hepatozoon sp. isolates from rodents (Akodon sp.) in Chile and Brazil. Global phylogenetic analyses have shown that Hepatozoon spp. tend to infect vertebrate hosts belonging to the same taxonomic family or closely related species (Matamoros-Suárez et al., 2025). Furthermore, Santodomingo et al. (2022) suggested a possible pattern of coevolution between Hepatozoon spp. and native and endemic rodents in Chile. Accordingly, P. darwini inhabiting both protected and anthropogenically altered areas may be infected with a host-specific Hepatozoon species. However, further studies using molecular markers with higher interspecies resolution, such as the mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (COI) and 28S rRNA genes, are needed to achieve species-level identification of Hepatozoon in Chilean endemic rodents (Matamoros-Suárez et al., 2025).

With regards to individual host factors, the SMI of P. darwini was positively associated with the likelihood of Hepatozoon sp. infection. These findings align with previous research on P. darwini in the same area, where prevalence rates of gastrointestinal parasites (i.e., of Schistosoma spp. and coccidia) and gastrointestinal parasite richness were positively associated with SMI (Carrera-Játiva et al., 2025). It is likely that P. darwini with a larger body mass may feed more frequently on infected arthropods acting as definitive hosts for Hepatozoon sp. (Morand, 2015).

Hepatozoon sp. prevalence was higher in rodents living in the altered rural areas. In a study examining the effect of anthropogenic disturbance on Hepatozoon infection in lizards (Hemidactylus flaviviridis; Calotes versicolor) in India, prevalence rates were also higher in individuals inhabiting more polluted areas, and these results were attributed to higher stress conditions and host densities (Bhaskar and Gupta, 2020). In relation to the stress levels, a side research in P. darwini showed no differences in hair corticosterone concentrations in protected and anthropized areas (Veloso-Frías et al., 2024). As Hepatozoon infection in mammals often occurs via ingestion of infected ectoparasites, the higher rates of Hepatozoon sp. infection in rodents inhabiting altered areas in the present study may be related to changes in rodent behaviour, such as diet or grooming (Taylor et al., 2016; Watkins et al., 2006). It is plausible that rodents in the altered area were eating more infected ectoparasites than those in the protected area. Alternatively, reduced host diversity (both rodents and ticks) in altered areas could have enhanced Hepatozoon transmission through a lower dilution effect (Keesing et al., 2006). Long-term studies are needed to better understand how habitat alteration influences arthropod-borne infection dynamics in wildlife, with implications for biodiversity conservation.

Hepatozoon sp. infection in P. darwini also varied seasonally, with higher prevalence rates recorded in winter 2022, summer 2023, and autumn 2023 than in the other assessed seasons. Higher prevalence rates can be attributed to the different seasonal activity of ectoparasites, which act as definitive hosts, during warmer months. This was evidenced in H. canis in Argentina in a 10-year study, showing maximum rates in summer (Vezzani et al., 2017). It is possible that Hepatozoon sp. transmission in rodents in north-central Chile intensified during summer 2023 due to higher ectoparasite densities, with effects extended into autumn 2023. The higher rates of Hepatozoon sp. in P. darwini during winter of 2022 may be associated with the abnormal rainfall and relative humidity (%) in that year, which could have intensified the parasite transmission patterns (Carrera-Játiva et al., 2025).

There are several limitations to this study. First, the study was conducted at only two site types, one protected and one rural altered, which constrains the generalizability of our findings across the broader semi-arid Mediterranean landscape of north-central Chile. Although both sites represent contrasting levels of anthropogenic disturbance and have been extensively used as ecological reference systems in the region, the inclusion of additional sites along a continuous gradient of habitat alteration would have strengthened the robustness and representativeness of the eco-epidemiological inferences obtained. Also, the identity of the arthropod definitive hosts responsible for Hepatozoon sp. transmission in P. darwini could not be confirmed, as ectoparasites were not systematically collected or screened. While ticks, fleas, and mites are plausible definitive hosts based on the known biology of Hepatozoon spp. in rodents (Taylor et al., 2016), their differential abundance and community composition across the two sites remain uncharacterized, limiting our capacity to attribute differences in infection prevalence to specific arthropod-host dynamics. Also, given that the molecular approach relied on a single genomic marker (the 18S rRNA gene), species-level resolution of the detected Hepatozoon lineages was not achievable. Although the use of two primer sets targeting different regions of the 18S rRNA gene enhanced detection sensitivity and allowed phylogenetic placement within Clade I, the discrimination of distinct Hepatozoon species would require markers with greater interspecific resolution, such as the cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (COI) or 28S rRNA genes, which were not included in the present research (Matamoros-Suárez et al., 2025). Finally, only the amplicon samples with the highest band intensity in the agarose gel were sequenced, which included three samples shown in the phylogenetic tree. As molecular identification was not performed for all PCR-positive Hepatozoon sp. amplicons, future studies should sequence all positive samples to provide a more detailed evaluation of parasite diversity and confirmation of positives. Despite these limitations, the longitudinal design spanning seven consecutive seasons, the use of validated molecular protocols with appropriate positive and negative controls, the standardized trapping methodology across sites, and the integration of ecological, host-level, and phylogenetic analyses collectively provide a robust framework for understanding the eco-epidemiology of Hepatozoon sp. infection in wild rodents under anthropogenic pressures in semi-arid Mediterranean Chile.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that the likelihood of Hepatozoon sp. infection in wild P. darwini rodents in north-central Chile varied seasonally and was higher at the altered sites and in individuals with higher body mass index. These results reinforce concerns regarding the anthropogenic impact on the health of wildlife populations. More long-term research is needed to determine patterns and temporal changes in arthropod-borne infection dynamics in wildlife inhabiting altered landscapes, with implications for both biodiversity conservation and human health.
