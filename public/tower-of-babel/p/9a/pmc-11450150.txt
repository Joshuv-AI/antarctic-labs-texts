Leveraging programmed cell death signature to predict clinical outcome and immunotherapy benefits in postoperative bladder cancer
Authors: Yifan Wang, Qi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bladder cancer is the fourth most common malignancy in men with poor prognosis. Programmed cell death (PCD) exerts crucial functions in many biological processes and immunotherapy responses of cancers. Cell death signature (CDS) is novel gene signature comprehensively considering the characteristics of 15 patterns of programmed cell death, which could affect the prognosis and immunotherapy benefits of cancer patients. Integrative machine learning procedure including 10 algorithms was conducted to construct a prognostic CDS using TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276 datasets. Immunophenoscore, intratumor heterogeneity (ITH), tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score and five immunotherapy cohorts were used to evaluate the predictive value of CDS in immunotherapy response. The prognostic CDS constructed by StepCox[backward] + Ridge algorithms was regarded as the optimal prognostic model. The CDS had a stable and powerful performance in predicting overall survival of bladder cancer patients with the AUCs at 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year ROC of 0.740, 0.763 and 0.820 in TCGA cohort. Moreover, CDS score acted as an independent risk factor for overall survival rate of bladder cancer patients. Low CDS score had a higher abundance of immuno-activated cells, higher PD1&CTLA4 immunophenoscore, higher TMB score, lower TIDE score, lower immune escape score, lower ITH score, lower cancer-related hallmarks score in bladder cancer. The CDS score was higher in non-responders in pan-cancer patients receiving immunotherapy. Our study constructed a novel prognostic CDS, which could serve as an indicator for predicting the prognosis in postoperative bladder cancer cases and immunotherapy benefits in pan-cancer. Low CDS score indicated a better prognosis and immunotherapy benefits.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the fourth most common malignancy in men1. An estimated of 573,278 cases are initially diagnosed with bladder cancer and 212,536 patients died of this disease worldwide in 20202. Despite some principal risk factors, including tobacco smoking3, have been identified for bladder cancer, the mechanisms mediating its development and progression have not been elucidated. Currently, treatment approaches for bladder cancer include surgery, chemotherapy and immunotherapy. However, the clinical outcome of postoperative bladder cancer cases remains unsatisfactory, resulting in the 5-year overall survival rates being flat for 30 years4. High recurrence and resistance to chemotherapy are major challenges in the management of the prognosis of bladder cancer patients5. At present, the TNM staging system is the major approach for predicting the prognosis of bladder cancer patients. However, the clinical outcome of bladder cancer patients with the same stage is also very different. Moreover, there is a lack of effective biomarker for the prognosis of bladder cancer patients in clinical practice. Immunotherapy offers more life-extending possibilities for bladder cancer patients with unresectable tumors6. However, immunotherapy also has certain limitations, which are mainly reflected in the uneven efficacy, the possibility of false progression of tumor, the possibility of immune-related adverse events, and the need for individualized treatment7. Due to the heterogeneity of bladder cancer, the response to immunotherapy is vastly different from patients to patients8,9, needing further investigation to identify novel biomarkers to predict the immunotherapy benefits.

Cell death could be clustered into accident cell death and programmed cell death (PCD). PCD refers to an active extinction process of cells after receiving a certain signal or being stimulated by certain factors, thus maintaining the stability of the internal environment10. As far as we know, PCD can further be divided into 15 subtypes (pyroptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, immunologic cell death, entotic cell death genes, cuproptosis, parthanatos, lysosome-dependent cell death, intrinsic apoptosis, extrinsic apoptosis, necrosis, anoikis, apoptosis-like morphology and necrosis-like morphology)11–13. Pyroptosis, an innate immune response being triggered by the activation of inflammasomes, plays a vital role in many biological processes14. Ferroptosis can be seen in radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and tumor immunotherapy, suggesting targeting ferroptosis as a potential strategy to overcome the drug resistance in the treatment of cancer patients15. Previous study highlighted the vital role of autophagy in tumor progression and the prognosis of patients16. Anoikis occurs when cells detach from the correct extracellular matrix, thereby disrupting integrin connections17. Cuproptosis, a novel form of copper-dependent PCD, can regulate tumor proliferation and metastasis, thus affecting immune response18. These programmed cell death patterns affect the proliferation and death of normal tissue cells or tumor cells by destructing cytoplasm or cell membrane integrity, producing toxicity, or activating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation19,20. At present, limited studies have fully clarified role of these PCD patterns in the prognosis, immune infiltration and therapeutic response of bladder cancer. By exploring the gene expression pattern of programmed cell death and clarifying how they affecting progression and prognosis of bladder cancer patients, we may use them to guide personalized therapy decisions.

In our study, we leveraged diverse PCD patterns to construct a 13 gene-based cell death signature (CDS) for bladder cancer using machine learning. The role of CDS in predicting the prognosis and immunotherapy benefits of postoperative bladder cancer patients was evaluated using TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276 cohort. Our results may provide more evidences about the vital role of PCD in bladder cancer.

Materials and methods

Datasets sources

Workflow of our study was shown in Fig. 1. PCD-related genes (PRGs) of these 15 PCD patterns were obtained from MSigDB (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, review articles, and manual collection of gene sets from Genecards (https://www.genecards.org/)21,22 (Supplementary Table 1). Bulk RNA-seq data of bladder cancer patients were downloaded from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/repository, TCGA BLCA (n = 396)) and GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, GSE13507 (n = 165), GSE31684 (n = 90), GSE32984 (n = 223) and GSE48276 (n = 73)). Inclusion criteria included: (1) pathological diagnosis of bladder cancer, (2) completed prognostic information. Exclusion criteria included: (1) those cases were followed up for less than one month or died within one month after surgery, (2) accompanied by other malignancies. Two bulk RNA-seq datasets, in which patients were treated with PD-1 or CTLA4 inhibitors, were downloaded from IMvigor210 dataset (n = 298, bladder cancer) and GSE91061 dataset (n = 98, skin cutaneous melanoma, SKCM). Three single cell datasets, in which patients were treated with PD-1 or CTLA4 inhibitors, were downloaded from GSE145281 dataset (n = 10, bladder cancer), GSE23813 dataset (n = 4, squamous cell carcinoma, SCC) and GSE120575 dataset (n = 48, SKCM).

Development and evaluation of a prognostic CDS

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in bladder cancer were identified with “limma” package with |LogFC| ≥ 1.5 as the cutoff. These DEGs were submitted to perform univariate cox analysis, which could generate potential prognostic biomarkers among PRGs (p < 0.05). Based on these potential prognostic biomarkers, we performed integrative machine learning analysis procedure to screen the genes used to construct the prognostic CDS. A total of 10 machine learning algorithms were used, including random survival forest (RSF), elastic network (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), supervised principal components (SuperPC), generalized boosted regression modelling (GBM), and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM). The prognostic CDS generation procedure was as follows: (1)A total of 89 algorithm combinations were performed on the prognostic signature to fit prediction models based on the leave-one-out cross-validation framework in the TCGA dataset; (2)All models were detected in four GEO cohorts (GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276); (3)For each model, the Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated across all TCGA and GEO datasets, and the model with the highest average C-index was considered the optimal. Similar machine learning algorithms could be seen in previous studies23–25. The parameter tuning details about the R scripts in our study is available on the Github website (https://github.com/Zaoqu-Liu/IRLS). The best cutoff value for separating bladder cancer cases into low and high CDS score (risk score) groups were determined by the “surv_cutpoint” function within the R package “survminer”. Using “rms” package, we then drew the C-index curves of clinical characters and 52 prognostic signatures (Supplementary Table 2), which had been developed for bladder cancer. To identify the risk factor for the clinical outcome of bladder cancer cases, we then conducted univariate and multivariate cox analysis. A nomogram was developed with “nomogramEx” R package to predict 1-year, 3-year and 5-year overall survival rate by combining the results of the CDS score with clinical attributes. The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was assessed by calibration plot.

Immune infiltration, drug sensitivity and gene set enrichment analyses

The abundance of immune cells of bladder cancer cases were evaluated by “immunedeconv” R package. “Immunedeconv” combined seven state-of-the-art algorithms (CIBERSORT, MCPcounter, QUANTISEQ, XCELL, CIBERSORT-ABS, TIMER and EPIC) to evaluate the immune cell infiltration26. The “estimate” R package was used to calculate the immune score and ESTIMATE score of bladder cancer patients27. The gene set score involved in immune cell, immune related functions, cancer related hallmarks and immune escape and surveillance of bladder cancer cases were calculated with single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using “GSVA” package. Cancer-related hallmark gene set and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) gene set was downloaded from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)28–30. Several tools were used to evaluate the role of CDS in predicting the immunotherapy benefits. Intratumor heterogeneity (ITH) score of bladder cancer was evaluated by DEPTH2 algorithm31. Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score of bladder cancer patients were obtained from TIDE (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu)32. And the immunophenoscore (IPS) of bladder cancer patients were obtained from the Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA, https://tcia.at/home)33. From TCGA database, we collected the tumor mutation burden (TMB) score of bladder cancer patients. The “oncoPredict” R package was used to calculate the IC50 value of drugs of bladder cancer cases based on the data of Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/).

scRNA-seq analysis

The Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub 2 (TISCH2, http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/) is a platform providing comprehensive characterization of gene expression in the tumor microenvironment across multiple cancer types34. The annotation of cell types in three immunotherapy cohorts (GSE145281, GSE23813 and GSE120575) were downloaded from TISCH2. The “Gene signature exploration” module of TISCH2 was used to explore the difference of CDS score in different immune cells of responders and non-responders. Responders were defined as those patients with partial response (PR) and complete response (CR). Non-responders were defined as patients with progressive disease (PD) and stable disease (SD).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.2.1). The difference between continuous variables was evaluated with Wilcoxon rank-sum test or student T test. Pearson’s or Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to analyze the correlations between two continuous variables. The two-sided log-rank test was used to test the difference in different Kaplan-Meier survival curve.

Results

Identification of potential prognostic biomarkers in bladder cancer

A total of 5084 DEGs were obtained in bladder cancer with |LogFC| ≥ 1.5 and p-value < 0.05 as the cutoff (Supplementary Fig. 1A), including 696 PRGs (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Among these 696 PRGs, a total of 35 genes were significantly associated with overall survival rate of bladder cancer patients (Supplementary Fig. 1C, p < 0.05), including H2BC5, BID, SLC25A10, H2AC8, STYXL1, TRAF4, ADPRS, H2BC8, SLC25A1, SEZ6L2, IGF2BP3, TUBA1C, TRIM24, TCN1, LBP, CADM3, SFRP1, CDO1, HGF, SETD7, IRAK3, STEAP4, CD22, C4A, VIM, TUBB6, MFN2.

FOXO3, YAP1, TCL1A. ACO1, CR2, TGFB2, LPL, MSN.

Integrative machine learning algorithms developed a prognostic CDS

We then submitted 35 potential prognostic biomarkers to an integrative machine learning procedure including 10 methods mentioned above, with which we constructed an optimal prognostic CDS. We obtained 89 kinds of prognostic models using integrative machine learning procedure and the C-index of each prognostic model in training and testing cohort was shown in Fig. 2A. As a result, the prognostic CDS developed by StepCox[backward] + Ridge was suggested as the optimal prognostic CDS with a highest average C-index of 0.71 (Fig. 2A). This optimal prognostic CDS was constructed using 13 PRGs and the CDS score (risk score) of each bladder cancer patient could be calculated with the formula: CDS score = 0.0356 × SFRP1exp + 0.0681 × CDO1exp + (-0.1015) × HGFexp + 0.0512 × SETD7exp + 0.03987 × IRAK3exp + (-0.08925) × STEAP4exp + 0.05907 × CD22exp + 0.01598 × C4Aexp + 0.06287 × VIMexp + 0.1054 × TUBB6exp + (-0.0826) × MFN2exp + 0.0679 × FOXO3exp + 0.1503 × YAP1exp. We then divided bladder cancer patients into high and low CDS score group based on the best cut-off. As shown in Fig. 2B and F, bladder cancer patients with high CDS score had a poor overall survival rate in TCGA (p < 0.001), GSE13507 (p < 0.001), GSE31684 (p < 0.001), GSE32984 (p < 0.001) and GSE48276 (p = 0.008) datasets, with the AUCs at 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year of 0.740, 0.763 and 0.820 in TCGA cohort; 0.695, 0.678 and 0.736 in GSE13507 cohort; 0.645, 0.688 and 0.703 in GSE31684 cohort; 0.748, 0.756 and 0.750 in GSE32984 cohort, 0.726, 0.663 and 0.661 in GSE48276 cohort, respectively (Fig. 2B and F).

Evaluation of the performance of CDS

As shown in Fig. 3A and B, univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis suggested CDS score as an independent risk factor for the overall survival rate for bladder cancer patients in TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276 cohorts (all p < 0.05). Further analysis revealed that the C-index of CDS for predicting the clinical outcome of bladder cancer was higher than that of age, gender, tumor grade, clinical stage, pT stage, pN stage, and pM stage in TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, and GSE48276 dataset (Fig. 3C). As many prognostic signatures have been developed for bladder cancer, we then randomly collected 52 published prognostic signatures that have been constructed in bladder cancer (Supplementary Table 2). To compare the predictive value of CDS with other prognostic signatures, we then calculated their C-index and the data showed that the C-index of our CDS was higher than most of these prognostic signatures in TCGA cohort (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the AUCs at 3-year and 5-year ROC curve of our CDS in TCGA cohort were 0.740 and 0.763, which were higher than some programmed cell death pattern-based gene signatures, including necroptosis-related miRNA signature for clear cell renal carcinoma35 and necroptosis and pyroptosis related signature for clear cell renal carcinoma36.

The correlation between CDS score and tumor immune microenvironment

A significant negative correlation between CDS score and the abundance of most types of immune cells (Fig. 4A, p < 0.05). Specifically, CDS score was negatively correlated with the abundance of immune-activated cells, including macrophage M1, CD8+ T cells, NK cells and dendritic cell (Fig. 4B and E, all p < 0.05). Similarly, ssGSEA analysis showed that low CDS score indicated a higher score of aDCs, B cells, CD8+ T, NK cells, Tfh, and TIL (Fig. 4F, all p < 0.05). Moreover, we found that bladder cancer with low CDS score had a higher gene set score involved in APC co-stimulation, CCR, cytolytic activity, HLA, inflammation-mromoting, T cell costimulation, and type II IFN response (Fig. 4G, all p < 0.05). Low CDS score indicated a higher stromal score, immune score and ESTIMAE score in bladder cancer (Fig. 4H and J, all p < 0.001).

CDS score acted as an indicator for predicting immunotherapy benefits in bladder cancer

Higher expression of immune checkpoints indicated a higher likelihood of receiving benefits from immunotherapy based on immune checkpoints blockade (ICB)37. The current showed that bladder cancer with low CDS score had a higher level of many immune checkpoints, including PD-1, CTLA4 and TIGHT (Fig. 5A, all p < 0.05). Higher expression of HLA-related gene suggested wider range of antigen presentation, increasing the likelihood of presenting more immunogenic antigens38. The data suggested that bladder cancer patients with low CDS score had a higher expression of HLA-related genes in bladder cancer (Fig. 5B, all p < 0.05). The tumor immune landscape can be divided into wound healing (C1), IFN-g dominant (C2), inflammatory (C3), lymphocyte depleted (C4), immunologically quiet (C5) and TGF-b dominant (C6) subtypes39. We found that bladder cancer patients with high CDS score had a higher proportion of C4 (Fig. 5C, p = 0.001). IPS was a predictor for immunotherapy response and higher IPS suggested a better response to immunotherapy33. The data revealed a higher PD1 IPS, higher CTLA4 IPS and higher PD1&CLTA4 IPS in low CDS score group (Fig. 5D, all p < 0.05). Higher TMB score indicated a better response to immunotherapy40. Bladder cancer patients with low CDS score had a higher TMB score (Fig. 5E, p = 0.023). Low TIDE and low ITH score suggested a less likelihood of immune escape and a better response to immunotherapy31,32. As shown in Fig. 5F and I, bladder cancer patients with high CDS score had a higher TIDE score, T cell dysfunction score, immune escape score, and ITH score (all p < 005). These evidences demonstrated that bladder cancer patients with low CDS score may have a better immunotherapy benefit. We then evaluated the value of CDS in predicting immunotherapy benefits using two immunotherapy-related datasets. In IMigor210 (bladder cancer) cohort, the CDS score was higher in non-responders (Fig. 5J, p < 0.05). The response rate in low CDS group was higher than that in high CDS group (Fig. 5J, p < 0.05). Moreover, high CDS score patients had a poor overall survival rate than that in low CDS score patients (Fig. 5J, p = 0.01). Similar results were obtained in GSE91061 (SKCM) cohort (Fig. 5K, all p < 0.05). In order to evaluate whether CDS score could act as an indicator for predicting immunotherapy benefits in pan-cancer, we also calculate the CDS score of responders and non-responders using three single cell RNA datasets from different types of cancers. The t-SNE map of cell types identified in bladder cancer, SCC and SKCM samples were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A,C. In bladder cancer, the CDS score of B cell, conventional CD4 T cells (CD4Tn), Central memory CD8 T cells (CD8Tcm), effective CD8 T cells (CD8Teff), conventional CD8 T cells (CD8Tn), M1-like macrophage (M1), M2-like macrophage (M2) and monocyte were higher in non-responders (Fig. 6A, all p < 0.001). In SCC, the CDS score of CD4Tn, CD8Teff, exhausted CD8 T Cell (CD8Tex), Tfh, Th1, Th17, proliferation T cell (Tprolif) and regulatory T cells (Treg) were higher in non-responders (Fig. 6B, all p < 0.001). Moreover, the CDS score of B cells, CD4Tn, CD8Tcm, M1, monocyte, plasma, Th1 were higher in non-responders in SKCM (Fig. 6C, all p < 0.05). These evidences showed that CDS score may act as an indicator for predicting immunotherapy benefits in pan-cancer. We also explored the predictive value of CDS the drug sensitivity of bladder cancer patients. As shown in Fig. 7A and B, the IC50 value of camptothecin, cisplatin, docetaxel, gemcitabine, epirubicin, dasatinib, foretinib, nilotinib, sapitinib, and osimertinib were lower in low CDS score group (all p < 0.05), suggesting that bladder cancer patients with low CDS score may have a better sensitivity to chemotherapy and targeted therapy.

The correlation between Cancer related hallmarks and CDS score in bladder cancer

To explore why the clinical outcome, immune infiltration, and therapy response of different CDS score group was significantly different in bladder cancer, we then performed functional enrichment analysis. As shown in Fig. 8A, bladder cancer patients with high CDS score had a higher enrichment score involved in cell cycle, DNA replication, and pathways in cancers. However, low CDS score indicated a higher potential in activating the pathways correlated with chemokine signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 8B). Further analysis showed that the gene set score correlated with angiogenesis, DNA repair, EMT signaling, G2M checkpoint, glycolysis, hypoxia, IL2-STAT5 signaling, IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, mTORC1 signaling, NOTCH signaling, P13K-AKT-mTOR signaling and P53 pathway were higher in bladder cancer patients with high CDS score (Fig. 8C, all p < 0.05).

Construction of a predictive nomogram in bladder cancer

Based on age, gender, tumor grade, clinical stage, and CDS score, we then constructed a survival prediction nomogram in TCGA, GSE13508, GSE31684 and GSE32894 dataset (Fig. 9A and D), with which clinicians may predict the overall survival rate of bladder cancer patients. The result showed that the calibration curves were very close to the ideal prediction curve in all these datasets (Fig. 9A and D), suggesting that the current nomogram had a relatively good predictive value for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival rates of bladder cancer patients.

Discussion

Our study constructed an integrative machine learning procedure including 10 methods to develop a prognostic CDS for bladder cancer. The prognostic CDS had a stable and powerful performance in predicting the overall survival of bladder cancer with the AUCs at 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year of 0.740, 0.763 and 0.820 in TCGA cohort. Moreover, CDS score could act as an independent risk factor for the overall survival rate for bladder cancer patients in TCGA, GSE13507, GSE31684, GSE32984 and GSE48276 cohorts. Further analysis revealed that bladder cancer patients with low CDS score had a higher abundance of immuno-activated cells, higher PD1&CTLA4 immunophenoscore, higher TMB score, lower TIDE score, lower immune escape score, lower ITH score, indicating that bladder cancer patients with low CDS score may have better immunotherapy benefits.

The prognostic CDS was constructed with 13 PRGs, including SFRP1, CDO1, HGF, SETD7, IRAK3, STEAP4, CD22, C4A, VIM, TUBB6, MFN2, FOXO3, YAP1. Some studies have shown that these PRGs were involved in the development of bladder cancer or other types of cancer. Upregulation of SFRP1 lead to decreased viability and migration in bladder cancer cells41. CDO1 acted as a tumor suppressor gene silenced by promoter methylation in multiple human cancers42. RAB27B-HGF-c-Myc positive feedback loop between bladder cancer cells and fibroblasts played a vital role in lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis43. SETD7 acted a prognostic biomarker for bladder cancer and accelerated the progression and immune escape44. MFN2 could suppress tumor cell proliferation and invasion via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in bladder cancer45. Another study showed that high TUBB6 expression was correlated with poor prognosis in bladder cancer46. Activation of FOXO3 played a vital role in polyphyllin I-induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in bladder cancer47. YAP1 activation was involved in the progression and response to immunotherapy of bladder cancer48. Our study showed that YAP1 was upregulated in bladder cancer and knockdown of YAP1 obviously suppressed tumor cell proliferation.

Immunotherapy offers more life-extending possibilities for bladder cancer patients with unresectable tumors6. Due to the heterogeneity of bladder cancer, the response to immunotherapy is vastly different from patients to patients8. At present, limited biomarker could be used to predict the immunotherapy benefit of bladder cancer. In our study, several scores were used to evaluate the predictive value of CDS in immunotherapy response of bladder cancer. Our results showed that bladder cancer with low CDS score had a higher level of many immune checkpoints, higher expression of HLA-related genes, higher PD1&CLTA4 IPS, higher TMB score, lower TIDE score, lower T cell dysfunction score, lower immune escape score, and lower ITH score. Higher expression of HLA-related gene suggested wider range of antigen presentation, increasing the likelihood of presenting more immunogenic antigens38. IPS was a predictor for immunotherapy response and higher IPS suggested a better response to immunotherapy33. Higher TMB score indicated a better response to immunotherapy40. Low TIDE and ITH score suggested a less likelihood of immune escape and a better response to immunotherapy31,32. Moreover, the CDS score was significant higher is no-responders in patients treated with PD1 or CTLA4 inhibitors. Therefore, CDS may act as an indicator for immunotherapy response and bladder cancer patients with low CDS score may benefit more from immunotherapy.

We also performed functional enrichment analysis to clarify why the clinical outcome, immune infiltration and immunotherapy response in bladder cancer patients with different CDS score were significantly different. As a result, the gene set score correlated with angiogenesis, DNA repair, EMT signaling, G2M checkpoint, glycolysis, hypoxia, IL2-STAT5 signaling, IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, mTORC1 signaling, NOTCH signaling, P13K-AKT-mTOR signaling and P53 pathway were higher in bladder cancer patients with high CDS score. Increasing evidences showed that angiogenesis disorders exert vital functions in the development and progression of bladder cancer49. Another study found that TEN/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway acts as central regulator of aerobic glycolysis and regulated bladder cancer metabolic switch and cell proliferation50. High NOTCH promote tumor progression and indicated poor prognosis in bladder cancer51. Therefore, these cancer-related hallmarks may be more active in bladder cancer with high CDS score. Resulting in a poor prognosis.

This investigation developed a CDS for bladder cancer using publicly available online databases. Additional in vivo and in vitro validation studies would yield more thorough understanding aimed at improving the performing of CDS in predicting the clinical outcome of bladder cancer. CDS may benefit more bladder patients if multicenter clinical studies and sample examinations could be performed. Some limitations could be found in our study. All analyses are performed at the RNA level and do not represent results at the protein level. It would be better to verify our results using in-house clinical cohort. Moreover, the underlying mechanisms and pathways involved in different patterns of PCD and their functions in bladder cancer require further exploration.
