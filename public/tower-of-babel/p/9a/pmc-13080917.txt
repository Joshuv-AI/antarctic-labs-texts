A Cardio-Obstetric Breakthrough Beyond Routine Pregnancy Care: Unmasking Hodgkin’s Lymphoma
Authors: Afifa Sabahat Baig, Sameen Jawed, Sumiya Gurmani, Sabha Bhatti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultidisciplinary care during pregnancy is essential to ensure safe maternal and fetal outcomes, particularly when managing serious conditions such as Hodgkin’s lymphoma.Case SummaryA 24-year-old pregnant woman presented with dyspnea and neck swelling. Echocardiography revealed cardiac tamponade, managed by pericardiocentesis. Histopathology confirmed Hodgkin’s lymphoma. Multidisciplinary care and third-trimester ABVD (adriamycin, bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine) chemotherapy led to clinical recovery and delivery of a healthy newborn. Postpartum positron emission tomography/computed tomography scan demonstrated a significant disease regression.DiscussionCardiac tamponade as an initial manifestation of Hodgkin’s lymphoma is rare. This case underscores the need to evaluate unexplained pericardial effusion in pregnancy for possible malignancy and highlights that early diagnosis, drainage, and timely chemotherapy are key to prevent instability and achieving recovery.Take-Home MessagesUnexplained pericardial effusion in pregnancy should prompt evaluation for underlying malignancy including lymphomas.

History of Presentation

A 24-year-old woman G3P1 at 26th week of pregnancy presented to a cardio-obstetric screening clinic for her first antenatal visit with complaints of persistent cough and low-grade fever for 7 months. She was previously treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. Over the preceding few months, she developed progressive shortness of breath and an enlarging neck mass; however, she had never been evaluated before. At presentation, she was unable to lie supine and was tachycardic and tachypneic with muffled heart sounds. Based on the initial screened point-of-care ultrasound findings and clinical presentation, the patient was admitted to a cardiac care facility for further diagnostic workup.Take-Home Messages•Pericardial tamponade in pregnancy is a life-threatening presentation that demands immediate diagnostic evaluation.•In rare cases, it may be the initial manifestation of occult malignancy, requiring timely oncologic therapy and coordinated obstetric planning to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

Past Medical History

The patient had no prior history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, autoimmune conditions, or malignancy. She has been diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis 6 months prior to presentation, based on a high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scan that revealed bilateral multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the anterior, middle, and posterior compartments with central necrosis consistent with granulomatous inflammation like tuberculosis. She received standard antitubercular therapy, and a biopsy was recommended; however, the patient did not adhere to the recommendation. Her antenatal course was otherwise unremarkable, except for her respiratory symptoms, which were initially attributed to normal pregnancy-related changes.

Differential diagnosis

The patient's presentation prompted consideration of following differential diagnoses. Pulmonary tuberculosis, Hodgkin’s lymphoma and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, thymoma, metastatic carcinoma, fungal infections (eg, histoplasmosis, aspergillosis), or viral pericarditis.

Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed anemia (hemoglobin 8.3 g/dL), leukocytosis (total leukocyte count 27.3 × 109/L), and thrombocytosis (platelet count 498 × 109/L). Differential leukocyte count showed neutrophilic predominance (88%) with relative lymphopenia (10%). High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I was within normal limits (10 ng/L).

Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia, low-voltage QRS complexes, and nonspecific T-wave inversions in leads V1–V3 (Figure 1). Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal left and right ventricular systolic function and moderate circumferential pericardial effusion. Upon further examination of the patient, the neck swelling was found to be a large posterior cervical lymph node which was excised and sent for histopathological analysis that revealed atypical cells with single-lobed to bilobed nuclei and prominent nucleoli in a polymorphous background. Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD30 and PAX-5 (Paired box protein-5) confirming the diagnosis of nodular sclerosing classical Hodgkin lymphoma (HL).

Management

As the patient presented initially with signs of cardiac tamponade, including hypotension, tachycardia, and muffled heart sounds, an urgent pericardiocentesis was performed resulting in hemodynamic stabilization. Following stabilization and after confirmation of Hodgkin’s lymphoma with a histopathological analysis, a multidisciplinary team—including cardiologist, obstetrician, and oncologist—planned to initiate chemotherapy with close follow-up. Given the gestational age and disease severity, ABVD (adriamycin, bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine) chemotherapy was initiated during the third trimester to minimize fetal risk while addressing maternal disease. Considering her antenatal history, an elective cesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks to allow for postpartum positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. The patient delivered a healthy baby of 2.5 kg with an APGAR (Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration) score of 6 at 1 minute and 7 at 5 minutes. Delivery was uneventful, and both mother and infant remained stable. An interim PET/CT scan was performed after 1 month of delivery which demonstrated interval reduction in size and metabolic activity of a hypermetabolic anterior mediastinal nodal mass compared to prior high-resolution CT findings. However, multiple discrete hypermetabolic lymph nodes persisted in the mediastinum, left cervical, and supraclavicular regions (Figures 4 and 5). Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient remained hemodynamically stable with no signs of infection or chemotherapy-related complications. The patient was scheduled to complete the remaining cycles of ABVD chemotherapy postpartum under close oncologic supervision. Cardiac function remained stable on follow-up echocardiography, with no recurrence of pericardial effusion (Videos 5 and 6).

The patient subsequently completed 6 cycles of ABVD chemotherapy. A follow-up PET/CT scan was performed 6 months after the initial postpartum imaging which revealed a mixed metabolic response: several lymph nodes showed interval reduction in size and metabolic activity, while others demonstrated interval progression (Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8). Discussion

HL during pregnancy is a rare but clinically significant diagnosis, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 6,000 pregnancies.1 While carcinoma is more commonly encountered in pregnancy (1 in 1,000),2 lymphoma ranks as the fourth most frequent malignancy, with HL occurring more often than non-Hodgkin lymphoma.3 Pericardial effusion is present in about 5% of patients with HL; however, it is usually asymptomatic and rarely the initial presentation of HL.4

Most cases of nodular sclerosing classical HL present with a mediastinal mass, and up to 25% of patients may experience B symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and night sweats.5 However, diagnosis during pregnancy can be delayed due to symptom overlap with normal gestational changes, including dyspnea, fatigue, and weakness. In our case, the patient experienced a 7-month delay from symptom onset to diagnosis, underscoring the diagnostic challenge.

Imaging modalities such as CT and PET scans are often deferred during pregnancy due to fetal radiation exposure risks,6 although lymph node biopsy remains safe for both mother and fetus. In our patient, echocardiography played a pivotal role in identifying cardiac tamponade, prompting urgent pericardiocentesis and facilitating further diagnostic workup.

Treatment decisions for HL during pregnancy depend on disease stage and gestational age. ABVD chemotherapy is considered safe during the second and third trimesters, with no known teratogenic effects, although risks of intrauterine growth restriction, prematurity, and stillbirth necessitate close fetal monitoring.7 In our case, chemotherapy was initiated in the third trimester, allowing for disease control while minimizing fetal exposure.

Termination of pregnancy is rarely indicated and is generally reserved for aggressive disease diagnosed in the first trimester or requiring intensive regimens throughout gestation.8 Our patient successfully completed 3 cycles of ABVD chemotherapy during pregnancy and delivered a healthy infant. Subsequently she completed 3 more cycles of ABVD, postpartum. Follow-up PET/CT imaging revealed a mixed metabolic response, highlighting the need for continued oncologic surveillance and potential treatment adjustment.

Identified through the PRESAP registry, which detects unrecognized structural heart disease in asymptomatic pregnant women,9 our findings during screening echocardiography unravel an uncommon scenario: a woman presenting for a routine antenatal visit who was incidentally diagnosed with Hodgkin’s lymphoma.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of early recognition, the strategic timing of interventions, and integration of multidisciplinary care in managing rare diseases during pregnancy with the aim to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes in complex scenarios through antenatal evaluation at a cardio-obstetric screening clinic using echocardiography.

The timely diagnosis of Hodgkin’s lymphoma during the third trimester, facilitated by multidisciplinary collaboration, enabled safe initiation of chemotherapy and successful maternal and neonatal outcomes. Despite a mixed metabolic response on follow-up imaging, ongoing coordinated care continues to guide therapeutic decisions.
