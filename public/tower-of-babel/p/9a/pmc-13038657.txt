Safety and effectiveness of clazosentan in patients after aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage: a second interim report of a post-marketing surveillance in Japan
Authors: Hiroyuki Sakata, Yuya Takayama, Yuna Iwasaki, Hiroyuki Uchida, Satoshi Nishimoto, Osamu Togo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJapanese phase 3 trials demonstrated efficacy and safety of clazosentan for aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage (aSAH) management. However, real-world data remain limited, particularly in elderly, poor-grade, outside of Fisher group 3 patients, or those on concomitant medications. Therefore, this post-marketing surveillance (PMS) programme evaluated safety and effectiveness of clazosentan in Japanese clinical practice. This second interim analysis of an ongoing PMS programme included patients with aSAH who received clazosentan postoperatively in Japan. Safety data included incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and their severity. Effectiveness endpoints were the incidence of cerebral vasospasm–related events. Exploratory endpoints were prognostic factors for fluid retention–related ADRs and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events identified by univariate and multivariate analyses. Of the 2967 included patients, ADRs occurred in 34.5%, serious ADRs in 8.2%, and fluid retention–related ADRs in 20.1%. Cerebral vasospasm was observed in 18.8% of patients, while 7.1% and 10.6% experienced vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events, respectively. Multivariate analysis identified age ≥ 75 years, thick and diffuse clot size, medical history, comorbidities, and vasodilatory concomitant medication use as key predictors of fluid retention–related ADRs and female sex, age ≥ 75 years, anterior circulation, thick and diffuse clot size, World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade IV/V, comorbidities, and cilostazol use (risk reduction factor) as key predictors of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Patients with fluid retention–related ADRs had a higher incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Introduction

Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) is a significant global health concern due to its high morbidity and mortality (hereafter referred to as morbidity/mortality) [1]. From 1990 to 2021, the global incidence of SAH increased by 37.1%, with countries in the Asia-Pacific region reporting the highest age-standardised incidence rate at 14.1 per 100,000 population in 2021 [1]. SAH cases are commonly attributed to the rupture of an intracranial aneurysm [2]. In Japan, the incidence of aneurysmal SAH (aSAH) is estimated at 28.0 (95% confidence interval [CI], 25.3–31.0) per 100,000 person-years, with an annual increase of 1.6% (95% CI, 0.8–2.3) since 1977 [3]. Cerebral vasospasm develops in 40%–70% of cases following aSAH, typically emerging between 4 and 14 days after the initial haemorrhage [4]. The occurrence of cerebral vasospasm is associated with delayed ischaemic neurological deficit (DIND), which affects up to 30% of aSAH cases and may progress to cerebral infarction [5]. Endothelin-1 (ET-1), a potent vasoconstrictor, interacts with two receptor subtypes, ETA and ETB [6]. Binding of ET-1 to ETA receptors induces vasoconstriction, whereas binding to ETB receptors contributes to vasodilation [7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ET-1 levels are elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with SAH and have suggested an association with the development of cerebral vasospasm [6].

Clazosentan, a selective ETA receptor antagonist, is approximately 1000 times more selective for ETA than for the ETB receptor [8]. This selective antagonism reduces vasoconstriction and mitigates the risk of cerebral vasospasm–related morbidity/mortality events [8]. Several clinical trials have been conducted to assess the efficacy and safety of clazosentan in patients after aSAH surgery [9–14]. In the phase 2 CONSCIOUS-1 trial conducted in Israel, Europe, and North America, clazosentan (1, 5, and 15 mg/h) was compared with placebo, and the incidence of cerebral vasospasm was observed to reduce dose-dependently [9]. However, the trial also highlighted notable adverse events (AEs) such as lung complications, anaemia, and hypotension. The subsequent phase 3 study, the CONSCIOUS-2 trial, compared clazosentan (5 mg/h) with placebo but found no significant reduction in cerebral vasospasm–related morbidity or overall mortality [10]. The results of the CONSCIOUS-2 trial led to the early termination of the concurrent CONSCIOUS-3 trial that compared clazosentan (5 and 15 mg/h) with placebo [11]. Despite early termination, the exploratory analysis of CONSCIOUS-3 suggested that 15 mg/h of clazosentan led to a significant reduction in cerebral vasospasm–related morbidity and overall mortality [11]. The interest in clazosentan resurfaced in Japan following the results of phase 2 and 3 trials conducted in regional patients with aSAH [12, 13]. Fujimura et al. conducted a phase 2 clinical trial comparing clazosentan (5 and 10 mg/h) with placebo and reported a significant reduction in the incidence of moderate or severe cerebral vasospasm with clazosentan in Japanese and Korean patients with aSAH [12]. Furthermore, they reported that clazosentan (10 mg/h) significantly reduced the overall incidence of death and vasospasm-related morbidity. In the subsequent two randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 3 clinical trials, clazosentan (10 mg/h) demonstrated a significant reduction in vasospasm-related morbidity and all-cause mortality in Japanese patients with aSAH [13]. These findings led to the first approval of clazosentan for clinical use in Japan in January 2022. The Japan Stroke Society Guideline (revised version 2025) included clazosentan for delayed cerebral vasospasm management (Recommendation B) [15]. The administration of clazosentan is associated with potential fluid retention complications, including pulmonary oedema, and pleural effusion [13]. Thus, the following precautionary measures are advised: monitoring early symptoms and adjusting body fluid volume during treatment [16]. Furthermore, previous clinical trials excluded elderly patients (≥ 76 years), patients with World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade V, patients outside of Fisher group 3, and those receiving concomitant medications such as fasudil or ozagrel [12, 13]. Consequently, the safety and efficacy of clazosentan in these patient groups were not evaluated. This limits the generalisability of the clinical trial findings for clazosentan in the real-world setting.

A national post-marketing surveillance (PMS) programme has been initiated in Japan to assess the safety and effectiveness of clazosentan through extended follow-up of patients with aSAH. This PMS programme also investigated the safety and effectiveness of clazosentan in patients who had been excluded from the phase 3 clinical trials in Japan [13]. The first interim report of this study with a cut-off date of 19 January 2024 has been published previously [17]. The second interim results presented in this article are based on data collected up to 19 January 2025 and include more patients than in the first interim report. Furthermore, exploratory analyses were also conducted to determine factors influencing fluid retention–related adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and cerebral vasospasm–related morbidity/mortality events. These results contributed to the fifth periodic safety report submitted to the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA).

Materials and methods

Study design and eligibility criteria

This special drug use-results PMS (registration no: UMIN000048297) is an ongoing, prospective, observational, multicentre study conducted across 262 sites (as of 19 January 2025) in Japan to obtain real-world data on the safety and effectiveness of clazosentan in patients with postoperative aSAH. The study period was planned from 20 April 2022 (launch date) to 30 April 2029. The study included all consecutive patients who experienced SAH due to a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, underwent aneurysm repair surgery (e.g., clipping or coiling), and received clazosentan postoperatively at participating facilities during the enrolment period. As indicated in the Japanese package insert, clazosentan was administered at a dose of 10 mg/h via continuous intravenous infusion for up to 15 days after aSAH onset.

The observation period for safety endpoints spanned up to 6 months after aSAH onset. The observation period for effectiveness endpoints was 6 weeks (or early discharge before 6 weeks) after aSAH onset. The severity of disability using modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was assessed at 6 weeks (or early discharge before 6 weeks), 12 weeks, and 6 months after aSAH onset.

This post-marketing drug use-results surveillance was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with the Good Post-marketing Study Practice Ordinance issued by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. Under the exemptions provided by this ordinance, institutional review board approval and informed consent are not required in Japan.

Outcomes

The following details were collected from the medical records of participating patients: age and sex, time and date of aSAH onset, aneurysm surgery date and type, WFNS grade and Fisher group classification, clazosentan administration details, presence of comorbidities, and concomitant medications. The analysis in this report focused on fasudil, ozagrel, and cilostazol, as these were the three most commonly used concomitant medications reported in the first interim analysis [17]. These drugs are also listed in the Japan Stroke Society Guidelines (revised version 2025) for the management of delayed cerebral vasospasm [15]. However, even in Japan, cilostazol is not covered by insurance as a prophylactic agent for delayed cerebral vasospasm after aSAH. Among the concomitant medications, those administered after the onset of new cerebral vasospasm, vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, and DIND were not considered concomitant medications. Safety endpoints focused on the incidence of ADRs and serious ADRs. Incidence rates of ADRs were also categorised based on demographics and baseline characteristics. Among the priority survey items in accordance with the risk management plan (RMP), the important identified risk items were fluid retention–related ADRs and intracranial haemorrhage. Effectiveness endpoints included the overall incidence of new cerebral vasospasm, vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events, as well as their occurrence rate stratified by background factors. Vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events were defined as follows: (1) death from any cause, (2) vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, and (3) vasospasm-related DIND. Exploratory endpoints included the background factors affecting the development of fluid retention–related ADRs and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Additionally, these endpoints included mRS scores and the incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events in patients with versus without fluid retention–related ADRs. Table S1 in the Online Resource provides the definition of the key assessment parameters used in this report.

Statistical analysis

The target sample size was determined based on the incidence of intracranial haemorrhage in previous Japanese clinical studies (Nxera Pharma Japan, data on file). Considering a two-sided significance level of 0.05, a power of ≥ 90%, and patient dropouts, the target sample size was set at 2000 patients. Following a protocol revision on 10 November 2024, new registrations were limited to patients aged ≥ 75 years. For safety evaluation items, the frequency and incidence rates of ADRs were tabulated. ADRs were coded using the Japanese translation of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 27.1 and classified according to Preferred Terms (PT) and System Organ Class (SOC). The frequency of ADRs was further analysed by patient background, alongside corresponding incidence rates and 95% CIs.

For efficacy endpoints, both the frequency and incidence of each event were calculated with accompanying 95% CIs. Forest plots were generated to assess the distribution of new cerebral vasospasm, vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events categorised by the background factors of the study participants. Exploratory endpoints were analysed using both univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Univariate logistic regression was first performed to explore associations between individual background factors and fluid retention–related ADRs, based on clinical considerations and findings from subgroup analyses. Variables identified as potentially relevant were then included in a multivariable logistic regression model, with stepwise variable selection applied using a predefined significance threshold of 10% to explore their combined associations. In addition, factors associated with vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events were evaluated using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses in an exploratory manner. Frequency, incidence rate, and 95% CI were calculated for vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events stratified by the presence or absence of fluid retention–related ADRs. All hypothesis tests were conducted at a two-sided significance level of 5%, with no adjustments for multiplicity. The Clopper-Pearson method was used to calculate 95% CIs. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient disposition

Case report forms were obtained from 3012 patients (consent for data publication has been obtained). A total of 45 patients were excluded from the analysis, 44 due to deviations in the registration process from the established protocol and one due to a duplicate entry. The remaining 2967 patients were included in the safety and effectiveness analysis set (Fig. 1).

Demographics and baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the patients included in this study are shown in Table 1. A total of 30.4% (903/2967) of the study population were male and 69.6% (2064/2967) were female. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age was 63.0 (14.4) years, and 24.6% (703/2861) of patients were aged ≥ 75 years. A total of 41.4% (1229/2967) and 57.4% (1704/2967) of patients had undergone clipping surgery and endovascular coiling, respectively. Overall, 11.8% of patients (342/2901) were classified as Fisher group 4, while 15.6% (461/2946) were categorised as WFNS grade V. The mean (SD) drug treatment period was 12.2 (3.0) days. At the end of clazosentan administration, 98.9% of patients (2933/2967) received a daily dose of 240 mg, consistent with continuous infusion at 10 mg/h as indicated in the package insert. In 13.1% (389/2967) of patients who discontinued clazosentan, 95.9% (373/389) discontinued treatment due to AEs (Table S2 in the Online Resource).

Safety endpoints

The incidence of ADRs in this study are shown in Table 2. The incidence of ADRs was 34.5% (1022/2967). The most commonly observed ADR was pleural effusion (9.6%; 285/2967), followed by pulmonary oedema (6.7%; 200/2967), cerebral vasoconstriction (4.5%; 132/2967), cerebral infarction (3.0%; 89/2967), and hepatic function abnormal (2.9%; 85/2967). The incidence of serious ADRs was 8.2% (243/2967). The incidence of serious ADRs in this study are shown in Table 3. The most commonly observed serious ADR was pulmonary oedema (1.8%; 53/2967), followed by cerebral infarction (1.8%; 52/2967), pleural effusion (1.7%; 51/2967), cerebral vasoconstriction (1.7%; 50/2967), and DIND (1.2%; 34/2967). Table S3 in the Online Resource summarises the incidence of all ADRs, including those classified as serious ADRs, observed during the study. The incidence of ADRs by background factors and concomitant medication use is shown in Table 4. Differences in incidence of ADRs were observed across categories such as age, Fisher group, WFNS grade, overall comorbidities, comorbidities (hepatic dysfunction), use of concomitant medications, and use of concomitant fasudil/ozagrel or cilostazol. Fluid retention–related ADRs occurred in 20.1% (596/2967) of patients overall, of which 4.3% (126/2967) were classified as serious ADRs (Table S4 in the Online Resource). Intracranial haemorrhage occurred in 0.6% (19/2967) of patients, of which 0.4% (12/2967) were classified as serious ADRs (Table S4 in the Online Resource).

Effectiveness endpoints

The forest plots of cerebral vasospasm–related events stratified by background factors in this study are shown in Fig. 2. Among the 2967 patients included in the effectiveness analysis, excluding those who were not evaluable, new cerebral vasospasm was observed in 18.8% (553/2940). Vasospasm-related cerebral infarction was observed in 7.1% (211/2956) of patients, while vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events were observed in 10.6% (311/2932).

Data are presented as n/N (%), where n indicates the number of patients who experienced the event and N denotes the total number of patients within each subgroup. For the efficacy analysis, patients for whom event occurrence was recorded as ‘not evaluated’ or ‘unknown’ were excluded from the dataset to ensure accurate assessment. In individuals aged ≥ 75 years compared with those < 75 years, no clear difference was observed between the incidence of cerebral vasospasm (20.3% vs. 18.6%). Both the incidence of vasospasm-related cerebral infarction (9.7% vs. 6.5%) and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events (16.2% vs. 9.0%) tended to be higher in patients aged ≥ 75 years compared with those aged < 75 years. In the Fisher group 4, the incidence of cerebral vasospasm was 25.2%, with vasospasm-related cerebral infarction occurring in 10.6% of patients. Additionally, 15.6% of patients experienced vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Overall, these events appeared to be more frequent in the Fisher group 4 compared with other Fisher groups. In patients classified as WFNS grade V, the cerebral vasospasm occurred in 23.7%, vasospasm-related cerebral infarction in 8.6%, and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events in 17.5%. Poor grade correlated with an increased incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. According to the use of concomitant fasudil/ozagrel, there were no clear differences in the incidence of cerebral vasospasm, vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events among the four groups, i.e., neither fasudil nor ozagrel use, concomitant fasudil use, concomitant ozagrel use, and concomitant fasudil and ozagrel use. The concomitant use of cilostazol was associated with a lower incidence of cerebral vasospasm (14.8%; 112/757), vasospasm-related cerebral infarction (4.6%; 35/758), and vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events (6.9%; 51/743). There was no clear bias in the concomitant medication use when assessed according to WFNS grades (data not shown).

Exploratory endpoints

Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to investigate background factors affecting fluid retention–related ADRs (Table 5). Multivariate analysis revealed that age ≥ 75 years, thick and diffuse clot size, past medical history, comorbidities, concomitant use of ozagrel, and concomitant use of cilostazol were associated with an increased risk of fluid retention–related ADRs. Table 6 presents data from univariate and multivariate analyses of background factors affecting vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Multivariate analysis identified female sex, age ≥ 75 years, anterior circulation, thick and diffuse clot size, WFNS grades IV or V, and comorbidities as significant risk factors for vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Conversely, concomitant use of cilostazol was associated with a reduced risk of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. The incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events was 10.1% (233/2308) in patients without fluid retention–related ADRs compared with 13.5% (78/576) in those with such ADRs, indicating a higher tendency for these events in the latter group (Table 7). The mRS scores obtained in this study are presented in Table S5 in the Online Resource. The mRS score of 0–2 was observed in 80.2% (995/1240) of patients discharged before 6 weeks and in 41.1% (577/1405) assessed at 6 weeks after aSAH onset. Although the mRS score rate of 0–2 was 58.3% (479/822) at 12 weeks and 75.0% (868/1157) at 6 months after aSAH onset, the data collected at these time points represented only about one-third of the total dataset.

Discussion

Safety profile and considerations

In this study, clazosentan demonstrated a safety profile consistent with previous clinical trials, with no new safety concerns identified [12, 13]. The overall incidence of ADRs was 34.5%, which closely aligns with the 35.3% incidence of drug-related AEs reported in the Japanese phase 3 studies [13]. The rates of pleural effusion (9.6%) and pulmonary oedema (6.7%), two hallmark manifestations of fluid retention–related ADRs, were also comparable with those previously reported in the Japanese phase 3 trials, where the incidences reached 16.5% and 11.9%, respectively [13].

Fluid retention–related ADRs were reported in 20.1% of patients. Multivariate analysis identified advanced age (≥ 75 years), thick and diffuse clot size, medical history, comorbidities, and use of vasodilatory concomitant medications as significant risk factors for fluid retention–related ADRs. Kondo et al. reported an increased incidence of clazosentan-associated fluid retention in individuals aged ≥ 76 years [18]. Similarly, Matsuoka et al. identified age > 70 years as a risk factor for developing this condition [19]. Although the underlying mechanisms contributing to the higher incidence of fluid retention–related ADRs in the elderly remain unclear, proper monitoring is warranted when administering clazosentan to elderly patients. Multivariate analysis revealed that the concomitant use of medications such as ozagrel and cilostazol was associated with a significantly higher incidence of fluid retention–related ADRs. In contrast, in patients receiving fasudil along with clazosentan, the risk of fluid retention–related ADRs was only numerically higher. The combination of clazosentan with fasudil has been previously reported to potentially increase the risk of pulmonary oedema [19, 20]. The mechanism by which the concomitant use of fasudil, ozagrel, and cilostazol with clazosentan may exacerbate fluid retention–related ADRs remains unclear. These findings underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring and fluid management, particularly when clazosentan is used in combination with other vasodilators.

Effectiveness—comparison with Japanese phase 3 trials

In this second interim analysis, the incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events and vasospasm-related cerebral infarction was 10.6% and 7.1%, respectively. These rates are comparable to those reported in the Japanese phase 3 trials (14.9% for vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events and 12.3% for cerebral infarction) [13]. The Japanese phase 3 trials employed blinded central imaging adjudication, while the current real-world, multicentre, observational study reflects clinical practice variability [13]. Importantly, the present study included patient populations typically excluded from Japanese phase 3 trials, which enhances the external validity of the findings. Collectively, the results suggest that clazosentan retains clinical effectiveness in broader real-world settings.

Outcomes in elderly patients

Although the overall incidence of cerebral vasospasm did not differ clearly between patients aged ≥ 75 and < 75 years, elderly patients tended to have a higher incidence of vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and morbidity/mortality events. There is no consensus on the effect of age on the incidence of cerebral vasospasm–related events, but there are reports of elderly patients having poorer outcomes despite having fewer cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral infarction than younger patients [21, 22]. Evaluating efficacy in elderly patients is not easy, but caution is required, due to comorbidities and medical complications.

Outcomes focusing on clot size and Fisher group

In general, patients with thick and diffuse clot size have a higher risk of cerebral vasospasm–related events [23, 24]. In the current study, the incidence of cerebral vasospasm–related events tended to be higher in patients in Fisher group 4. This might have been attributed to the similar proportions of thick and diffuse clot size observed in patients in Fisher group 3 and 4, along with a higher prevalence of WFNS grades IV and V in patients who were classified into Fisher group 4 compared with those classified into Fisher group 3. However, the incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events was 10.6% in Fisher group 3 and 15.6% in Fisher group 4, suggesting that clazosentan may be effective in patients with thick and diffuse clot size. In a recent study, Ogawa et al. reported that the vasodilatory effect of clazosentan was not associated with thickness of blood clot and that clazosentan may exert a robust vasodilatory effect even in patients with thick clot size [25]. This observation is also supported by the findings from previous studies on clazosentan [26].

Outcomes in poor-grade aSAH

Patients with poor grade exhibited higher rates of cerebral vasospasm–related events. The American Heart Association and American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guideline recommend the use of nimodipine (currently unapproved in Japan) for the prevention of delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI) following aSAH, as well as for improving clinical outcomes [27]. In a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials, the incidence of cerebral vasospasm in patients treated with nimodipine after aSAH ranged from 11.0% to 56.9% (mean: 27.9%; heterogeneity: 57.0%), while the incidence of cerebral infarction ranged from 21.9% to 63.6% (mean: 26.4%; heterogeneity: 56.4%) [28, 29]. Although comparative studies are limited [30], the present results suggest that the effectiveness of clazosentan may be comparable to that of nimodipine considering the relatively lower incidence of cerebral vasospasm (23.7%) and vasospasm-related cerebral infarction (8.6%) observed in the WFNS grade V group.

Influence of concomitant medication use

Multivariate analysis identified several risk factors associated with vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events, including female sex, age ≥ 75 years, anterior circulation, thick and diffuse clot size, WFNS grades IV/V, and the presence of comorbidities. The concomitant use of fasudil or ozagrel was not found to affect the risk of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events. Conversely, the concomitant use of cilostazol was associated with a decreased incidence of vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events, suggesting a potential synergistic effect when used alongside clazosentan. Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase 3 inhibitor, exerts antiplatelet, endothelial-protective, and vasodilatory effects [31]. Several randomised controlled trials have reported the benefits of cilostazol in improving cerebral vasospasm, DCI, and neurological outcomes [32, 33]. However, further investigation is necessary to elucidate the potential therapeutic role of cilostazol when used in combination with clazosentan.

Impact of fluid retention on vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events

Fluid retention–related ADRs were found to be associated with vasospasm-related morbidity/mortality events in this study, suggesting that they may contribute to ischaemic complications. Wu et al. insisted that pulmonary comorbidities exacerbate cerebral hypoxia and negatively affect neurological outcomes following aSAH [34]. Similarly, Kimura et al. demonstrated an association between pleural effusion and poor outcomes following clazosentan therapy [35]. Moreover, Sugiyama et al., in a multicentre, prospective, registry study, identified non-ischaemic complications as contributing to unfavourable outcomes in clazosentan-treated patients, with pulmonary oedema representing the most significant risk factor [36]. However, it is possible that overlapping background risk factors across all analyses, such as ‘age ≥ 75 years’, ‘thick and diffuse clot size’, and ‘comorbidities’, may have acted as confounding factors.

Insights from a recently reported global clinical trial

Recently, the REACT study, a phase 3 trial conducted in Europe, Canada, Israel, and the United Sates, found that clazosentan 15 mg/h did not significantly reduce clinical deterioration due to DCI compared with placebo [14]. However, rescue therapy was required less frequently in patients receiving clazosentan than in those given placebo (21/202 [10.4%] vs. 37/204 [18.1%]). Differences in study design between the Japanese phase 3 and REACT study, including variations in patient populations and treatment practices, limits the direct comparison of these studies [13, 14]. Suzuki et al. attributed differences between the REACT and Japanese phase 3 trials to patient ethnicity (97.5% non-Asian vs. 100% Japanese), nimodipine use (97.5% vs. none), clazosentan dose (15 mg/h vs. 10 mg/h), and timing of treatment initiation (within 96 h vs. 48 h after aSAH) [37]. They also highlighted that the higher rate of treatment discontinuation (13.0% vs. 5.5%) due to ADRs in the clazosentan arm of the REACT study may have contributed to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes.

Evaluation based on real-world data from Japan

Before the approval of clazosentan in Japan, fasudil was commonly used for managing cerebral vasospasm following aSAH. Several single-centre studies compared clazosentan with fasudil-based standard care [38–41]. Sakata et al. and Kajiwara et al. reported that clazosentan reduced vasospasm-related events and improved outcomes, although they also highlighted an increased risk of pulmonary complications, underscoring the importance of strict fluid management [39, 40]. More recently, a multicentre retrospective cohort study by Muraoka et al. reported improved mRS scores at discharge in the clazosentan-treated group compared with the fasudil-treated group [42]. Pulmonary complications occurred in 6.8% of clazosentan-treated patients, similar to that in the fasudil-treated group, and the authors attributed this to careful euvolemic management and the use of active diuretics [42]. Kondo et al. further demonstrated that implementation of a perioperative management protocol, including a base fluid volume of 1 mL/kg/h, a target fluid balance of 0–500 mL, and structured diuretic criteria, significantly reduced systemic complications and fluid retention in patients treated with clazosentan [18]. Furthermore, a multicentre, prospective cohort study conducted by Sakata et al. reported that the clazosentan treatment group demonstrated improved mRS scores at 90 days compared to the conventional treatment group. Additionally, it was reported that the discontinuation rate of clazosentan was 5.9% under strict fluid management protocols [43]. Together, these findings suggest that while clazosentan shows clinical benefit, optimising fluid management is essential for minimising ADRs and achieving favourable outcomes.

Strength

The present study had several notable strengths. As a post-marketing, drug use-results survey, this study provides important real-world data on the safety and effectiveness of clazosentan in routine clinical practice, offering insights beyond the controlled environment of clinical trials. The large sample size (2967 patients) and the prospective design of the study strengthens the reliability of the safety and effectiveness analysis. Additionally, the study provided preliminary data on the use of clazosentan in combination with commonly used vasospasm-preventive agents, fasudil, ozagrel, and cilostazol, which was previously unexplored.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this interim analysis. First, this PMS study is observational in nature. Therefore, this study has several biases (e.g., selection and reporting bias) relating to the nature of its design (e.g., open-label and single-arm design with a lack of a control arm). Accordingly, careful interpretation of these findings is warranted. Second, no statistical adjustments were made in this study for multiplicity due to its exploratory purpose. Findings should be interpreted cautiously, with emphasis on effect sizes and CIs. Third, detailed information on laboratory test results and fluid balance was required only when ADRs occurred and was optional for other cases. Consequently, the available data were insufficient for analysis, potentially introducing unmeasured confounding factors affecting fluid retention–related ADRs. In light of the interim nature of this analysis, further analyses and discussion regarding fluid retention–related ADRs will be considered. Finally, the mRS scores were assessed at multiple intervals for up to 6 months after aSAH onset. However, maintaining consistent assessment timing in real-world clinical settings proved challenging. Consequently, the mRS data collected at 12 weeks or 6 months after aSAH onset represent only about one-third of the total dataset, which may introduce selection bias and overestimation of outcomes.

Conclusion

Clazosentan demonstrated effectiveness and a manageable safety profile in real-world clinical practice among patients with aSAH in the postoperative period, including in underrepresented populations such as elderly patients and those in critical condition. Fluid retention–related ADRs were commonly observed, and thus should be closely monitored, especially in elderly patients and those on concomitant vasodilators. The findings of this study support the use of clazosentan in clinical practice while underscoring the importance of careful monitoring. As this study represents an interim analysis, the study is ongoing, and the final analysis is expected to provide a comprehensive and final evaluation of the long-term safety and effectiveness of clazosentan.
