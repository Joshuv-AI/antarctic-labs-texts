Defective neutrophil-derived exosomes facilitate macrophage activation through miR-122-5p in Behçet’s disease
Authors: Xin Yu, Menghao Zhang, Na Kang, Chaoran Li, Zhimian Wang, Yiyuan Ao, Lu Li, Xiao’ou Wang, Yeling Liu, Lidan Zhao, Li Wang, Min Shen, Jinjing Liu, Yan Zhao, Fengchun Zhang, Hua Chen, Wenjie Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Behçet’s disease (BD) is a life-threatening systemic vasculitis characterized by polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMN) and macrophage activation. However, the interaction of PMN and macrophages remains elusive. To elucidate the potential dysregulation of BD PMN exosomes on macrophage activation, PMN exosomes from both BD patients and healthy controls are isolated, quantified and incubated with macrophages. We find that BD PMN exosomes are decreased and negatively correlated with C-reactive protein (CRP). PMN exosomes can suppress IL-6, TNF, CD80 and CD86 expressions on macrophages, which are attenuated in BD PMN exosomes. In addition, by miRNA sequencing of PMN exosomes, RNA sequencing of miRNA-transfected macrophages, and dual luciferase reporter assay validation of the miRNA target, we find that miR-122-5p is decreased in BD PMN exosomes, targeting IRF5, suppressing TLR4 signaling and IFN-β autocrine, eventually downregulating macrophage activation. Our study illustrates that BD PMN exosomes are decreased in both quantity and miR-122-5p, which impairs the potential immunoregulatory effects on macrophages through degrading IRF5 and suppressing IFN-β autocrine, shedding light on the interaction mechanism between PMN and macrophages.

Introduction

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic systemic vasculitis characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, and potentially life-threatening manifestations1, with heavy disease burden and high mortality. Current targeted therapies for BD are largely limited by its unknown etiology. Hence, uncovering the underlying mechanism of BD for developing novel targeted therapies is of great significance to improve the outcomes of BD.

The pathogenesis of BD remains unclear. Current evidence suggests that it involves dysregulation of both innate and adaptive immunity, driven by genetic and environmental triggers. BD exhibits overlapping features with autoinflammatory diseases, characterized by recurrent acute inflammation mediated by innate immune cells overactivation, as well as autoimmune disorders marked by T-helper (Th) 1 and Th17 cell activation2. Notably, BD also aligns with MHC-I-opathies such as spondyloarthritis, sharing clinical and molecular mechanisms linked to aberrant MHC class I signaling3. Excessive infiltration of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMN) into inflamed tissues is a hallmark of BD, which is also known as “neutrophilic vasculitis”4. Activated PMN in BD release massive neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) with enhanced pro-inflammatory effects, leading to IL-8 and TNF overproduction, and promote Th1 differentiation through macrophages5, and further exacerbating vascular endothelial injury6. Conversely, monocytes and macrophages are activated and polarized under the BD immune microenvironment7, and enhance neutrophil adhesion to endothelial cells8. Transcriptional analysis further suggests chemotaxis of BD PMN by monocytes via the CCR6-CCL20 pathway9–11.

Exosomes are 30-130 nm extracellular vesicles (EV) with less heterogeneity, and serve as intercellular communication mediators12 beyond cytokines, chemokines and NETs. Plasma exosomes and monocyte-derived exosomes potentially promote the inflammatory response of BD13,14. PMN releases a major proportion of circulating exosomes due to abundant cell quantity. PMN exosomes exhibit versatile functions. PMN exosomes degrade extracellular matrix via integrin Mac-1 and neutrophil elastase (NE) to exacerbate alveolar destruction in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)15 and stimulate keratinocytes to express chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines16 in psoriasis. In contrast, accumulating evidence suggests the immunoregulatory effects of PMN exosomes on innate immune system17–23, including attenuation of PMN chemotaxis24, M2 macrophage polarization23, and dendritic cell (DC) maturation25.

Given the crucial roles of PMN in BD pathogenesis and exosomes in cell-cell communication, this study investigates the abnormalities of BD PMN-derived exosomes, uncovering a reduction in miR-122-5p that targets IRF5, suppresses TLR4 signaling and IFN-β autocrine, and downregulate macrophage activation. These findings not only elucidate a novel immunoregulatory mechanism mediated by PMN exosomes in BD pathogenesis, but also highlight the therapeutic potential of miR-122-5p/IRF5 pathway, which may extend to other autoimmune and autoinflammatory diseases.

We then treated HMDM with LPS-stimulated BD or HC PMN exosomes (5ug/ml) (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Given that TNF and IL-6 are crucial pro-inflammatory cytokines released by macrophages and play an essential role in BD pathogenesis26,27, we tested TNF and IL-6 production by macrophages treated with BD or HC PMN exosomes. Intriguingly, both BD and HC PMN exosomes suppressed IL-6, TNF, CD80 and CD86 expression in HMDM and THP-1 macrophages (Fig. 1D–F, and Supplementary Fig. 2D), but BD PMN exosomes suppressed less IL-6 (p = 0.0011), TNF (p = 0.0486), CD80 (p < 0.0001) and CD86 (p = 0.0056) than HC PMN exosomes. Taken together, these data indicated that BD PMN produced less quantity and quality of immunoregulatory exosomes, which potentially contributed to macrophage overactivation in BD.

Downregulated miR-122-5p in BD PMN exosomes dampens the immunoregulatory property

Considering the close relationship between exosome and epigenetics, we performed microRNA (miRNA) sequencing on 4 paired BD and HC PMN exosomes to explore the mechanism of dampened immunoregulatory BD PMN exosomes. The miRNA profiling of BD PMN exosomes showed a transcriptionally inactive gene signature (Fig. 2A), with 6 downregulated and 5 upregulated miRNAs, including 3 unconservative miRNAs (Fig. 2B, and Supplementary Data 1). Given decreased exosomes in BD PMN, we focused on the downregulated miRNAs. To further investigate the regulation of macrophage activation by miR-122-5p in vivo, we peritoneally injected miR-122-5p agomir into mice. miR-122-5p agomir significantly inhibited TNF (p = 0.0359), IL6 (p = 0.0486) and CD86 (p = 0.0216) expression in mouse peritoneal macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3), and a trend towards decreasing IRF5 expression levels. Collectively, our data suggested that decreased miR-122-5p in BD PMN exosomes played a key role in dampened immunoregulatory effects on macrophages.

Exosomal miR-122-5p targets IRF5 and inhibits macrophage inflammation

To gain insight into the mechanism of immunoregulatory effects of exosomal miR-122-5p, we performed RNA-sequencing on LPS-stimulated THP-1 macrophages transfected with miR-122-5p or control miRNA mimics. PCA analysis clearly distinguished two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4A), and mRNA profiling showed a distinct transcriptionally inactive gene signature in miR-122-5p-transfected macrophages, with 779 upregulated and 1037 downregulated differentially expressed protein-coding genes (DEGs), corresponding to miRNA’s negative regulation on mRNAs (Fig. 4A, and Supplementary Data 3). GSEA of transcriptional profiles illustrated significantly downregulated inflammatory immune responses in miR-122-5p-transfected macrophages, including TLR, TNF, NF-κB and JAK-STAT pathways (Fig. 4B, C, and Supplementary Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Data 4). We next performed transcription factor (TF) co-regulatory network analysis to explore the key regulatory target regulated by miR-122-5p. miR-122-5p-regulated TFs were filtered by miRanda from DEGs, and the relevant downstream regulated DEGs were predicted by ChEA3 database, and further filtered by Pearson correlation coefficient analysis between TFs and DEGs, which showed that decreased transcription factor IRF5 together with its downstream regulated genes was the crucial target of miR-122-5p (Fig. 4D). IRF5 is the key TF controlling M1 polarization, which was closely correlated with our phenotype according to KEGG pathway (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 4C). miRanda, TargetScan and miRWalk database predicted IRF5 as miR-122-5p target (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter assay verified that miR-122-5p directly bound to IRF5 3’UTR (relative LUC activity: p < 0.0001, Fig. 5B) and downregulated IRF5 expression in both mRNA (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5C) and protein (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5D) levels. Exosomal miR-122-5p suppresses IFN-β autocrine and inhibits macrophage inflammation

We also noted IFN-β as the most downregulated cytokine (Supplementary Fig. 4D) as well as dampened JAK-STAT pathways (Fig. 4C) in miR-122-5p-transfected macrophages, suggesting inhibited IFN-β autocrine by miR-122-5p. Both miR-122-5p mimics (supernatant: p < 0.0001; relative mRNA expression: p < 0.0001) and IRF5 silencing (supernatant: p = 0.0019; relative mRNA expression: p = 0.0006) downregulated IFN-β production in macrophages (Fig. 6A, B), while restoration of IRF5 by plasmid transfection upregulated IFN-β production (Fig. 6C), supporting IRF5 induced transcription of type I IFN genes32. Furthermore, the attenuated TNF and IL-6 by miR-122-5p was restored by exogenous IFN-β (Fig. 6D, E) in macrophages. Additionally, STAT1, the IFN-β signaling adaptor, was downregulated (Supplementary Data 3, Fig. 7A), and STAT1 phosphorylation was inhibited by miR-122-5p and restored by exogenous IFN-β (Fig. 7B). STAT1 inhibition dampened IFN-β-induced TNF and IL-6 production in macrophages (Fig. 7C-D). Finally, restoration of IFN-β increased STAT1 (p < 0.0001, Fig. 7E) and IRF5 expression (p < 0.0001, Fig. 7F), suggesting a positive feedback loop of IFN-β during macrophage activation. Discussion

In this study, we found that BD PMN released less exosomes which were negatively correlated with BD inflammation. Moreover, PMN exosomes suppressed macrophages to express pro-inflammatory cytokines and costimulatory molecules, which was significantly impaired in BD PMN exosomes. Mechanistically, BD PMN exosomes contain lower levels of miR-122-5p, which targeted IRF5 and subsequently suppressed TNF, IL-6 as well as IFN-β autocrine, highlighting a dysregulated immunoregulatory mechanism by PMN exosomes in BD pathogenesis.

PMN are essential inflammatory immune cells in BD, which promote inflammation through pro-inflammatory cytokines, NETs and secretory granules33,34. Although PMN dominate the early stage of inflammation through rapid and potent immune activation, these cells are also reported to contribute to the resolution of inflammation, the aberrance of which leads to persistent immune responses and chronic inflammation35,36. In lupus and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis model, PMN inhibit T cell proliferation via Mac-1, H2O2, NO and arginase I37–39. Intriguingly, we found that PMN exosomes also had immunoregulatory potentials. PMN extracellular vesicles inhibit chemotaxis, adhesion and migration of PMN to endothelial cells through annexin 123,24, and suppress maturation and pro-inflammatory functions of immature monocyte-derived DCs via phosphatidylserine23,25. TNF-stimulated PMN exosomes inhibit M1 polarization and promote TGF-β secretion in macrophages40, and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine production in synoviocytes in rheumatoid arthritis (RA)18. In gout, PMN microvesicles induced by C5a suppress C5a-primed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and trigger TGF-β secretion19. Hence, PMN exosomes may serve as a ‘brake’ of inflammation in macrophages. Defective BD PMN exosomes downregulated suppression of IL-6 and TNF, and facilitated macrophage activation in BD. Given that PMN exosomes are characterized by low immunogenicity, prolonged circulation, and deep penetration41,42, PMN exosomes may provide promising therapy for BD.

Exosomal miR-122-5p suppressed TLR axis in macrophages. Previous studies report that miR-122-5p regulates innate immune response through TLR/NF-κB axis, alleviates intestinal epithelial injury by targeting NOD2 and SBP1 in Crohn’s disease43,44, attenuates inflammatory response and ROS production by targeting CPEB1 in spinal cord injury model45, downregulates inflammatory responses by targeting TLR4 in hepatoma cells and hepatic stellate cells46, and inhibits NF-κB and IRF3 pathways in teleost fish47, suggesting a conservative immunoregulatory function of miR-122-5p across species. Importantly, TLR/NF-κB axis promotes transcription of miR-122-5p precursor miR-122HG48. Therefore, miR-122-5p serves as negative feedback to inflammatory response, which is defective in BD PMN exosomes and induces impaired suppression of macrophages.

miR-122-5p also regulates the innate immune response through JAK-STAT1 signaling activated by IFN-β in macrophages. miR-122-5p modulates interferon/interferon-stimulated response element axis in HCV infection49,50. Autocrine of IFN-β is enhanced during TLR activation and promotes TLR-induced inflammation51, such as MCP-1 production by macrophages52 and IL-12 secretion by DCs53. Additionally, miR-122-5p promotes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) through overproduction of mtDNA-encoded proteins54, and inhibits glycolysis by targeting PKM255 and ALDOA56, which are reversed during M1 macrophage polarization57,58. Consistently, we found that miR-122-5p enhanced oxidative phosphorylation, suggesting that miR-122-5p might inhibit macrophage M1 polarization and inflammation via metabolic pathways. Of note, neutrophil exosomal miR-122-5p regulates apoptosis and proliferation of endothelial cells59. Taken together, miR-122-5p regulates macrophage activation and inflammation through multiple pathways, and delivering miR-122-5p through exosomes might offer a new target-based therapy for BD.

Our study had limitations. First, we focused on miRNAs in PMN exosomes, but other exosome components such as lncRNA, small molecule metabolites might also have immunoregulatory properties. Second, we investigated the immunoregulatory mechanism of PMN exosomes on macrophages, and other immune cells such as Th1 and Th17 cells are potentially regulated by PMN exosomes. Third, we showed that exosomal miR-122-5p suppressed macrophages in vitro, and whether the suppression is evident in BD patients in vivo remains elusive. Fourth, the mechanism of decreased exosomes in BD PMN remains unknown; we analyzed our transcriptome dataset (GSE205867)9 but didn’t find clues. We speculated proteomics approach might provide new insights for short-lived neutrophils.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that BD PMN exosomes were decreased in both quantity and miR-122-5p, which impaired the immunoregulatory potentials on macrophages through targeting IRF5 and suppressing IFN-β autocrine. Defective BD PMN exosomes suggested a new interaction mechanism between PMN and macrophages and played a role in BD pathogenesis.

Cell isolation and culture

Peripheral blood samples were collected, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from whole blood by density gradient centrifugation using Human Lymphocyte Separation Medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions (7111011, DAKEWE, SZ, China). PMN were isolated by erythrocyte lysis with subsequent centrifugation, as previously described61. The Neutrophil purity was checked by flow cytometry using CD11b and CD16 staining. Density gradient-isolated PBMC and PMN were maintained in complete DMEM (12491, Gibco, New York, USA) and X-VIVO 15 (BE02-054Q, LONZA), respectively. Anti-CD14 magnetic-isolated monocytes (130-050-201, Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) were seeded in complete DMEM supplemented with 50ng/mL M-CSF (m6518, Sigma, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA) for 7 days to differentiate into HMDM. THP-1 cells were purchased from Procell Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China (CL-0233) with authentication by STR analysis. THP-1 cells were stimulated with 50 ng/ml PMA (P1585, Sigma) in complete DMEM for 24 h to differentiate into adherent macrophages, which were rested for 24 h until use. The polarization of HMDM and THP-1 was assessed by flow cytometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Exosome isolation and identification

PMN (3*106/ml) were stimulated by 100 ng/ml LPS (437620, Sigma) or blank control (PBS) in X-VIVO for 24 h. The supernatant was centrifuged at 2000g, 30 min to remove cells and debris, then 20 ml supernatant was mixed with 10 ml Total Exosome Isolation reagent (4478359, Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, California, USA) and incubated at 4°C overnight, which was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 1 h to obtain the exosomes. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) were employed to identify exosomes. Western Blot of positive markers TSG101 (ab134045, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and CD63 (ab125011, Abcam) and negative exosome marker PCNA (HRP-60097, Proteintech, China) were performed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Supernatant TNF and IL-6 of HMDM or THP-1 macrophages stimulated with LPS and PMN exosomes, and supernatant TNF, IL-6 and IFN-β of HMDM or THP-1 macrophages stimulated with LPS and miRNA mimic/inhibitors or siRNA, were measured. Supernatant exosomes from PMN stimulated with or without LPS were measured. Quantification of supernatant exosomes (Abcam), IL-6 (430504, Biolegend, California, USA), TNF (430201, Biolegend) and IFN-β (EK1511, Sabbiotech, Greenbelt, Maryland, USA) were conducted in duplicate using a Multiskan FC Photometer (Thermo, USA) according to the manufacture’s instructions.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from macrophages using Trizol (15596026, Invitrogen), and was reverse transcribed using Fast All-in-One RT Kit (ES-RT001, ES Science, China). RT-qPCR was performed in triplicate with SYBR Green Master Mix (DBI-2043, DBI bioscience, German) and LightCycler 480 Thermocycler (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 5. The results were normalized to β-actin expression levels.

Flow cytometry

Macrophages were stained with the following fluorochrome-labeled antibodies after Fc block (564765, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA): CD14 (550787, M5E2, BD Biosciences), CD86 (374203, BU63, Biolegend), CD80 (305207, 2D10, Biolegend). For intracellular staining, HMDMs were fixed and permeabilized by Cytofix/Cytoperm and Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Biosciences) and were stained with anti-CD68 antibody (333807, Y1/82 A, Biolegend) for 50 min on ice. Cells were immediately analyzed with a BD FACSAria II Flow cytometry (BD Biosciences) and data were processed by FlowJo (Tree Star, Woodburn, Oregon, USA).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (HX-1862-2, Huaxingbio, China) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78442, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), quantified by BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Scientific), boiled at 95 °C for 10 min with SDS, subjected to 10% SDS–PAGE, and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). The membranes were blocked and incubated with the primary antibodies and then secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescent blots were detected by Tanon-5200 (Bio-Tanon, China), quantified by Image J v.1.50 g and normalized to β-actin expression as relative expression. Primary antibodies used included anti-IRF5 (ab33478, Abcam), anti-STAT1 (9172 T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA), anti-phos-STAT1 (7649 T, Cell Signaling Technology) and anti-β-actin (ab8227, Abcam).

Small RNA transfection

miRNA mimic (30 nM, RiboPharm, China) (including miR-122-5p (miR10000421-1-5), miR-134-5p (miR10000447-1-5) and miR-6501-5p (miR1CM001)), miRNA inhibitor (30 nM, RiboPharm) (including miR-122-5p inhibitor (miR20000421-1-5), miR-134-5p (miR20000447-1-5) and miR-6501-5p (miR1CM001)) and siRNA (50 nM, GenePharma, China) were transfected into macrophages using RNAimax (13778150, Life Technologies Corporation) according to manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Dual-luciferase assay

HEK293T cells (Procell) were transfected with 0.5ug wild-type or mutant IRF5 3’UTR (Supplementary Data 5) pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase miRNA Target Expression Vector (GenePharma, China) by lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Life Technologies Corporation), and 30 nM miR-122-5p by RNAimax. Cells were collected after 24 h to perform dual-luciferase reporter assay (E1910, Promega Corporation, USA). Data were normalized by firefly luciferase activity to Renella luciferase activity.

miRNA sequencing and data analysis

miRNA sequencing was conducted on Illumina HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA). Raw data of fastq format were processed through in-house perl scripts to obtain clean reads and the sequences kept were between 18nt and 32nt. Using Bowtie tools soft, the Clean Reads were mapped to genes from with Silva database, GtRNAdb database, Rfam database and Repbase database to filter ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) and other ncRNA and repeats. The remaining reads were used to detect known miRNA and novel miRNA predicted by comparing with Genome and known miRNAs from miRBase. The count matrix was analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.30.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with threshold of |log2 (Fold Change)|> 2 and p < 0.05.

Bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing was conducted on Illumina Novaseq 6000 (Illumina), and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. The reads were QCed using FastQC and were mapped to the GRCh38 reference human genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.5), and feature Counts (v1.5.0) was applied to count the reads mapped to each gene. The count matrix was analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.30.0). DEGs were identified with a threshold of |log2 (Fold Change)|> 0.8, adjusted p < 0.01 and baseMean ≥100. Detailed procedures are accessible on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession no. GSE252885).

The pathways from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database were assessed by Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using clusterProfiler (v3.0.4). Significantly enriched pathways were determined by the threshold of |normalized enrichment score | ≥1, p < 0.05 and adjusted p < =0.25.

miRanda was used to predict the target of miR-122-5p among downregulated differentially expressed transcriptional factors (DETF) by the threshold of score threshold(-sc)> 150 and energy(en)< −20. The downstream regulated DEGs of candidate DETFs were filtered by ChEA3 Database and Pearson correlation coefficient> 0.9. Finally, pathways from KEGG database were assessed by hypergeometric testing in downstream regulated DEGs of certain candidate DETF. Significantly enriched pathways were determined with p < 0.05.

Animal experiment

Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Strain NO. N000013) and were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, in a room with standard ambient temperature and humidity, and the animals had unrestricted access to food (Xietong Organism, 1010001) and water, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Female and male mice were evenly and randomly assigned to groups to receive intraperitoneal injection with miR122-5p agomir (10 µM, miR40000246-4-5, Ribobio, China) or PBS followed by 10 ng LPS after 24 h. 4 h later, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) asphyxiation inhalation, and cervical dislocation was performed as a secondary euthanasia procedure. And then peritoneal macrophages were harvested and were isolated with anti-F4/80 MicroBeads UltraPure (Miltenyi Biotec). TNF, IL-6, IRF5, CD80, and CD86 expression in peritoneal macrophages were detected by qRT-PCR, with β-actin as the internal reference gene. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of PUMCH (Approval No.: XHDW-2024-225).

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range. To compare two groups, independent-sample or paired t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed variables. One-way ANOVA test or two-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons with adjusted p using two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations were calculated using Pearson correlation analysis. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with p < 0.05 indicating significant differences. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.17.0 and R v4.0.2.
