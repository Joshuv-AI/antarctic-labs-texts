The dataset of multi-target vital signs monitored by FMCW radar
Authors: Guangyu Lei, Wei Cheng, Xipeng Yin, Yuqing Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dataset presents the raw data of respiration and heartbeat of two human subjects monitored by 60GHz Frequency-Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar and the validation data obtained by the electrocardiogram (ECG) front-end. During the radar-based vital signs monitoring, the subjects were simultaneously equipped with the ECG front-end, integrated with an Arduino board, to collect muscle electric signal waveforms, which enabled the estimation of the actual number of respirations and heartbeats of the subjects during the radar monitoring. To assess the impact of radar parameters, as well as the angle and distance between the subjects and the radar, on monitoring accuracy, the dataset classifies the positions of the human subjects within the scene into symmetric and asymmetric cases. Monitoring was conducted using various radar modulation bandwidths across different angles and distances in an open environment where only the human subjects were present. The objective of this dataset is to facilitate the development of a reliable system for monitoring multiple subjects' respiration and heartbeat using a single-view FMCW radar, emphasizing enhancing the radar's ability to distinguish between different targets and improving the accuracy of respiratory and heartbeat rate monitoring.Keywords: Contactless monitoring, Vital signs, Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW), Respiration, Heartbeat

Background

With the rapid development of intelligent sensing technology, FMCW radar has leveraged its unique advantage of high resolution to analyse vital signs such as respiratory and heart rates by capturing minute chest wall movements, enabling non-contact vital sign monitoring [1].

However, current research primarily focuses on precisely monitoring single-person respiratory and heartbeat activity using FMCW radar. Reference data for these studies are typically collected through methods such as pulse counting and heart rate wristbands. This experimental approach has two main drawbacks:

Single-person respiratory and heartbeat monitoring is limited in scope and needs to adequately meet the future demand for indoor multi-person vital sign monitoring [2].

The reference data collected through these methods are often inaccurate. Pulse counting by manual techniques is highly subjective, leading to potential inaccuracies. Although heart rate wristbands eliminate manual counting, they are limited by the simplicity of the devices, resulting in less accurate monitoring. Furthermore, pulse counting and heart rate wristbands only collect heartbeats over fixed periods to obtain heart rate and do not capture detailed information about the heartbeat and respiratory waveforms [3,4]. This lack of thorough data hinders the monitoring of abnormalities in vital signs.

Data Description

This dataset utilized the IWR6843ISK radar board in conjunction with the DCA1000EVM data capture card as the primary radar measurement equipment for monitoring respiratory and heartbeat data for two human subjects. The IWR6843ISK, a high-performance FMCW radar device, boasts high resolution and high sensitivity, making it significantly advantageous for monitoring human physiological parameters [5]. The DCA1000EVM data capture board facilitates high-speed data transmission and real-time data processing, providing reliable data support for the experiment [6].

To further collect reference data, this dataset also employed an Arduino board paired with the ADS1492 module to measure the number of heartbeats and breaths over a fixed duration by capturing the bioelectrical signals of the human body. The ADS1492 module, with its high precision and high sampling rate, can capture subtle signals from heart and lung activities [7]. The Arduino board offers high expandability and a convenient programming environment, allowing the bioelectrical signal waveforms collected by the ADS1492 module to be transmitted in real time to the PC. After synchronous processing on the PC, this setup enables precise measurement of human respiration and heartbeats [8]. This combination not only enhances the accuracy of data collection but also increases the flexibility and reliability of the experiment.

Additionally, this dataset set multiple radar bandwidths and human positions to monitor respiratory and heartbeat rates at various angles and distances, exploring the impact of these factors on the monitoring results. By adjusting the radar bandwidth and human positions, the dataset supports other studies in better understanding the parameter optimization of FMCW radar in multi-person vital sign monitoring. Collecting data from different angles and distances also provides studies with abundant experimental data to investigate the influence of the subjects’ angle and distance relative to radar on the accuracy of monitoring, which aids in developing more advanced direction-finding algorithms, signal separation, and interference elimination algorithms, thereby enhancing the application effectiveness of FMCW radar in complex environments.

The dataset provides empirical data and theoretical support for applying FMCW radar in measuring human physiological parameters and offers valuable references for future multi-target measurement and signal processing technology development. This is significant for promoting the application of FMCW radar technology in fields such as medical and sports monitoring.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Overview of the test scenario

This dataset utilizes an ECG module synchronized with the radar for concurrent monitoring. In the experimental scenario, the subjects face the radar directly without any stationary objects obstructing the radar and the target. The ECG module is connected to the subjects' wrists and the right side of their abdomen using medical electrodes. The ECG module synchronously outputs the respiration and heartbeat waveforms obtained from muscle electric signal acquisition. These waveforms are then analyzed on a PC to calculate the number of respirations and heartbeats during the test period, providing reference values for validating the accuracy of the radar-based monitoring solution. The accuracy and reliability of the radar's vital sign monitoring at different distances can be assessed by comparing the radar and ECG module monitoring results. The data collection scenario is shown in Fig. 1, with the total test duration being one minute.

Experimental scenario. The geometric relationship between the human body and the radar is strictly parametric. The human body remains stationary during the measurements and is not displaced with respect to the radar.

To further explore the impact of the subjects’ positions within the scene on monitoring accuracy and to provide additional data for research on target localization within the scene, this dataset includes multiple experimental positions. The subjects were placed in different locations for data collection, with the variables for each position illustrated in the Fig. 2.

The angles between the forward line of the radar and the line connecting the subjects to the radar are denoted as θ1, θ2, and the lengths of the lines connecting the subjects to the radar are denoted as r1 and r2, respectively. Based on the spatial distribution of the two subjects in the monitoring scenario, the positions are categorized as symmetrical and asymmetrical. The monitoring data for these two positions are stored in the “1_AsymmetricalPosition” and “2_SymmetricalPosition” folders, respectively. A total of nine different positions were tested, with data stored in folders labeled “position_ (#)” (#=1, 2, 3…9). The IWR6843ISK radar board is equipped with three transmitting antennas and four receiving antennas. The arrangement of the transmitting and receiving antennas, as shown in Fig. 4, forms a virtual antenna array, as shown in Fig. 5.

This dataset enabled all transmitting and receiving antennas of the IWR6843ISK. Since the subjects are distinguished by their horizontal angles relative to the radar, using the virtual antenna array data formed only by Tx1 and Tx3 is recommended, resulting in a horizontal array of eight antennas.

Data framework and log file processing

The dataset consists of two types of files: the radar monitoring data files, which are binary bin files transmitted back by the FMCW radar board, and the human muscle electric signal waveforms synchronously recorded during radar monitoring, stored in .csv format. Each radar frame contains only one chirp signal, with a frame period of 50ms. A total of 1200 frames were obtained over one minute, while the muscle electric signal waveforms were sampled with a period of 8ms by ADS1292R, resulting in 7500 data points for one minute of acquisition.

The Dataset folder is divided into “1_AsymmetricalPosition” and “2_SymmetricalPosition” subfolders based on whether the positions of human subjects are symmetrical. The “1_Radar_Raw_Data” radar monitoring data and the “2_Log_data” folder contain muscle electric signals from both subjects. Within each subfolder, “position_ (#)” folders correspond to the testing positions. The radar bandwidth and test group numbers are annotated in the file names within the position folders. The data distribution framework is shown in Fig. 8.

The dataset distribution framework.

The muscle electric signal data collected by the ADS1292R, processed by the Arduino board, can be plotted and recorded on a PC, resulting in respiration and heartbeat waveforms. The respiration waveform captured by the ECG module is not a smooth and distinct periodic signal, requiring multiple moving average filtering processes to obtain a smooth waveform suitable for respiration counting. The number of respirations can then be determined by finding peaks in the time domain. A heartbeat counting threshold is set for the heartbeat data based on the characteristics and distribution of the ECG waveform values. A heartbeat is detected whenever the muscle electric signal signal exceeds this threshold. The pre-processed respiration and heartbeat waveforms are shown in Fig. 9, Fig. 10. The code for processing the log data to obtain the number of subjects’ respirations and heartbeats is included in the dataset under the name “read_log.m”.

Heatbeat waveform and count. The waveform contains the changes in the electrical signals of the body's muscles when the heartbeat is beating, reflecting the specific details of the heartbeat. The counting threshold is set at the peak of the heartbeat signal, and a heartbeat is considered to exist when the counting threshold is exceeded, so that the heartbeat can be counted.

Respiratory waveform and count. The waveform contains the subjects' muscle electrical signal changes when the subject is breathing, and the number of signal peaks after smoothing process can reflect the number of respirations.

In addition, the dataset is accompanied by the basic methodology and code for processing radar data, and the entire code is placed in “RadarDataProcessing.zip”.

The radar data processing code first decodes the radar's received binary data to obtain a 1200 × 200 × 8 data matrix (1200 frames, 200 sample length, eight virtual receiving antennas). In the main program, Main.m, the first step is to use Moving Target Indication (MTI) to eliminate the influence of stationary objects in the scene (the code provides two options: complex average subtraction and MTI). Next, based on the data from multiple antennas, an angle estimation algorithm is employed (the code includes three options: Conventional Beamforming (CBF) and Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR)) to generate a range-angle spectrum of the scene. After determining the positions of the human subjects within the scene, a weight vector is formed, and the beam is steered toward the angles corresponding to the subjects. The chest wall movement information of the subjects can then be extracted individually. This data is subsequently passed into the get_heartBreath_rate.m function, where differentiation, filtering, and frequency extraction are performed on the chest wall movement information, enabling the monitoring of vital signs for multiple targets. Limitations

While experimenting, all subjects were instructed to remain stationary. However, in some instances, there were deep breathing, hiccups, and slight movements from individual participants. These subtle actions can introduce challenges, as they may cause fluctuations in the radar data, leading to potential inaccuracies in the detection of monitoring vital signs. The anomalies introduced by these minor movements are visible in both the radar's raw data and the corresponding validation data. At the same time, however, these fluctuating experimental data are often consistent with the actual physiological habits of the subjects' bodies. In the future, these fluctuations can be addressed by developing more robust methods that can eliminate the effects of random physiological movements of the human body on vital sign monitoring.
