Comprehensive patient navigation is associated with increased active treatment retention in lung cancer care in rural Appalachia
Authors: John Harcha, Jenny Woodyard, Rory Phillips, Wendi Waugh, Jeremiah Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer mortality is disproportionately concentrated in rural Appalachia. Patient navigation may improve access, but evidence supporting comprehensive lung cancer navigation across all diagnostic pathways remains limited. We evaluated an all-pathway navigation program at a community cancer center serving a high-burden, predominantly publicly insured Appalachian population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 470 patients diagnosed with lung cancer at a single Commission on Cancer (CoC)-accredited community center between 2018 and 2022. In January 2019, a respiratory therapist-led program expanded to cover screening-detected, incidentally detected, and symptomatic presentations. The primary outcome was active treatment retention at the diagnosing institution, ascertained from the institutional cancer registry.ResultsTreatment retention was higher in the navigation era than before navigation [83.4% vs. 68.1%; absolute difference 15.3 percentage points; number needed to treat (NNT) approximately 7; odds ratio (OR) =2.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.43–3.89; P=0.001]. The association persisted after multivariable adjustment (OR =2.67), under inverse-probability weighting (OR =2.29), and under conservative bounding of patients absent from the registry (worst-case OR =1.63, still significant); single-year contrasts were underpowered and non-significant. Median diagnosis-to-treatment time was 22 days, without prolongation during coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Survival showed no detectable insurance gradient (an underpowered comparison) despite predominantly public coverage (approximately 77% Medicare or Medicaid); active treatment [time-averaged hazard ratio (HR) =0.47] and stage IV disease (3.42) were the strongest survival predictors.ConclusionsIn a high-burden Appalachian population, comprehensive all-pathway navigation was associated with higher treatment retention at the diagnosing institution; navigation itself was not independently associated with survival, which was more strongly associated with treatment receipt, consistent with an indirect care-delivery mechanism. These findings show that a respiratory therapist-led, all-pathway navigation program can be built and sustained in a small community center, and support further consideration of navigation as core community cancer infrastructure.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Comprehensive navigation across screening, incidental, and symptomatic pathways was associated with higher active treatment retention at the diagnosing institution [83.4% vs. 68.1%; odds ratio (OR) =2.36, 95% confidence interval: 1.43–3.89; multivariable OR =2.67; number needed to treat approximately 7].

• Navigation was not independently associated with survival; active treatment was the dominant survival driver, consistent with navigation acting indirectly through treatment (a care-delivery rather than biologic mechanism).

• Diagnostic timeliness was preserved throughout the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, and survival showed no detectable insurance gradient (an underpowered comparison) in this predominantly publicly insured population.

What is known and what is new?

• Patient navigation improves access to cancer care in underserved populations, but the lung cancer literature has focused mainly on the screening pathway, time-to-diagnosis outcomes, and nurse-, social-worker-, or lay-navigator models.

• This is among the first evaluations of an all-pathway lung cancer navigation program using treatment retention as a pragmatic primary endpoint, in a respiratory therapist-led model serving a high-burden rural Appalachian population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Treatment retention should be considered a primary endpoint for navigation evaluation, alongside time-based metrics that can underestimate program value.

• This respiratory therapist-led, all-pathway model is feasible in a small community setting and may be adaptable to other community and critical-access hospitals.

• The findings support further consideration of navigation as core community cancer infrastructure, as Medicare’s Enhancing Oncology Model and Commission on Cancer accreditation increasingly recognize.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States, with an estimated 229,410 new cases and 124,990 deaths projected in 2026 (1). Although advances in screening, diagnostic pathways, and systemic therapies have improved outcomes overall, these gains have not been equitably realized. Rural and persistent-poverty counties experience lung cancer mortality rates approximately 20% higher than the national average, and recent analyses demonstrate that declines in lung cancer mortality have occurred more rapidly in urban than in rural areas, widening rather than closing the rural-urban gap (2,3).

Appalachian Ohio exemplifies this persistent inequity. Scioto County, where our institution is located, has an age-adjusted lung cancer incidence of 88.9 per 100,000 (68% higher than the national rate of 52.9) and a mortality rate of 60.6 per 100,000, compared with 36.7 nationally (4). Structural disadvantage is deeply embedded: in our cohort, only about one in five patients carried private insurance, and roughly four in five relied on public coverage or were uninsured. The intersection of extreme disease burden, economic vulnerability, and limited access to specialty care defines the context in which lung cancer diagnosis and treatment occur and underscores the need for interventions that operate effectively within real-world community settings.

Patient navigation has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate barriers to cancer care, particularly in underserved populations. A recent systematic review confirmed that navigation improves access to treatment and clinical outcomes across cancer types (5). However, the lung cancer navigation literature remains fragmented. A 2025 scoping review found that most studies focus narrowly on screening programs or isolated segments of the care pathway, rather than the full diagnostic continuum (6). Moreover, commonly reported outcomes such as time to diagnosis or screening adherence may not fully capture a navigation program’s effectiveness in ensuring that patients ultimately receive definitive treatment (7).

This study addresses three critical gaps in the current evidence. First, prior work has not evaluated a navigation model that comprehensively spans all realistic diagnostic pathways in lung cancer (screening-detected, incidentally detected, and symptom-detected) within a single coordinated program. Second, treatment retention at the diagnosing institution, a pragmatic indicator of whether navigation successfully retains patients through definitive therapy, has been underexamined as a primary outcome. Third, few community-based intervention studies have been conducted in populations with such extreme socioeconomic disadvantage, where approximately 77% of patients rely on public insurance. We therefore evaluated the association of an all-pathway lung cancer navigation program with treatment retention, diagnostic timeliness, stage distribution, and survival at a community cancer center serving a high-burden Appalachian population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1255/rc).

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients identified through institutional case-finding for lung cancer at Southern Ohio Medical Center (SOMC) for the calendar years 2018 through 2022. Under standardized registry date-of-diagnosis coding, 56 of these cases carry a diagnosis date in late 2017, so the pre-navigation period spans the 2017–2018 diagnosis years. This was an uncontrolled before-after (pre/post) comparison with a historical control, contrasting outcomes among patients diagnosed before the January 2019 program expansion with those diagnosed afterward. SOMC is a Commission on Cancer (CoC)-accredited community cancer center that serves the southern Ohio and northern Kentucky Appalachian region. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Southern Ohio Medical Center Institutional Review Board on June 6, 2022, under expedited review (No. 2022-04; FWA00002833). Given the retrospective record-review study design, the IRB granted a waiver of informed consent.

Lung cancer navigation program

Origin and timeline

Coordinated navigation at SOMC began in November 2015 as a registered respiratory therapist (RRT)-led service supporting low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening, initially comprising a single full-time clinical navigator. In late 2018, a structured internal review of consecutive radiology reports identified a substantial number of incidentally detected pulmonary nodules, frequently discovered on imaging ordered by other service lines such as the emergency department (ED), for which patients had been discharged with instructions to follow up with their primary care physician; on audit, at least half did not. On the basis of this gap and its attendant patient-safety and medicolegal implications, a second full-time clinical navigator was added in December 2018, and comprehensive, all-pathway navigation was launched in January 2019; coverage of symptomatic presentations was incorporated at the same time through full integration of the navigators into the weekly multidisciplinary thoracic tumor board. The program is presently staffed by 2.5 clinical full-time equivalents. The two registered respiratory therapist leads who form the program’s consistent footprint divide responsibility by pathway (one originating on the screening pathway and one, added in December 2018, focused on the incidental pathway), supported by a registered nurse navigator, with coverage shared across pathways as case volume requires. Screening-detected patients were navigated throughout the study period; incidental and symptomatic navigation began with the January 2019 expansion.

Workforce, structure, and access

The program is RRT-led and is situated administratively within pulmonary services, to which the navigators report. Navigators are co-located in the pulmonary office, affording direct access to the pulmonary physicians and to the pulmonary function laboratory, where same-day spirometry can be obtained during diagnostic triage. Navigator roles require registered-nurse or registered-respiratory-therapist credentialing; the program does not require a separate navigation certificate. A respiratory-therapy or pulmonary background offers practical advantages for lung cancer navigation, including familiarity with pulmonary function and bronchoscopy workflows, established relationships with pulmonologists, and comfort with the diagnostic procedures central to lung cancer evaluation. The founding navigator developed expertise through dedicated weekly imaging-review sessions mentored by the institution’s pulmonary physicians, followed by on-the-job training in institutional systems, care pathways, and longitudinal follow-up. Wrap-around social work and financial and nutritional counseling are provided through the cancer center. The team maintains a single dedicated telephone line accessible to both patients and clinicians and is integrated into the institutional electronic health record (EHR) through a task-based referral workflow alongside dedicated navigation tracking software. Navigators coordinate care but do not make clinical decisions; when a clinical decision is required, a pulmonary physician is engaged directly or the thoracic surgeon is contacted through the EHR.

Lung cancer patient-navigation process map. DD, detection-to-diagnosis interval; DT, diagnosis-to-treatment interval; EHR, electronic health record; LDCT, low-dose computed tomography; PET, positron emission tomography.

The program serves the three pathways through which lung cancer presents: screening-detected (LDCT), incidentally detected (a pulmonary nodule identified on imaging performed for another indication, including ED encounters), and symptomatic. Patients enter at pathway-specific points and converge on a common downstream workflow. After detection, the navigator becomes the patient’s consistent point of contact and coordinates the diagnostic and staging cascade [biopsy or bronchoscopy, staging imaging including positron emission tomography (PET), insurance pre-certification, and the associated specialty appointments], expediting the workup and, for early-stage disease, the determination between surgical and radiation treatment. Every newly diagnosed lung cancer is presented prospectively at the weekly multidisciplinary thoracic tumor board as an institutional requirement; participants include navigation, radiology, pathology, thoracic surgery, pulmonary medicine, medical and radiation oncology, genetic counseling, survivorship, and clinical-trials enrollment, conducted by videoconference from 2020 onward to maximize attendance. During conference, the navigator records treatment decisions and supports their execution. Navigation generally concludes at the initiation of first treatment, continuing in complex cases at the treating physician’s request.

Conceptual framework of navigation barriers and interventions.

In this rural Appalachian population, the predominant barriers to timely treatment were transportation and travel distance and, prominently, limited understanding of the diagnosis and longstanding mistrust of the healthcare system. The model addresses these chiefly through relationship building (providing a single, familiar point of contact as patients move through a fragmented system) and through persistent telephone follow-up, coordination of transportation and family support, insurance pre-certification, referral for financial counseling, and proactive outreach for missed appointments.

Tracking

Navigators maintained a prospective registry of patients with incidental nodules and active lung cancer in a structured workbook, with the screening population tracked in dedicated screening software. The tracked dataset was developed in connection with the institution’s participation in the Association of Community Cancer Centers (ACCC) and centered on standardized timeliness metrics: the detection-to-diagnosis interval (DD) and diagnosis-to-treatment interval (DT), with the latter recorded separately for treatment delivered at the institution versus at an outside entity.

Patient population

All patients with a new diagnosis of lung cancer during the study period were included. No exclusion criteria were applied, and the analytic cohort represents a complete census of lung cancer diagnoses at the institution. Patients diagnosed prior to January 1, 2019 who were neither screening-detected nor incidentally detected (i.e., symptomatic presentations prior to program expansion) comprised the pre-navigation comparison group. Patients diagnosed through LDCT screening were navigated throughout the entire study period. Navigation status was defined as follows: patients diagnosed before January 1, 2019 who were neither screening-detected nor incidentally detected were classified as the pre-navigation group, and all other patients were classified as the navigation era.

Variables and outcomes

The primary outcome was active treatment at the diagnosing institution (treatment retention), defined as receipt of surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or combination-modality treatment at our institution. Treatment status was ascertained from coded first-course therapy in the institution’s CoC registry, abstracted under standardized North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR) definitions. Patients who received no cancer-directed therapy, hospice-only care, or palliative-only care, or who received their treatment entirely at another facility, were classified as not receiving active treatment at our institution.

Diagnostic pathway was categorized as screening-detected (LDCT), incidentally detected (imaging performed for other indications, including ED imaging), or symptomatic/other. Disease stage was categorized according to registry-abstracted American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging as stages I through IV. Insurance coverage at diagnosis, abstracted from the cancer registry, was grouped as Medicare, Medicaid, private, or uninsured. Histology was abstracted from the registry as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), small cell lung cancer (SCLC), or carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumor, and was dichotomized as SCLC versus NSCLC/other for multivariable modeling.

Timeliness metrics included DD and DT. Follow-up time was calculated in months from the date of diagnosis, with vital status ascertained from institutional records.

Registry adjudication of the treatment endpoint

The primary endpoint underwent a source-data audit. The original treatment field, maintained in a clinical tracking record, could not reliably distinguish an unrecorded value from a confirmed absence of treatment; we therefore re-ascertained treatment for every patient from the institutional CoC registry, which records first-course therapy under standardized NAACCR definitions, linking patients by medical record number under the approved IRB protocol. Registry abstraction is performed by certified tumor registrars applying these standardized definitions independently of, and blind to, the study hypothesis, and the re-ascertainment was undertaken as a data-quality correction to ensure accurate outcome classification. The registry was designated a priori as the gold-standard source for the treatment endpoint, and the endpoint definition and the discrepancy-resolution rule were fixed under the IRB-approved amendment before the era-stratified rates were examined, so that the correction could not be tuned to the comparison of interest. This registry-based validation was conducted under an institutional review board-approved amendment to the study protocol (SOMC-IRB-2026-2; approved June 16, 2026), determined exempt under 45 CFR 46.104(d)[4] as secondary research on existing data; no new variables were added, and investigator access to identifiable information was limited to data reconciliation within a secure, access-controlled environment. Of the 470 patients, 416 were located in the registry and 399 had a first-course treatment episode matching the study diagnosis; 390 had an unambiguous treatment determination and constitute the adjudicated treatment cohort (Figure 3). Agreement between the original field and the registry was 83.8%, and discrepancies were bidirectional: 53 patients coded as untreated in the original field had in fact received treatment per the registry (25 pre-navigation, 28 navigation era), and 10 coded as treated were not confirmed. Because the discrepancies occurred in both eras, adjudication raised the absolute treatment rates in both groups (from 50.4% to 68.1% before navigation and from 67.8% to 83.4% in the navigation era); the unadjusted odds ratio (OR) was essentially unchanged by the correction (2.07 on the original field versus 2.36 adjudicated), whereas the adjusted and weighted estimates increased for the reason given below. The adjusted and inverse-probability-weighted estimates were higher than in the earlier complete-case analysis primarily because registry abstraction documented stage and payer for the large majority of patients [payer for 388 of the 416 registry-located patients (93%), versus 73% in the original field], substantially reducing the complete-case deletion that had attenuated those estimates; residual missing payer was modeled with an indicator category rather than by listwise deletion. All treatment analyses use the registry-adjudicated endpoint; patients not located in the registry are handled in a bounding sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with percentages and compared using chi-squared tests. Continuous variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The primary analysis used logistic regression to estimate the association between navigation status and receipt of active treatment. A multivariable logistic regression model included navigation status, histologic subtype (SCLC vs. NSCLC/other), stage group, insurance group, age at diagnosis, and rurality [rural-urban continuum code (RUCC)]. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs).

Overall survival was estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods, with differences assessed using log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models included navigation status, stage group, insurance group, age, receipt of active treatment, and SCLC histology. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. A sensitivity analysis employing a stratified Cox model (stratified by stage group) was performed to address potential violations of the proportional hazards assumption.

Pre-specified sensitivity analyses included: (I) reclassification of pre-2019 incidentally detected cases as non-navigated; (II) restriction to symptomatic patients only; (III) restriction to 2018 versus 2019 diagnoses (tightest temporal comparison); (IV) restriction to NSCLC cases; and (V) restriction to early-stage disease (stages I–II). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for baseline comparisons. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using stabilized propensity-score weights was conducted to assess sensitivity to measured confounding. To complement the conditional ORs, marginal (standardized) treatment effects were estimated by g-computation and by augmented inverse-probability weighting (AIPW), reporting the absolute risk difference and risk ratio with bootstrap confidence intervals. To bound the influence of patients not located in the registry, the primary comparison was repeated under (I) their originally recorded treatment status and (II) a worst-case assumption that all such patients were untreated.

The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated from the absolute risk difference. Interaction terms tested whether the navigation effect differed by stage or insurance. Mediation of navigation’s survival association through treatment receipt was assessed informally using the Baron-Kenny framework. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, with a two-sided significance threshold of α=0.05.

Results

By registry-abstracted histology, NSCLC accounted for 79.1%, SCLC for 17.3%, and carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumors for 3.6% of cases with known histology (n=416). Among patients with a known registry stage (n=404, 86.0%), 28.7% were stage I, 11.1% stage II, 22.8% stage III, and 37.4% stage IV. Among the 388 patients (82.6%) with a documented registry payer, 57.2% had Medicare, 19.6% Medicaid, 20.9% private, and 2.3% were uninsured (76.8% Medicare or Medicaid).

Diagnostic pathways and stage distribution

Of 470 patients, 54 (11.5%) were screening-detected, 130 (27.7%) incidentally detected, and 286 (60.9%) presented through other pathways. Screening-detected patients had markedly earlier registry-abstracted stage: 51.1% presented at stage I, compared with 26.5% of incidental and 25.4% of symptomatic patients (P=0.001; Table 1), and only 10.6% of screening patients presented at stage IV, versus 40.7% incidental and 41.0% symptomatic. Screening patients also had the highest rates of surgical first-course therapy (37.5% vs. 15.0% incidental and 17.5% symptomatic, registry-coded) and of registry-adjudicated active treatment at our institution (95.8% vs. 81.4% incidental and 73.8% symptomatic; P=0.002).

Navigation and treatment retention

In the registry-adjudicated cohort, active treatment retention at our institution was higher in the navigation era than before navigation: 83.4% (226/271) versus 68.1% (81/119) (P=0.001; Figure 4). The absolute risk difference was 15.3 percentage points (NNT of approximately 7), and the unadjusted OR was 2.36 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.43–3.89]. Standardized (g-computation) estimates gave a marginal risk difference of 14.6 percentage points (95% CI: 4.6–24.2) and a risk ratio of 1.21 (95% CI: 1.06–1.39); the doubly robust (AIPW) risk difference was 15.2 percentage points (95% CI: 5.3–25.0). When the endpoint was broadened to treatment received anywhere (not only at our institution), the association was essentially identical (87.1% vs. 73.9%; OR =2.38, 95% CI: 1.38–4.08); this broadened endpoint was ascertained from the registry’s treatment-location and Class-of-Case fields, which were not available in the original tracking record.

In multivariable logistic regression adjusting for registry-abstracted stage and payer, histology, age, and rurality, the navigation OR remained elevated at 2.67 (95% CI: 1.49–4.78; n=379; Table 3); the treated-anywhere model gave 2.97 (95% CI: 1.58–5.57). Because registry abstraction documented stage and payer for the large majority of patients, the complete-case deletion that had collapsed the original insurance-adjusted estimate (which retained only 328 of 470 patients) no longer occurred. That the adjusted OR slightly exceeded the unadjusted estimate is consistent with the non-collapsibility of the OR rather than with confounding or model instability: the marginal, standardized OR (2.31, by g-computation) was essentially equal to the unadjusted estimate (2.36) and below the conditional estimate (2.67), the signature of non-collapsibility rather than of confounding. Because the OR is non-collapsible and is inflated at the high treatment rates observed here, we regard the absolute risk difference (approximately 15 percentage points) and the risk ratio (1.21) as the more stable summaries of effect magnitude. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed adequate model fit (P=0.85), and VIFs indicated no multicollinearity (maximum generalized VIF 1.5).

Sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrated a positive navigation association with treatment retention across subgroups (Table S1). The association was strongest among early-stage patients (OR =5.11, 95% CI: 1.45–17.95; an imprecise, exploratory subgroup estimate) and was preserved on restriction to symptomatic presentations (OR =1.88, 95% CI: 1.02–3.44) and to NSCLC (OR =2.11, 95% CI: 1.19–3.73), and after reclassifying pre-2019 incidentally detected patients as non-navigated (OR =2.17, 95% CI: 1.32–3.57). The single-year 2018-versus-2019 contrast was directionally consistent but not statistically significant (OR =1.31, 95% CI: 0.66–2.59), as expected given the small number of patients and high treatment rates within any single year.

In inverse-probability-weighted analysis on the adjudicated endpoint, the stabilized-weight OR was 2.29 (95% CI: 1.34–3.91), concordant with the unadjusted and multivariable estimates; a complete-case analysis on the unadjudicated endpoint had attenuated this estimate to 1.20, reflecting the misclassified endpoint compounded by complete-case deletion of patients with missing insurance. Propensity-score methods remain limited for a time-based exposure (pre/post January 2019) that cannot fully separate the intervention from secular trends, and the weighted estimate is therefore interpreted alongside, rather than in place of, the bounding and sensitivity analyses.

The cohort spanned two pre-navigation diagnosis years (2017 and 2018) rather than one. Treatment rates were similar across these 2 years (pre-intervention trend OR 1.10 per year, P=0.56), showing no rise before the January 2019 expansion, although two calendar years provide limited power to characterize a secular trend; a non-significant step increase followed at 2019 (OR =1.43, P=0.46). Even so, two pre-navigation calendar years at a single center cannot fully separate the program expansion from concurrent secular change, and the single-year and narrowest-window contrasts (for example, 2018 versus 2019, OR =1.31) are individually underpowered and not statistically significant (Table S1). We therefore do not rest the inference on the temporal pattern; instead, we frame the navigation-treatment relationship as an association supported by the absence of a pre-intervention trend and by its consistency across multivariable adjustment, inverse-probability weighting, doubly robust estimation, and conservative bounding. A formal interrupted time-series analysis would require a longer pre-implementation baseline and is planned within a prospective extension of this cohort. No significant interaction was found between navigation and stage (P=0.14) or navigation and insurance (P=0.81).

Diagnostic timeliness and coronavirus disease (COVID) resilience

The median DD was 14 days (IQR, 6–24 days) and DT was 22 days (IQR, 12–32 days). There was no prolongation of DT during COVID-era years (Kruskal-Wallis P=0.54): median DT was 22 days in 2018, 20 in 2019, 22 in 2020, 24 in 2021, and 22 in 2022. DD showed a modest improvement over time (Kruskal-Wallis P=0.047) with no disruption during the pandemic period. These findings stand in contrast to national data showing a 9.6-day increase in time to systemic therapy initiation during the initial COVID period (8) and an estimated 17,390 excess cancer deaths during the first two pandemic years (9).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significant survival differences by stage group (log-rank P<0.001; Figure 5) and diagnostic pathway (log-rank P=0.003; Figure 6), but not by navigation status alone (log-rank P=0.56; Figure S1) or insurance group (log-rank P=0.30; Figure S2). Survival analyses used the 416 registry-located patients with ascertained vital status (264 deaths; median follow-up 16.1 months); the stage-group analysis was restricted to the 404 with documented stage and the payer analysis to the 388 with a documented registry payer category. Receipt of active treatment in the survival models is the same registry-ascertained variable used for the primary endpoint; the survival cohort [416] is slightly larger than the treatment-adjudication cohort [390] because survival analysis requires only ascertained vital status, not the unambiguous first-course-episode match required to adjudicate the binary treatment endpoint.

The Schoenfeld residual test identified a violation of the proportional hazards assumption globally (P<0.001), driven by stage (P<0.001) and treatment status (P<0.001); the primary exposure, navigation, did not violate the assumption (P=0.10). A stratified Cox model allowing each stage group its own baseline hazard left the estimates materially unchanged (Table S2): navigation HR 1.05 (95% CI: 0.79–1.39; P=0.76), active treatment HR 0.51 (95% CI: 0.37–0.70; P<0.001), SCLC HR 1.15 (P=0.40), and age HR 1.03 (P=0.001). Stratification did not fully resolve the global violation (P<0.001), which reflects a time-varying treatment effect (the survival benefit of active treatment is strongest early after initiation); we therefore present the stratified model as a sensitivity analysis rather than as evidence that the assumption holds.

Survival findings are consistent with mediation through treatment

The lack of an independent navigation-survival association is consistent with a mediation model. In the Baron-Kenny framework: (I) navigation predicted treatment receipt (OR =2.36, P=0.001); (II) treatment receipt predicted survival (HR =0.47, P<0.001); and (III) the navigation-survival association was non-significant after adjusting for treatment (HR =1.06). The pattern is consistent with a pathway running from navigation to treatment to survival; we interpret navigation as acting on survival indirectly, by increasing the probability that patients receive active therapy, rather than through any direct biologic effect. Because the total navigation-survival association is itself null and imprecisely estimated, and the treatment-survival association is subject to confounding by indication, we present this as a descriptive pattern consistent with mediation rather than as formal statistical mediation of a demonstrated survival effect. This is the expected mechanism for a care-delivery intervention.

Insurance equity

Insurance status showed no significant effect on survival in either univariate (log-rank P=0.30, an underpowered comparison) or multivariable analysis, in a population where approximately 77% of patients carry public insurance. The small uninsured subgroup (n=9) trended toward worse survival, but the number of events precludes inference. This absence of a detectable payer gradient contrasts with recent national data demonstrating persistent survival disparities by insurance status even in the immunotherapy era (10), and with analyses showing that Medicaid patients have worse survival than privately insured patients for resectable NSCLC (11). While our study cannot determine whether navigation specifically caused this equity, the finding is hypothesis-generating: comprehensive navigation may mitigate the insurance-linked barriers that drive payer-based disparities elsewhere.

Discussion

In a rural Appalachian community cancer center serving one of the nation’s highest-burden lung cancer populations, comprehensive patient navigation spanning all diagnostic pathways was associated with substantially higher active treatment retention at the diagnosing institution (83% in the navigation era vs. 68% before navigation; OR =2.36). This corresponds to an absolute between-era difference of approximately 15 percentage points in treatment retention (a number-needed-to-treat equivalent of approximately 7). This association was consistent across sensitivity analyses, remained positive in every examined subgroup, was directionally largest among early-stage patients (an imprecise, exploratory estimate; Table S1), and persisted under multivariable adjustment (OR =2.67), inverse-probability weighting (OR =2.29), and conservative bounding of patients not located in the registry (worst-case OR =1.63). Importantly, the observed survival association of navigation was consistent with mediation through treatment, aligning with the expected mechanism of a care-delivery intervention.

Treatment retention as a navigation outcome

The higher active treatment retention in the navigation era (83%, vs. 68% before navigation) is clinically meaningful. National benchmarks indicate that a substantial minority of patients with lung cancer never receive cancer-directed treatment (12), and receipt of guideline-concordant treatment is associated with approximately 24% lower mortality (13). Even with the higher baseline revealed by registry adjudication, roughly one in three patients in the pre-navigation era did not receive active treatment at the diagnosing institution. This reflects not only patient refusal or medical inoperability, but also the operational reality of community oncology care, where patients may fragment to outside facilities, default from follow-up, or fail to initiate therapy because of logistical, financial, or informational barriers.

Existing navigation literature predominantly emphasizes time to treatment, screening adherence, and treatment completion (5,6). In contrast, treatment retention at the diagnosing institution, defined as whether patients ultimately receive therapy where their cancer is identified, has received limited attention, despite being a pragmatic, patient-centered outcome with direct implications for continuity of care and multidisciplinary coordination. Our findings suggest that treatment retention merits consideration as a primary endpoint for future lung cancer navigation studies.

The all-pathway navigation model

Most published lung cancer navigation programs focus on a single diagnostic pathway, typically screening (6). Our model simultaneously navigates patients detected through screening, incidental imaging findings, and symptomatic presentations under a single coordinated team. This structure reflects the operational reality of a community cancer center, where all three pathways coexist and compete for shared diagnostic and treatment resources. In our cohort, screening accounted for only 11.5% of lung cancer diagnoses. Had navigation been limited to screening-detected patients, 88.5% of patients would have remained unsupported. The incidental detection pathway, which accounted for 27.7% of diagnoses, represents a particularly under-studied population in the navigation literature, despite its growing contribution as cross-sectional imaging becomes increasingly prevalent (14,15). Indeed, structured incidental-nodule programs have been reported to identify substantially more lung cancers than screening alone in community health systems (15). Our findings highlight the necessity of inclusive navigation models that address the full spectrum of real-world lung cancer presentation.

The navigator workforce

Our navigation program is respiratory therapist-led (with registered-nurse or registered-respiratory-therapist staffing) and operates under the umbrella of pulmonary services, a workforce model that to our knowledge has not been the focus of published lung cancer navigation studies. The American Lung Association includes RRTs among lung health navigators (16), but the peer-reviewed literature is dominated by nurse, social worker, and lay navigator models led from oncology rather than pulmonary services (5,6). A respiratory-therapy or pulmonary grounding brings specific advantages in lung cancer navigation: familiarity with pulmonary function and bronchoscopy workflows, established relationships with pulmonologists, and comfort with the diagnostic procedures central to lung cancer evaluation. Embedding navigation within pulmonary services ensures continuity from detection through treatment and positions navigators alongside the clinicians managing diagnostic workup. Formal comparison of navigator workforce models is beyond the scope of this study, but the feasibility and effectiveness of this RRT-led pulmonary services model in a community setting merits documentation, consistent with reports that community and regional centers can meaningfully improve access to lung cancer care through coordinated, navigation-anchored pathways (17).

Insurance equity in a predominantly publicly insured population

One of the more notable findings was the absence of detectable insurance-based survival disparities (an underpowered comparison) in a population where approximately 77% of patients carried public insurance. Nationally, insurance status is a robust predictor of lung cancer outcomes, with private insurance consistently associated with improved survival compared with Medicaid, even in the contemporary immunotherapy era (10,11).

We do not attribute this equity signal exclusively to navigation. It may reflect population homogeneity, a limited privately insured comparator group, or local care-delivery culture. However, this finding is hypothesis-generating and suggests that in settings where navigation effectively mitigates logistical, informational, and coordination barriers, the survival gradient typically associated with payer status may be attenuated. This interpretation is consistent with the mediation model: if navigation increases treatment receipt regardless of payer, and treatment is the primary survival driver, then payer becomes less relevant to outcomes.

COVID-era resilience

Diagnostic timeliness metrics demonstrated no prolongation during the COVID-19 pandemic (median time from diagnosis to treatment 22 days in 2020 and 24 days in 2021; overall median 22 days, with no significant variation across years, Kruskal-Wallis P=0.54). This contrasts with national reports documenting delays in systemic therapy initiation (8) and excess cancer mortality during the pandemic period (9). This stability is consistent with a navigation infrastructure that supported sustained patient tracking, follow-up, and scheduling coordination during widespread healthcare-system disruption, although, in the absence of a contemporaneous control, it cannot be causally attributed to that infrastructure. This descriptive observation is modest and hypothesis-generating, given the sparse literature on navigation as a COVID-era care-continuity mechanism.

Propensity score analysis and causal inference

On the registry-adjudicated endpoint, the inverse-probability-weighted OR (2.29) was concordant with the unadjusted (2.36) and multivariable (2.67) estimates, and the standardized risk difference (approximately 15 percentage points) was stable across g-computation, AIPW, and weighted analyses. An earlier complete-case analysis on the unadjudicated endpoint had attenuated this estimate to 1.20, an artifact of the misclassified endpoint compounded by complete-case deletion of patients with missing insurance, not evidence that confounders explained the association. We nonetheless emphasize that propensity-score methods are intrinsically limited for a time-based exposure such as navigation expansion, because secular trends, including the rising use of immunotherapy over the study period and institutional learning, cannot be fully separated from the intervention. A difference-in-differences design is likewise infeasible, as this single center provides no contemporaneous non-navigated comparison group. We therefore frame the navigation-treatment relationship as an association rather than a definitively causal effect, and we rely on the bounding and sensitivity analyses to constrain alternative explanations, while acknowledging that the short, 2-year pre-navigation baseline precludes a formal interrupted time-series test of secular change.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design limits generalizability, and the pre-post comparison relies on a historical control; navigation status reflects an era-level exposure rather than individual-level randomization, so the association we report cannot be interpreted as a definitively causal effect. Moreover, the January 2019 expansion bundled several concurrent changes (a second navigator, incidental- and symptomatic-pathway coverage, and tumor-board integration), and the short, 2-year pre-navigation baseline precludes a formal interrupted time-series analysis; the design therefore cannot isolate navigation from co-occurring secular or programmatic change, including the rising use of immunotherapy over the study period. We note, however, that treatment rates did not rise across the two pre-navigation years (pre-intervention trend OR 1.10 per year, P=0.56), which is reassuring against a simple pre-existing secular drift, though two calendar years cannot firmly establish the absence of a trend. Relatedly, because navigation was delivered as an integrated bundle with no component-level exposure variables recorded, the relative contribution of individual navigation functions (care coordination, barrier mitigation, multidisciplinary case presentation, financial navigation, and longitudinal follow-up) is collinear by construction and cannot be disaggregated in these data; we have committed this component-level analysis to a planned prospective extension of the cohort. Second, the primary endpoint relied on registry re-ascertainment because the original clinical tracking field could not reliably distinguish an unrecorded value from a true absence of treatment; although the correction was bidirectional and did not alter the direction of the association, the need for it reflects the limits of clinically maintained tracking data. Third, 80 patients fell outside the adjudicated cohort: 54 (11.5%) could not be located in the registry, and a further 26 either lacked a matched first-course episode or an unambiguous treatment determination. Because these patients were proportionally more common in the navigation era and were most plausibly treated entirely elsewhere, they were addressed through explicit bounding analyses, under which the association remained positive and statistically significant. Fourth, key clinical confounders were not available across both eras: smoking history was systematically captured only from the navigation era (2019) onward, and comorbidity, performance status, and molecular profiling were not abstracted; residual unmeasured confounding therefore cannot be excluded. We did not rely on a single quantitative robustness index for this threat; rather, we note that the principal measured case-mix confounder (registry-abstracted stage) did not differ significantly across navigation eras (P=0.11), that payer differed in a direction that would bias against the observed association (the pre-navigation group was more privately insured) and was adjusted for, and that the cohort is socioeconomically homogeneous (approximately 78% of patients fell within a single high-deprivation census-tract stratum), leaving little socioeconomic variation to confound the association beyond the rurality already included in the adjusted model; these features limit, but do not eliminate, the scope for residual confounding. Fifth, treatment was categorized as active versus inactive without formal assessment of guideline concordance, medical inoperability, or patient refusal. Sixth, the cohort was predominantly White, limiting inference regarding racial disparities. Seventh, the proportional hazards assumption was violated for stage and treatment status, although stratified Cox models produced materially unchanged results; relatedly, because active treatment is received after diagnosis but was modeled as a baseline attribute, the treatment HR is subject to guarantee-time (immortal-time) bias and likely overstates the early survival effect, so it should be read as associational. Finally, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons; the sensitivity, subgroup, and interaction analyses are interpreted as descriptive consistency checks rather than independent confirmatory tests, and the imprecise early-stage subgroup estimate in particular should be regarded as exploratory.

Conclusions

In a rural Appalachian community cancer center serving a population with lung cancer incidence 68% above the national average and with approximately 77% of patients insured through public programs, comprehensive patient navigation spanning all diagnostic pathways was associated with higher active treatment retention at the diagnosing institution (83% in the navigation era vs. 68% before navigation; OR =2.36, robust to conservative bounding at a worst-case 1.63), with a number-needed-to-treat equivalent of approximately 7. Navigation was not independently associated with survival; active treatment, not navigation, was the dominant survival driver, a pattern consistent with navigation acting indirectly by delivering patients to therapy rather than through any direct biologic effect, as expected for a care-delivery intervention. Insurance status was not associated with survival in this underpowered, predominantly publicly insured cohort. Diagnostic timeliness was preserved throughout the COVID-19 pandemic.

These findings support further consideration of comprehensive patient navigation as core community cancer infrastructure rather than a supplementary service layered onto existing care. As the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Enhancing Oncology Model recognizes navigation as an enhanced service eligible for per-beneficiary payments (18,19), and as CoC accreditation standards increasingly emphasize barrier identification and reduction (20), our experience demonstrates that a respiratory therapist-led navigation program housed within pulmonary services can be built and sustained in a community setting and was associated with higher treatment retention in one of the nation’s highest-burden lung cancer communities. The all-pathway model, which simultaneously navigates screening-detected, incidentally detected, and symptomatic patients, reflects the operational reality of community cancer centers, where leaving any diagnostic pathway unserved risks perpetuating treatment inequities.
