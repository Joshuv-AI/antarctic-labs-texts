Introduction

Muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) tends to infiltrate surrounding tissues and organs, has a high risk of metastasis, and may involve lymph nodes, lungs, liver, bones, and peritoneal carcinomatosis. For the surgical treatment of MIBC, robot‐assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) has been widely adopted in recent years owing to its advantages, including minimally invasive surgery and fast recovery. Compared with open radical cystectomy (ORC), RARC shows no difference in local recurrence rates [1]; however, some reports indicate a slightly higher incidence of extrapelvic lymph node metastasis and peritoneal carcinomatosis with RARC [2]. Potential causes include the dissemination of tumor cells into the abdominal cavity during pneumoperitoneum manipulation during RARC and inadequate postoperative intraperitoneal irrigation [3]. Therefore, managing the risk of intraoperative and postoperative peritoneal dissemination is crucial.

We present a rare case in which peritoneal dissemination originated from a previously undiagnosed colorectal cancer detected during follow‐up after RARC for MIBC, contrary to expectations of bladder cancer recurrence.

Case Presentation

A 67‐year‐old man with a history of diabetes mellitus and current smoking was admitted after experiencing macroscopic hematuria for 8 months. An abdominal ultrasound and computed tomography (CT) showed bladder lesions measuring up to 4 cm in diameter (Figure 1a), and no distant metastases or signs of colorectal cancer were observed. As the patient had no gastrointestinal symptoms, colorectal cancer screening examinations, including fecal occult blood testing, were not conducted at this time. Urinary cytology confirmed the presence of cancer cells. Transurethral resection of the bladder tumor confirmed high‐grade, muscle‐invasive urothelial carcinoma (Figure 1b,c). After two courses of neoadjuvant gemcitabine‐cisplatin chemotherapy, RARC with extracorporeal urinary diversion was performed. Functional end‐to‐end anastomosis was used for bowel reconstruction, and a 15 cm ileal conduit was created for urinary diversion. The operative time was 429 min, and blood loss was 20 mL. Postoperative pathology revealed small foci of residual urothelial carcinoma in situ with no lymph node metastasis. On postoperative day 2, a surgical site infection developed and subsequently resolved spontaneously. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 14. One month after surgery, an infected lymphocyst was found, and puncture drainage was performed. Subsequently, he was admitted twice because of complaints of slight abdominal pain and constipation, which improved quickly.

Seven months postoperatively, the patient was admitted with small bowel obstruction (Figure 2). The CT scan did not detect any tumor mass suggestive of bladder cancer recurrence or of any other primary malignancy. Accordingly, the patient was diagnosed with postoperative ileus. As symptoms did not improve with ileus tube placement, open surgery was performed on the 22nd day of hospitalization to resolve the bowel obstruction. Intraoperatively, numerous disseminated tumor nodules were found in the pelvis, involving the small intestine, resulting in its obstruction. Bypass surgery was performed at the level of the transverse colon. Pathological examination of the peritoneal nodules suggested adenocarcinoma (Figure 3), different from the primary urothelial carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry revealed that the tumor cells were GATA3‐negative and CDX2‐positive, indicating a colorectal cancer origin. Although colonoscopy was not feasible because of prior bypass surgery, positron emission tomography (PET)‐CT showed strong accumulation in the ascending colon and disseminated tumors (Figure 4). The findings of PET‐CT and pathological diagnosis confirmed primary colon cancer and its peritoneal metastasis. Genetic analysis to select therapeutic agents for unresectable colorectal cancer detected a BRAF mutation. The patient was then treated with capecitabine and oxaliplatin by a medical oncologist.

Discussion

We present an unusual case of peritoneal carcinomatosis occurring 7 months after RARC for MIBC, which was not a recurrence but originated from an unexpected colorectal cancer.

An advanced stage of bladder cancer (cT3–4 or cN+) is associated with an increased risk of peritoneal carcinomatosis [4]. The incidence of peritoneal carcinomatosis after RARC was reported to be 3%–5% [1, 4], but this was not significantly different from ORC treatment [1, 4]. In this case, initially, based on its course, this peritoneal lesion was suspected to be a recurrence of bladder cancer. However, pathological examination detected adenocarcinoma, suggesting that it originated from a previously undetected colon cancer.

While more than 90% of colon cancers are adenocarcinomas, only approximately 2% of bladder cancers are primary adenocarcinomas [5]. Genetic analysis for chemotherapy selection identified BRAF mutation (V600E) in the disseminated tumor in this case. BRAF mutation colorectal cancer is more common in women and older adults, particularly in the ascending colon, and has a poor prognosis [6, 7]. Colorectal cancers with BRAF mutations are more prone to peritoneal dissemination than wild‐type colorectal cancers [8]. In this case, the disseminated tissue showed similar characteristics to those of colon cancer, including BRAF mutations.

The primary colorectal cancer site could not be resected; therefore, its pathological stage remains unknown. The peritoneal dissemination from the colon cancer may have been caused not only by the advanced stage with possible serosal invasion, but also by tumor cell spillage during urinary/bowel reconstruction. In Tsunoda et al.'s [9] study (n = 34) using intestinal fluid obtained from resected colorectal cancer specimens, cancer cells were found in 75% of the specimens, many of which were viable, even at 15 cm apart from the oral‐side edge. McGrew et al. [10] ligated the oral and anal bowel of the tumor immediately after laparotomy and found no tumor cells in the oral or anal bowel at the ligated site (n = 50). These findings suggest that intraoperative bowel manipulation could result in the spillage of cancer cells from the bowel opening. In this case, the possibility of tumor cell spillage from the ascending colon during bowel and urinary reconstruction cannot be excluded.

Abdominal contrast‐enhanced CT before RARC and the bypass surgery did not indicate any abnormality in the ascending colon. Recently, Shkurti et al. [11] reported a 59% overall agreement between clinical T stage with CT and postoperative pathological T stage of colon cancer (n = 44 471), with a positive predictive value of 0.92 and negative predictive value of 0.51. These findings suggest that detecting colon cancer precisely using CT alone is difficult owing to the high incidence of false negatives. In this case, even from a retrospective view, the preoperative CT scan did not confirm the ascending colon cancer. Abdominal accumulation in the ascending colon was detected only by PET imaging.

Conclusions

In this case, intestinal obstruction occurred 7 months after RARC for advanced bladder cancer, which was confirmed to be peritoneal carcinomatosis through laparotomy. Pathological examination of peritoneal nodules revealed metastasis from colorectal cancer. After BRAF testing, standard chemotherapy for colorectal cancer was administered. Although bladder cancer recurrence was suspected from the course, avoiding preconceptions and considering metastasis from another primary cancer enabled appropriate treatment.
