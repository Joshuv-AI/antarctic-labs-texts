An Anatomic Study of Normal Humeral Shaft Bowing
Authors: Margaret A. Sinkler, Robby Goldberg, Drew Williamson, Marsalis Brown, Harry A. Hoyen, Raymond W. Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Although humeral bowing is well known, the character and magnitude of this bow have not been studied. Through the use of paired osteological specimens, we examined the full length of the humerus with the use of a digitizer to analyze the diaphyseal humeral anatomy.Methods:Eight hundred forty-nine cadaveric humeri from an osteological collection were assessed with a digitizer to determine humeral morphology and bowing. Each bow was evaluated for the magnitude, angulation, and location. Multiple regression analysis was done to determine the relationship between sex and race and the measured parameters.Results:The average overall humeral length was 310 ± 20 mm. Six hundred fifty-two humeri (77%) had one bow, and 197 (23%) had two bows. The single-bow apex was located at 71% ± 4% of the full humeral length with a magnitude of 2.0 ± 1.4-mm displacement from the central shaft axis. Double-bow apices were located at 32% ± 4% and 72% ± 3% of the full humeral length with magnitudes of 4.6 ± 2.6 and 2.0 ± 1.8 mm. Single-bowed humeri demonstrated shaft angulation of 2.9° ± 1.7° at the bow apex, whereas double-bowed humeri demonstrated shaft angulations of 4.7° ± 2.2° proximally and 3.3° ± 1.8° distally. Female sex correlated with shorter length and single-bowed humeri. Black specimens correlated with longer length, more proximally located bows and single-bowed humeri.Conclusion:The humeral diaphyseal morphology in a large osteological collection demonstrate that almost one fourth of the specimens had a double bow with notable correlations with sex and race. As the indications for intramedullary fixation of humerus fractures grow, the bow characteristics should be considered in humeral nail placement and future designs.Level of Evidence:V

Because of increased interest and advances in both shoulder and elbow arthroplasty, the proximal and distal morphology of the humerus has been well described in the literature.1-4 Studies regarding the proximal humerus have highlighted the variability between subjects but its relative consistency when comparing laterality within the same patient.5 The extramedullary proximal landmarks on the humerus, including the inclination angle, medial wall angle of the bicipital groove, radius of the curvature, retroversion angle, humeral head offset, and humeral head height, have all been well characterized.2,3,5 These studies highlight the wide range of head and shaft dimension and head position in relation to the shaft that exists between the patients.3 Similarly, for the distal humerus, anthropometric studies have extensively described the surface of the capitellum and trochlea, geometric center of each articulation, olecranon fossa shape and depth, trochlear constriction, extension, and symmetry, and flexion-extension axis.6-8

Understanding the complexity and variability of the morphology of the proximal and distal humerus has modernized and optimized the available arthroplasty implants based on anatomy and function. This anatomic knowledge has proved clinically valuable because improper understanding of the native anatomic variation among patients led to reduced range of motion and implant migration from the ideal anatomic position.9-11 Although several studies have demonstrated an in-depth understanding of the morphological variations of the proximal and distal humerus,7,12 there is a lack of knowledge regarding the transition from the proximal humerus continuing distally and the distal humerus moving proximally especially in the sagittal plane, likely because full-length CT scans of the humerus are uncommon.

We sought to characterize the diaphyseal region of the humerus, especially the sagittal bow. The osteology of this region may have notable implications for long intramedullary nailing of humeral shaft fractures, long diaphyseal humeral plate fixation, and intermediate and long-stemmed humeral components used in arthroplasty. Through the use of paired osteological specimens, we examined the full length of the humerus in a reproducible and consistent orientation. The quantity of specimens in this study combined with the use of a digitizer is a novel and innovative analysis of the diaphyseal humeral anatomy.

Methods

This study used dry cadaveric samples taken from the Hamann-Todd Osteological Collection, a collection that is well-documented and validated in the literature. It contains the complete disarticulated skeletons of more than 3000 individuals, collected from Cleveland's unclaimed dead between 1912 and 1938.13 The collection has associated demographic information for each individual, including age at death, sex, and ethnicity.

Measurements from the cadaveric humeri were collected using a MicroScribe G2 digitizer (Solution Technologies). The MicroScribe records 3D positional data with an accuracy of ±0.015 (0.381 mm) into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft). The digitizer was calibrated and was assessed before measurements of each specimen and was recalibrated after every fifth specimen or as needed. Each cadaveric humerus was secured at the surgical neck and positioned vertically to establish the 3D grid (Figure 1).

Picture demonstrating set up of the cadaveric humerus specimen and the MicroScribe.

The data establishing the central axis of the humerus were collected by scanning the shaft of the humerus in three dimensions using the digitizer. However, in “scan planes” mode, the software recorded the tip of the digitizer's X-Y-Z coordinates every 5 mm of travel in the Z direction, along the shaft of the humerus. Specimen orientation was based on the medial and lateral epicondyles establishing the rotation axis, and orientation of the shaft establishing position in the coronal and sagittal planes. Four enveloping lines were traced vertically along the anterior, posterior, medial, and lateral aspects of the humeral shaft, thus recording the humeral shaft shape. Each line was drawn from the level of the epicondyles to the anatomical neck. The mean of each set of four points at each 5-mm level corresponds to the center of the humeral shaft at that level. Therefore, connecting these centers represents the overall shape of the humeral shaft (Figure 2). This singular linear representation of the humeral shaft was used to determine the bow angulation and magnitude.

Graphical representations alongside cadaveric humeri created by the MicroScribe digitizer. A, A humerus with a single bow. B, A humerus with a double bow.

The total length was measured from the center of the humeral head to the center of the trochlea along the central axis. The length to maximal bow percentage was calculated from the length to the center of the humeral head to the maximal bow divided by total length. In addition, the number of distinct bows present within a single humeri was recorded based on the number of observed bends in the line created by the digitizer (Figure 3). The presence of a bow in addition to the location of the apex and end point was determined using a custom analysis in the Python programming language using the line created by the digitizer. The program determined the presence of a bow by deviation from the overall vector of the humerus.

Diagrammatic representation of collected and calculated measurements including total length of the humerus, length to maximal bow, the magnitude of the bow, and the shaft angulation.

To find the angle of each bow, two vectors were calculated: one connecting the most proximal center point to the apex and another connecting the most distal center point to the apex. The angle between these two vectors is the angle of the bow. The magnitude of the bow was computed as the distance between each bow apex and the vector connecting the most proximal and most distal center points, representing the total length. If there were two apices, a third line was drawn representing the center segment. Shaft angulation was the angle created between the line representing the total length of the humerus and the perpendicular line of the magnitude of the bow (Figure 3). A custom data analysis tool coded in the Python programming language was used to compute these lengths and angles.

Statistical analysis was conducted in SPSS (Version 25, 2017; IBM). Descriptive analysis analyzed the relationships of age, sex, race, and bowing. A McNemar test was used to evaluate for differences in the number of bows between paired humeri from the same cadaver. Multiple regression analysis was done to analyze the relationship between the measured variables with sex or race. The alpha level was set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 874 adult humeri were analyzed from 437 individuals. Three with obvious pathologies were excluded before scanning, and 22 additional specimens were excluded due to incomplete data collection. Ultimately, 849 humeri were included in the study. Of the specimens analyzed, the average age was 55 ± 10 years. Overall, 138 humeri (16%) from female specimens and 711 (84%) from male specimens were there. Overall, 318 humeri (37%) from specimens were designated as Black and 531 from specimens as White by the collection.

The average total length was 310 ± 20 mm. Of the 849 humeri, 652 humeri (77%) had one bow and 197 (23%) had two bows. The average single-bow apex was located at 71% ± 4% of the full humeral length with a magnitude of 2.0 ± 1.4-mm displacement from the central shaft axis. The average double-bow apices were located at 32% ± 4% and 72% ± 3% of the full humeral length with respective magnitudes of 4.6 ± 2.6 and 2.0 ± 1.8 mm. Singly-bowed humeri demonstrated shaft angulation of 2.9° ± 1.7° at the bow apex, whereas double-bowed humeri demonstrated shaft angulations of 4.7° ± 2.2° proximally and 3.3° ± 1.8° distally (Table 1).

Humerus Morphological Characteristics

In addition, there were 416 cadavers with paired humeri available for a total of 832 humeri. To evaluate for bilateral symmetry in humeral morphology, paired categorical data were analyzed using the McNemar test. The analysis revealed a notable difference in the frequency of humeral bowing across the paired specimens (P = 0.009).

Regression analyses were done using age and sex as independent variables with a specific humerus parameter as the dependent variable. Dependent variables in multiple regression analyses included total length, number of bows, distance to proximal and distal bow, magnitude of proximal and distal bow, and angulation of proximal and distal shaft (Table 2). Decreased total length and single bowed humeri was found to correlate with female sex (beta = −0.477, P = 0.00; beta = −0.105, P = 0.002). In addition, regression analysis was done using the measurements and race. Black race was found to correlate with increased total length, single bowed humeri, and decreased distance to distal bow apex (beta = 0.270, P = 0.00; beta = −0.197, P = 0.00; and beta = −0.097, P = 0.005).

Discussion

Within this study, osteological specimens were carefully positioned and analyzed using a digitizer to optimally characterize the morphology of the humeral shaft. Although most specimens contained a single bow within the humeral shaft, a sizable cohort contained two bows. The single bow was commonly located in the distal third of the humeral shaft, whereas double bows were found at the proximal third and distal third. Of note, the proximal bow was larger in magnitude than the distal bow in humeri with double bows. In addition, female specimens had a shorter total length and more single-bowed humeri, whereas Black specimens were found to have a longer total length, more proximal bow apex, and more single bowed humeri.

Prior work characterizing the humeral shaft has provided insight on the shape, length, diameter, and curvature of the medullary canal.14 The first observation of a double bow was described by Akpinar et al14 who noted an anterolateral angulation in the proximal third with anterior angulation in the distal third. In a study of 57 cadaver bones, the anterior angulation was an average of 21 cm from the greater tubercle with a mean degree of angulation of 9° with a mean total length 29.3 cm. This is comparable to our measured apex location at 71% of total humeral length but was larger than our described angulation of 2.9° at the bow apex. The differences in the reported measurements can be explained by methodology as Akpinar et al used radiographs measuring angulation with a protractor, potentially introducing both positional and measurement errors.

In addition to the morphologic characteristics identified, our study adds to the existing literature noting differences based on sex. Drew et al15 found that the humeral head radius and proximal medullary canal was larger in men, whereas women had higher canal aspect ratios at mid shaft and a lower cortical thickness. Based on our results, female specimens also have shorter total humerus length and less commonly had a double-bowed humerus. No correlation was found between sex and other characteristics, including distance to distal bow, magnitude, or angulation. This is the first correlation of humeral shaft anatomy and race, showing that Black specimens were more likely to have a longer total length, less commonly had a double-bowed humerus, and had decreased percentage distance to distal bow.

The magnitude of the bow may impact intramedullary fixation of humeral shaft fractures, revision shoulder arthroplasty using long stems, and diaphyseal plate fixation as the proximal bow was found to be larger in magnitude when present. Intramedullary fixation of proximal third humerus fractures has an increasing popularity with orthopaedic surgeons due to advantages of preserving the periosteal blood supply while being a load sharing implant.16 Humeral nails were designed based on principles established by femoral and tibial nails and have experienced staged optimization over the years. The first generation had problems due to large diameters, excessive length, and lack of rotational control. The second generation introduced curved nails, which had to be inserted through the supraspinatus footprint and also had inadequate security of proximal screws. Currently, the third generation is hallmarked by smaller diameters and are cannulated to facilitate percutaneous approaches.16-18 Both antegrade and retrograde humeral nailing have known complications, including rotator cuff pain, difficult reduction, iatrogenic greater tuberosity fracture, varus malalignment, and iatrogenic supracondylar fracture.19 Based on current outcomes, humeral nails have potential to be further optimized.

One key component of the design of modern femoral nails is the ability to mimic the anterior bow of the femur. In femoral and tibial nails, the anatomic fit improved markedly when the nail radius of curvature was closer to the native anatomy.20 Therefore, the discrepancy in outcomes across studies of humeral nails may be explained by variation in humeral bowing. Although the general presence of a humeral bow is recognized, before this cadaveric investigation, it had not been studied in a large data set or considered in current implant designs, which generally use a straight humeral shaft portion of the nail. Even with a correct entry point, humeral nails have been shown to disrupt cortical apposition. Noda et al used 20 cadaveric humeri divided into groups based on nail design: straight or angled with a 6° bend at the proximal one third of the nail. A fracture at the surgical neck was created, and the nail was inserted through the correct entry hole. The average displacement of a straight nail was 2.5 mm, and the angled nail was 1.9 mm. In three of the 20 specimens, the displacement could not be reduced with a force over 35 N.21 When assuming an average cortical thickness of 3 mm, cortical apposition could be lost with the reported displacement. In addition, the nail design affected the direction of displacement with straight nails causing a medial and posterior displacement and curved nails leading to medial displacement.21 Thus, the data presented in this study had the potential to optimize humeral nail and revision shoulder arthroplasty design to better align with the shape of the proximal humeral canal and reduce these complications.

There are limitations of the study. The medullary canal was assumed to be central throughout the study as CT scans were not used. The overall shape of the bow was defined by the outer cortical morphology analyzed by the digitizer, and it cannot account for patients who may have cortical width asymmetry or the relationship between the presence of a bow and location of the medullary canal. As the specimens included in the study were from a historical collection, there is a possibility that that minor skeletal differences may have developed. Because of the slow process of evolution, these changes would likely be insignificant. The collection does not have information regarding occupation, social history, or nutrition, which could not be correlated with the findings in the study. However, the large number of specimens used in the study would hopefully decrease the impact of specimens with outstanding circumstances.

Conclusion

Our study quantifies the presence of humeral bowing in a large osteological collection using precise positioning and high-quality digital measurements. The presence of distal humeral bowing in all specimens and proximal bowing in 23% of specimens carries important implications for plate and intramedullary humeral fixation. The bowing characteristics have notable correlations with both sex and race, which should be considered when planning humeral nail placement and future designs.
