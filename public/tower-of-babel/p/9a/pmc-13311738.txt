Lipocalin‐2 Restores Mitochondrial and Antioxidant Adaptation in a Strain‐Specific Manner During LPS Induced Sepsis
Authors: Vinita Kushwaha, Mrunmayee R. Kandalgaonkar, Sukhvinder Singh, Piu Saha, Ashok Kumar, Beng San Yeoh, Matam Vijay‐Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSepsis induces profound metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to multiple organ injury and mortality. Lipocalin‐2 (Lcn2), an acute‐phase protein, regulates iron homeostasis and oxidative stress, but its impact on mitochondrial resilience remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated the role of Lcn2 in modulating mitochondrial function and hepatic stress responses in C57BL/6 J (BL6) and BALB/c mice in LPS‐induced endotoxemia. Lcn2‐deficient (Lcn2KO) mice exhibited reduced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity, indicating impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Administration of recombinant Lcn2 (rLcn2) restored mitochondrial respiration in both mouse strains under basal conditions; however, during LPS challenge, only BL6 mice partially preserved mitochondrial function, whereas BALB/c mice remained compromised. To explore underlying mechanisms, we assessed hepatic gene expression by qRT‐PCR. LPS induced Acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and suppressed lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), markers associated with lipid remodeling, as well as altered antioxidant genes glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) in both strains. rLcn2 treatment in BL6 mice normalized ACSL4 and LPCAT3 expression and enhanced antioxidant gene transcription, whereas BALB/c mice showed minimal recovery. In BL6 mice, Lcn2 supports oxidative phosphorylation while simultaneously modulating lipid metabolism and antioxidant defenses, highlighting its integrated role in cellular adaptation to endotoxemia. Our results reveal that differential Lcn2 responsiveness contributes to inter‐strain variation in susceptibility to sepsis‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction and identify Lcn2 as a potential therapeutic target for enhancing metabolic resilience during sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life‐threatening condition that arises from a dysregulated host immune response to infection, leading to widespread inflammation, tissue injury, and organ dysfunction (Wiersinga and van der Poll 2022). A hallmark of sepsis is the disruption of cellular metabolism, particularly within the liver and immune system, where excessive production of inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species amplifies tissue damage (Caraballo and Jaimes 2019). Sepsis frequently impairs liver function, affecting up to 46% of patients (Protzer et al. 2012; Yan et al. 2014), highlighting the organ's dual role as both a central mediator and a vulnerable target of septic injury. The liver is a key immunological hub in sepsis, producing acute‐phase proteins and cytokines that shape systemic inflammation. One important mediator of this systemic inflammatory signaling is lipocalin‐2 (Lcn2), an innate immune protein that is rapidly upregulated during stress and inflammation (Kim et al. 2022). Traditionally known for its role in sequestering bacterial siderophores (Huang et al. 2020), Lcn2 is now understood to influence host metabolic adaptation, mitochondrial activity, and the regulation of inflammatory responses (Su et al. 2023; Song et al. 2018). By regulating iron homeostasis, inflammatory signaling, and oxidative stress, Lcn2 helps govern the balance between tissue injury and adaptive responses during endotoxemia (Srinivasan et al. 2012).

Mitochondria are central to the pathophysiology of sepsis (Singer 2014; Nedel et al. 2023), integrating energy metabolism and immune signaling (Weinberg et al. 2015). During septic stress, oxidative phosphorylation and spare respiratory capacity decline while antioxidant defenses are overwhelmed, promoting metabolic collapse and organ failure (Jang et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2025). Inflammation further drives mitochondria into a quiescent state, marked by a slowdown of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and electron transport chain to limit ROS production and curb inflammation (Ji et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2025). Aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1 aka immune responsive gene‐1; IRG1) plays a central role in this process by converting cis‐aconitate to itaconate, a metabolite that inhibits succinate dehydrogenase and restrains TCA cycle activity, thereby promoting an anti‐inflammatory metabolic response (Lin et al. 2021; Choi et al. 2021). Given Lcn2's established roles in regulating oxidative stress and mitochondrial integrity, it may act in concert with the ACOD1‐itaconate axis to fine‐tune metabolic reprogramming during sepsis. This interplay suggests that Lcn2 could contribute to mitochondrial resilience, influencing whether cells adapt to or succumb to inflammatory stress. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism(s) underlying this potential crosstalk remains to be determined.

Mouse models have been indispensable for dissecting the complex links between immunity and metabolism (Hartmann et al. 2021). However, responses to inflammatory and metabolic stress vary considerably across mouse strains, and these differences can influence experimental outcomes and their translational relevance. C57BL/6 J (BL6) and BALB/c mice are two of the most widely used inbred strains in biomedical research, yet they display striking differences in immune polarization, mitochondrial regulation, and susceptibility to inflammatory disease (Shanks and Kusnecov 1998; Zhao et al. 2025). BL6 mice exhibit Th1‐skewed immunity and a higher susceptibility to diet‐induced metabolic disturbances, whereas BALB/c mice are biased toward Th2 responses and display distinct metabolic adaptations (Saha et al. 2020). In addition, a key anti‐inflammatory hormone, i.e., corticosterone, is approximately 50% lower in BL6 mice compared to BALB/c mice (Laber et al. 2008). This reduction may further potentiate Th1‐driven inflammation and oxidative stress, contributing to their heightened susceptibility to LPS‐induced sepsis (J. Xu et al. 2025). These inherent differences highlight the importance of comparing the two strains to better understand how genetic background shapes inflammatory and metabolic outcomes. Furthermore, the use of Lcn2‐deficient (Lcn2KO) mice on both backgrounds provides a unique opportunity to define the specific contribution of Lcn2 in Th1 and Th2 biased responses. Together, comparative studies across BL6, BALB/c, and Lcn2KO mice in both backgrounds offer valuable insights into how genetic background and Lcn2 signaling converge to shape inflammatory and metabolic outcomes.

In this study, we investigated the role of Lcn2 in hepatic and mitochondrial adaptation during LPS‐induced sepsis in BL6 and BALB/c mice. Lcn2 deficiency reduced basal, maximal, and spare respiratory capacities, indicating impaired mitochondrial function. Recombinant Lcn2 (rLcn2) restored respiration under basal conditions in both strains, but during LPS challenge, only BL6 mice partially preserved mitochondrial functions. Hepatic gene expression analyses showed that Lcn2 more effectively normalized LPS induced injury markers involved in phospholipid regulation and antioxidant pathways in BL6 mice than in BALB/c mice. These findings suggest that Lcn2 enhances mitochondrial resilience and redox balance in a strain‐dependent manner, supporting its protective role in mitigating sepsis‐induced hepatic dysfunction.

Materials and Methods

Materials

LPS O128:B12 (L2887), Recombinant Lcn2 (Sino biologicals Inc. 50060‐MNAH), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Corning 10‐013CV), Mouse macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (PeproTech CRM800B), Seahorse XFe96 (103793‐100), Mitochondrial complex inhibitors (Agilent 103270‐100), Anti‐IRG1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 17805), Anti‐cleaved caspase‐3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9661S), Anti‐GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 5174) and Anti‐VDAC (Cell Signaling Technology, 4866), TRIzol reagent (Sigma‐Aldrich, 15596026), qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quanta BioSciences, 95047), SYBR Green Master Mix (Quanta BioSciences, #95071), Serum triglycerides, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate transaminase (Kearneysville, VA, USA; Cat # TR210, AL146, AS147).

Mice

C57BL/6 J and BALB/c wild‐type (WT) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. BALB/c Lcn2 knockout (Lcn2KO) mice were generated by Dr. Thorsten Berger (University Health Network) and provided through Dr. Marit Nilsen‐Hamilton (Iowa State University). C57BL/6 J Lcn2KO mice, originally developed by Dr. Shizuo Akira (Osaka University, Japan), were obtained via Dr. Kelly Smith (University of Washington, Seattle, WA). All mice were maintained under specific pathogen‐free conditions at 23°C with a 12‐h light/dark cycle, housed on corncob bedding with nestlets, and had ad‐libitum access to standard chow and water. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocols approved (#108799 & #108790) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at The University of Toledo, College of Medicine and Life Sciences. This institution has an Animal Welfare Assurance on file in the Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare (OLAW). The assurance number is “A3414‐01”.

LPS Induced Sepsis

8 week‐old male WT and Lcn2KO mice on both C57BL/6 J and BALB/c backgrounds (n = 4−5 per group) received an intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 5 mg/kg) and were monitored for 24 h. Animals were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, and blood was collected from the retro‐orbital plexus into BD Microtainers (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Hemolysis‐free serum was collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. In selected experiments, rLcn2 (20 μg/mouse) was administered intraperitoneally 1 h after LPS treatment, and samples were collected 24 h post‐injection.

Bone Marrow Derived Macrophages (BMDMs) Isolation

Bone marrow cells were harvested aseptically from the femurs and tibiae of 8 week‐old male WT and Lcn2KO mice on both C57BL/6 J and BALB/c backgrounds. Marrow was flushed with cold Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) using a 25 G needle, and the resulting suspension was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to remove debris. Cells were pelleted at 300× g for 5 min at 4° C, washed, and plated in non‐tissue culture‐treated dishes at 1 × 106 cells/mL in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin−streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF). Cultures were maintained at 37° C with 5% CO2 for 7 days, with half‐medium replacement containing fresh M‐CSF on day 3. On day 7, adherent cells displaying macrophage morphology were collected by gentle scraping or PBS‐EDTA (1X) treatment for subsequent experiments.

Seahorse Assay

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using a Seahorse XFe96 Flux Analyzer (Agilent) as described previously (Van den Bossche et al. 2015). Bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) were plated at 1 × 105 cells per well in XF96 plates (Agilent) and stimulated for 24 h with LPS (1 µg/mL), recombinant Lcn2 (1 µg/mL), or left untreated as controls. 1 h prior to the assay, cells were washed and incubated in Seahorse base medium (Agilent) lacking glucose, phenol red, and sodium bicarbonate but supplemented with 5 mM HEPES and 2 mM l‐glutamine. The assay consisted of a 2‐min mixing period followed by 3 min of measurement, with sequential injections of, oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (1.5 µM), and a combination of antimycin A (0.125 µM) and rotenone (1.25 µM). After completion, cells were lysed in 0.1 N NaOH, and total protein content was quantified for normalization of OCR values using Bicinchoninic acid assay.

Western Blot

Protein extracts were prepared from liver tissue and splenocytes of experimental mice. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were resolved on 4−20% Tris‐glycine SDS‐PAGE gels (Bio‐Rad) and transferred to 0.2 µm nitrocellulose membranes (Bio‐Rad). Membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in 5% skim milk prepared in TBST, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies. After four washes in TBST (15 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). The following primary antibodies were used that are listed in the materials.

Quantitative Reverse‐Transcription PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 1000 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using the qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit under the following cycling conditions: 25°C for 10 min, 37°C for 120 min, 85°C for 5 min, followed by a hold at 4°C. Subsequently, 80 µL of nuclease‐free water was added to each reaction to achieve a final cDNA concentration of 10 ng/µL, and samples were stored at −20°C. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a Step OnePlus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) in 20 µL reactions containing 50 ng of cDNA, 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, and gene‐specific primers (Supplemental Table 1). Relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2ΔΔCT method, normalized to β‐Actin, and expressed as fold change relative to control samples.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Blood was collected at euthanasia via the retro‐orbital plexus into BD Microtainers and centrifuged to obtain hemolysis‐free serum, which was stored at −80°C. Liver tissue was homogenized in RIPA buffer (100 mg/mL), and the lysate was used to measure KC, IL‐6, TNF‐α, MPO, and NE via ELISA. Serum LCN2 and SAA levels were quantified using Mouse Duoset ELISA kits (R&D Systems), and the same approach was applied to assess hepatic cytokines and enzymes, and the values were normalized with protein concentration.

Biochemical Parameters

Non‐heme iron was measured following acid digestion as described by Torrance and Bothwell: 50 µL of serum was incubated in 3 M HCl with 10% trichloroacetic acid for 15 min, centrifuged, and 30 µL of supernatant was transferred to a 96‐well plate. Fresh working chromogen reagent (0.1% bathophenanthroline sulfonate and 1% thioglycolic acid mixed with saturated sodium acetate and water, 1:5:5) was added for 10 min at room temperature, and absorbance was read at 562 nm. Iron concentrations were calculated from a standard curve using an atomic absorption standard. Serum triglycerides, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) were measured using Randox assay kits following the manufacturer's instructions.

LC‐MS/MS

Hepatic itaconate levels were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) at the Pharmacology and Metabolomics Core, Karmanos Cancer Institute, Wayne State University. All LC‐MS/MS analyses were performed on an AB SCIEX (Foster City, CA) QTRAP 6500 system, which consists of an enhanced high performance hybrid triple quadrupole/linear ion trap mass spectrometer, interfaced with a SHIMADZU (Kyoto, Japan) Nexera ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system. Chromatographic separation of itaconic acid was achieved on a Phenomenex SynergiTM Polar‐RP column (150 × 2 mm, 4 µm) using a gradient elution consisting of mobile phase A (0.03% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.03% formic acid in acetonitrile), at the flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The gradient was programmed as: 0.3 min, 0% B; 5 min, 18.1% B; 7 min, 95% B; 7.5 min, 0% B; 13 min, 0%. The column oven was maintained at 35°C. Column eluents were monitored under negative electrospray ionization mode using the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Itaconic acid and 13C5 itaconic acid were monitored at the mass transitions m/z, 129.0 > 85.0 and 134.0 > 89.0, respectively. Other optimized mass spectrometric parameters were as follows: ion spray potential, −4500 V; nebulizer gas (GS1) and bath gas (GS2), 50 psi; curtain gas, 35 psi; collision gas, medium level; and source temperature, 475°C. The dwell time for each MRM transition was 100 ms. Frozen tissue was homogenized in water using a Precellys® homogenizer (at 2000 g for two 10 s with 5 s pause). The homogenate was spiked with 13C5 itaconic acid and followed by protein precipitation twice with 4x sample volume of ice‐cold methanol and 100 µL 80% methanol. The supernatant was combined and dried in a CentriVap Concentrator (Labconco, Kansas City, MO) at 10°C. The residue was reconstituted in LC‐MS grade water, and 5 µL of the supernatant was injected into the LC‐MS/MS system. Analyst® 1.6 software was used for system control and data acquisition and MultiQuant® 3.0 software was used for data processing and quantitation.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired t‐test. For comparisons among more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA was performed followed by Dunnett's post hoc test when each group was compared to a control, or Tukey's post hoc test for all pairwise comparisons (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 vs LPS, # p < 0.01, ## p < 0.01, ### p < 0.001, #### p < 0.0001 vs BALB/c). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Results

BALB/c Mice Show Heightened Sensitivity to LPS‐Induced Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Neutrophil Activation Compared to BL6 Mice

To investigate strain‐specific responses to sepsis, WT and Lcn2KO mice on BL6 and BALB/c backgrounds were challenged with LPS (5 mg/kg) and euthanized after 24 h, as illustrated in the schematic (Figure S1A). No significant difference in lipocalin‐2 (Lcn2) levels was observed between the two strains (Figure S1B). In contrast, serum amyloid A (SAA) levels were elevated in BL6 mice compared to BALB/c mice under basal conditions (Figure S1C). Upon LPS stimulation, serum amyloid A (SAA) levels were significantly higher in BL6‐Lcn2KO than in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice (Figure S1C). LPS administration increased serum myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE) levels in both strains compared with controls (Figure S1D‐E). BALB/c mice exhibited significantly higher MPO levels than BL6 mice in the presence of LPS, while NE levels remained comparable between strains, suggesting distinct activation thresholds of neutrophil degranulation and oxidative enzyme release. To evaluate the metabolic impact of LPS, mitochondrial respiration was assessed in bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) using oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis (Figure 1A). LPS treatment reduced basal respiration (Figure 1B) in BL6 mice, whereas maximal respiration (Figure 1C) and ATP production (Figure 1D) were not significantly altered. Proton leak (Figure 1E) was affected in BALB/c mice, while spare respiratory capacity (Figure 1F) was reduced in BL6 mice, indicating strain‐specific patterns of mitochondrial dysfunction in response to LPS. Although distinct respiratory parameters were affected in each strain, the magnitude of reduction was greater in BALB/c macrophages, whereas BL6 macrophages retained higher overall respiratory capacity, indicating greater mitochondrial stability under endotoxemic stress.

BL6 Mice Display Heightened Splenocyte Apoptosis Linked to LPS‐Induced Sepsis

During endotoxemia, dysregulated immune activation is followed by immunosuppression, characterized in part by extensive lymphocyte apoptosis within the spleen, a central immune‐regulatory organ. To assess apoptosis, cleaved caspase‐3, the active form of the executioner protease caspase‐3, was analyzed by immunoblotting. LPS treatment markedly increased cleaved caspase‐3 levels in splenocytes from both WT (Figure 2A) and Lcn2KO mice (Figure 2B) compared with their respective controls. Densitometry quantification confirmed a pronounced induction of cleaved caspase‐3, with BL6 mice showing higher expression across both strains (Figure 2C,D). Notably, the increase in cleaved caspase‐3 was more substantial in BL6‐Lcn2KO than in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice, indicating strain‐dependent susceptibility to apoptosis under septic conditions. To determine whether inflammation‐driven metabolic changes contribute to the observed strain‐specific differences in splenocyte apoptosis, we next examined ACOD1 expression as a potential regulator of cell survival (Zhou et al. 2024) (Figure 2E,F). This heightened apoptotic response inversely correlated with the expression of aconitate decarboxylase 1 (ACOD1), which was elevated in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice (Figure 2F). ACOD1 expression was elevated in BL6 mice (Figure 2F); however, when considered together with apoptotic markers, distinct differences were observed between strains. In BL6‐Lcn2KO mice, higher ACOD1 levels were associated with increased cleaved caspase‐3, whereas in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice, higher ACOD1 levels were accompanied by lower cleaved caspase‐3. These results indicate that ACOD1 levels alone do not reflect the outcome, and that the relationship between ACOD1 expression and apoptosis differs between strains.

Strain‐Specific Differences in ACOD1 Expression and Itaconate Levels During Endotoxemia

Given that LPS activates TLR4 signaling in the liver and drives acute‐phase metabolic changes, we evaluated hepatic ACOD1 expression to determine whether systemic immune metabolic regulation accompanies splenic apoptotic responses during endotoxemia. Hepatic ACOD1 expression was markedly induced following LPS treatment, with BALB/c mice displaying higher protein levels than BL6 mice (Figure 3A). BALB/c mice exhibited higher basal ACOD1 expression compared to BL6 mice. However, following LPS stimulation, ACOD1 levels in the liver of Lcn2KO mice were comparable between strains, in contrast to splenocytes, where more pronounced strain‐dependent differences were observed. Densitometric analysis confirmed these findings, providing quantitative support for the observed tissue‐specific differences (Figure 3B). However, LC‐MS/MS analysis showed detectable basal itaconate levels in BL6 mice, while it was nearly absent in BALB/c mice (Figure 3C); this difference persisted following LPS treatment, with BL6‐Lcn2KO mice displaying higher itaconate levels than BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice. Collectively, the differential patterns of macrophage mitochondrial function, hepatic ACOD1 induction, and splenocyte ACOD1 expression delineate distinct metabolic strategies, with BALB/c mice exhibiting a more restrained inflammatory phenotype that mitigates splenocyte apoptosis relative to BL6 mice.

Recombinant Lcn2 Attenuates LPS Induced Hepatic Inflammatory and Systemic Responses in BL6 Mice

Based on our previous data showing increased susceptibility of Lcn2KO mice to LPS‐induced inflammation (Srinivasan et al. 2012), we next examined the direct effect of rLcn2. The dose of recombinant lipocalin‐2 was determined based on our previous study (Srinivasan et al. 2012), in which LPS administration (5 mg/kg body weight, consistent with the dose used here) resulted in circulating lipocalin‐2 levels of approximately 15 µg/mL after 24 h. To this end, Lcn2KO mice of both BL6 and BALB/c strains were treated with LPS (5 mg/kg), followed by therapeutic administration of recombinant Lcn2 (rLcn2, 20 μg/mouse) to assess its impact on hepatic inflammation and systemic metabolic parameters. Serum analysis revealed that LPS increased circulating triglyceride (as a measure of lipemia of inflammation) levels in both strains, whereas administration of rLcn2 reduced triglycerides in BL6‐Lcn2KO mice compared to BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice, suggesting attenuation of the lipemia of inflammation. (Figure 4A). Because LPS‐induced inflammation is known to trigger hypoferremia (anemia of inflammation) as part of the acute‐phase response, we next quantified serum iron levels. Consistent with this, iron concentrations were markedly reduced following LPS treatment in both strains, and administration of rLcn2, indicating that exogenous Lcn2 does not counteract inflammation‐driven iron sequestration during endotoxemia (Figure 4B). Next, we measured serum ALT and AST levels and found that rLcn2 administration significantly reduced ALT levels in BL6, indicating partial hepatoprotection, whereas AST levels remained unchanged, suggesting that Lcn2 selectively mitigates specific aspects of hepatocyte injury (Figure 4C‐D). rLcn2 treatment orchestrated systemic responses in a genotype and strain‐dependent manner, with Lcn2KO mice exhibiting more pronounced effects in BL6 than in BALB/c mice. Analysis of hepatic inflammatory markers showed that LPS significantly increased KC (Figure 4E), IL‐6 (Figure 4F), TNF‐α (Figure 4G), MPO (Figure 4H), and neutrophil elastase (Figure 4I) levels in all groups. Administration of rLcn2 attenuated these inflammatory responses, with the effect being more prominent in BL6‐Lcn2KO mice compared to BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice. These observations indicate that rLcn2 can partially restore inflammatory homeostasis in the liver following LPS challenge. Overall, these results demonstrate that rLcn2 modulates systemic metabolic parameters and hepatic cytokine responses following LPS‐induced inflammation, highlighting its role in controlling liver inflammation in a strain and genotype‐dependent manner.

rLcn2 Restores Mitochondrial Function and Antioxidant Responses More Effectively in BL6‐Lcn2KO Mice Than in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO Mice

To determine the role of exogenous Lcn2 in mitochondrial function, we evaluated OCR in BMDMs of Lcn2KO mice with or without rLcn2 administration, under both basal conditions and during endotoxemia induced by LPS (Figure 5A). Upon LPS administration, mitochondrial function was compromised in both strains, with basal and maximal respiration reduced. However, striking differences were observed when rLcn2 was administered an hour after LPS treatment. In BL6‐Lcn2KO mice, rLcn2 partially mitigated the suppressive effect of LPS on mitochondrial function. Specifically, basal and maximal respiration remained significantly higher than in LPS‐treated Lcn2KO controls, indicating partial preservation (Figure 5B). In contrast, BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice failed to exhibit comparable recovery upon combined LPS and rLcn2 administration. These data suggest that, unlike BL6‐Lcn2KO, the BALB/c‐Lcn2KO background lacks sufficient adaptive capacity to sustain mitochondrial function under inflammatory stress, even when supported by exogenous Lcn2. These results demonstrate BL6‐Lcn2KO mice partially preserve oxidative phosphorylation capacity under inflammatory stress, highlighting strain‐specific differences in Lcn2‐mediated metabolic resilience.

Because lipid homeostasis (Amunugama et al. 2021) (Teixeira et al. 2024) and antioxidant defenses (Zou et al. 2026) are key determinants of hepatic injury during sepsis, we next examined the expression of Acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (ACSL4), lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and superoxide dismutase (SOD2) in the liver of BL6‐Lcn2KO and BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice after LPS and/or rLcn2 administration. qRT‐PCR analysis showed that Acsl4 expression was strongly induced by LPS in BL6, consistent with its role in driving pro‐oxidant lipid metabolism in sepsis. Importantly, rLcn2 administration reduced Acsl4 expression more effectively in BL6‐Lcn2KO mice than in BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice, indicating better regulation of stress‐associated lipid metabolism (Figure 5C). In line with this, Gpx4 expression, a critical antioxidant enzyme that counteracts lipid peroxidation, was also more responsive in BL6 mice. LPS treatment elevated Gpx4 transcript levels, but the reduction with rLcn2 was significantly more pronounced in BL6‐Lcn2KO compared to BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice, mirroring the pattern observed for Acsl4 (Figure 5D). In contrast, Lpcat3 expression, which is important for polyunsaturated phosphatidylcholine lipid metabolism and protection against ferroptotic damage (Cash and Hui 2016; D. Li and Li 2020), was suppressed by LPS in both strains. rLcn2 restored Lpcat3 expression in BL6‐Lcn2KO mice, but BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice showed no recovery (Figure 5E). Similarly, Sod2, a key mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme, followed the same trend as Lpcat3 as it was downregulated during LPS treatment in both strains but was efficiently restored by rLcn2 in BL6‐Lcn2KO mice, while BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice exhibited little improvement (Figure 5F). Together, these findings demonstrate that BL6‐Lcn2KO mice exhibit a more coordinated transcriptional recovery of both lipid metabolic genes (Acsl4, Lpcat3) and antioxidant (Gpx4, Sod2) markers in response to rLcn2 during LPS challenge. This suggests that the antioxidant system and phospholipid metabolism pathways in BL6‐Lcn2KO are more responsive to Lcn2‐mediated protection, whereas BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice lack this adaptive capacity, potentially due to a Th2‐biased response.

Discussion

Sepsis is characterized by excessive inflammation and uncontrolled oxidative stress that disrupts mitochondrial function and exacerbates organ injury (Nedel et al. 2023), making redox regulation a key determinant of host resilience (Li et al. 2025). In our study, mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity was higher in BL6 mice compared to BALB/c mice, suggesting intrinsic differences in metabolic and oxidative stress responses between these strains (Depke et al. 2014). Despite exhibiting higher basal mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity, BL6 mice exhibited a more pronounced septic phenotype following high‐dose LPS challenge compared to BALB/c mice. Although BALB/c mice exhibit higher circulating iron levels, which could theoretically exacerbate oxidative stress (Saha et al. 2020), their relatively milder septic phenotype suggests that compensatory metabolic or inflammatory regulatory mechanisms may override potential iron‐driven injury. Notably, ACOD1 induction following LPS challenge was significantly greater in BALB/c mice, indicating that enhanced immunometabolic adaptation may contribute to their relative resilience (Liu et al. 2026). Our data demonstrates that dynamic control of inflammatory and metabolic stress, rather than basal mitochondrial function, dictates endotoxemic severity (Jaiswal et al. 2022).

ACOD1 abundance does not necessarily correlate with itaconate accumulation (Qian et al. 2024), consistent with reports indicating that ACOD1 can exert immunoregulatory signaling functions that are only partially dependent on its metabolite product (Patiño‐Martinez et al. 2024). In the present study, although BALB/c mice displayed higher ACOD1 expression following LPS stimulation, this did not translate into proportionally elevated itaconate levels. One possible explanation is the relatively lower mitochondrial bioenergetic activity observed in this strain, which may limit metabolic flux through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Cortassa et al. 2003) and thereby restrict the availability of cis‐aconitate, the substrate required for itaconate synthesis (Patiño‐Martinez et al. 2024). In addition, mitochondrial metabolic flux and the efficiency of metabolite export can influence intracellular itaconate accumulation (Schuster et al. 2022; Cordes & Metallo 2021), suggesting that enzyme abundance alone may not determine net metabolite production. Thus, the dissociation between ACOD1 expression and itaconate levels in BALB/c mice likely reflects constraints imposed by mitochondrial metabolic activity and substrate availability, while still allowing ACOD1 to participate in regulatory signaling pathways that modulate inflammatory responses during endotoxemia (Sun et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2022).

Lcn2 emerges as a critical modulator of the hepatic response to endotoxin (Sunil et al. 2007), potentially bridging strain‐dependent differences in immune polarization, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress (Asimakopoulou et al. 2016). Previous studies have demonstrated that Lcn2 deficiency results in delayed hypoferremia during the early phase of inflammation, leading to increased oxidative stress at later stages (Srinivasan et al. 2012). Specifically, Lcn2‐deficient mice exhibit a lag in the reduction of circulating iron at the onset of inflammation, which subsequently contributes to enhanced oxidative stress (Sunil et al. 2007). By regulating iron availability and limiting reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, Lcn2 may help optimize mitochondrial respiration and support redox homeostasis (Jiang et al. 2024), particularly in the context of Th1‐biased BL6 mice with inherently higher bioenergetic capacity (Song et al. 2018).

In line with this mechanism, our observations in Lcn2 deficient mice revealed strain dependent differences in susceptibility to endotoxemia, with BL6 mice exhibiting a more pronounced septic response compared with BALB/c mice. These findings suggest that impaired regulation of iron homeostasis in the absence of Lcn2 may exacerbate oxidative stress‐mediated injury (Lim et al. 2021; Shin et al. 2021), particularly in strains with higher intrinsic mitochondrial activity, such as BL6 mice. Conversely, BALB/c mice may retain a greater capacity to buffer oxidative stress (Depke et al. 2014) despite Lcn2 deficiency. Together, these results support the concept that Lcn2‐mediated control of iron availability and redox balance plays an important role in shaping strain‐specific outcomes during endotoxemia. Collectively, these findings highlight that the severity of sepsis is governed by the interplay between immune polarization, mitochondrial resilience, and metabolic regulators(Jiang et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2022).

Notably, rec‐Lcn2 provided significantly greater protection against LPS induced cytokine responses in BL6 mice (Ondee et al. 2019) than in BALB/c mice, in both ex vivo and in vivo settings. We speculate that this difference may be due to more efficient uptake of rec‐Lcn2 in BL6 mice or the presence of a Th1‐skewed immune phenotype. This differential responsiveness may also reflect underlying differences in Lcn2 bioavailability and functional activity, which are influenced not only by cellular uptake but also by its iron‐binding capability. It is well established that Lcn2 alone cannot bind ferric iron directly; rather, it chelates ferric iron only when complexed with a siderophore, such as catechol‐type small molecules (Flo et al. 2004). However, the specific mammalian‐derived siderophore that rec‐Lcn2 utilizes under ex vivo and in vivo conditions remains unclear. Identifying such a siderophore could inform the use of siderophore‐bound, iron‐free Lcn2 as a potentially more effective intervention in septic conditions. As a future direction, our current findings are based on an abacterial (sterile inflammation) model of sepsis. It will be important to determine whether rec‐Lcn2 exerts similar protective effects in models of microbial sepsis.

Conclusion

In summary, the differential sepsis response between BL6 and BALB/c mice reflects a complex interplay between immune polarization, mitochondrial function, and redox regulation. The Th1‐biased BL6 mice exhibit heightened inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, whereas the Th2‐skewed BALB/c mice display greater resistance, potentially supported by higher ACOD1 expression and Lcn2‐mediated control of iron and ROS. Exogenous Lcn2 enhanced oxidative phosphorylation in both strains; however, only BL6‐Lcn2KO mice retained mitochondrial function (Ondee et al. 2019) and transcriptional recovery of lipid metabolic (ACSL4, LPCAT3) and antioxidant (GPX4, SOD2) pathways during inflammatory stress. Conversely, BALB/c‐Lcn2KO mice showed limited metabolic recovery but may compensate through ACOD1‐associated anti‐apoptotic mechanisms, contributing to their milder septic phenotype. Future studies are warranted to delineate the mechanistic interplay between ACOD1 and Lcn2 in regulating iron homeostasis, mitochondrial function, and cell survival pathways during sepsis.
