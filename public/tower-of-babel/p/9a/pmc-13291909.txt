Computed tomography-guided vs conventional catheter ablation for ventricular tachycardia: the InEurHeart trial

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsCatheter ablation is performed in patients with recurrent ventricular tachycardia (VT) but remains complex and limited to experienced centres. Ventricular tachycardia ablation guided by pre-procedural imaging was shown promising in non-randomized trials. InEurHeart aims to evaluate computed tomography (CT)-guided VT ablation vs conventional ablation in a multicentre randomized controlled trial.MethodsIn 14 European centres, 113 patients with prior myocardial infarction and clinically significant VT were randomly assigned to CT-guided (n = 57) or conventional (n = 56) VT ablation. The primary objective was to demonstrate reduced procedural duration when using CT guidance. Secondary endpoints included efficacy (incidence and burden of ventricular arrhythmia), safety, as well as composite endpoints.ResultsThe primary endpoint showed a significant decrease in procedure time favouring CT-guided ablation: 149 ± 51 to 120 ± 50 min, −19% [95% confidence interval (CI) −32; −7]; P = .0027 in intention to treat, and 149 ± 51 to 107 ± 38 min, −28% (95% CI −40; −16); P < .0001 per protocol. Major adverse events occurred in two (3.5%) in the conventional group vs one (1.8%) in the CT-guided group [−1.8% (95% CI −7.9; 4.3)]. Ventricular tachycardia–free survival at 1 year was achieved in 37 (67.3%) patients for conventional vs 43 (76.8%) for CT-guided VT ablation [9.5% (95% CI −10.4; 29.4), P = NS]. Ventricular tachycardia burden was decreased by 90% in the CT-guided group.ConclusionsIn patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy, CT-guided VT ablation reduces the procedure duration while maintaining a favourable efficacy and safety profile when compared with VT ablation without image integration.Keywords: Ventricular tachycardia ablation, Cardiac imaging, Cardiac CT scan, Procedure outcome

Introduction

Catheter ablation of ventricular tachycardia (VT) has a Class I/IIa indication in patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICMP) and recurrent VT.1 Over the past decade, the strategy has progressively shifted from clinical VT activation mapping/ablation2 to substrate-based ablation. However, substrate-based ablation is a tedious procedure with highly heterogeneous endpoints that heavily relies on electrophysiologist experience.3–11 Several millions of patients with ICMP are equipped with implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) worldwide, and only a minority of these have access to VT ablation. Even in the USA and European Union, limited access to the technique results in patients being ablated late in the course of the disease, when higher VT burden and lower cardiac function often result in adverse intra- and post-procedural outcomes.12,13 Thus, efforts to make VT ablation simpler, shorter, and as effective are critically needed. Strategies based on cardiac imaging [cardiac computed tomography (CT) scan and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)] have been developed to better understand the underlying substrate.14–18 These strategies aim to create a digital twin of the heart19,20 (including anatomy, scar, and VT channel identification within the scar) imported into a 3D navigation system to guide VT ablation. The approach has the potential to substantially streamline VT ablation by deporting a substantial part of the procedure, the part devoted to mapping, pre-procedurally. To date, no randomized controlled trial has evaluated this approach for VT ablation. The institutional InEurHeart multicentre clinical trial was therefore designed to identify the value of CT guidance in shortening VT ablation procedures.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

InEurHeart (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05225935) is an investigator-initiated, multicentre open-label randomized trial carried out at 14 centres in Austria, France, Germany, and Switzerland (see Supplementary data). The trial design and conduct were overseen by an academic steering committee. The study protocol was approved by the institutional research ethics committee of each country/centre depending on national and local requirements.

Database cleaning and analysis were performed by the Clinical Epidemiology Unit (USMR) of CHU Bordeaux.

InEurHeart was funded by an EIT Health grant, an entity of the European Union. It had no role in the trial design, selection of participating centres, enrolment of patients, data collection, data storage, data analysis, data interpretation, preparation of the manuscript, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Trial participants and randomization

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a prior history of myocardial infarction and at least one of the following: (i) ≥3 episodes of VT treated with anti-tachycardia pacing, (ii) ≥1 appropriate ICD shocks, (iii) sustained VT below detection rate of the ICD documented by electrocardiogram (ECG) or any cardiac monitor, or (iv) sustained VT recorded on 12-lead ECG in the absence of ICD. Patients with contraindication to contrast injection for CT scan or catheter ablation were excluded. Further inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in the Supplementary data. Computed tomography scan was performed before randomization, as part of standard care to exclude intra-cardiac thrombus.21,22 The workflow is depicted in Figure 1. All participants provided written informed consent for study participation.

Workflow of the study

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either catheter ablation guided by CT scan or conventional ablation without CT imaging. Randomization was performed using a randomization list with permuted blocks of varying sizes (4 and 6 in random order), stratified by country to ensure balance between the groups within each site. The randomization procedure was centrally generated by a biostatistician at Bordeaux University Hospital using SAS Statistical Software version 9.3. The personnel who enrolled and those who assigned participants to the interventions did not have access to the random allocation sequence. Randomization was conducted by the site investigators through the trial’s electronic case report form.

Interventions

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced and ECG-gated CT scan, the imaging protocol being detailed in the Supplementary data. After randomization, images from both study arms were uploaded on an InHeart web-based platform dedicated to the inEurHeart study. A clinically available (CE mark and FDA approved) image processing technology was employed to obtain whole heart models including detailed anatomy and substrate via artificial intelligence (AI)–based segmentation tools. Substrate analysis included wall thickness mapping (Figure 2) as well as intra-myocardial fat and calcification. No adaptive or decision-making AI was applied during the clinical workflow. A core lab composed of two physicians with 10-year experience with the technology independently analysed the models in a blinded fashion to annotate thickness heterogeneity-based isthmuses, defined as channels of relatively preserved thickness within severely thinned scar, as these were shown to correlate with critical isthmuses during post-infarction VT16,23 (Figure 2). In case of discrepancy, a third reader was involved and a consensus reading was obtained between the three readers. This analysis was conducted for all patients from both arms. All image segmentation and core-lab adjudication were blinded to treatment allocation.

Computed tomography–guided arm

For patients randomized to the CT-guided arm, the annotated models were released on the web-based platform within 24 h and downloaded by the centres for import into their respective 3D electro-anatomical mapping system for VT ablation. Computed tomography–guided VT ablation has been described previously24 and is detailed in the Supplementary data. Merging was a critical step completed using electro-anatomical reconstruction of the pulmonary artery bifurcation (in case of transseptal access)/aortic arch (in case of retro-aortic access) and the coronary sinus (see Supplementary data online, Video S1). Adequate merging was confirmed by positioning the ablation catheter at the left ventricular apex and the inferior, lateral, septal, and anterior left ventricular base.

Ablation was targeted to thickness isthmuses (Figure 3 and Supplementary data online, Video S2). The strategy aimed to connect the thinnest parts of the scar, acting as boundaries, to one another or to anatomical boundaries (e.g. mitral annulus) when close by. On each thickness isthmus, the local ablation endpoint was to render these areas non-capturable using a current strength of 10 V and 2 mA. Ablation was performed using a contact force catheter (>10 g), 40–50 W for at least 45–60 s. Once all thickness isthmuses were ablated, inducibility (two sites: right ventricle and left ventricle, up to S4, down to 200 ms) was attempted. In the absence of induced monomorphic VT, the procedure was ended. If monomorphic VT was induced, it was ablated using a combination of activation, substrate, and/or pace-mapping until non-inducibility or until the operator judged that the procedure should be concluded.

Conventional arm

Operators were free to use their standard workflow but should include substrate mapping and ablation without CT imaging integration. The use of contact force ablation catheters and electro-anatomical mapping systems was required in both arms. Computed tomography images were available for review but the 3D models were not made available. After gaining access, the procedure generally started with substrate mapping followed by inducibility testing. Ablation was not limited to clinical VT but extended to substrate modification. The procedural endpoint was similar in both arms: non-inducibility (with the same protocol) of any monomorphic VT.

Implantable cardioverter defibrillator programming and follow-up

To minimize differential measurement bias, ICDs were programmed according to a standardized protocol based upon published guidelines25 (see Supplementary data).

Trial endpoints

The primary endpoint was procedure duration, measured from catheter introduction to last radiofrequency (RF) application. Secondary efficacy endpoints included VT-free survival over the 12-month follow-up and evolution of VT burden between the 6 months before vs 6 months after ablation. Secondary safety endpoints were major adverse events occurring during the procedure and/or hospitalization following VT ablation. They were defined as those requiring prolonged hospitalization for cardiovascular causes or being fatal. Two ad hoc analyses compared clinical outcomes: (i) a composite endpoint of the absence of unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization, cardiovascular death, and VT event; (ii) a composite endpoint of the absence of cardiovascular-related death, electrical storm, appropriate shock, or sustained VT not treated by ICD. Follow-up is described in the Supplementary data.

Statistical analysis

Based on prior assumptions, including a mean procedure duration of 5 h [standard deviation (SD) 3.5] in the conventional group, an expected reduction of at least 2 h, and a SD of 2.5 in the CT-guided group, we estimated that a sample size of 56 patients per group (112 patients in total) would provide 90% power to detect this difference with a two-sided type I error of 5%, accounting for an anticipated 5% loss to follow-up.

The primary analysis was conducted in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, defined as all randomized patients who underwent the procedure, analysed according to their assigned group by randomization. A per-protocol population was also analysed, excluding patients from the CT-guided arm with a full left ventricular electro-anatomical substrate map performed with a high-density mapping catheter before CT-guided ablation. All analyses were performed on the available data.

Participants’ baseline and follow-up characteristics were described: qualitative variables were expressed as proportions and quantitative variables as either means with SDs or medians with inter-quartile ranges, as appropriate.

The primary endpoint (procedure duration) was first described within each group, and the unadjusted between-group difference with its 95% two-sided confidence interval (CI) was calculated. A second step consisted in comparing procedure durations using a linear mixed-effects model, including a random effect on countries (randomization stratification factor) and a fixed effect for treatment arm. The condition of validity (normality and homoscedasticity of residuals) of this model was systematically checked. The adjusted between-group mean difference and corresponding 95% CI were estimated from this model.

Statistical analyses for secondary endpoints included descriptive statistics and, when pre-specified, inferential analyses, using mixed-effect logistic regression models for estimating differences in percentages and corresponding 95% CI, or linear regression models for estimating differences in means and corresponding 95% CI, accounting for a random effect on participating countries and a fixed effect on procedure arm.

The family-wise error rate was controlled within each of the three pre-defined subfamilies of secondary endpoints—incidence of ICD therapies, safety outcomes, and anti-arrhythmic drug use—using the Holm–Sidak procedure26 to adjust for multiple comparisons. Furthermore, we used a hierarchical approach where we tested the first endpoint in each subfamily, and if not significant the other comparisons in the subfamily were only descriptive.

A two-sided type I error threshold of 5% was used for the primary analysis. P-values for non-statistically significant results according to the adjusted significance level are not reported.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Population

From June 2022 to January 2024, 113 patients [103 (91%) male, age 65 ± 9 years] were included in 14 centres (2 in Austria, 5 in France, 5 in Germany, and 2 in Switzerland). Of these, 11 centres (79%) were not using CT-guided VT ablation as part of their standard workflow prior to the study. The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 35 ± 11%, and 44 (39%) showed prior history of electrical storm. Substrate characteristics

Patients were referred for VT ablation 15 ± 10 years after their myocardial infarction. Infarct territories could be multiple and are described in Table 1. Image processing and isthmus annotation were successfully performed in all 113 participants. Wall thinning ≤5 mm was identified in all but one patient of the CT-guided group (in whom ablation was performed with activation mapping). Fatty metaplasia and calcification were identified in 58 (51%) and 32 (28%), respectively. A mean of 3.0 ± 1.3 thickness isthmuses were identified on CT scan. The agreement in isthmus detection between readers was 87% (95% CI 84%–90%) on an isthmus basis and 64% (95% CI 55%–73%) on a patient basis, discrepancies between readers occurring in patients with a larger set of isthmuses (agreement 85%, 95% CI 79%–92% in patients with ≤3 isthmuses).

These participants were the population used in per-protocol analyses. The CT-guided arm deviations included procedures in which a full left ventricular substrate map was performed before ablation as for the conventional arm. Image registration was successfully achieved in all 57 participants from the CT-guided arm.

In the CT-guided group, 43 (75%) patients were non-inducible after thickness isthmus ablation. In the remaining 14 (25%) patients, a total of 18 VTs were induced and targeted using conventional mapping. The additional areas targeted to eliminate these VTs were located within previously ablated regions in 4 (29%) and on the scar periphery in 10 (71%).

In the conventional group, targets identification for ablation used, in addition to voltage map (100%), pace-mapping (45%), activation mapping (62%), entrainment mapping (9%), and substrate mapping (ILAM/DEEP/LAVA/LP) in 80%.

The total RF time was 44 ± 19 min in the CT-guided arm vs 37 ± 20 min in the conventional arm. At the end of the procedure, non-inducibility of monomorphic VT was obtained in 48/51 (94%) in the conventional arm and 45/51 (88%) in the CT-guided arm. In five and six patients, respectively, the operators felt that they should end the procedure without the final electrophysiological study (EPS) attempt.

The mITT analysis of the primary endpoint showed a 19% (95% CI −32; −7; P = .0027) reduction in procedure duration in the CT-guided group (120 ± 50 vs 149 ± 51 min in the conventional group). In the per-protocol analysis, procedure duration was reduced by 28% (95% CI −40; −16; P < .0001) in the CT-guided group (Table 3).

Safety endpoints

Major adverse events during the procedure occurred in two (3.6%) patients in the conventional arm and one (1.8%) in the CT-guided arm [−1.8% (95% CI −7.9; 4.3), P = NS]. Major adverse events during hospitalization for VT ablation (including those during the procedure) were present in three (5.4%) patients in the conventional arm (one tamponade, one stroke, and one acute heart failure) and four (7%) in the CT-guided arm [one tamponade, one haemopericardium identified after the procedure and drained percutaneously, one pulmonary oedema, and one patient with pneumonia following the procedure (possible inhalation during anaesthesia)], which progressed to sepsis and death.

Follow-up

On the mITT analysis, VT-free survival at 12 months was obtained in 43 (76.8%) in the CT-guided arm vs 37 (67.3%) in the conventional arm [+9.5% (95% CI −10%; 29%), P = NS]. Ventricular tachycardia burden was reduced by 90% in the CT-guided arm vs 30% in the conventional arm, although this difference was due to one patient from the conventional arm exhibiting 180 VT episodes in the 6 months after ablation and may not represent any difference between the two strategies (Figure 5). Looking at the subgroup of patients with ablation performed with contact force catheters, VT-free survival at 12 months was 41 (80.4%) in the CT-guided arm vs 33 (66.0%) in the conventional arm [+14.4% (95% CI −1%; 32.7)].

The composite endpoint of the absence of unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization, cardiovascular death or VT events was met in 33/56 (59%) in the CT-guided arm vs 26/55 (47%) in the conventional arm (Table 3 and Figure 6). Discussion

InEurHeart is the first randomized controlled trial comparing image-guided ablation to conventional strategies based on electrophysiological mapping. It demonstrates that CT guidance significantly reduces the duration of post-infarction VT ablation procedures, while maintaining favourable efficacy and safety profiles (Structured Graphical Abstract). These findings suggest that non-invasive scar characterization via CT and digital infarct modelling can streamline complex substrate-based ablation procedures, offering a promising alternative to current practice.

The utility of imaging for VT ablation has been described for many years14,16–18,24,27 and is included in the 2019 expert consensus28 statement on scar-related VT ablation as well as in a recent consensus statement.22 Using MRI, prior non-randomized studies have shown that image guidance was associated with a lower need for RF delivery, higher non-inducibility rates after substrate ablation, higher VT-free survival,15 and favourable long-term outcomes.27 In the present study, CT scan was preferred over MRI because of its availability, safety, and image quality in the presence of ICDs, robustness of acquisition, and automated analysis. Computed tomography is routinely performed prior to VT ablation in many centres to rule out intra-cardiac thrombus,21,22 to detect protruding aortic plaques in the aortic arch that may preclude a retrograde aortic approach,29,30 and to assess the anatomy and substrate (scar distribution, presence of aneurysm). Using CT, image processing techniques have been developed to reconstruct a detailed cardiac anatomy in 3D and to characterize the substrate. On routine arterial-enhanced CT images, wall thickness analysis can identify areas of major thinning, likely acting as barriers during VT. These areas were found to typically delineate channels with relatively preserved wall thickness, able to sustain VT and act as the slow conduction path. These CT thickness channels (referred to as ‘ridges’ in some centres) were shown to match with the VT isthmus as identified during VT mapping.14,16,31 Thickness channel ablation has been used in post-infarction VT ablation with favourable outcome.17,18,24,32 In non-randomized case series, the procedure time was shorter when using CT-guided VT ablation (−17%) with similar non-inducibility and VT-free survival rates over a period of 19 ± 8 months18 when compared with conventional ablation. Englert et al.17 reported similar findings including patients with ischaemic and non-ischaemic substrates. Another advantage of displaying wall thickness is the ability to identify the thinnest and thereby most vulnerable part of the CT channel where ablation should be delivered to increase the likelihood of achieving transmural and durable lesions. Of note, target identification based on wall thickness heterogeneity may be less appropriate in patients with non-ICMP (NICMP)31 as severe and/or heterogeneous wall thinning is not consistently present in this population. Thus, late iodine-enhanced CT scan33,34 or late gadolinium-enhanced MRI15,27,35 is likely to provide more accurate substrate characterization in NICMP.

The substantial reduction in procedure time—19% in the mITT analysis and 28% in the per-protocol analysis—carries important clinical implications. The differences between intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations are likely due to centres with less experience in using the CT-guided approach, as some centres initially performed conventional substrate mapping prior to image registration in the CT-guided arm, in disagreement with the study protocol. Prolonged procedural duration has been recognized to be associated with increased hospital mortality, resource consumption, and patient discomfort, particularly in the fragile VT population often burdened by comorbidities.36 The approach described in the investigational arm of this study allows to create a digital model of the patient’s heart with detailed and precise anatomy as well as substrate description. It provides the operator with potential VT circuits before the ablation procedure, allowing for treatment planning without extensive invasive substrate mapping. It also allows to work in sinus rhythm, thereby reducing the number of induction attempts and other caveats of poorly tolerated VT episodes and related complications. Finally, it mitigates the need for experienced operators. Most of these aspects favourably contributed to reduce procedural duration. Of note, 11/14 centres participating in the study were not using routinely a CT-guided VT ablation workflow before the study but were experienced in conventional VT ablation. The protocol only required that they perform one CT-guided VT ablation before starting enrolling patients to ensure a smooth workflow. This, along with the variety of centres involved, supports the generalizability of our findings with a limited learning curve.

Importantly, the safety profile was maintained between groups, with rates of major adverse events comparable to conventional ablation. Previous observational studies suggest that reduced catheter dwelling time could mitigate risks such as cardiac perforation or thromboembolic events.17 Procedure-related complications in our study are in line with what has been reported in large randomized studies.37,38 Notably, although our population shared a similar left ventricular ejection fraction with these reference cohorts, they were likely referred later given a high rate of patients under amiodarone and with a history of electrical storm at inclusion.

Although not statistically significant, a numerically higher VT-free survival rate at 1 year was observed in the CT-guided group (77% vs 67%). This supports the hypothesis that ablation of thickness isthmuses may be sufficient to eliminate most arrhythmogenic substrates, with 75% of patients being non-inducible after thickness isthmus ablation. These findings align with prior work emphasizing the critical role of isthmus identification in achieving durable VT ablation success. The reduction in VT burden and rate of inducibility after CT-guided ablation suggest that procedural efficacy was comparable to conventional methods, but underpowered to draw definitive conclusions. The absence of VT recurrence at 1 year was obtained in 60% in VTACH,39 68.5% in VANISH 2,37 and 84.5% (substrate group) in VISTA40 (51.7% in the clinical VT group) and 80% in our pilot study.24 Interestingly, RF duration was found to be non-significantly but substantially longer in the CT-guided group (44 ± 19 vs 37 ± 20 min in the conventional arm). This suggests that part of the diagnostic time saved with pre-operative planning is converted into time devoted to therapy. The longer RF duration may have played a role in the higher number of patients with VT-free survival at 1 year in the CT-guided arm. In addition, CT isthmuses may lead to a more comprehensive treatment of the substrate, particularly by identifying VT channels that may not be functionally active at the time of the procedure. This had been nicely demonstrated by Vázquez-Calvo et al.41 They showed that image-based isthmuses (with MRI) were identifying even hidden deceleration zone that was unmasked during re-mapping after initial ablation.41

The substantial reduction in procedure time in combination with the reduction in the use of high-density mapping catheters improves the efficiency of the ablation procedure, likely leading to cost savings via reduced lab occupation, shorter procedure and limited induction favouring patients safety, and material cost. This is particularly relevant in healthcare systems under pressure to optimize resource utilization while maintaining high-quality care. An extensive health economics analysis is currently being conducted as part of the InEurHeart project and will be the subject of a separate publication.

Results from this study pave the way towards faster, simpler, and less costly VT ablation procedures, which may be key to ultimately make it more available to patients. The ongoing refinement of imaging technologies and integration of AI for scar characterization may further enhance the potential of image-guided ablation in the future. Similarly, increasing spatial resolution and tissue characterization with photon counting CT will help to better identify VT substrate and thus improve the specificity of target identification by imaging. To further improve both efficacy and efficiency, image-based strategies should ideally be combined with innovations in ablation technologies.42 Large footprint catheters delivering pulsed field energy were shown to induce larger, more transmural and durable lesions in preliminary studies and these may represent good candidates for ablation of imaging-defined channels. These efforts will likely replicate the course of innovations in atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation, which has evolved into a streamlined procedure thanks to a combination of anatomical target definition and single shot ablation devices, ultimately making it more accessible to patients.

Several limitations of our study merit discussion. First, the exclusion of patients with NICMP restricts the generalizability of our findings to the broader VT population. Scar patterns in non-ischaemic disease are extremely variable but less heterogeneous in terms of wall thinning. However, other modalities such as late-enhanced CT, photon counting CT, or MRI have been shown to be extremely helpful in these patients.17,27,33,34 Second, the conventional group was designed to adhere to the field practices of the various centres. Mapping and substrate ablation were recommended, but information on the substrate mapping method was not collected. Third, the number of enrolled female patients is limited (8.8%) as myocardial infarction is much more frequent in men. Fourth, sparse events such as sustained VT not treated by the ICD, and cardiovascular death yielded non-convergent multivariable models. However, unadjusted differences and corresponding 95% CI could still be reported and were sufficiently informative. Lastly, the study was not powered to detect a difference in VT recurrence; we calculated that a trial aiming to detect a difference in VT recurrence of at least 10% would require the inclusion of 316 patients per arm, considering a power of 80%, and a bilateral alpha risk of 5%. However, InEurHeart will help guiding the design of new studies evaluating the combination of imaging-guided ablation with better ablation tools.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CT guidance significantly reduces the duration of ablation procedures in patients with post-infarction VT. This represents a significant advance towards more standardized, predictable, efficient, and patient-tailored arrhythmia care. This strategy could meaningfully impact both clinical outcomes and healthcare system sustainability. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings across diverse patient populations and to explore the integration of multimodality imaging in VT ablation workflows, particularly in combination with new ablation energies.
