Encapsulation of essential oils from tropical plants as a strategy to mitigate methane emissions and improve nutritional status of ruminants: A review
Authors: Uswatun Muslykhah, Srisan Phupaboon, Sukruthai Sommai, Chaichana Suriyapha, Metha Wanapat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change, primarily driven by rising greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, presents a critical global challenge. Among agricultural sources, enteric methane (CH4) from ruminant livestock contributes significantly to global GHG emissions and represents a considerable energy loss from feed. Phytogenic feed additives (PFAs), particularly essential oils (EOs), have shown promise in modulating rumen fermentation, suppressing methanogenic archaea, and enhancing nutrient utilization due to their antimicrobial, antioxidant, and bioactive properties. The EOs can enhance animal health and productivity while mitigating CH4 emissions. Tropical plant-derived EOs, which contain volatile chemicals such as terpenoids and phenylpropanoids, have antimicrobial, antiviral, antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory characteristics, which are beneficial for livestock health and performance. The bioefficacy of EOs is influenced by their phytochemical composition, including terpenoids and phenylpropanoids, which are susceptible to environmental degradation. To overcome stability and delivery challenges, encapsulation techniques, especially microencapsulation, are employed to protect EOs, prolong shelf life, and enhance bioavailability in the rumen. The physicochemical characteristics of encapsulated particles, such as size, wall material, and oxidative stability, critically influence their efficacy. This review critically discusses the potential of tropical plant-derived EOs and encapsulation technologies to improve ruminant productivity and mitigate CH4 emissions, while highlighting research gaps and promoting sustainable livestock production systems.

Introduction

Climate change, largely driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, is an urgent global crisis that affects environment, economic growth, and human health (Anam et al., 2023; Ghanbari Maman et al., 2020). Methane (CH4), a potent GHG with a global warming potential 28 to 34 times greater than carbon dioxide (CO2) over 100 years, is predominantly emitted from enteric fermentation in ruminants (Bach et al., 2023). Beef and dairy cattle contribute approximately 35% and 30% of global CH4 emissions, respectively (den Toorn et al., 2020; Tseten et al., 2022). Numerous strategies have been explored to reduce ruminant CH4 emissions, including genetic selection, improved animal management, rumen microbiota manipulation, and dietary interventions (Vargas et al., 2020). Among these, dietary modulation, particularly through the use of feed additives, has gained traction due to its practicality and potential for immediate implementation (Honan et al., 2021). Feed additives derived from biological sources, such as essential oils (EOs), lipids, and direct-fed microbials, have shown promise in enhancing nutrient utilization while mitigating CH4 output (Tseten et al., 2022).

Essential oils, volatile secondary metabolites extracted from plants, have been widely studied, in vitro and in vivo, for their ability to modulate rumen fermentation. Their antimicrobial properties allow them to influence rumen microbial populations, either by directly inhibiting methanogens or indirectly suppressing protozoa and other microbes that facilitate methanogenesis (Amin et al., 2021; Bach et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2018; Ku-Vera et al., 2020). Additionally, these actions can shift rumen fermentation patterns, notably by reducing acetate production while increasing propionate and butyrate proportions, thereby improving feed efficiency and reducing CH4 emissions (Zhang et al., 2024), resulting in a reduced molar ratio of acetate and increased molar ratios of propionate and butyrate (Busquet et al., 2005).

Despite their potential, the application of EOs in ruminant feeding systems is limited by their instability, sensitivity to environmental conditions, and rapid degradation in the rumen (Mutlu-Ingok et al., 2020). To address these challenges, encapsulation technologies have been developed to protect and maintain the bioactive components of EOs, enhance their stability, and allow for targeted and sustained release (Muslykhah et al., 2024a; Sousa et al., 2022).

This review explores the use of encapsulated EOs, particularly those derived from tropical plants, as a CH4 mitigation strategy in ruminant nutrition. It highlights encapsulation techniques used to stabilize EOs, their effects on rumen fermentation, microbial dynamics, nutrient utilization, and potential implications for animal health. By synthesizing current knowledge and identifying research gaps, this review aims to support the development of effective, practical, and sustainable feed additive strategies for ruminant CH4 mitigation.

Phytogenic feed additives

Phytogenic feed additives (PFAs), also referred to as phytonutrients or phytobiotics, are generally characterized as a diverse group of plant secondary compounds (PSCs) and metabolites known to exert beneficial effects on livestock health and performance (Sharifi-Rad et al., 2017). The PFA are derived from various plants, including EOs, terpenoids, polyphenols, and flavonoids, have mechanisms of action to serve as regulators of the immune system, antioxidants, antimicrobials, and growth promoters (Biswas et al., 2024). Additionally, PFAs can positively influence rumen fermentation by altering microbial populations and pathways, thus reducing CH4 emissions, enhancing nutrient digestibility, and improving overall feed utilization (Dey et al., 2021), impact on ruminal fermentation and methanogenesis (Ahmed et al., 2024; Kholif and Olafadehan, 2021), mitigated CH4 production (Muslykhah et al., 2024c) enhanced the digestibility and nutrient utilization (Kholif and Olafadehan, 2021). Some studies have also reported the favorable effects of PFAs on reproductive performance in ruminants (Swelum et al., 2021).

Among the various categories of PFAs, EOs represent one of the most extensively studied and applied groups, due to their potent antimicrobial and antioxidant properties (Yang et al., 2015). The EOs are volatile aromatic compounds extracted from various plant parts, and historically used in traditional medicine, food preservation, cosmetics, and aromatherapy. In recent years, their applications have expanded to include pharmaceuticals, agricultural products, biopesticides, and animal feed additives (Marrelli et al., 2020; Ni et al., 2021). The wide-ranging biological activity of EOs is largely attributed to the complex mixtures of volatile compounds produced through the secondary metabolism of aromatic and medicinal plants, including antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal properties (Mishra et al., 2023; Tosato et al., 2021).

Essential oils from tropical plants

Plant EOs from tropical plants are composed of a complex blend of predominantly volatile and lipophilic compounds that contribute to their distinct aroma, flavor, and therapeutic potential. The volatile fraction of EOs consists mainly of terpenoids and phenylpropanoids, key contributors to their biological activities. Terpenoids are further subdivided into monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and diterpenes, while phenylpropanoids are synthesized from the shikimate pathway (Mollaei et al., 2023; Sharma and Sharma, 2021). In addition to volatile components, EOs may contain non-volatile constituents such as alkaloids, coumarins, anthraquinones, and fatty acids, which also contribute to their functional properties (Ríos, 2016; Sharma and Sharma, 2021). Most EOs are colorless liquids at room temperature and are generally less dense than water, although exceptions exist, such as cinnamon, sassafras, and vetiver oils, which exhibit higher densities. These properties, along with the chemical variability of EOs, are highly influenced by plant species, environmental conditions, and methods of extraction (Dhifi et al., 2016).

The chemical composition of EOs

Plant EOs are natural plant-derived with a complex chemical composition, containing over sixty (>60) low molecular weight organic compounds. These include phenolic (carvacrol, thymol, and eugenol) and terpene compounds (α-pinene, limonene, α-phellandrene, m-cymene, p-cymene, and γ-terpinene). Additionally, EOs contain aliphatic alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, and iso-flavonoids (Cobellis et al., 2016; Drozd et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2023). Pure EOs are generally categorized based on their chemical composition: oxygenated compounds (esters, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, phenols, and oxides) and hydrocarbons (monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and diterpenes) (Rushendran et al., 2023).

Most EO compositions are lipophilic and generally have a density lower than that of water (Pawlowicz and Yerubandi, 2023). The primary EO compounds, which make up 20%–70% of the total, can be divided into two main groups: terpenes (C10H16), synthesized by the mevalonate and deoxyxylulose pathways, and phenylpropanoids, synthesized by the shikimate pathway. Terpenoids play crucial roles in plant interactions, including intra- and inter-species communication signaling, attracting pollinators, and defending herbivores and microbes. Phenylpropanoids contribute to the biosynthesis of plant hormones, pigments, and other commercially important products, supporting plant metabolism and environmental stress defense (Bergman et al., 2024; Shende et al., 2024).

The chemical compounds of EOs are primarily influenced by several biotic and abiotic factors, including their source, plant species, growth stage, and parts used for extraction, as well as environmental factors, including soil composition and temperature, resulting in a wide range of components (Aqeel et al., 2023; Nath et al., 2024; Rushendran et al., 2023; Sharma and Sharma, 2021). Additionally, EOs may be found in all parts of plants, including leaves (e.g Thym, Eucalysptus, and Salvia), flowers (e.g rose, jasmine, violet, and lavender), rhizomes (e.g ginger), seeds (e.g cardamom), wood (e.g sandal), fruits (e.g anis and star anise), twigs, bark (e.g cinnamon), and zest (e.g citrus) (Dhifi et al., 2016; Nath et al., 2024). Furthermore, environmental stresses, both biotic and abiotic, can significantly impact plant growth and development, leading to specific plant responses at morphological, anatomical, and physiological levels. These stresses include temperature, drought, and nutrient deficiency, which can influence the geographical distribution of plant species and alter plant metabolism (Aqeel et al., 2023; Tiwari et al., 2020). Moreover, various environmental factors can modify the elemental composition of plants, leading to significant variability in the concentrations of macro- and micro-elements depending on the plant species and the proportional composition of components in a mixture (Aqeel et al., 2023).

The properties and biological activities of EOs, including their antimicrobial, antioxidant, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory effects, are determined by their composition, concentration and the proportions of their chemical components (Phupaboon et al., 2022a; Rushendran et al., 2023), as well as their medicinal application. Due to their bioactivities, for centuries, EOs have capabilities to act as antioxidant agents, enhance gut health, and alter rumen fermentation in small ruminants (Caroprese et al., 2023).

The EO properties and activities

Collectively, EOs exhibit numerous beneficial health properties due to their well-documented antioxidant activities and their capacity to scavenge free radicals. These compounds are capable of inhibiting lipid membrane peroxidation, oxidative chain reactions caused by free radicals, potentially preventing inflammatory diseases, cancer, atherosclerosis, and aging (Shanaida et al., 2018). They exhibit a diverse array of properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, insect repellent, anti-cancer, antiviral, anticancer, anti-parasitical, acaricidal, fungicidal, insecticidal, antipyretic, expectorant, and cytotoxic effects (Calo et al., 2015; Marrelli et al., 2020; Prakash et al., 2015). Many studies have demonstrated that EOs can effectively inhibit the production of mycotoxins, such as aflatoxin, and an antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria. Thyme and pine oil, containing phenolic or aldehydic terpenoids as major constituents, have shown remarkable efficacy in combating Gram-positive bacterial strains, generating large inhibition zones (Patterson et al., 2019).

The mechanism of action of EOs involves their lipophilic and low molecular weight components, which readily penetrate the fungal cell's plasma membrane. This penetration disrupts membrane permeability and osmotic balance, ultimately leading to cell death and downregulation of metabolic genes in fungal cells, affecting the plasma membrane and triggering antifungal effects (Mani-López et al., 2021). A primary mechanism of EOs is their ability to alter cell membrane permeability, leading to the depletion of intracellular components or the penetration of external substances into the cell (O'Bryan et al., 2015). The antifungal properties of EOs should be established before determining their targeted point or mode of action. It is crucial to ascertain whether a specific EO concentration can inhibit or kill fungal cells. Additionally, it's essential to distinguish between assays that determine the targeted point and those that elucidate the mechanism of action. The target site denotes the specific cellular component that interacts with EO compounds, which may include the cell membrane and wall, nucleic acids, intracellular proteins, enzymes, or metabolites.

Nutritional benefits of tropical plant oils on ruminants

Plant-derived EOs can significantly impact livestock species biologically. Scientific research on EOs in livestock has explored their use for addressing and treating microbial infections and parasites, alongside improving milk yield, animal performance, and rumen efficiency (Wells, 2024). Likewise, several investigations (Estrada-Angulo et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2018) have demonstrated improved weight gain, feed conversion efficiency, and nutrient digestibility in ruminants when EOs were included in their diets. Conversely, other studies have found that supplementing ruminant diets with EOs resulted in either adverse (Teobaldo et al., 2020) or neutral effects (Arteaga-Wences et al., 2021) on nutrient digestibility and overall productive performance, respectively. Furthermore, several research indicated enhanced meat quality, including a good color, increased tenderness, and reduced lipid peroxidation, in ruminants fed with EOs (Ortuño et al., 2017; Parvar et al., 2018).

The dietary EOs impact on various aspects of ruminants, especially in ruminal fermentation (Fandiño et al., 2020; Zotti et al., 2017). Despite these efforts, the findings remain inconsistent and controversial. To create EO-based products that effectively enhance ruminant productivity and health, it is important to elucidate the factors influencing these divergent results. Factors such as the main bioactive component of EOs, the duration of the experiment, the concentrate ratio in the feed, and the EO dosage are believed to influence the observed effects in EO-supplemented beef cattle (Benchaar, 2021; Irawan et al., 2021).

Improving fermentation end-products

Certain EOs positively influence rumen fungal and fibrolytic bacterial populations (Zhou et al., 2019), leading to enhanced fermentation process, the digestibility of dry matter (DMD), and neutral detergent fiber (NDFD). Encapsulation serves as a protective delivery system, enabling the gradual release of EOs in the rumen, which helps maintain their biological activity and prolongs their beneficial effects. Zhou et al. (2019) reported that the inclusion of EOs in sheep diets promoted fungal growth in the rumen. Additionally, both in vitro (Patra and Yu, 2015) and in vivo (Naseri et al., 2022) studies have demonstrated that encapsulated EOs increase the populations of fiber-degrading bacteria such as Ruminococcus albus, Fibrobacter succinogenes, and Ruminococcus flavefaciens, in the rumen fluid of sheep. Similarly, studies on beef cattle have shown that encapsulated EOs enhance the relative abundance of ruminal microbes, including Fibrobacteres, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes, which are strongly associated with improved NDFD (Xu et al., 2020), and elevated levels of β-glucosidase and cellulase enzymes in the rumen (Zhang et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the same EO can have different effects on ruminal microbiota based on the proportion in the feed. The effect of EOs on rumen microbiota is significantly influenced by ruminal pH, which determines the dissociated or undissociated state of EO molecules (Caroprese et al., 2023; Spanghero et al., 2008). Rumen pH serves as a vital marker for the internal stability of the rumen environment (Suriyapha et al., 2021). Several researchers have suggested that the undissociated hydrophobic form of EO molecules exhibits superior antimicrobial activity, attributed to its enhanced ability to integrate into the lipid bilayer of bacterial membranes. This mechanism is particularly pronounced under conditions of low rumen pH (Caroprese et al., 2023; Spanghero et al., 2008). The influence of rumen pH, which is inherently shaped by dietary composition, also governs the effects of EOs on VFA profiles and CH4 production. For instance, when thymol oil was introduced to ruminal fluid sourced from cattle consuming a 60:40 roughage-to-concentrate (R:C) diet at pH 6.4, an increase in the acetate-to-propionate (C2:C3) ratio was observed. In contrast, its addition to ruminal fluid from cattle fed a 10:90 R:C diet at pH 5.5 resulted in a decreased C2:C3 ratio (Calsamiglia et al., 2007). Several review articles (Benchaar, 2021; Irawan et al., 2021; Kholif and Olafadehan, 2021; Simitzis, 2017) have highlighted the potential of EOs to optimize rumen fermentation processes, enhance livestock productivity, and improve the quality of ruminant-derived products.

Antimicrobial effects

The EOs have been found to impact multiple targets simultaneously in bacterial cells, producing a multi-target effect that differs from traditional antimicrobial agents. The antimicrobial mode of action of EOs involves disrupting cellular structures and processes in microbial pathogens. In fungal pathogens, EOs induce a membrane potential across the cell wall, impair ATP synthesis, and destabilize the mitochondrial membrane, ultimately disrupting the electron transport chain. In bacterial pathogens, EOs predominantly disrupt cellular architecture, resulting in compromised membrane integrity, interference with cellular processes, and leakage of intra-cellular components and ions (Tariq et al., 2019). In fungi, EOs destabilize mitochondrial membranes, disrupt ATP synthesis, and compromise membrane potential. These effects arise largely from the hydrophobic nature of EO compounds, which integrate into the lipid bilayer of microbial membranes (Dorman and Deans, 2000).

The addition of oregano extracts significantly influenced the regulation of bacterial populations in the gastrointestinal tract of calves. This effect can be attributed to the antimicrobial properties of EOs, which are primarily due to their hydrophobic characteristics, facilitating interactions with the cell membrane, leading to their accumulation in the phospholipid bilayer. The cell walls of gram-positive bacteria primarily consist of peptidoglycan, which is interconnected with various molecules, including proteins and teichoic acids. Prolonged interactions result in structural alterations and expansion of the membrane, reduced ion transfer, and ultimately a decreased ruminal bacterial population (Dorman and Deans, 2000; Nikaido, 1994). Consequently, gram-negative bacteria are generally regarded as less susceptible to the antimicrobial effects of EOs compared to gram-positive bacteria (Trombetta et al., 2005). Nonetheless, the hydrophobic constituents of EOs can traverse the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria by diffusing through porin proteins into the periplasmic space, albeit at a relatively slower rate (Helander et al., 1998; Plésiat and Nikaido, 1992).

Supplementation at a dosage of 60 mg/kg body weight per day has been reported to enhance bacterial diversity within the rumen and jejunum, influencing the abundance of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Ritt et al., 2023). Notably, the populations of pathogenic genera, including Streptococcus, Escherichia, and Clostridium were reduced, along with a decrease in Bifidobacterium populations in the jejunum and cecum. Additionally, dietary supplementation with oregano EO at 52 mg per head per day in small-tailed Han Hu female lambs was associated with improved average daily gain (ADG) and a reduced feed-to-gain (F/G) ratio (Jia et al., 2022).

The impact of EOs on rumen microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, and protozoa can vary, exhibiting either inhibitory or stimulatory effects on microbial populations based on the specific EO used. In an in vivo study with sheep, the use of Pistacia atlantica subspecies Kurdica gum EO did not affect protozoa populations but resulted in a 10% increase in total bacteria, a 62% increase in R. flavefaciens, and a 32% decrease in rumen methanogens (Naseri et al., 2022). Conversely (Kim et al., 2018), reported an increase in protozoal populations when administering a commercial EO mixture containing cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and thymol. In their in vitro study using a fermentation culture from heifers, lower concentrations of the EO mixture led to elevated abundances of Selenomonas ruminantium and Ruminococcus albus, whereas higher concentrations increased the abundance of fungi, Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens, and R. flavefaciens. However, supplementation of dairy cow diets with oregano leaves resulted in a reduction in the population of R. flavefaciens in the rumen (Oh and Hristov, 2016). Additionally, the effects of EOs on rumen microbiota can vary not only due to different types of EOs but also because the variability in the composition of EOs from the same plant species, which can be influenced by factors such as harvest seasons and geographical locations (Vokou et al., 1993).

Mitigation of CH4 production

Essential oils are recognized for their role as rumen modifiers, positively influencing starch and protein degradation, ammonia concentration, VFA production, and CH4 emissions. They can alter and influence the rumen and gut microbiota. These effects often include alterations in fermentation processes, which may result in decreased CH4 production (Caroprese et al., 2023). However, Cobellis et al. (2016) noted that while EOs may decrease the rumen populations of methanogens, they could also have adverse effects on R. flavefaciens, R. albus, and F. succinogenes. In contrast, the meta-analysis conducted by Dorantes-Iturbide et al. (2022) found that EOs increased the abundance of R. flavefaciens without impacting R. albus and F. succinogenes, possibly due to the reduction in total rumen protozoa, which is negatively correlated with R. flavefaciens abundance in dairy goats. The reduction in total rumen protozoa could be beneficial, as increased protozoan populations are associated with higher ruminal protein degradation (Newbold et al., 2015) and CH4 productions (Guyader et al., 2014), reducing the efficiency of protein and energy utilization and limiting small ruminant productivity.

Mode of action of EOs or PFAs on rumen microbial cells

Essential oils or PFAs mitigate CH4 production in ruminants through multiple complementary mechanisms that directly or indirectly influence rumen microbial ecology and fermentation pathways. Unlike antibiotics, which typically exert a narrow spectrum of antimicrobial activity, PFAs possess structurally diverse bioactive compounds such as terpenoids, phenolics, saponins, and flavonoids, which allow them to act through more diverse and adaptable mechanisms, modifying the ruminal microbiome and consequently influencing fermentation efficiency and CH4 production (Bature et al., 2024; Bodas et al., 2012). These mechanisms include disruption of microbial cell membranes, interference with signal transduction and gene expression, inhibition of enzyme activity, and prevention of microbial adhesion and colonization within the rumen environment (Lillehoj et al., 2018; Wanapat et al., 2013).

Methane formation in the rumen primarily depends on methanogenic archaea, which utilize hydrogen (H2) and CO2 generated from primary fermentation products to produce CH4 (Wanapat et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). Supplementation with specific phytogenic compounds such as condensed, hydrolysable tannins (Jayanegara et al., 2015), and mixed phytogenic supplementation (Phupaboon et al., 2025), combinations of saponin, tannin, and EO (Jafari et al., 2019), as well as banana flower (Dagaew et al., 2025), has been shown to significantly reduce both the population and metabolic activity of methanogens. These reductions suggest that one of the major modes of action of PFAs involves suppression of methanogenic archaea, leading to decreased CH4 formation.

Another key CH4-reducing mechanism involves defaunation or suppression of ruminal protozoa, which play a vital role in interspecies H2 transfer. Hydrogenosomes within protozoal cells generate H2, which is subsequently utilized by methanogens through the hydrogenotrophic pathway to produce CH4 (Belanche et al., 2014). A reduction in protozoal numbers therefore diminishes H2 availability for methanogenesis. Several phytogenic compounds, such as some fruit peels, mulberry leaf flavonoids, and mixed some phytogenic sources (lemongrass and dragon fruit peel), have demonstrated protozoa-suppressing effects, leading to lower CH4 output (Hassan et al., 2020; Wanapat et al., 2021; Suriyapha et al., 2025).

Essential oils also exhibit both direct and indirect effects on methanogenesis. Directly, they can inhibit methanogen growth by disrupting membrane potential and enzyme systems critical for methanogenesis. Indirectly, EOs reduce CH4 emissions by modulating the populations of bacteria, archaea, and protozoa that coexist symbiotically with methanogens (Zhang et al., 2024). The extent of their impact on rumen microbial fermentation and CH4 production varies with concentration, low inclusion levels often exert moderate or negligible effects, whereas higher concentrations significantly suppress CH4 formation (Nunes et al., 2023).

Furthermore, the inclusion of EOs in ruminant diets can alter rumen microbial diversity, composition, and structure, particularly by shifting the methanogen community toward less hydrogen-dependent taxa (Ku-Vera et al., 2020). These microbial changes often correspond with improved fermentation efficiency, enhanced nutrient digestibility, and reduced CH4 emissions. Beyond their anti-methanogenic potential, EOs have been associated with improved animal performance, better feed efficiency, and enhanced oxidative stability of animal products such as milk and meat (Dorantes-Iturbide et al., 2022).

Collectively, these findings indicate that EOs and PFAs modulate rumen fermentation through synergistic antimicrobial, antiprotozoal, and anti-methanogenic actions. By selectively targeting microbial populations, regulating fermentation end-products, and minimizing hydrogen transfer to methanogens, these natural additives provide an effective and sustainable strategy for improving rumen function and mitigating CH4 emissions in ruminants.

Impact on ruminant health and performance

The inclusion of EOs in the feed resulted in an enhanced propionate concentration and a reduction of protozoa diversity, without affecting the concentrations of acetate and butyrate in the rumen. Consistent with this study, Orzuna–Orzuna et al. (2022) demonstrated that EOs decreased rumen protozoa population and enhanced ruminal propionate concentration in beef cattle. The increase in propionate observed in this study indicates that EOs may improve energy availability for growth and production. This is because propionate serves as a key energy source for anabolic processes in ruminants (Chen et al., 2020).

Several review articles (Dorantes-Iturbide et al., 2022; Mucha and Witkowska, 2021; Anam et al., 2022Nehme et al., 2021) have concluded that the addition of EOs in the diets had the potential to enhance animal productivity, rumen fermentation parameters, product quality (meat and milk), milk yield, and health in ruminants. In vitro studies (Wang et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2020) have demonstrated that the addition of EOs enhanced the populations of bacterial families such as Lachnospiraceae, Rikenellaceae, and Christensenellaceae, which are positively associated with ADG and feed conversion ratio (FCR) in sheep (Yang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022). Additionally, certain EOs have been found to decrease the populations of Veillonellaceae bacteria (Zhou et al., 2020), which are negatively associated with the total volatile fatty acid (TVFA) productions and ADG in sheep (Mao et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). Further research has shown that EO additives improved dietary energy utilization efficiency by up to 17% for maintenance and 23% for weight gain in lambs (Estrada-Angulo et al., 2021). Similarly, studies by An et al. (2020) and Wu et al. (2021) found that EO dietary supplementation in sheep elevates serum concentrations of immunoglobulins (immunoglobulin [Ig] A, IgG, and IgM), which may potentially enhance health status and productive performance. These beneficial effects were corroborated with both beef cattle and dairy cows, suggesting that EOs can improve animal performance and the health and quality of livestock products. Consequently, there is growing interest in using natural products to improve livestock health and productivity, with EOs gaining significant economic importance. Several bovine studies have investigated the incorporation of EOs in ruminant diets to boost milk production. This enhancement is often attributed to the positive effects of adding EOs on immune responses and oxidative status, leading to improved animal health and welfare Caroprese et al. (2020). In small ruminants, the inclusion of EOs is intended to augment milk and meat production by improving feed flavor and palatability, which, in turn, can increase voluntary intake and weight gain. However, while this outcome is not consistently achieved, a rise in the antioxidant activity of both meat and milk is commonly reported (Caroprese et al., 2023).

Encapsulation methods for tropical plant oils

Encapsulation is a crucial method for protecting EOs from degradation and preserving their properties (Reis et al., 2022). Encapsulation involves incorporating one or more bioactive compounds (which may be coated materials, core, or internal phase) within a uniform or diverse matrix (such as shells, walls, or carrier materials) at microscopic (ranging from 1 to 5000 μm) or nanoscopic (less than 1 μm) scales (Aguiar et al., 2016; Phupaboon et al., 2022a). This technique effectively shields the encapsulated molecules from harmful external factors such as degradation, oxidation, and evaporation (Tian et al., 2018). The decreased size and increased surface-to-volume ratio of micro- and nano-particles enhance bioavailability and promote the diffusion of EOs to their intended targets, thereby ensuring effective long-term activity (Campana et al., 2017; Cui et al., 2016; Shishir et al., 2018). Furthermore, encapsulation allows the use of smaller quantities of EOs, reducing the likelihood of microorganisms developing resistance, minimizing toxic effects, and decreasing economic costs.

Various encapsulation techniques have been explored, including spray drying, coacervation, and emulsion-based delivery systems (Phupaboon et al., 2022b; Reis et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Currently, microencapsulation is the most commonly used method in research. Many previous studies have highlighted the use of materials such as maltodextrin, gum arabic, alginate, and polylactic acid for encapsulating EOs (Bajac et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2022; Reis et al., 2022). Many earlier studies investigated different microencapsulation technologies for plant EOs. These include emulsification procedures utilizing hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose, chitosan, or silica for peppermint oil (Lai et al., 2021), and ionic gelation using cassava starch or polybutylene adipate-co-terephthalate for oregano oil (de Medeiros et al., 2019). Lemongrass oil has undergone simple coacervation with poly vinyl alcohol (Leimann et al., 2009), but oregano, citronella, and lavender oils have undergone sophisticated coacervation with gelatin encapsulants (Bezerra et al., 2016; de Matos et al., 2018; Hernández et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2014). Interfacial polymerization has encapsulated lemongrass and lavender oils with polyurea (Scarfato et al., 2007), whereas in situ polymerization has been used for cinnamon and thyme oils with silicon dioxide and melamine (Chung et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020). Extrusion methods encapsulated nutmeg and rosemary oils with sodium alginate (Dolçà et al., 2015; Matulyte et al., 2020), and spray drying was applied to basil, cinnamon, rosemary, juniper berry, and citrus oils using maltodextrin (Asensio et al., 2017; Bajac et al., 2022; Fernandes et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2020; Ozdemir et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2020). Freeze-drying techniques have been used for Origanum onites L. oil with gum-arabic or collagen (Ocak, 2020), and supercritical fluid technology has encapsulated oregano, lavandin, and limonene oils using starch (Almeida et al., 2013; Machado et al., 2016; Varona et al., 2010). The most commonly employed microencapsulation techniques include spray drying and coacervation (Sousa et al., 2022).

Characterization of particles

Encapsulation can be classified based on the particle size produced: nano- and microencapsulation are the two most commonly employed methods (Marcillo-Parra et al., 2021; Shishir et al., 2018). Microencapsulation processes are further divided into three categories: 1) physical methods, which encompass techniques such as extrusion, solvent evaporation, spray drying, supercritical fluid precipitation, and lyophilization (Abdul Mudalip et al., 2021); 2) physicochemical methods, such as emulsion systems, coacervation, liposome formation, and ionic gelation (Chopde et al., 2020); and 3) chemical methods, including interfacial polymerization and molecular inclusion complexation (Ozkan et al., 2019). Additionally, nano-encapsulation is a method that entails incorporating bioactive compounds into nano-carriers smaller than 1000 nm, utilizing layers, films, or nano-dispersions (Muslykhah et al., 2024a).

Microencapsulation technologies serve multiple purposes, particularly by protecting the core active ingredient from exposure to O2, light, and moisture by preventing interactions with other substances. Nevertheless, the primary objective of encapsulating an active ingredient is to enable controlled release (Petrusic and Koncar, 2016). The design of a microencapsulation system is heavily influenced by the intended application of the microcapsules. Key attributes such as shape, size, permeability, biodegradability, and biocompatibility are tailored according to the specific application of the material. Additionally, the evaluation of other physical and mechanical properties, including strength and flexibility, must also be assessed (Zhang and Rochefort, 2012).

Physical and mechanism of microencapsulation

The physicochemical properties of microcapsules are primarily determined by both the encapsulating and the bioactive substances. The coat material should form a uniform film that effectively adheres to the encapsulated content (Mehta et al., 2022). Various materials could be utilized for the coating process, including carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins, which are frequently utilized. Additionally, these materials must exhibit chemical compatibility, and the encapsulating agent should be chemically inert to avoid reactions with the core substance (Nesterenko et al., 2013).

Key properties commonly evaluated in microcapsule analysis include moisture content, water activity, particle size, surface oil, and entrapment efficiency. Among these, moisture content plays a crucial role in determining the storage stability of microcapsules. Excessive moisture can result in caking or clumping of the dried powder, promote microbial growth, and accelerate lipid oxidation, leading to undesirable off-flavors. The moisture content of microcapsules is influenced by several factors, including the water retention capacity of the wall materials, the relative humidity within the drying chamber, and the thermal conditions during spray drying, particularly the inlet and outlet temperatures. Enhancing the physical properties of microcapsules for improved bioavailability and functionality necessitates careful consideration of wall material selection, encapsulation techniques, and release mechanisms (Orlova et al., 2021; Price, 2023).

Water activity affects microbial spoilage and lipid oxidation in microcapsules by creating a microenvironment that influences the oxidation reactions of lipids and the stability of microemulsions, thereby impacting the product's shelf life. Oxidative degradation generally occurs at a reduced rate when water activity is maintained within the range of 0.2 to 0.3, indicating that maintaining water activity within this range can enhance the stability of microcapsules (Budilarto and Kamal-Eldin, 2015). The microenvironment created by surfactant compounds, such as trace amounts of water and amphiphilic minor components, affects lipid oxidation in a complex fashion, while antioxidants inhibit lipid oxidation by physically stabilizing the micelles at the reaction sites within their microenvironments (Budilarto and Kamal-Eldin, 2015; Linke et al., 2020). The water activity of spray-dried microcapsules is generally affected by factors including the choice of wall materials, the drying parameters (such as temperature and airflow), and the design of the dryer.

The particle size of capsules in microencapsulation techniques is largely determined by the specific microencapsulation method employed. Key factors influencing particle size include the dimensions of the nozzle, the feeding rate, air pressure, and the total solid content involved in the process. The size of the nozzle impacts particle size by allowing precise control, while the feeding rate and air pressure directly affect the particle size of microcapsules. Although the total solid content was not directly addressed in the provided abstracts, it is a known factor that can also influence particle size (McNamee et al., 1998; Narsaiah et al., 2014).

Oxidative stability

Encapsulating oils enhances the oxidative stability of their lipids and shields sensitive components, including bioactive substances, oils, vitamins, and flavor compounds, from environmental factors. This process improves the solubility and mixability of the core material while regulating its release to ensure a controlled delay until the target stimulus is reached. Additionally, encapsulation minimizes the evaporation of volatile compounds and masks or conceals any unpleasant flavors associated with the core material (Mohammed et al., 2020).

Oils rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, leading to undesirable flavors during processing and storage, are highly susceptible to oxidative degradation. Encapsulation methods offer a promising approach to enhancing the oxidative stability of these oils. The oxidative stability of encapsulated oils is determined by various factors, including the composition of wall materials (type and concentration of polymer solutions), processing parameters, and specific feed formulation needs (Khoshnoudi-Nia et al., 2022).

For instance, García-Moreno et al. (2018) investigated electro-sprayed encapsulation using carbohydrate and whey protein-based materials, such as glucose syrup, pullulan, and dextran, for fish oil. Their results indicated that rotor-stator emulsification yielded superior oxidative stability compared to high-pressure homogenization. Among the encapsulated materials, glucose syrup exhibited the highest oxygen stability, attributed to its lower molecular weight, which reduced oxygen diffusivity by minimizing the free volume within the glassy matrix.

Similarly, Dórame-Miranda et al. (2021) evaluated the encapsulation of sardine oil using pecan nutshell extract and gliadin proteins as coating materials via electro-spraying techniques. They observed that ethanol-based encapsulated sardine oil migrated to the core during electrospray deposition due to phase separation, resulting in high encapsulation efficiency (EE). The migration of lipids enhanced oxidative stability by promoting interactions between gliadin and lipid molecules through electrostatic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen bonding forces.

In a comparative study, Fallahasghari et al. (2024) assessed the oxidative stability of cod liver oil encapsulated using mono-axial and co-axial electro-spraying methods. Mono-axial microcapsules demonstrated a 6.27-fold greater differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve area at 160 °C compared to co-axial microcapsules, reflecting superior oxidative stability. This improvement was attributed to the reduced oxygen permeability on the mono-axial microcapsule surfaces and the hydrophobic interactions facilitated by ethyl cellulose as coating material.

The choice of wall materials is crucial for enhancing oxidative stability. For example, β-carotene encapsulated with a mixture of whey protein isolate and pullulan (30/70 w/w) exhibited significantly higher oxidative stability compared to encapsulation using pullulan alone. This effect is linked to the capacity of whey protein isolate to establish hydrogen bonds with lipophilic compounds, thereby mitigating carotenoid–oxygen interactions (Drosou et al., 2022).

Release behavior

The bioavailability of oils rich in omega-3, -6, and -9 fatty acids, which determines their health benefits, is significantly influenced by their release from microcapsules. Microencapsulation provides a controlled method for regulating the release of these oils at the appropriate time and location, ensuring effective delivery of the required dose. Several mechanisms can trigger the release of core materials from microcapsules, including 1) diffusion, controlled by the physicochemical properties of the wall materials when the structure remains intact; 2) degradation, induced by enzymes such as protease and amylase that break down the wall materials; 3) dissolution, promoted by solvents that expand the wall materials and facilitate release; 4) changes in pH and ionic strength, which alter the solubility of the wall materials; and 5) increased temperatures, which can cause the wall material to collapse or melt, thereby enhancing the release (Silva et al., 2014). The dynamics of core material release are influenced by factors such as solubility in the medium, diffusion capacity of the solvent, polymer swelling, wall material decomposition, and external conditions like touch, light, and pH. Microencapsulation release processes encompass diffusion, dissolution, erosion, osmosis, and rupture (Ahmad et al., 2021).

The release behavior of oil encapsulation in simulated gastrointestinal systems is modulated by several critical factors, including the composition of the simulated gastric fluid, EE, and the mechanical properties of the encapsulating matrix (Cheng et al., 2022; Evteev et al., 2018). It is evident that the release behavior of encapsulated oil in simulated ruminal fluids is influenced by the composition of the simulated ruminal fluid, the presence of pepsin and NaCl (pH 1.2), and the differences in release behavior between simulated ruminal fluid and simulated intestinal fluid in ruminants. The EE, mechanical properties, and stabilities of the emulsions in different simulated rumen conditions are pivotal in determining the release behavior of encapsulated oil. Additionally, the presence of pepsin and the pH level of the simulated ruminal fluid significantly impact the release behavior of encapsulated oil (Evteev et al., 2018; Guan et al., 2022; Jayanudin and Wirawan, 2018). The primary determinants of release characteristics involve the interactions between wall and core substances, the physical attributes of the microcapsules (such as particle size and surface oil content), the solubility and viscosity of the wall materials, and the structural design of the microcapsules (Silva et al., 2014).

Many previous studies on encapsulation have shown varying oil release outcomes due to differences in the methods and wall matrices used. According Muslykhah et al. (2024b) conducted the in vitro study comparing un-protected duckweed extract and microencapsulation duckweed extract. This study resulted that microencapsulation showed more effectiveness in increasing cumulative gas production and the rumen fermentation product while mitigating the CH4 emissions. In addition research by Prachumchai et al. (2024) adding microencapsulation moringa leaf and lemongrass could enhance cumulative gas production, NDF and true digestibility, C3 production, and microbial population, while reducing CH4 concentration and Methanobacterial abundance in in vitro study.

To ensure that essential strains successfully transit through the rumen and avoid degradation, enabling effective delivery to the intestinal tract, it is crucial to improve current strategies. The microencapsulation process, however, faces several limitations, including challenges related to embedding volume, stability, and metabolite expression. Factors such as excessive oxygen, high relative humidity and temperature, as well as excessive pressure and heat generated during mechanical stirring, can compromise the viability of microorganisms (Wei et al., 2022).

Advantages for ruminant nutrition

Plant-based products, particularly EOs, are known for their strong antimicrobial properties, making them promising candidates as novel preservatives. However, their applications in ruminant nutrition are limited by their hydrophobicity, susceptibility to oxidation, high volatility, and poor stability and solubility. Encapsulation offers a promising solution to these challenges by creating a protective barrier around the EOs, shielding them from exposure and degradation (Sharma et al., 2023). As in the previous studies, this technique improves the stability and shelf-life of the oils, facilitates controlled release and targeted delivery in the digestive tract, and protects the oils from degradation in the rumen environment. Consequently, encapsulation enhances the absorption and bioavailability of nutrients, ultimately improving the efficacy of EOs in ruminant nutrition.

Moreover, encapsulation technology can substantially alter the ruminal fatty acid profile. For example, Besharati et al. (2022) studied encapsulation of flaxseed oil with chitosan at levels of 14% and 7% led to a marked decrease in the proportion of ruminal saturated fatty acids, while significantly increasing the proportion of oleic (unsaturated) fatty acids in the rumen. Specifically, encapsulating flaxseed oil with chitosan at 14% effectively reduced the extent of ruminal biohydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids, demonstrating that the protective encapsulation layer limits microbial access to unsaturated lipids. This mechanism reduces the conversion of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids in the rumen, preserving the beneficial lipid profile while lowering hydrogen utilization, which in turn limits substrate availability for methanogenesis.

A significant advantage of microencapsulation is its ability to regulate, sustain, or target the release of active ingredients. This release can occur through mechanisms such as wall rupture or diffusion, or it may take place at specified periods. Variations in temperature, solubility, pH shifts, or the wall material's biodegradability can all trigger it (Hoyos-Leyva et al., 2018). Microencapsulation techniques might be chemical, physicochemical, or mechanical, depending on the type of interaction that occurs between the encapsulating and encapsulated materials (Jamekhorshid et al., 2014). Generally, an effective microencapsulation method should be rapid, straightforward, reproducible, and easily scalable for industrial applications. The previous study by Muslykhah et al. (2024b) stated that microencapsulation of duckweed extract (mDWE) and Phupaboon et al. (2025) enhanced higher than duckweed powder the fraction gas b, |a|+b, cumulative gas production, DMD and organic matter degradability, and propionic acid levels. Conversely, it reduced CH4 production, acetate levels, and the abundance of R. albus, R. flavefaciens, and Methanobacteriales. As reviewed by Besharati et al. (2024), the encapsulation of unsaturated lipids provides a simple yet efficient means of improving the fatty acid profile of ruminant-derived meat and milk, enriching them with beneficial unsaturated fatty acids.

Challenges and limitations

Despite the promising benefits of tropical plant EOs in animal nutrition, their application faces several challenges and limitations. One significant challenge is the variability in the chemical composition of EOs, which can be influenced by numerous factors such as plant species, geographical location, growth conditions, and extraction methods. This variability can lead to inconsistent effectiveness and biological activities when used in animal feed. Moreover, the highly volatile nature of EOs compromises their stability and preservation, often necessitating advanced encapsulation techniques to maintain their efficacy over time.

Encapsulation itself introduces complexities, including the need for appropriate materials and methods to ensure stability and controlled release of EOs. The cost and technical expertise required for encapsulation processes can be prohibitive, limiting their practical application in large-scale animal feed production. Additionally, interactions between EOs and other feed components, as well as their potential impacts on the sensory properties of feed, can affect animal intake and overall feed efficiency.

Furthermore, there are concerns regarding the potential development of microbial resistance due to the prolonged use of EOs with antimicrobial properties. This necessitates careful consideration of dosage and administration to mitigate such risks. Regulatory and safety issues also present significant challenges, as approval processes for the use of EOs in animal feed vary across regions, often requiring extensive testing to demonstrate their safety and efficacy. These challenges underscore the need for continued research and development to overcome the limitations and harness the full potential of tropical plant EOs in animal nutrition.

Conclusion and future perspective

Plant-derived EOs offer promising benefits in ruminant nutrition through their ability to modulate rumen microbial populations, enhance fiber degradation, and reduce enteric CH4 emissions. Their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties not only influence rumen fermentation pathways, promoting higher propionate production and improved energy efficiency, but also contribute to improved gut health by suppressing pathogenic microbes. However, challenges such as volatility, low stability, and variable bioactivity under rumen conditions have limited their practical application. Recent advancements in encapsulation technologies, including nano-emulsions, spray drying, and polymeric carriers, have addressed many of these issues by improving EO stability, protecting active compounds, and enabling targeted release in the gastrointestinal tract. These technologies have shown potential to enhance nutrient digestibility, optimize rumen fermentation, and even improve reproductive performance in ruminants, as highlighted by several studies summarized in Table S1.

However, the practical application of EOs in ruminant diets faces challenges due to their instability, volatility, and susceptibility to degradation during feed processing and ruminal fermentation. To address these limitations, encapsulation technologies have emerged as a pivotal innovation. By protecting EOs from environmental degradation and enabling controlled release, encapsulation enhances their bioavailability and efficacy in the digestive tract. Techniques such as spray drying, coacervation, and nanoencapsulation have shown promising results in stabilizing tropical plant-derived EOs, reducing required dosages, and minimizing microbial resistance risks.

Looking forward, future research should prioritize the optimization of encapsulation methods tailored to specific EOs and their functional roles in ruminant nutrition. This includes developing cost-effective, scalable, and biocompatible encapsulating agents. Furthermore, standardized protocols for EO extraction, formulation, and quality control are essential to ensure consistency and efficacy. In-depth in vivo studies are needed to better understand EO interactions with rumen microbiota, animal metabolism, and immune responses. Additionally, economic feasibility, long-term safety, regulatory clarity, and environmental impacts of EO supplementation must be critically evaluated. Addressing these aspects will enable the integration of encapsulated EOs as sustainable feed additives, ultimately supporting enhanced animal performance, improved product quality, and reduced environmental footprint in ruminant livestock systems.
