The Minamata Convention – The Road to Safer Practices
Authors: Girish Malleshappa Sogi
Published: 2024

The Minamata Convention (MC), a global treaty adopted internationally in 2013, aims to safeguard public health and the environment from anthropogenic emissions of mercury and mercury compounds. The MC is a reminder, to the global community, of the precious lives lost due to catastrophic mercury poisoning in Minamata Bay in Japan in the late 1950s and onward. In 2003, the Global Mercury Assessment was presented to the 22nd United Nations’ Environmental Program Governing Council, and after being formally adopted and opened for signature at the Diplomatic Conference of Plenipotentiaries in Kumamoto, Japan, on October 10, 2013, it entered into force on August 16, 2017, and its first Conference of the Parties met in September 2017 in Geneva. The MC recognizes mercury as a pollutant of worldwide concern considering its ability of long-range transport, persistence, and bioaccumulation, which ultimately results in elevated human exposure levels associated with a range of negative health effects. The convention provides controls and reductions across a range of products, processes, and industries where mercury is used, released, or emitted.

Owing to the ever-increasing economic development in recent decades, India is among the world’s top emitters of anthropogenic mercury to the environment, with projections of its continuous significant contributions to the global mercury emissions even in the times to come. Globally, India is the second-largest mercury emitter to the environment with estimated emissions of 144.7 tons Hg/year. One of the major contributors of mercury emissions in India is the waste of electronic and healthcare products, accounting for 13.6 tons Hg/year. To minimize India’s contribution to global mercury pollution, India signed the convention on September 30, 2014, and ratified it on June 18, 2018, with the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, Government of India, being the national focal point.

The convention has 35 articles and the dental amalgam is among a number of products listed in the convention as “Mercury-added Products” under the Article 4 (product or component that contains mercury or a mercury compound that was intentionally added). The use of mercury in dental amalgam represents the most common form of human exposure to elemental mercury (recognized as a toxic substance) and along with its use in laboratory/medical devices and comprises 53% of total global mercury emissions, leading to potential environmental damage. This convention could have a lasting impact on the future of global oral health practices too. The consequential health impact of mercury exposure from dental restoration is yet in dispute although research has shown a positive correlation between the number of dental amalgam restoration surfaces and the levels of mercury in human blood, tissue, and urine, whether such exposure equates to significant negative health risk is still under debate and requires further exploration. Ongoing debate regarding the future use of dental amalgam in global oral health care and exploring a phase-down strategy has been the subject of international public health and environmental discussions.

Dental amalgam is the only mercury-added product specifically to be subject to a phase-down (Convention Article 4, Paragraph 3, and Part II of Annex A), which states that each party shall take measures as per the convention provisions by setting national objectives aiming at promoting the use of cost-effective and clinically effective mercury-free alternatives for dental restorations, research promotion, and development of mercury-free materials for dental restorations, encourage dental institution/professionals on the use of mercury-free dental restorations alternatives and best management prevention, restrict the use of dental amalgam to its encapsulated form, promoting the use of best environmental practices in dental facilities to reduce release of mercury and its compounds to water and land. Middle- and low-income countries that increasingly face challenges in dental treatment capacity and availability of resources, a complete ban on mercury amalgam or switch to more costly composite materials may not be currently feasible that can catalyze the shift toward preventive and minimal intervention. The efforts to phase down the use of amalgam should be directed toward a multipronged approach that combines waste management, knowledge management, and health systems strengthening.

India does not have primary mercury resources. Mercury and mercury compounds are imported from various countries for allowable uses. State pollution control boards through consent mechanisms give permissions for storage of mercury or its compounds. The intricacies of the mercury trade are very difficult to understand, the articles which are not produced in India are imported from developed countries but later re-exported. Goods and products made in India are also imported and re-exported.

Despite mercury’s toxicity and related hazards, the import policy (Year 1997–2002 and 2015–2020) has licensed mercury as a free product for imports. The wastes of mercury and its compounds are included in the waste streams of Basel Convention. Mercury compounds are also included as hazardous and toxic chemicals in the manufacture, storage, and import of Hazardous Chemicals Rules-1989.

Although phase-out in use of dental amalgam is not required by the convention, two-thirds of countries in the European region and region of the Americas have already phased out the use of dental amalgam and few are expected to phase it out by 2025.

The road toward phasing down of dental amalgam in dentistry is challenging in India and a national oral health policy has been drafted to strengthen oral healthcare delivery system.
