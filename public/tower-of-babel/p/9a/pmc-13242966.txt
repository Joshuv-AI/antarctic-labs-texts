Global trends and geographic variations of hypertension in childhood and adolescence 1990–2021: a systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Yu Hyeon Chu, Jueon Kim, Sarah Soyeon Oh, Jong Hoon Han, Min Seo Kim, Dong Keon Yon, Jae Il Shin, Joseph T Flynn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood hypertension is strongly associated with the development of cardiovascular diseases in adulthood. Understanding the epidemiological patterns of disease burden is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021, including 204 countries and territories. The analysis estimated deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and years of life lost (YLLs) attributable to high systolic blood pressure (sBP) among individuals under the age of 20 within the GBD comparative risk assessment (CRA) framework, covering the period from 1990 to 2021. Trends and regional disparities in disease burden were examined, emphasizing correlations between DALY rates and socio-demographic indices (SDI).ResultsIn 2021, the global DALY rate was 2.39 (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 1.75 to 2.88) per 100,000 population, representing a 20.11% decrease (95% UI, −35.02 to 10.21) compared to 1990. The YLL rate showed a decrease of 21.06% (95% UI, −36.21 to 9.95), contributing to the decline in the DALY rate, whereas the YLD rate showed an increase of 22.33% (95% UI, 4.16 to 40.74). The DALY rates were negatively correlated with Socio-demographic Index. Among the 21 GBD regions in 2021, Middle East and North Africa (MENA) showed the highest disease burden across all metrics. Western Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) demonstrated a pronounced gender disparity. High-income North America experienced the largest increase in disease burden.ConclusionsGlobal DALYs and deaths attributable to high sBP among children and adolescents suggested a declining trend from 1990 to 2021, although the 95% UIs were compatible with no clear change. DALYs showed a negative correlation with SDI, with highest burden in MENA and notable gender disparities in Western SSA. High-income North America showed the largest increase since 1990.Keywords: Childhood hypertension, High systolic blood pressure, Children and adolescents, Global Burden of Disease, Disability-adjusted life years, GBD 2021

BACKGROUND

Hypertension is a leading contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality globally, with an estimated 30% prevalence among the world's adult population in 2015, representing a substantial public health burden that leads to approximately 10 million premature deaths and exceeds 200 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per year [1]. In contrast to adult hypertension, which has clear diagnostic criteria based on absolute values, childhood hypertension is defined statistically by a diagnostic framework that requires systolic or diastolic blood pressure (BP) readings above the 95th percentile for age, sex, and height on three or more occasions [2].

Hypertension in children demonstrates a strong association with markers of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in adulthood, and longitudinal prospective cohort studies have established significant correlations between elevated BP in youth and increased carotid intima-media thickness, premature atherosclerosis, and left ventricular hypertrophy in adulthood [2,3]. Hypertensive target organ damage, including left ventricular hypertrophy, microalbuminuria, and cognitive impairment, has been observed in hypertensive children, confirming that this condition represents an active health concern [4,5].

Finally, it also appears that childhood BP levels can be linked to increased risk of “hard” cardiovascular events in adulthood, including stroke, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death [6,7].

Given the extensive immediate and future health implications of childhood hypertension, early detection and management are essential for reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality throughout the life course. Furthermore, existing global epidemiological studies [8,9] have largely focused on prevalence estimation and have not comprehensively evaluated the disease burden associated with integrated analyses of sociodemographic factors, geographic patterns, and temporal trajectories.

Therefore, this study aims to perform a comprehensive analysis of the high sBP-attributable burden in individuals under 20 from 1990 to 2021 using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 and to establish an evidence base for the development of global prevention and intervention strategies.

This manuscript was produced as part of the GBD Collaborator Network and in accordance with the GBD Protocol.

METHODS

Study methods

Numbers and rates of DALYs and deaths attributable to high systolic blood pressure (sBP) in individuals under the age of 20 were gathered from the GBD 2021 available on the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) website, managed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME; http://ghdx.healthdata.org). This study followed the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement. The disease burden associated with high sBP was estimated through the GBD risk factor estimation process, based on a CRA conceptual framework which links risks and health outcomes through risk-outcome relationships. Detailed information on the methodology including the overall analytical framework for risk factor estimation (Supplementary Fig. 1) is available in Supplementary Data 1 and previous studies [10,11].

Within the GBD CRA, high sBP is modeled as a risk factor rather than a disease entity. Accordingly, the burden estimates presented in this study represent deaths and DALYs attributable to high sBP through downstream cardiovascular and renal outcomes—including ischemic heart disease, stroke, atrial fibrillation, aortic aneurysm, peripheral arterial disease, hypertensive heart disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD)—rather than morbidity or mortality caused directly by hypertension. Additionally, the GBD framework does not model individual-level latency between childhood exposure to elevated sBP and subsequent adult cardiovascular events. Attributable burden is estimated by applying age-specific relative risks (RRs) and population-attributable fractions to the concurrent burden of each downstream outcome within the same age stratum. Thus, the estimates reported here reflect attributable burden observed within the under-20 age group at the same point in time and do not capture the future cardiovascular burden that may arise in adulthood from elevated BP during childhood.

Study design

Risk-outcome relationships were included in the analysis if the association was biologically plausible and established through multiple epidemiological studies conducted in diverse populations. For high sBP, relationships with 7 disease outcomes were included in the analysis: ischemic heart disease, stroke (ischemic, cerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage), atrial fibrillation, aortic aneurysm, peripheral arterial disease, hypertensive heart disease, and CKD.

Literature reviews of randomized clinical trials and cohort studies were conducted for each outcome to gather input for the risk-outcome relationship model. Possible biases were evaluated following the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations criteria and were represented as bias covariates in the model. The search strings used in the literature review and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 flow diagrams are reported in the GBD capstone paper [10].

For high sBP, RRs were modeled using the meta-regression Bayesian, regularized, trimmed, a meta-regression tool developed by the IHME. The bias covariates were added to adjust for systematic biases associated with study attributes and random effects across studies were included to address between-study heterogeneity and data sparsity. The implementation of effect size estimation is detailed in a previous study [12] and is available online as open-source Python libraries [13,14]. The RRs were assumed to be constant for locations and sex, while a second model was built to estimate RRs for each age group, based on data from the Asia Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration and the Prospective Studies Collaboration [15]. The uncertainty of RR originated from the uncertainty of the fixed effect and between-study heterogeneity.

Exposure estimation

Data for exposure to high sBP were collected from the literature and household surveys with microdata and reports. No updated literature review of risk factor exposure was done for high sBP; however, nine new data sources were added to the GBD 2019 list from the repository, resulting in a total of 1,116 sources from 166 countries.

Population-based studies that directly measured sBP using a sphygmomanometer were included in the analysis. Studies were excluded if the geographic area or population was specifically selected due to its connection to hypertension or hypertensive outcomes. Additionally, studies lacking methodological details for evaluation were excluded. Certain data points were treated as potential outliers if their values were deemed excessively low or high, based on expert judgment and comparisons with data from other countries. Individual-level data were obtained from survey microdata whenever possible and if such data were unavailable, information was gathered from published literature or survey reports.

Exposure for each age group, sex, location, and year was estimated using a spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR) model, a mixed effects model that borrows strength across age, time, and locations assuming the trends follow a Gaussian process. Covariates for the model were chosen by searching for variables with a potential causal relationship with sBP within high-quality prospective cohort studies. An ensemble of linear models with possible combinations of the covariates was constructed with weights given based on the predictive validity. The standard deviation of sBP was estimated by a linear regression incorporating the mean sBP estimates and fixed effects on age group and sex, with standard deviation values obtained from person-level microdata and tabulated data used as inputs. The ST-GPR analysis was performed using the PyMC2 package in Python.

The theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL) of high sBP was defined as a uniform distribution between 105 and 115 mmHg based on cohort and randomized controlled trial studies. Combined with the estimates for RRs and exposure, TMREL was used to calculate population attributable fractions (PAFs) of high sBP for each outcome. The uncertainty of the PAF estimate was determined by performing 500 draws from the previously calculated distribution of RR, exposure, and TMREL. These PAFs were then applied to DALYs and deaths from each outcome to estimate the burden attributable to high sBP, with uncertainty quantified through 500 draws that accounted for the variability in PAF and the burden of each outcome DALYs. DALY is a measure defined as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) due to disease and years lived with disability (YLDs). The estimation process for DALYs and deaths from each health outcome is described in other publications [16,17].

Socio-demographic Index (SDI)

For the GBD 2021, the SDI was calculated for 1,075 national and subnational locations spanning the years 1950 to 2021. The SDI is determined as the geometric mean of indices for 3 key parameters: the total fertility rate for individuals under 25 years of age (TFU25), the mean years of education for individuals aged 15 and older (EDU15+), and the lag-distributed income (LDI) per capita.

Patient and public involvement

This study is part of the GBD 2021, an international collaboration involving over 7,500 researchers across approximately 150 countries. The analysis was based entirely on secondary data, and no patients were involved in the study design or conduct. Because the research objectives did not pertain directly to clinical care or patient experiences with pediatric hypertension, patients and the public were not involved in formulating research questions, collecting or analyzing data, interpreting findings, or drafting the manuscript.

RESULTS

Global burden attributable to high sBP in children and adolescents

In 2021, the global high sBP-attributable DALY rate was 2.39 (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 1.75 to 2.88) per 100,000 population, of which the YLL rate was 2.31 per 100,000 (95% UI, 1.67 to 2.8), accounting for 96.7% of the total DALY rate (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Consequently, the DALY rate and YLL rate showed almost similar trends (Fig. 1). Although YLD increased by 22.33% (95% UI, 4.16 to 40.74) in 2021 compared to 1990, the DALY rate showed a lower point estimate in 2021, declining by 20.11% (95% UI, −35.02 to 10.21), driven by a decrease in YLL rate (−21.06%; 95% UI, −36.21 to 9.95) (Table 1, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Between 1990 and 2021, DALY counts showed a point estimate decrease of 6.76% (95% UI, −24.16 to 28.62) from 67,465.55 (95% UI, 42,399.31 to 85,938.84) in 1990 to 62,904.92 (95% UI, 46,216.50 to 75,864.14) in 2021 (Table 1). Globally, the total number of deaths attributable to high sBP in individuals under the age of 20 showed a point estimate decrease of 7.86% (95% UI, −25.54 to 28.34) to 837.97 cases (95% UI, 605.93 to 1,016.62) compared to 909.46 cases (95% UI, 569.65 to 1,165.62) in 1990 (Supplementary Table 3).

Burden attributable to high sBP according to SDI

There was a significant negative correlation between high sBP-attributable DALY and YLL rates and SDI levels (Pearson r = −0.6690, −0.6348, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, YLD rate showed no significant correlation with SDI (Fig. 2). DALY and YLL rates decreased across all SDI regions from 1990 to 2021, with sharp declines observed in high-middle SDI and middle SDI regions (Fig. 2). The high-middle SDI region showed the largest decrease, with DALY rates decreasing by 56.58% (95% UI, −67.11 to −32.04) and YLL rates by 58.67% (95% UI, −68.98 to −33.89) in 2021 compared to 1990 (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Conversely, YLD rates increased across all SDI regions, with the high SDI region showing the greatest increase at 44.8% (95% UI, 13.35 to 75.32) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2).

Burden attributable to high sBP according to geographic distribution

In 2021, among the 21 GBD regions, North Africa and Middle East had the highest DALY rate (6.37 per 100,000; 95% UI, 4.65 to 8.22), mortality (0.08 per 100,000; 95% UI, 0.06 to 0.11), DALY counts (15,055.77; 95% UI, 10,991.52 to 19,443.61), and death counts (200.12; 95% UI, 141.4 to 259.98) (Table 1, Supplementary Table 3). Among countries, Egypt showed the highest DALY rate (13.64 per 100,000; 95% UI, 8.32 to 20.5), mortality (0.18 per 100,000; 95% UI, 0.11 to 0.28), DALY counts (6,348.54; 95% UI, 3,871.49 to 9,541.64), and death counts (85.76; 95% UI, 51.18 to 129.94) (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Between 1990 and 2021, High-income North America had the largest relative increase in DALY rate, with the greatest increases in both YLL rate (83.14%; 95% UI, 66.25 to 99.59) and YLD rate (68.43%; 95% UI, 20.43 to 113.3) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Among countries, Lesotho showed the largest increases in DALY rate and mortality, with increases of 184.57% (95% UI, 49.4 to 475) and 188.65% (95% UI, 49.62 to 493.52), respectively (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Burden attributable to high sBP according to sex

In 2021, the global DALY rate for males was 1.75 per 100,000 (95% UI, 1.30 to 2.07) and for females was 3.06 per 100,000 (95% UI, 1.98 to 3.89) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 6). The global YLL rate in 2021 was higher in females (2.98 per 100,000; 95% UI, 1.93 to 3.84) than in males (1.67 per 100,000; 95% UI, 1.20 to 1.98), while YLD rates were identical for both females and males at 0.08 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.04 to 0.14) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Between 1990 and 2021, DALY counts for males decreased to 23,822.95 (95% UI, 17,645.29 to 28,178.38), whereas for females, they increased to 39,081.97 (95% UI, 25,350.67 to 49,746.94) (Supplementary Table 6). The DALY counts for females in the Western Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) were 12,252.5 (95% UI, 6,051.45 to 17,677.96), representing the highest burden among both females and males across all 21 GBD regions (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 6). In the Western SSA, the YLL rate for males was 1.07 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.53 to 1.47) while for females it was 9.05 per 100,000 (95% UI, 4.44 to 13.08), showing the largest gender disparity (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 8). However, for YLD rate, males had slightly higher values at 0.09 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.04 to 0.18) compared to 0.07 per 100,000 (95% UI, 0.03 to 0.13) for females (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 7).

DISCUSSION

This assessment of the global burden attributable to high sBP in children and adolescents, estimated within the GBD CRA framework, suggested a decrease in the global DALY rate over the past 32 years. Despite the 22.33% increase in YLD rate from 1990 to 2021, the 21.06% decrease in YLL rate led to a reduction in the global DALY rate, as YLL rate accounted for approximately 97% of the global high sBP-attributable DALY rate in children and adolescents. To our knowledge, this is the first GBD 2021 analysis specifically focusing on high sBP-attributable burden in individuals under the age of 20, covering the period from 1990 to 2021. These estimates reflect the contemporaneous attributable burden within the under-20 age group and do not capture the future cardiovascular burden that may arise in adulthood from BP elevation during childhood. Previous studies on childhood hypertension have primarily focused on prevalence, with relatively limited research evaluating DALYs attributable to hypertension [8]. While research published in 2017 encompassed all age groups, it provided limited analysis specific to children [1]. The latest study has concentrated on adolescents and young adults under 39 years of age, thus inadequately representing the characteristics of the population under 20 years old [18]. Childhood represents a distinct physiological developmental period with CVD manifestations that differ significantly from those in adulthood [19]. Furthermore, hypertension originating in childhood has associations with both early onset and increased severity of CVD in adulthood [6,7]. In this context, the findings of our research can serve as foundational data for future policy development related to high sBP in childhood, and may contribute to informing early interventions aimed at reducing the future burden of CVD in adulthood.

According to previous studies, the global prevalence of childhood hypertension shows an increasing trend [8]. Despite this increasing prevalence trend, the decreasing trend in DALY rate can be explained by the following factors. First, there has been an epidemiological shift with the childhood obesity epidemic contributing to an increased prevalence of primary hypertension worldwide [20]. In childhood, the possibility of death caused by primary hypertension is lower than secondary hypertension due to the absence of serious underlying diseases [21,22]. Second, advances in medical care and improved access to medical services have reduced childhood mortality rates and extended the period of survival with disease [23,24,25]. Thus, the decrease in DALY rate occurred with a pattern of large decrease in YLL rate along with less pronounced incremental YLD rate.

A negative correlation was observed between SDI level and high sBP-attributable DALY, YLL rates. Previous studies have shown that lower income and education levels are associated with higher prevalence rates attributable to high sBP [26,27]. Conditions such as CKD, one of the main causes of childhood secondary hypertension, show mortality rates 30 to 1,000 times higher than their healthy counterparts if left untreated [28,29]. Thus, early detection and treatment is essential to reduce the disease burden. However, in regions of low SDI quintile, limited access to medical services has significantly impeded early detection and timely therapeutic intervention [24]. Consequently, higher prevalence combined with limited medical access may lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, resulting in higher DALY rate.

Among the 21 GBD regions, Middle East and North Africa (MENA) showed the highest burden attributable to high sBP, followed by Western SSA. These 2 regions showed different patterns of disease burden. MENA showed much higher YLL and YLD rates than the global average for both sexes, with no significant gender disparities in each indicator. In contrast, in Western SSA, only girls’ YLL rate was much higher than the global average, showing an 8-fold difference compared with boys. Additionally, YLD rates did not show significant differences between sexes and did not significantly differ from the global average. These differences suggest that the disease burden in the two regions may have originated from different epidemiological backgrounds.

In MENA, the high sBP-attributable disease burden may be attributed to regional characteristics. MENA, which has one of the highest childhood obesity rates in the world, had the largest increase in childhood diabetes incidence between 1990 and 2021 [30,31]. Additionally, this region has a cultural background where consanguineous marriages frequently occur, resulting in high prevalence of hereditary kidney diseases such as autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease [32,33]. Because of these and potentially other factors, disease burden from both secondary and primary hypertension in childhood is likely to be high. Previous studies demonstrated that high temperatures were significantly associated with the number of deaths and DALYs from CVD [34,35]. According to a GBD study published in 2024, MENA had the highest mortality rate from childhood CVD due to high temperatures [36]. Such extreme heat environments may lead to complications from childhood hypertension or worsening of underlying conditions, resulting in high DALYs.

In Western SSA, early deaths among girls contributed to the female excess in YLL rate and the overall high burden in this region. Similar patterns were observed in Eastern and Central SSA. Although previous studies suggest that the prevalence of childhood hypertension in SSA does not show significant differences between sexes [37,38,39], such differences in YLL rate may reflect, in part, the high burden of pregnancy-related complications among adolescent girls in this region. Although maternal hypertensive disorders are not included among the seven outcome categories in the present GBD analysis, SSA has the lowest medical accessibility and the highest adolescent pregnancy rates (estimated at about 19.3%), which are more than double the global average [24,40,41,42,43]. According to the GBD study published in 2015, maternal hypertensive disorders ranked as the 4th leading cause of death for girls aged 15–19 in Western SSA and 5th in Central SSA [23]. This is significantly higher ranking compared to the other 21 GBD regions. In addition, maternal hypertensive disorders rank first or second among causes of maternal death in children aged 10–19 in SSA regions [23]. Cultural and social characteristics of these regions further limit girls' access to medical services compared to boys [44], and the inequality in sexual and reproductive health for girls is thought to have exacerbated the gender disparity in disease burden [45].

This study has several limitations. First, due to the study’s design, we were unable to differentiate the burden of primary and secondary hypertension despite their different etiology and prognosis. Second, this study relied on DALY and mortality metrics to present disease burden without providing prevalence data. This is because the objective of the study was to investigate the burden attributable to high sBP via downstream outcomes, rather than simply analyzing prevalence that overlooks sequelae and resulting health effects. Third, the GBD dataset itself presents inherent limitations. Missing data in some regions, accessibility barriers, and variable data quality were the main limitations. In regions with such data constraints, we had to rely on predictive models using covariates rather than direct statistical calculations. Finally, seven diseases were selected and analyzed to evaluate the burden attributable to high sBP in childhood; however, these outcomes are predominantly adult-onset conditions, and the GBD framework does not directly account for the temporal latency between elevated BP in childhood and the subsequent development of cardiovascular events in adulthood. Consequently, the estimates reported here reflect attributable burden observed within the under-20 age group at the same point in time and do not capture the future cardiovascular burden that may arise later in adulthood from elevated BP during childhood. Unlike adult hypertension, childhood hypertension manifests primarily as long-term vascular damage and cardiac structural changes rather than immediate cardiovascular complications, further highlighting the need for child-specific disease burden indicators.

CONCLUSIONS

The global DALY rates attributable to high sBP in children and adolescents showed lower point estimates in 2021 than in 1990, although UIs included no change. However, the high sBP-attributable burden varied by geography, and regions with lower SDI experienced greater burden. Considering the strong association between childhood hypertension and the risk of CVD development and mortality in adulthood, it is time to establish prevention strategies tailored to the changing epidemiological patterns of high sBP in childhood. Additionally, as YLLs account for most of the high sBP-attributable DALYs in childhood, efforts are needed to reduce early deaths attributable to high sBP in children and adolescents. It is urgent to develop more cost-effective preventive measures and treatment strategies to mitigate the impact of the risk factors.
