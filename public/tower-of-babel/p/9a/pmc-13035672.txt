Cardiac contractility modulation in heart failure management: unraveling molecular mechanisms to improve patient selection in heart failure management
Authors: Faro R Verelst, Lauranne Van Assche, Ward Heggermont, Sander Trenson, Wim Huybrechts, Hein Heidbuchel, Andreas B Gevaert, Emeline M Van Craenenbroeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManaging patients with heart failure remains continues to be a global health challenge despite advances in medical therapy. For patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) who are ineligible for cardiac resynchronization therapy, cardiac contractility modulation (CCM) represents a promising device-based therapeutic alternative. CCM delivers non-excitatory, high-voltage electrical impulses during the absolute refractory period of the cardiac cycle, thereby enhancing myocardial contractility without increasing myocardial oxygen demand. Clinical studies have shown that CCM can improve exercise tolerance, quality of life, and left ventricular function. However, its long-term effects on mortality and heart failure hospitalizations in the diverse patient populations ranging from end-stage HFrEF to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, remain inconclusive. This review explores the underlying molecular mechanisms of CCM contributing to reverse cardiac remodeling, including improved intracellular calcium handling, phosphorylation of key proteins related to contractility, and reversal of maladaptive gene expression. We also highlight the results of clinical trials using CCM and emphasize the need for improved patient selection criteria. Early identification of potential responders may be based on left ventricular ejection fraction and fibrosis burden. Furthermore, the expanding role of CCM in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and specific cardiomyopathies underscores its evolving therapeutic potential. Future research should focus on large-scale randomized controlled trials to establish efficacy on hard clinical endpoints and evaluate long-term benefits, alongside targeted studies aimed at refining patient selection.Keywords: Cardiac contractility modulation, Heart failure, Device therapy, Cardiomyopathy, Electrophysiology

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a prevalent condition affecting approximately 2% of adults worldwide [1]. Despite recent improvements in medical therapy, HF remains a disease with substantial morbidity and mortality and significant health care costs [2]. In patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) with persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy and prolonged QRS duration, cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) has been shown to improve symptoms and quality of life (QoL), as well as reduce hospitalizations and mortality [3]. However, around 30% of cohorts treated with CRT do not experience disease remission after implantation [4]. Also, benefits of CRT do not extend to those with normal or marginally increased QRS duration, which amount to 60% of HF patients [5]. Therefore, there is a large unmet therapeutic need for (device-based) therapies in patients with HF.

Cardiac contractility modulation (CCM) is a device therapy that induces non-excitatory high-voltage electrical impulses during the absolute refractory period. The CCM device, approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, consists of a subcutaneously implanted pulse generator, most commonly placed in the right pectoral region, connected to two bipolar leads positioned in the right ventricular septum. It is hypothesized that CCM improves myocardial calcium handling, therefore improving cardiac contractility, but the exact mechanism of action remains unproven. This review provides an overview of molecular mechanisms influenced by acute and long-term CCM therapy, as well as clinical evidence of its efficacy. We emphasize how current data aids in identifying HF patients with greater potential benefit of CCM therapy.

CCM device characteristics, implantation, and programming

The Optimizer Smart® device is developed by Impulse Dynamics and consists of a pulse generator programmer with rechargeable battery and 2 bipolar ventricular leads. The CCM device uses a rechargeable battery that requires weekly charging, which takes approximately one hour. The battery has an expected longevity of about 15 years. The two CCM leads are inserted transvenously and positioned on the right ventricular septum, most commonly at the “crux”, the junction between the septum and the anterior free wall of the right ventricle, and are secured approximately 2 cm apart. The device delivers high voltage (between 4 and 7,5 V on each ventricular lead) biphasic electrical pulses with a mean duration of 20 msec during the absolute refractory period of the cardiac cycle. Thus, preventing induction of a new action potential.

Older CCM devices consisted of three cardiac leads, including a right atrial lead, to rely on the atrial depolarization for adequate timing of the CCM impulse. (Fig. 1) The initial algorithm inhibited the delivery of CCM signals during irregular heart rhythms such as atrial fibrillation (AF) or premature ventricular contractions, to prevent electric signal delivery during repolarization, which is known to increase the risk of arrhythmia. Software refinements eliminated the need for atrial-sensed pulse-delivery, rendering an atrial lead redundant. As P-wave detection is no longer necessary in the newer CCM devices [6], AF patients no longer need to be excluded from the therapy as was done in the initial trials [7–10]. Regardless of the underlying rhythm, the maximum heart rate at which CCM therapy can be delivered is 110 beats per minute.

A safety analysis of the FIX-HF-5C2 study, in which all patients received a 2-lead CCM device, revealed only one minor device-related complication (local hematoma post-implantation) after 6 months among 60 implantations [6]. Of note, patients with the now obsolete three-lead CCM device experienced a device-related complication rate of 10.3% (confidence interval (CI), 4.2%–20.1%) after 6 months of follow-up, consistent with the approximately 9% complication rate observed in other dual-chambre cardiac devices [9, 11]. No cases of new or worsening tricuspid regurgitation were reported among the 283 patients in FIX-HF-5 and FIX-HF-5C who received an Optimizer device alongside an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD), despite having three leads crossing the tricuspid valve [7, 9]. In the FIX-HF-5C2 subgroup treated with the current 2-lead CCM system (n = 60), there were likewise no indications of additional valve issues, although this was not explicitly detailed in any of the published reports [6]. This is supported by a small echocardiographic study in 30 patients with ICDs, which found that right ventricle (RV) lead implantation for CCM therapy did not increase tricuspid regurgitation [12].

The Optimizer® device is typically programmed to deliver one hour of therapy followed by four hours of inactivity. While extended therapy durations of up to 12 h per day have been shown to be safe, they did not lead to improvements in QoL, peak oxygen uptake (peak VO₂), NYHA class, 6-minute walking distance (6MWD) in a small patient cohort in comparison to the regular 5 h of therapy [13]. To distinguishes normal heartbeats from premature ventricular ectopic beats, the CCM device analyzes the signals from both the right ventricular (RV) and left septal (LS) leads. Following a sensing window on the LS lead, a programmable LS-CCM delay (ranging from 3 to 140 msec) is started, after which a biphasic CCM impulse “train” of electric signals is delivered during the absolute refractory period. This is followed by a 40 msec balancing phase, which facilitates the dissipation of residual lead polarization. Unlike other device-based therapies such as pacemakers and ICD, the current CCM devices lack the capability to transmit recorded CCM data via home monitoring systems and do not log arrhythmic events.

Many patients with HF already carry an ICD before undergoing CCM implantation. Because an ICD can misinterpret the CCM pulse as an additional ventricular beat, crosstalk testing during CCM implantation is essential to identify any oversensing of the ICD. If ICD oversensing occurs, it can usually be corrected by reducing ICD ventricular sensitivity, extending the ventricular blanking period, or shortening the LS–CCM delay. Undersensing of the native ECG is also possible; therefore, automatic native R-wave template acquisition in the ICD should be disabled after CCM implantation to avoid CCM pulses being incorporated into morphology-based tachycardia discrimination [14]. There are no data available yet on the effect of CCM therapy on the arrhythmic burden in patients with HF. An increase in the incidence of ventricular arrhythmias, ICD shocks, or anti-tachycardia pacing in patients having both ICD and CCM devices, wasn’t seen however [8].

Clinical evidence of CCM efficacy improving cardiovascular outcomes

Four randomized controlled trials (RCT) showed that CCM modestly improves exercise capacity and QoL at 6 months in HF patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 40%, QRS duration < 130 msec and persisting HF symptoms (NYHA II-IV) despite optimal medical therapy [7–9, 15]. An individual patient data meta-analysis including 4 of these RCT confirmed that CCM significantly improved peak VO2, 6MWD and QoL (Fig. 2) [16]. The average difference in peak VO2 of CCM therapy vs. control of + 0.93 ml/kg/min observed in these trials, corresponds to a significant reduced mortality in previous HF trials [17]. Compared to conventional CRT, CCM therapy demonstrates comparable — and in some domains even superior — efficacy in improving peak VO₂, 6MWD, and QoL, with particularly pronounced benefits observed in patients with an ejection fraction between 35% and 45%. A meta-analysis of all RCTs involving CCM in 930 patients did not demonstrate a significant reduction in all-cause mortality at 12 weeks follow-up (risk ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.29–1.35; P = 0.23, I² = 0%), but with a trend in favour of CCM therapy [21]. The lack of statistical significance may be due to the relatively short follow-up duration, which might be insufficient to detect mortality benefits, considering the delayed effects of CCM on cardiac remodeling. Data on HF hospitalizations remain limited. In the FIX-HF-5 C trial, combined freedom from cardiac death and HF hospitalization improved from 89.8% in the control group to 95.5% in the treatment group (p = 0.042 at 24 weeks). Separate data on HF hospitalizations alone were not reported. The CCM-REG registry, a prospective study of 503 patients with HF, demonstrated a reduction in HF hospitalization rates from 0.74 events per patient-year (95% CI: 0.66–0.82) before enrolment to 0.25 (95% CI: 0.21–0.28) during the 2-year follow-up (p < 0.0001) [10].

As a result, while CCM therapy is acknowledged in the current ESC and AHA/ACC/HFSA heart failure guidelines for its benefits on QoL and exercise capacity, formal endorsement for its use is still pending, awaiting further data from larger randomized controlled trials [3, 22, 23]. Fig. 4 presents a proposed treatment algorithm outlining how CCM therapy integrates into contemporary HF management. The ongoing AIM HIGHer trial (NCT05064709) is the first randomized, quadruple-blind, sham-controlled study designed to formally evaluate the impact of CCM on HF hospitalizations and all-cause or cardiovascular mortality. The trial aims to enroll 1,500 patients with HF and LVEF between 40% and 60%. Co-primary endpoints include changes in six-minute walk distance and QoL at 6 months, as well as a hierarchical composite clinical efficacy outcome at 18 months comprising cardiovascular mortality, HF hospitalizations,urgent HF visits requiring intravenous diuretics and change in health status. Notably, AIM HIGHer is restricted to patients with LVEF > 40%; to date, no randomized controlled trial has definitively assessed the effect of CCM on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with LVEF < 40%.

Preclinical studies on short-term effects of CCM therapy on calcium handling and cardiac contractility

Preclinical studies are summarized in Table 1. In the failing heart, the molecular pathways responsible for calcium( Ca2+) regulation are dramatically altered. In normal physiological conditions, the contraction of the heart relies on the in- and efflux of Ca2+ across the cellular borders. During the action potential, Ca2+ ions flow into the cell through L-type Ca2+ channels, sparking a cascade that induces the release of additional Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) through ryanodine receptors (RyR2). Following myocardial contraction, the Ca2+ gradient decreases by Ca2+ efflux into the extracellular space and the SR. This process is facilitated by SR Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA2a) and the sarcolemma Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX). In heart failure, the expression of SERCA2a is reduced and hypophosphorylation of phospholamban (PLN) occurs, resulting in depressed Ca2+ loading of the SR. In addition, RyR2 are oxidized, causing leakage of Ca2+ out of the cell and contributing to decreased levels of intracellular Ca2+ [24, 25].

One of the mechanisms by which CCM improves the cardiac contractility is by augmenting the intracellular Ca2+ flow and thereby Ca2+ loading of the SR [29, 30]. Prior mechanistic studies have shown that CCM prolongs the action potential, in a dose-dependent manner, and thereby increases Ca2+ influx by activation of L-type voltage dependent Ca2+ channels, NCX mediated Ca2+ entry, and phosphorylation of PLN and SERCA2a [27, 30, 33, 39, 42]. Following a CCM impulse, enhanced Ca2+ loading of the SR increases the contractile force of the subsequent systole [30]. However, a plateau effect is observed: increasing intracellular Ca2+ above physiologic levels does not further improve cardiac contractility (Table 1; Fig. 5) [29, 39].

(B) Effect of CCM therapy on the failing Cardiomyocyte: CCM therapy improves Ca²⁺ handling by enhancing LCC-mediated Ca²⁺ entry, reversing the abnormal activation of NCX, and promoting greater sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca²⁺ loading. In addition, CCM may increase SERCA2a activity by reducing PLN-mediated inhibition, further supporting improved SR Ca²⁺ uptake and enhanced contractility with subsequent cardiac cycles

By enhancing intracellular Ca2+ flow, CCM functions as an inotropic therapy, effectively improving cardiac contractility, without compromising myocardial relaxation or increasing myocardial oxygen consumption [32, 43]. Sustained activation of β1 adrenergic receptors by traditional inotropic agents on the contrary, is known to increase oxygen consumption and may lead to further cardiac dysfunction [44, 45]. As shown in both ex vivo and in vivo experiments, the effect of CCM on cardiac contractility remains unaltered even in the presence of betablockers, suggesting that beta-adrenergic receptors are not implicated in its mechanism [29–31]. Experimental results on this matter are however contradictory: Winter et al. demonstrated an increase in norepinephrine in coronary effluent and a diminished increase in left ventricular pressure after CCM stimulation [35, 36]. The difference in these studies might be explained by differences in experimental set-up: Ex vivo intact hearts have more sympathetic nerve endings than papillary muscle samples. Epicardial stimulation may also activate more of these nerves, which first travel along the heart’s surface before reaching deeper areas, unlike endocardial stimulation of papillary muscles [44]. In healthy cardiomyocytes, the force-frequency relationship (FFR) reflects increased contractile force with rising heart rate. In myocardial slices from end-stage heart failure patients, CCM stimulation did not significantly change overall FFR. However, it did significantly attenuate the decline in maximal contractility observed with increased heart rates in the failing heart [41].

Long-term effects of CCM on cardiac gene and protein expression

In addition to the immediate impact on Ca²⁺ management, prolonged CCM therapy leads to the upregulation of genes associated with Ca²⁺ cycling and handling. Also, the HF-induced reactivation of fetal genes, which is known to induce maladaptive cardiac remodeling, is attenuated by CCM in the long term [42, 46].

In animal models of HF, including canine and rabbit models induced by coronary microembolization or aortic root ligation, the mRNA expression of fetal program genes (β1-AR, alpha-myosin heavy chain (MHC) and A- and B type natriuretic proteins (ANP, BNP)), as well as genes associated with the sarcoplasmatic reticulum (SERCA2a, PLN and RyR2) recovered to nearly normal levels after initiation of CCM therapy [31, 37]. CCM also restored the protein expression of these genes, except for PLN. These effects were not only seen at the right ventricular septum, the site of CCM signal delivery, but also on remote locations in the myocardium e.g. the left ventricle [31].

A beneficial impact on cardiac remodeling is also seen. CCM therapy notably diminishes the volume fraction of both interstitial and replacement fibrosis by suppressing collagen production and fibroblast differentiation [31, 33, 38] and it also normalizes the expression of key cytoskeletal proteins and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP1, 2 and 9) [31, 33]. This latter effect is thought to occur through the inhibition of the transforming growth factor beta 1/Smad3 signaling pathway [38].

Data regarding the impact of CCM therapy on human myocardial tissue are limited. Nonetheless, a substudy of FIX-HF-4 in 11 HF patients, effectively showed reversal of the cardiac maladaptive fetal gene program in the myocardium by CCM therapy [46]. After 3 months, the expression of ANP, BNP, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase stretch receptor (p38MAPK), p21 Ras stretch receptor gene (p21 RAS), and NCX, was significantly reduced in RV endomyocardial biopsies compared to the control group in a cross-over design study (Table 2). Additionally, CCM therapy facilitated the upregulation of genes typically suppressed in HF such as alpha-MHC, SERCA2a, PLN, and RyR2. Gene expression response did not differ between patients with ischemic or idiopathic cardiomyopathy. Moreover, the amplitude of the observed increase in SERCA2a gene expression following CCM therapy correlated with clinical endpoints such as changes in peakVO2 and QoL. Of note, protein content or protein function analysis was not determined in this study. As myocardial samples were obtained from the RV in proximity to the CCM leads, uncertainty was raised regarding the generalizability of these effects to the left ventricle.

Secondly, in a small observational study of six CCM-treated patients, myocardial biopsies taken 10 months after implantation from responders—defined by reduced end-systolic volume—showed upregulation of genes related to mitochondrial function, mitophagy, sarcomere structure, and cardiomyocyte architecture. These changes, including increased CASA-related gene expression, reflect late-stage CCM effects and differ from the early Ca²⁺-handling gene responses [55].

Cracking the code: understanding enzyme modulation and gene expression in CCM therapy

The precise mechanism by which CCM therapy initiates enzyme modulation and gene expression remains elusive. It is uncertain whether CCM directly induces gene expression through electrical impulses, or if the observed changes are merely a consequence of reduced mechanical stress. The latter hypothesis is supported by the diminished expression of genes typically influenced by mechanical stress, such as ANP, BNP and p21 RAS. [46]

However, another possibility exists: CCM signals might generate electromagnetic fields (EMF) that impact the myocardium’s mechanical structure. EMF have the capacity to stimulate biosynthesis and interact with gene expression. CCM could modify enzyme activity as an early onset effect through various mechanisms. EMF can trigger refolding of denatured enzymes which can restore their activity [59]. In addition, they could enhance enzyme orientation and substrate alignment through increased quantum tunneling of protons and electrons, facilitating faster biochemical reactions [60, 61]. Consequently, EMF generated by CCM therapy have been observed to augment the phosphorylation of cardiac enzymes [31]. Furthermore, low-frequency, low-intensity EMF have the ability to induce DNA transcription by destabilizing hydrogen bonds between DNA strands within minutes of stimulation in vitro [62].

The process through which a localized electrical signal triggers widespread ventricular remodeling across the entire heart remains a mystery. Although the immediate effects of CCM therapy are limited to enhancing local myocardial contractility, prolonged CCM stimulation results in changes that extend across the entire heart [28]. Lyon et al. put forth a hypothesis comparing CCM to SERCA2a gene therapy [44]. Just as with gene therapy, when administered at a specific myocardial site, CCM demonstrates effects that extend throughout the myocardium [63]. They propose that local reverse molecular remodeling functions as a metabolic “sink” for other compromised areas of the myocardium, effectively extracting excessive diastolic Ca2+ and reactive oxygen species from the failing myocardium [44, 63]. Consequently, this “restored” myocardium may release mediators into affected areas via gap junctions.

Elements in heart failure phenotype that identify patients that might benefit from CCM therapy

All published clinical studies using CCM have been designed to assess surrogate endpoints such as peak VO2 or QoL or have been compared to historical cohorts (Table 2). This methodological limitation affects the robustness of subsequent subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, since peak VO₂ is a strong prognostic marker in heart failure, it is worthwhile speculating on the characteristics clinicians can use in patient selection for CCM, which could inform future trials by enhancing statistical power (Fig. 6).

Left ventricular ejection fraction

A better response to CCM therapy has been observed in patients with LVEF at the higher end of the HFrEF category (above 25–35%). In a subgroup analysis of the FIX-HF-5 study, patients with an LVEF > 25% and NYHA class III symptoms who received CCM therapy (n = 109) showed a greater improvement of the ventilatory anaerobic threshold (0.64 mL/kg/min vs. −0.14 mL/kg/min, p = 0.03) and peak VO2 (1.31 mL/kg/min vs. 0.25 mL/kg/min, p = 0.003), compared to those receiving optimal medical therapy (n = 97) [7]. The FIX-HF-5 C trial demonstrated similar findings, revealing a more significant response to CCM therapy in peak VO2, MLWHFQ, and 6MWD among patients with an EF greater than 35% [9].

In the CCM-REG prospective registry, 140 patients HF and EF between 25% and 45% were followed for three years to assess the effect of CCM on all-cause mortality and HF hospitalizations [56]. (Table 2) This was compared to survival as calculated by the Seattle HF Model, a validated predictive model used in multiple RCTs. While there was a tendency towards decreased mortality in the overall patient group, this effect did not reach statistical significance. Subgroup analysis of patients with LVEF between 35% and 45% did reveal a statistically significant reduction in overall mortality with CCM (survival rate 88.0% vs. predicted 74.7%, p = 0.046). There was no observed difference in the etiology of heart failure on mortality rates. Additionally, the study reported a significant 79% reduction in HF and cardiovascular hospitalizations compared to the model after two years of follow-up (1.2 vs. 0.35 hospitalizations per patient-year, p < 0.001) in the overall cohort, with a greater effect observed in the higher EF group [56]. Besides the obvious limitations of comparing to calculated survival, we should also note that the Seattle HF model was developed in HF patients with LVEF < 30% recruited in 1992–1994.

One possible explanation for the more pronounced improvements in exercise capacity, QoL, and outcomes in patients with higher ranges of LVEF, could be that CCM is potentially insufficient to counteract the extent of fibrosis or dilatation characteristic of severe LV dysfunction. Extensive myocardial scarring is well-known to compromise the effectivity of device therapy [64]. Positioning of CRT leads on scarred myocardial segments often yields poorer clinical outcomes [65]. A prospective observational trial assessed the predictive value of scar at the lead position on the response to CCM therapy. Patients with less than 25% late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) at the RV septum at lead position demonstrated a significant improvement in NYHA class and a trend towards enhanced LVEF compared to patients with more than 25% LGE [66]. As cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) is recognized for predicting CRT response, its use before CCM implantation could be considered [66].

Secondly, in advanced heart failure, there is a notable decrease in the activity of SERCA2a [67]. It is plausible that when SERCA2a activity diminishes, the effect of CCM is attenuated. (Table 1) Consequently, in patients with moderately severe heart failure and preserved SERCA2a activity, CCM might attain its full effect [44].

Contrary to these findings, in the MAINTAINED registry patients with a lower baseline LVEF (< 30%) exhibited greater improvements in both left and right systolic function, as assessed by various parameters including LVEF, TAPSE, left ventricular end-systolic and end-diastolic diameter, after five years of follow-up. However, it is worth noting that the study included a small subset of patients with LVEF > 30% [68]. Also, in a case report CCM therapy has been successfully used as a bridge to heart transplantation in a patient with advanced heart failure who was not suitable for left ventricular assist device implantation [69].

Theoretically, CCM therapy could also be beneficial for patients with preserved LVEF. In HFpEF, the decreased phosphorylation of vital cardiac proteins such as titin, and the disruption in calcium handling are key contributors to myocardial stiffness [70, 71]. In a mechanistic study on rabbit right ventricular papillary muscles, CCM signals not only boosted peak myocardial force but also enhanced cardiac muscle relaxation, leading to a 69% improvement in invasively measured diastolic function (Table 1) [30].

In 2016, Tschöpe et al. documented the cases of two female HFpEF patients with HF symptoms and exercise intolerance (NYHA II-III) [72]. Following device implantation, both patients experienced improvements in LVEF of 10–20%, reduction in echocardiographic E/e’, improved QoL scores and increased 6MWD. Endomyocardial biopsies were conducted before, 30 min, and 3 months after CCM implantation in both patients, revealing an increase in phosphorylated titin immediately after CCM implantation, which persisted up to 3 months. Although the effect was less pronounced, CCM also triggered phosphorylation of other essential cardiac proteins such as myosin light chain 2, troponin I, and phospholamban. Additionally, alterations in collagen composition were observed, modifying matrix regulation and enhancing LV compliance. These changes were evident in both LV and RV biopsies, underscoring the biventricular effect of CCM [72].

The CCM-HFpEF trial, a prospective multicenter, single-arm, pilot study assessed the efficacy and safety of CCM therapy in a therapy-refractory HFpEF population (Table 2) [58]. Over 17 sites in Europe and Australia, a total of 47 patients were enrolled, exhibiting various comorbidities including AF (46.8%), diabetes mellitus (40.4%), and arterial hypertension (63.8%), and were followed up for 6 months. The study revealed a significant enhancement in QoL measured by the KCCQ (18.0 ± 16.6 points, p < 0.001), evident in patients with both AF and sinus rhythm. However, the treatment effect exceeded expectations, prompting consideration of a potential placebo effect affecting observed improvements. Additionally, enhancements in left atrial volume index (LAVi) (−2.8 ± 8.2 ml/m2, p = 0.034) and septal E/e′ (−0.9 ± 4.7, p = 0.038) were noted at 6 months. A high event-free rate of 93.6% (95% CI 82.5–98.7%) was demonstrated, with only three procedure-related adverse events reported: two lead dislodgements and one exacerbation of tricuspid regurgitation following device implantation. Compared to the rate of HF hospitalizations pre-implantation, a remarkable 67% reduction in HF hospitalizations was observed one year after CCM implantation [58].

The AIM-Higher trial, described in detail above, will provide further insight in the benefits of CCM in patients with HF and LVEF > 40%.

Ischemic versus non-ischemic etiology of heart failure

The response to CRT may vary depending on whether the underlying heart disease is ischemic or non-ischemic. While CRT demonstrates comparable benefits in reducing mortality and HF hospitalizations in both cohorts, there appears to be a more pronounced impact on cardiac remodeling in those with non-ischemic heart disease [73–75]. This observation raises the possibility that similar patterns could extend to CCM therapy.

The majority of patients included in current CCM studies had ischemic HF (approximately 60–70%, Table 2) [6–9]. In a subgroup analysis of the FIX-HF-5 study, the etiology of heart failure did not show significant interaction with increases in peakVO2 at 1 year follow-up [7]. Additionally, analysis of myocardial biopsies revealed that the effect of CCM on maladaptive gene expression did also not vary significantly between patients with ischemic or non-ischemic HF (Table 2) [46]. However, a meta-analysis of conducted RCTs identified a notable response in 6MWD among patients with ischemic HF (n = 232), with a mean improvement of 21.32 m (95% CI 8.13 to 34.51 m, p = 0.002), but no significant change in 6MWD observed in patients with non-ischemic HF (n = 134), with a mean difference of 4.87 m (95% CI −16.55 to 26.3 m, p = 0.66). However, the formal test for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance. QoL assessed by MLWHQ also improved in the ischemic HF cohort, but there were no significant changes observed in patients with non-ischemic HF, reaching statistical significance in subgroup analysis (χ2 = 5.11, p = 0.02) [16]. In a retrospective analysis with 3-year follow-up, the observed mortality in patients with ischemic heart failure was 35%, significantly lower than the 43% predicted by the MAGGIC Heart Failure Risk Score (p < 0.04). In contrast, among patients with non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, observed mortality was 29% compared to an expected 37%, a difference that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.19) [53]. In a recent in vitro study using live myocardial slices from seven patients with end-stage HF, the biomechanical effects of CCM therapy appeared more pronounced in those with ischemic heart disease (Table 1). This may be explained by intrinsic differences in transmural heterogeneity between ischemic and non-ischemic myocardium potentially affecting CCM signal transmission across the myocardium. However, the findings are limited by the small sample size [41].

Findings from the MAINTAINED study indicate that patients with non-ischemic HF experienced a larger improvement in LVEF and TAPSE at 5-year follow-up compared to those with ischemic HF [53]. Also, the increase in GLS with CCM therapy seems to be larger in patients with non-ischemic HF [57].

Genetic dilated cardiomyopathies

Data regarding the utilization of CCM therapy in genetic cardiomyopathies remains limited, predominantly sourced from case reports. Hesselson et al. documented the case of a 52-year-old man diagnosed with a genetic cardiomyopathy caused by a heterozygous titin (TTN) variation (c.95264G. A (p.Trp31755)). Following CCM implantation, LVEF increased from 25% to 55% at three months. Similarly, in two other patients with heterozygous TTN variants, complete normalization of LVEF was observed after CCM implantation [76]. This observed reverse remodeling is hypothesized to be linked to enhanced TTN phosphorylation facilitated by CCM therapy [33, 77].

In addition, a 48-year-old patient with LMNA-associated dilated cardiomyopathy, CCM implantation led to an increase in LVEF and improved QoL [78]. A registry, CARDILAM CCM registry (NCT 04904393), has been established to further explore effects of CCM in patients with laminopathies. Thirdly, the use of CCM therapy in wild type ATTR cardiomyopathy led to near-normalization of systolic and diastolic function in a patient [79]. The AMY-CCM Registry (NCT05167799) will further evaluate the efficacy of CCM therapy in amyloid heart disease. Finally, in a patient with PLN p.Arg14del-related cardiomyopathy who was intolerant to medical therapy, CCM showed improvement in both LVEF and heart failure symptoms [80]. Interestingly, CCM has shown to diminish the MAPK pathway activation, involved in adverse cardiac remodeling and cell death of cardiomyocytes, likely contributing to the positive response observed in patients with LMNA and amyloid cardiomyopathy [46, 81].

The average of improvement of LVEF with CCM therapy is approximately 5% [49]. However, in these cases, LVEF nearly fully recovered following CCM therapy. These preliminary results raise the question whether a similar “super” response could be anticipated in other genetic cardiomyopathies. To date, no other cases are described in literature.

Female patients

Historically, women have been underrepresented in device studies, including CRT trials, and undergo CRT implantation at one-third the rate of men. Despite this inequality, women demonstrate a more prominent increase in LVEF and have lower mortality rates after CRT implantation compared to men [82, 83]. Presently, this sex gap persists as women are significantly underrepresented in current CCM trials, comprising only about 30% of all patients included in RCTs (Table 2) [7–9].

A meta-analyses of current RCTs revealed no differences in improvement of VO2peak (overall effect Z = 3.52, P = 0.0004; test for subgroup differences: χ2 = 0.00, P = 0.98) or QoL (overall effect: Z = 4.93, P < 0.00001; test for subgroup differences: χ2 = 0.29, P = 0.59) related to sex [16]. However, there was no difference seen in 6MWD in women (mean difference 1.05, 95% CI −21.22 to 23.22 m, p = 0.57), compared to men (mean difference 21.12, 95% CI 7.39 to 34.84 m, p = 0.003) after CCM implantation, however, there was no statistically significant interaction by sex [16]. Currently, all trials are underpowered to assess sex-specific evaluation of efficacy and safety of CCM therapy. There is a pressing need to enhance the enrollment of women in HF trials to achieve greater balance and ensure thorough assessment of treatment outcomes in female patients with HF.

Atrial fibrillation

Approximately half of patients with HF also suffer from AF, a comorbidity that can worsen both prognosis and symptoms in this population [84]. The FIX-HF-5C2 trial, the sole clinical trial that studied the two-lead CCM system, included 9 patients (15%) with permanent AF. The number of total CCM signal delivery did not significantly differ from patients in sinus rhythm making CCM therapy a safe and effective device option in HFrEF patients with permanent AF [6]. However, clinical efficacy of CCM in patients with AF remains to be studied.

Background medical therapy

In all current randomized controlled trials enrolled patients were receiving guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), consisting of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin-receptor blocker, aldosterone inhibitor and beta-blocker. In general, patient in all published trials were well-medicated [6–9]. FIX-HF-5 C and FIX-HF-5C2 were the only trials in which patients received angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (respectively 4 and 15% of patients) [6, 9]. However, many trials took place before the widespread use of modern heart failure pharmacotherapies such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors. As a result, the implications of CCM within this evolving treatment landscape remain somewhat unclear.

Clinical response to HF therapy might offer some clues in predicting who could benefit from CCM therapy. In a small observational pilot trial, levosimendan was utilized to assess CCM response in patients prior to device implantation [85]. The mechanism of action of levosimendan, acting as myofilament calcium sensitizer, is similar to the calcium handling improvement reported by CCM and described in detail above [39, 86]. Levosimendan infusions were administered 72 h before CCM implantation to 15 patients. Patients experiencing a > 15% reduction in left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) two days after levosimendan infusion, also exhibited a significant decrease in LVEDV after six months of CCM therapy (138.3 ± 18.8 mL vs. 119.7 ± 8.1 mL, p < 0.05), in contrast to patients not responsive to levosimendan (137.2 ± 7.7 mL vs. 129.5 ± 9.8 mL, p = 0.48) [85].

Summary identifying the optimal candidate for CCM therapy

The ideal candidate for CCM therapy appears to be a patient with a moderately reduced LVEF between 25% and 45%, persistent HF symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, and limited myocardial septal scarring. This subgroup has demonstrated the most consistent improvements in exercise capacity and QoL. In HFpEF, CCM therapy appears promising, but its efficacy remains to be confirmed by ongoing RCTs. While the impact of HF etiology on CCM response remains uncertain, some studies suggest a more pronounced benefit in patients with ischemic HF in terms of QoL and 6MWD, although findings are inconsistent. Data on non-ischemic genetic cardiomyopathies are limited to case reports, which describe so-called “superresponses” with normalization of LVEF, particularly in variants involving TTN, PLN, and LMNA. Evidence in female patients and those with AF is also limited, largely due to underrepresentation in clinical trials. Patients in CCM trials were generally well-treated, but modern therapies (e.g. SGLT2 inhibitors) were underused. Levosimendan response may help predict CCM benefit. (Summary Figure)

Knowledge gaps and future directions

CCM has proven to be effective in improving functional and exercise capacity in patients with HF. The effect on hard clinical endpoints like cardiovascular mortality or HF hospitalizations remains to be determined in large-scale RCTs [21]. Additionally, more evidence is required regarding the use of CCM in combination with other device therapies. The ongoing INTEGRA-D clinical trial (NCT05855135) aims to provide data on the performance of the CCM-D device, which combines CCM and ICD therapy in a single device (Optimizer Integra CCM-D System). Importantly, improving our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms of CCM may help refine patient selection. Identifying the biological substrates that mediate response to CCM may help pinpoint which patients are most likely to benefit, thereby optimizing therapeutic outcomes and avoiding unnecessary interventions.

Conclusion

CCM is a device therapy for patients with heart failure. CCM works by delivering a high-voltage electrical pulse during the heart’s absolute refractory period, thereby enhancing cardiac contractility. Underlying mechanisms of action include improved Ca2+ handling, reversed fetal gene expression, and possibly direct effects of EMF on myocardial proteins, but these need to be explored further. CCM improves peak VO2 and QoL in patients with LVEF up to 45%, with a more pronounced effect in patients at the higher end of this LVEF range. Small studies in HFpEF and specific cardiomyopathies, including genetic cardiomyopathies, offer an insight into other areas where CCM may be considered. A formal evaluation of the effect of CCM on clinical cardiovascular outcomes is ongoing and will recruit patients with LVEF 40–60%. Through better understanding of molecular changes induced by CCM, we may improve selection of patients with the largest potential benefit. Addressing underrepresentation of specific subgroups in clinical trials, such as women, patients with AF, and patients with non-ischemic HF will be crucial for maximizing the therapeutic potential of CCM in heart failure management.
