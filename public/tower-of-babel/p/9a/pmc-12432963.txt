Colorectal cancer prognosis: insights from the tumor immune microenvironment and gut microbiota
Authors: Quan Chen, Youtao Zhou, Zikai Lin, Cuiyan Yang, Hui Chen, Chuanfeng Ke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide. Despite recent advancements in screening strategies and treatments, the prognosis of CRC patients remains poor. Emerging evidences suggest that tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and gut microbiota play a pivotal role in CRC progression and treatment. However, the clinical utility of these findings remains limited due to the absence of robust biomarkers. We sought to establish a clinically actionable tool that could guide personalized treatment decisions and ultimately improve outcomes for CRC patients.MethodsWe analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and identified the prognostic genes for CRC by integrating the TIME- and gut microbiota-related genes from GeneCards. Using Mendelian randomization (MR), we examined the causal relationships between the prognostic genes, gut microbiota, and CRC. We then developed a risk model and independently validated its predictive performance using The Cancer Genome Atlas-Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COADREAD) dataset as an external validation cohort.ResultsWe established a risk model comprising the six identified genes and found that the high-risk group had a significantly higher mortality rate than the low-risk group. Additionally, the high-risk group showed increased immune cell infiltration and a diminished response to immunotherapy. The two-step MR analysis revealed that Deltaproteobacteria and Methanobrevibacter mediated the causal relationship between the prognostic genes and CRC. Further, the risk score was shown to be an independent prognostic factor for CRC survival, and the newly established nomogram demonstrated strong concordance between the predicted and observed clinical outcomes.ConclusionsWe developed a six-gene risk model and showed that gut microbes mediate the causal link between the prognostic genes and CRC. Further research on the regulation of the TIME and gut microbiota in CRC may provide valuable insights.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our novel six-gene risk model was able to stratify colorectal cancer (CRC) patients into high- and low-risk groups, and the high-risk group had significantly worse overall survival than the low-risk group (P=0.003).

• Despite elevated immune infiltration, the high-risk patients show a diminished immunotherapy response, indicative of an immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).

• Mendelian randomization implicated Deltaproteobacteria and Methanobrevibacter as microbial mediators of genetic risk and the pathogenesis of CRC.

• The risk score is an independent prognostic factor (multivariate P<0.001), and the nomogram showed excellent calibration (concordance index =0.708).

What is known, and what is new?

• Despite research advances, CRC prognosis remains suboptimal; thus, better risk stratification tools need to be developed.

• Our model not only predicted patient survival but also revealed a microbiota-mediated effect and TIME dysregulation, providing actionable therapeutic targets.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The gut microbiota-TIME axis should be incorporated into CRC therapeutic decision making.

• Prospective trials need to be conducted to validate microbiota modulation strategies and combined immunotherapy approaches for high-risk patients.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide (1). Three major types of genetic aberrations—chromosomal instability, microsatellite instability (MSI), and cytosine-phosphate-guanine island methylator phenotype—are involved in the pathogenesis of CRC (2). Despite recent improvements in screening strategies and the development of more effective treatments (3,4), the prognosis of advanced CRC patients remains poor (5), highlighting the need for specific, sensitive, and non-invasive biomarkers for early detection.

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a critical role in tumor progression and the response to immunotherapy (6). A study has emphasized its effect on cancer biology and treatment resistance (7). Mechanistically, the TIME regulates CRC through complex and dynamic pathways (8,9).

There is increasing evidence that dysbiosis of the gut microbes can promote neoplasia (10,11). For example, the increased secretion of bacterial toxins and carcinogenic metabolites, combined with the decreased production of beneficial metabolites, may compromise intestinal barrier function, leading to immune dysregulation and cellular proliferation (12). Direct interactions between gut microbes and intestinal immune cells (e.g., activation of mucosal macrophages via M cells) further facilitate CRC development (13). Additionally, gut microbes modulate the efficacy of CRC treatments, primarily through immune-related mechanisms (14,15).

Despite growing recognition of the complex interactions between genes, the TIME, and gut microbiota in CRC, their collective prognostic significance remains poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we developed a comprehensive predictive model through bioinformatics analysis to evaluate the combined effect of the TIME and gut microbiota on CRC patient outcomes. Methods

Data collection

Gene expression profiles were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi) database. The GSE44076 dataset included 98 paired colon tumor and normal tissue samples. After excluding cases with incomplete survival data [not available (N/A) or zero survival times] from GSE39582, 572 CRC samples were retained for analysis. The Cancer Genome Atlas-Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COADREAD) cohort (n=434) was obtained from the University of California Santa Cruz Xena browser as an independent validation set.

Associated genes and enrichment analysis

A gene expression analysis of the GSE44076 dataset was conducted to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the tumor and normal samples based on the following criteria: |log fold change| >1 and P value <0.05. To explore the interplay among the TIME, intestinal bacteria, and CRC, 5,820 TIME-related genes (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jgo-2025-517-1.xlsx) and 176 gut microbiota-related genes (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jgo-2025-517-2.xlsx) were downloaded from GeneCards. The overlapping genes were extracted from these three gene sets for further analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) (16) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (17) enrichment analyses were performed to identify the enriched molecular mechanisms and cellular functions associated with the overlapping genes.

Construction of the prognostic risk gene model using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression

Based on the GSE39582 dataset, a LASSO Cox regression analysis was conducted to construct an optimal prognostic gene signature (18). The Cox regression model with the LASSO penalty performed the variable selection and regularization simultaneously. The optimal value of the penalty parameter (λ) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. Validation of the prognostic value of the risk model

Based on the median cut-off value, the patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups. A survival analysis was performed, and Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to evaluate whether the risk model could predict the overall survival (OS) of the CRC patients. Additionally, the χ2 test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences in six prognostic genes expression level between the two groups. The results were visualized using heatmaps and box plots. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the model’s predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in the CRC patients.

Estimation of immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoints, and the immunotherapy response

Using the GSE39582 dataset, the Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) score, immune score, stromal score, and tumor purity were calculated for each sample using R (version 4.3.0). The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm (19) was employed to quantify the relative abundance of 28 immune cell types [source: Tumor-Immune System Interaction Database (TISIDB)] (20), and to evaluate their association with the risk groups and the risk genes. The expression levels of the immune checkpoint genes, including BTLA, CD274 (PD-L1), CTLA4, IL7R, LAG3, and TIGIT, were compared between the high- and low-risk groups. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score was generated to predict the immunotherapy response in CRC patients (21).

Gnome-wide association study (GWAS) data sources, instrumental variable selection, and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

The GWAS summary statistics for CRC were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS project (ebi-a-GCST012879) and served as the outcome dataset. It had a sample size of 32,072. To investigate the causal effect of risk genes on CRC, we performed a summary-based MR (SMR) analysis using the SMR software (version 1.3.2) (22). The exposure data comprised the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) for the risk genes derived from whole blood, transverse colon, and sigmoid colon tissues in the Genotype-Tissue Expression project (23). The results are reported as the odds ratio (OR) for CRC per standard deviation increase in gene expression. The heterogeneity in dependent instruments test was applied by default in the SMR.

Subsequently, we conducted a MR analysis to assess the relationship between the expression of the risk genes in the colonic tissues, gut microbial abundance, and CRC. The overlapping single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the risk genes in both the transverse and sigmoid colon eQTL summary statistics were excluded (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jgo-2025-517-3.xlsx). The gut microbial data included 211 microbial abundance-associated SNPs from the MiBioGen consortium GWAS (24). To account for linkage disequilibrium, a linkage disequilibrium pruning threshold of R2<0.01 within a 10,000-kb window was applied. For the instrumental variable selection for gut microbiota, the SNPs were filtered at a locus-wide significance level (P<1×10−5) for bacterial taxa across four taxonomic levels (order, family, genus, and species), excluding chromosome 23, and SNPs with multiple alleles (>2) or minor allele frequency <0.01 were removed to minimize bias. The inverse-variance weighted (primary method) assumed all the instrumental variables were valid. A leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of the individual SNPs. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic.

Construction and validation of the prognostic nomogram

The multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that gender, pathological tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, and risk score were independent prognostic factors for OS in the CRC patients. A nomogram integrating age, gender, pathological TNM stage, and risk score was developed to predict individual survival probabilities. The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was assessed using calibration curves.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.0). Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

Identification of risk genes and construction of the prognostic risk model

The analysis of the GSE44076 dataset identified 742 upregulated and 897 downregulated DEGs (Figure 2A). Through cross-dataset screening, 13 candidate genes were selected (Figure S1A). To refine the prognostic signature, the LASSO algorithm was applied (Figure 2B,2C) to the GSE39582 dataset. The multivariate Cox regression analysis yielded six prognostic genes, and the risk score was calculated as: (−0.08272 × ABCB1) + (0.10385 × CALB2) + (−0.06062 × CCL20) + (−0.16687 × IDO1) + (−0.21283 × ODC1) + (−0.30291 × TJP3). The patients were stratified into high-risk (n=275) and low-risk (n=297) groups based on the median risk score. Significant differences in six prognostic genes expression level were observed between the groups (Figure S1B). The Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed poorer OS in the high-risk group (P=0.003; Figure 2D), and the risk score distribution plots showed an inverse correlation between risk score and survival time (Figure 2E). The time-dependent ROC analysis revealed area under the curve values of 0.606 (1-year), 0.633 (3-year), and 0.596 (5-year) for OS prediction (Figure 2F). The external validation using TCGA-COADREAD dataset replicated these findings (Figure 2G-2I), confirming the model’s robust prognostic ability.

Immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy response

The ssGSEA revealed distinct immune cell infiltration patterns between the risk groups. Notably, >50% of the immune cell subtypes showed elevated infiltration levels in the high-risk group (Figure 3A). The correlation analysis between immune cell abundance and risk gene expression revealed an inverse association between ABCA1, CCL20, ODC1, and TJP3, and most immune cell types, but showed positive associations between CALB2 and IDO1, of which, IDO1 was found to be particularly strongly correlated with the activated CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells (Figure 3B). The ESTIMATE analysis revealed significantly lower tumor purity, a result concordant with ssGSEA findings. However, higher ESTIMATE and stromal scores were observed in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Figure 4A). No significant difference was observed in the immune scores between the groups (Figure S1C). The analysis using the TIDE algorithm revealed significant associations between the risk groups and immunotherapy response markers, such that the high-risk group showed positive correlations with the TIDE and exclusion scores, indicating greater immune evasion potential, and a negative correlation with the MSI scores; no significant difference was observed in the dysfunction scores between the groups (Figure 4B and Figure S1D). Clinically, the low-risk CRC patients had significantly higher immune checkpoint expression (Figure 4C), which might explain their superior OS outcomes.

Assessment of gut microbes in mediating the causal relationship between risk genes and CRC

The functional enrichment analysis revealed that the target genes were associated with anti-microbial humoral immune responses mediated by anti-microbial peptides, defense responses against fungi/bacteria/Gram-negative bacteria, and key signaling pathways, including Staphylococcus aureus infection, parasitic diseases, and tumor necrosis factor signaling (Figures S2,S3). The SMR analysis failed to find any significant associations between the risk genes and CRC (Table S1); however, the MR analysis (Figure 5) revealed a significant positive association between the risk genes eQTL and CRC [OR =0.9672; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.9437–0.9914; P=0.008; Table S2], and a causal relationships between the risk genes eQTL and specific gut microbes; that is, Deltaproteobacteria (OR =0.9719; 95% CI: 0.9461–0.9984; P=0.03) and Methanobrevibacter (OR =0.9392; 95% CI: 0.8851–0.9966; P=0.03; Table S3). Notably, CRC was strongly correlated with 11 gut microbial taxa, including these two mediators (Table S4). Further, the gut microbes were found to mediate the causal relationship between the risk genes and CRC. The mediated proportion values [(indirect causal effect of coefficient product)/(total causal effect)] of these gut microbes were 16.02% and 12.99%, respectively. No reverse causation was observed in these results.

Construction and validation of a prognostic nomogram

A multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed using the GSE39582 dataset to evaluate the risk model’s clinical independence as a prognostic factor for CRC patients. The results showed that age, gender, TNM stage IV, and risk score were all statistically significant independent prognostic factors for survival prediction in CRC (Figure 6A). A nomogram was subsequently developed to quantitatively predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities (Figure 6B). The ROC curve showed good consistency between the model’s predicted clinical outcomes and actual results (concordance index =0.708; Figure 6C). The decision curve analysis showed a net benefit higher than the “all” and “none” lines, indicating the model’s practical clinical utility (Figure 6D). Additionally, the calibration curve showed strong agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Figure 6E). To validate these findings, we generated a validation cohort using TCGA-COADREAD dataset and performed the same analyses (Figure S4).

Discussion

Recently, Xie et al. developed a 20-gene prognostic model that showed robust performance in predicting CRC outcomes (25). Similarly, another study has proposed novel gene signatures for predicting OS in CRC (26). However, the prognostic value of these models remains limited in addressing the heterogeneity and aggressiveness of CRC (27). Thus, innovative risk stratification tools need to be developed to facilitate personalized treatment strategies and improve patient prognosis.

A previous study has suggested that the immune microenvironment is closely related to CRC (28). Research has shown that the macrophage migration inhibitory factor may regulate the development of CRC (29). Further, a previous study showed that high mast cell density indicates a good prognosis in colon cancer patients (30). While another study showed that the high expression of monocytes is related to a poor prognosis in CRC patients (31). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio has also been shown to be associated with the prognosis of CRC patients (32). A recent study exploring the prognostic value of immune cells in the CRC microenvironment showed that tumor-infiltrating immune cells are highly correlated with the progression and prognosis of CRC (33). Additionally, CRC is closely associated with gut microbes, which form an essential part of the TIME.

Various gut microbes are believed to cause the development of CRC. Microbial imbalance further exacerbates tumorigenesis by changing microbial metabolites. Clostridium difficile toxin activated NF-κB/STAT3 signaling, promoting CRC (34). Similarly, colibactin-producing Escherichia coli induced DNA damage in intestinal epithelial cells, driving carcinogenesis and correlating with poorer survival in CRC patients (35,36). Notably, the oncogenic effects of bacteria extend beyond genotoxicity and signaling pathway activation; they may also promote tumor development by modulating immune regulation. Some gut microbes exert immunosuppressive regulatory effects on the TIME (37). Mima et al. found that the number of Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) in CRC tissue was inversely correlated with CD3+ T cell density (12). The study has shown that certain gut microbiota enhances the expression of dendritic cells (DCs) and increase the number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, thereby inhibiting CRC growth (38). Further, preclinical and clinical evidence has highlighted the role of the gut microbiota in modulating the therapeutic responses of CRC patients to chemotherapy and immunotherapy (39). The oral administration of Bifidobacterium restored the anti-tumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody therapy by enhancing the maturation of DCs and promoting the activation and accumulation of CD8+ T cells (40). Similarly, Bacteroides fragilis was shown to enhance the anti-tumor effects of anti-CTLA4 therapy by triggering DC maturation and stimulating an IL-12-dependent T helper 1 immune response (41).

In this study, we analyzed the role of the TIME and gut microbiota in CRC. Specifically, this study sought to identify targeted risk genes, construct and validate a risk model, and establish it as a non-invasive biomarker for CRC screening and patient stratification, as well as a novel target for immunotherapy and a potential target for chemoprophylactic drugs. Our LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified a six-gene signature that demonstrated prognostic value in CRC. Reduced ABCB1 expression has been shown to occur early in CRC and promote tumorigenesis by increasing the accumulation of carcinogenic substrates (42). Research has shown that CALB2 modulates 5-fluorouracil sensitivity in CRC via the regulation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway (43). F. nucleatum-induced CCL20 has been shown to promote CRC metastasis and macrophage recruitment (44). Pflügler et al. discovered that IDO1-expressing Paneth cells in tumor stem cells promote immune escape in CRC (45). Moreover, ODC1 was shown to mediate the tumor suppression of miR-378a in CRC, and thus has therapeutic potential (46). We calculated the risk scores of the CRC patients and found that those in the high-risk group had significantly worse OS than those in the low-risk group. These findings offer a new perspective on CRC prognosis by linking the TIME and gut microbiota. Further, our findings suggest that the risk-score could serve as an independent prognostic factor for CRC patients and potentially guide the development of blood-based biomarkers for CRC.

To assess whether the new risk model could serve as a potential prognostic marker for CRC patients receiving immunotherapy, we evaluated immune cell infiltration, ESTIMATE scores, TIDE scores, and immune checkpoint expression. The results showed that the high-risk group had significantly higher immune cell infiltration, including of DCs, natural killer cells, macrophages, and mast cells. However, the high-risk group had lower TIDE scores and higher exclusion scores. This suggests the infiltrating immune cells in high-risk groups may exhibit a pro-tumor polarization phenotype, with macrophages skewing toward tumor-promoting M2-type (47), DCs maintaining an immature state that impairs T-cell activation (48), and mast cells predominantly promoting angiogenesis instead of mounting antitumor immune responses (49). Moreover, although exclusion scores principally reflect CD8+ T-cell sequestration (50), chemokine-mediated recruitment of alternative immune populations including macrophages and mast cells through CCL2 and CXCL12 signaling may still occur at the tumor periphery (51,52). However, their inability to infiltrate the tumor core renders this immune accumulation functionally ineffective (53). A recent study has shown that the CRC microenvironment exploits the plasticity of DCs to promote tumor progression (54). Macrophages drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to facilitate CRC metastasis (55). This pattern of abundant but dysfunctional immune cells accumulation reveals fundamental defects in TIME, creating an immunosuppressive milieu that contributes to both poor outcomes and limited response to immunotherapy in high-risk CRC patients.

Notably, gut microbiota influences the immune system and modulates immunotherapy efficacy (56,57). Research has shown that gut microbes can enhance T cell movement into CRC tumors (58). Further research is needed to establish whether gut microbiota could serve as reliable biomarkers for predicting the immunotherapy response in CRC (59). In this study, the MR analysis identified 11 gut microbial species as risk factors for CRC. Previous studies have shown that pathogenic gut microbes alter colonic epithelial gene expression and are correlated with CRC oncogene levels (60,61). Our MR analysis identified specific gene-microbiota associations in CRC, revealing Deltaproteobacteria and Methanobrevibacter as independent mediators. This crucial finding may provide a theoretical basis for gut microbiota involving of the pathogenesis of CRC. In summary, we explored the interplay between gut microbiota and the TIME in CRC, elucidating how microbial communities influence prognosis through key gene regulation and potential synergy with immune modulation. Our findings provide novel insights into how gene-microbe-immune interactions influence CRC progression and treatment response.

Finally, we developed a novel nomogram incorporating the risk score and other significant clinical indicators to systematically predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of the CRC patients. Validation in an independent cohort confirmed the nomogram’s strong predictive performance and higher net benefit. However, this study had several limitations, including reliance on existing datasets, a retrospective design, and a lack of contemporary clinical data validation for the nomogram prediction model. Further studies need to be conducted to validate the clinical utility of these results for CRC prognosis and treatment guidance.

Conclusions

This study developed a six-gene risk model to predict the prognosis of CRC patients. It also explored immune cell infiltration and the immunotherapy response across different risk groups. Through the MR analysis, gut microbes were identified as potential mediators between the risk-associated genes and CRC. Further, a nomogram that incorporated prognostic risk scores and clinical indicators was constructed to estimate patient survival rates. Our findings provide valuable insights that may inform personalized and precise treatment strategies in CRC.
