Improving Access to Cognitive Interventions for People With Dementia in Australian Community‐Based Settings
Authors: Stephanie Mulhall, Diane Gibson, Wendy A Longley, Nathan M D'Cunha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCognitive interventions, including cognitive stimulation therapy, cognitive rehabilitation and cognitive training, are increasingly recommended as key components of non‐pharmacological post‐diagnostic support for people with dementia. Cognitive interventions may help delay cognitive decline, enhance goal‐directed functional abilities and improve quality of life. Despite inclusion in clinical guidelines and recommendations, guidance on the delivery of these interventions within Australian community settings remains limited and is underutilised. This article addresses a critical translation gap in cognitive interventions for people with dementia, synthesises the evidence through an Australian practice and policy lens, examines current uptake in community settings and identifies barriers, enablers and delivery models to inform implementation strategies. Community settings are defined as memory clinics, primary care, hospital outpatient services, allied health providers, community aged care and non‐government providers. Current evidence indicates cognitive interventions have varying benefits across different outcomes, including cognitive function, social engagement, everyday functioning, quality of life and goal attainment. International practices related to implementation are explored, along with future directions for expanding access through technology, flexible delivery models, group‐based approaches and integrating these interventions into existing care structures. Addressing the gap between recommendations and current practices requires building community awareness, improving access to professional education and training, and careful resource allocation. Cognitive interventions should be part of comprehensive rehabilitation and can be personalised to individual needs and goals. Expanding access and improving the availability of a range of cognitive interventions in community settings is crucial to ensure people with dementia receive best practice post‐diagnostic support.Keywords: Alzheimer disease, community health services, dementia, neuropsychology, quality of life, rehabilitation

Introduction

Cognitive interventions are an essential component of post‐diagnostic support for people with dementia and are consistently included in dementia clinical guidelines, recommendations and Australian Memory Clinic Guidelines [1, 2, 3]. Dementia is a major public health concern characterised by progressive cognitive decline affecting independence and social roles. Although disease‐modifying treatments remain under investigation, non‐pharmacological approaches, including psychosocial care, play an essential role in addressing the immediate needs of people with dementia, care partners and family members.

Cognitive interventions, including cognitive stimulation therapy (CST), cognitive rehabilitation and cognitive training, are supported by evidence and demonstrate measurable benefits across multiple domains, including cognitive function, quality of life, social engagement, everyday functioning and goal attainment [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Despite calls to improve access to post‐diagnostic support, the delivery of cognitive interventions remains fragmented and underutilised as part of routine post‐diagnostic care across community settings, such as memory clinics, primary care, hospital outpatient services, allied health professionals, community aged care and non‐government organisations [9, 10].

This article focuses on community‐based post‐diagnostic support for people with dementia, which refers to structured services delivered after diagnosis and delivered outside the inpatient hospital setting and residential aged care. Prior reviews have highlighted the efficacy and comparative effectiveness of cognitive interventions [11]; however, there is limited information for clinicians and policymakers on the rationale for incorporating cognitive interventions into routine practice in Australia. The current article addresses this gap by synthesising evidence on cognitive interventions, with attention to delivery models and outcomes in community settings, perceived service gaps and context‐specific implementation barriers and enablers through an Australian practice and policy lens. Cognitive interventions align with rehabilitation‐based approaches for people living with dementia, and the priorities of the Alzheimer's Disease International 2025 World Alzheimer's report [12] and the World Health Organization [2]. Furthermore, it is consistent with the priorities of the National Dementia Action Plan 2024–2034 to strengthen post‐diagnostic care, inclusivity and access to support [13]. These approaches also align with what people with dementia and care partners consider important; supporting cognition and engagement in everyday life [14, 15].

Cognitive Interventions

Cognitive interventions aim to improve or preserve daily function and support quality of life. Cognitive interventions with the strongest evidence base for people with dementia are cognitive stimulation, cognitive rehabilitation and cognitive training. The current evidence by intervention is summarised in Table 1, including population, outcomes, effect sizes and evidence strength, as appropriate. A recent systematic review by Jeon et al. [2] examined a range of rehabilitation‐focused clinical recommendations and guidelines for people with dementia, which included cognitive interventions. The importance of cognitive interventions as a vital component of post‐diagnostic dementia support and rehabilitation for people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia, including group‐based cognitive stimulation, reminiscence therapy, cognitive rehabilitation and cognitive training, has been highlighted [2, 12]. The comparative efficacy of cognitive interventions for people with Alzheimer's disease was examined in a recent review by Xiang and Zhang [11], which found greater benefits from cognitive training and cognitive stimulation when combined with other non‐pharmacological interventions. This evidence indicates CST and cognitive training rank highly for cognitive outcomes, whereas cognitive rehabilitation‐based approaches yield superior results related to everyday functioning and goal attainment outcomes.

Most studies of cognitive interventions in dementia have primarily focused on samples of people with Alzheimer's disease. Evidence for cognitive interventions across other forms of dementia, such as vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, Parkinson's disease and non‐language forms of frontotemporal dementia, and other rare dementias, including young‐onset dementia, remains less well established [17]. Due to the varied underlying pathology and neurocognitive profiles of people with non‐Alzheimer's disease type dementia, their response to cognitive interventions may differ. Although evidence to date supports the use of a range of cognitive interventions as part of a multidisciplinary rehabilitation approach for people with other forms of dementia (Table 1), more evidence is needed in the Australian context.

Internationally, approaches to post‐diagnostic support for people with dementia vary, with differences in national healthcare systems and national dementia care guidelines [12]. Dementia care models in some high‐income countries (e.g., Canada, Netherlands and United Kingdom), include defined recommended pathways from diagnosis to post‐diagnostic support, with CST and cognitive rehabilitation often offered within memory clinics and community settings. However, inconsistencies in the availability and access to cognitive interventions may persist, even when clear recommendations and pathways are available, due to geographical variability and other factors.

Cognitive Stimulation

Cognitive stimulation encompasses elements of reminiscence therapy, reality orientation and multisensory stimulation through discussion, games and creative tasks, aiming to improve engagement in both cognitive and social activities. Cognitive stimulation therapy is a form of cognitive stimulation, with the CST program typically delivered in a small group by two facilitators during 14, 45‐min bi‐weekly sessions across seven consecutive weeks [18]. Each session maintains a consistent structure, based on themes and activities that can be adapted to the group, and includes activities, games and sensory elements aimed at stimulating memory and communication. Resources and training to support the delivery of CST groups are widely available, leading to extensive use within the United Kingdom where it originated and internationally across 42 countries. Cognitive stimulation therapy is strongly recommended in several clinical guidelines for mild‐to‐moderate dementia, including the NICE Dementia Guidelines [3].

Findings from a Cochrane review of 43 studies identified small but significant improvements in cognitive function among people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia who took part in cognitive stimulation (various interventions) [8]. Furthermore, a meta‐analysis of 11 studies of group‐based CST found small‐to‐moderate benefits for global cognition, language and working memory among those with mild‐to‐moderate dementia [5]. The extent to which benefits persist after the intervention ends remains unclear. However, in the context of progressive decline associated with dementia, it would be logical to expect the benefits to gradually decrease over time [19]. Beyond cognitive outcomes, there is evidence cognitive stimulation is associated with improvements in communication, social participation, everyday functioning, mood and behaviour in people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia [8]. There is also good evidence supporting the use of reminiscence therapy, a form of cognitive stimulation that uses prompts or familiar objects or music to promote discussion, as a stand‐alone intervention, including small benefits for cognition, communication, mood and quality of life [20]. Cognitive stimulation therapy in practice: The SPICE program.

University of Canberra Hospital, Canberra, ACT Australia Program context The Sustainable Personalised Interventions for Cognition, Care, and Engagement (SPICE) program is a 10‐week multicomponent post‐diagnostic program for people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia and their care partners delivered by Canberra Health Services outpatient rehabilitation [21]. The SPICE program includes groups of up to eight people with dementia plus their eight care partners. Cognitive stimulation therapy (CST) is a core component of the SPICE program, which includes 20 CST sessions (twice‐weekly, 60 min each). Session themes may include current affairs, physical movement, food, travel and childhood memories Delivery model The SPICE program components include CST, physical activity, care partner education and peer support, nutrition advice and the Care of People with dementia in their Environment (COPE) program. The CST components are delivered to small groups of up to eight people with dementia. Two CST‐trained allied health professionals or allied health assistants facilitate the group sessions. Eligibility to participate in the program is open to people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia who live independently in the community. Participants can self‐refer, or a clinician can refer. The program is publicly funded at no cost to participants Implementation insights CST groups, as part of the SPICE program, are delivered within the existing outpatient rehabilitation clinic in a hospital setting. This integration of CST into a multicomponent post‐diagnostic program within an outpatient rehabilitation service serving older people eliminates the need for specialised staff, additional space or new referral pathways. Existing resources and infrastructure can be used, although there may be competing demands or priorities in this setting. Staff demand on time can amount to up to 3 h per cohort per week. Ongoing sustainability in this setting requires support and relies on funding cycles and clinical priorities within the service. The program is not appropriate for people with severe dementia

Cognitive Rehabilitation

Cognitive rehabilitation aims to improve or maintain independence and everyday functioning by supporting people with dementia to work towards personally meaningful goals, improve problem‐solving ability and support overall quality of life. Cognitive rehabilitation typically includes individual goal setting, the development of compensatory strategies and modification of activities [17]. Cognitive rehabilitation may be delivered in individual or group‐based formats but is typically tailored to individual needs and personalised goals. A Cochrane review (including 44 trials) found moderate‐quality evidence for the use of cognitive rehabilitation among people with mild‐to‐moderate dementia, with outcomes including goal attainment, defined as personally meaningful goals identified at the start of therapy, such as activities of daily living, social engagement and leisure activities [6]. Furthermore, a meta‐analysis of 14 clinical trials found cognitive rehabilitation was associated with significant improvements in quality of life and goal attainment for people with Alzheimer's disease, although no significant benefit was identified for cognitive outcomes [22].

Structured programs of cognitive rehabilitation have been developed specifically for dementia. The first large randomised controlled trial, the Goal Orientated Cognitive Rehabilitation in Early‐Stage Alzheimer's Trial (GREAT) program, based in the United Kingdom, focused on working towards individually meaningful goals which could be delivered over six to eight 60–90‐min sessions [16, 23]. Improvements in goal attainment and satisfaction were observed among participants with mild dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia and mixed dementia, with benefits persisting at 3‐ and 6‐month follow‐ups. People with dementia indicated participation in the trial improved their confidence and independence [16]. However, there were no significant improvements on measures of cognitive function, quality of life, mood or self‐efficacy [16]. Additionally, there is evidence supporting language‐ and communication‐focused cognitive rehabilitation for the language‐led frontotemporal dementias, such as primary progressive aphasia [24]. Cognitive rehabilitation in practice: The iREADi program.

Uniting War Memorial Hospital, Sydney, NSW Australia Program context Integrated Rehabilitation for Early‐Stage Dementia (iREADi) is a group rehabilitation and education program for up to 10 people with early‐stage dementia plus their 10 care partners. The program aims to build knowledge, skills, confidence, wellbeing, and quality of life after a dementia diagnosis. It is built on a cognitive rehabilitation framework and contains specific cognitive rehabilitation components. The initial rehabilitation and education group phase of the program is delivered over 9 weeks (approximately 2 h per week). This is followed by a 12‐week, individualised, goal‐oriented component in which allied health and nursing professionals support people with dementia and their care partners to achieve meaningful goals Delivery model Delivered as part of the iREADi outpatient program delivered at Uniting War Memorial Hospital, Sydney, by the Management of Dementia (MOD) Squad, a specialist multidisciplinary team (medicine, nursing, clinical psychology, neuropsychology, speech pathology, social work, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, dietetics, diversional therapy, and health law). In the initial group phase of the program, a range of dementia rehabilitation skills are taught. One of the specific cognitive rehabilitation skills training components involves a 2‐h face‐name strategy learning workshop for people with dementia, followed by 2 weeks of homework practice, review and guidance from the neuropsychologist. In the second, individualised goal‐oriented phase of the program, people with dementia and their care partners are supported by allied health and nursing professionals to identify one or two goals they want to work on to improve their ability to live well with dementia, and are then helped to achieve those goals utilising a range of cognitive rehabilitation and general aged rehabilitation approaches. The program includes an assessment of support needs, individualised care coordination, structured goal setting, health behaviour change facilitation methods and aged rehabilitation approaches aligned with the agreed goals. Cognitive Training

Cognitive training involves practising specific tasks to improve cognitive function across different domains, such as memory, attention or problem‐solving, and is often delivered using a computerised or online format or mobile application. Findings from a Cochrane review identified small‐to‐moderate benefits following cognitive training in people with mild dementia on global cognitive function and verbal semantic fluency, with improvements maintained at follow‐up intervals between 3 and 12 months [4]. However, there was insufficient evidence to support the use of cognitive training for other outcomes, such as functional ability, behavioural symptoms or dementia severity [4]. Findings from a systematic review and meta‐analysis of cognitive interventions in people with Alzheimer's disease showed evidence of improvements in global cognitive function across 20 trials of cognitive training [19]. Findings from a randomised controlled trial of the La Trobe Caulfield Hospital (LATCH) Memory Group Program, a structured group program, found further evidence of the benefits of targeted memory strategy training for participants (n = 132) with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia [25]. The LATCH program is delivered over six to eight sessions and includes psychoeducation, compensatory strategies and practical exercise (but not computerised‐based tasks or practice), and has been shown to improve everyday memory function, functional outcomes and quality of life for people with mild cognitive impairment compared with waitlist controls [25]. Participants continued to use strategies at the 6‐month follow‐up, suggesting enduring benefits from a targeted intervention [25]. An example of this program in practice is depicted in Box 3.

Cognitive training in practice: LATCH memory group.

Age Right Malvern East, Victoria, Australia Program context The La Trobe Caulfield Hospital (LATCH) Memory Group is a manualised, evidence‐based, small‐group program that aims to teach practical strategies to support memory and daily function for people living with mild cognitive impairment or dementia. The program includes memory strategies including internal strategies (visualisation, association and chunking), the use of external memory aids (calendars, smartphone Applications and lists) and strategies applied to everyday tasks. The LATCH program is delivered over 6 weeks (2‐h sessions) within small group (8–12 participants) Delivery model The LATCH program is delivered by Age Right Malvern East, a private multidisciplinary geriatric medicine practice located in metropolitan Melbourne. The program is run by a trained facilitator using the LATCH manual and resources. It is available to AgeRight patients and their families with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. The delivery of the LATCH program by AgeRight since approximately 2022 demonstrates successful translation from research to practice within primary care setting for older people. Features including offering the program within an existing service structure for older people and opening the program to patients of the clinic. The groups are held at the clinic location using existing staff and group space. Fidelity to the program components is maintained through delivery by a trained facilitator. Participants in the group requires sufficient cognitive capacity to apply new strategies and learn in a small‐group environment, which may limit the suitability of the program to people with mild dementia, and it is not suitable for moderate‐to‐severe dementia

A common criticism of cognitive training is the apparent lack of transfer and generalisability to other tasks, or lack of long‐term benefit following the intervention [19]. However, its benefits seem to be optimised when cognitive training is paired with other cognitive interventions [11]. Thus, this form of cognitive intervention may be best suited to multicomponent programs.

Technology offers promising opportunities for providing cognitive interventions to people with dementia. A recent systematic review reported virtual reality‐based cognitive training can improve outcomes, underscoring the potential of technology‐driven adaptations [26]. Cognitive training offered through digital platforms and mobile applications may reduce barriers to participation and enhance accessibility [26]. Furthermore, cognitive training may offer a form of engagement that is enjoyable and fosters confidence and a sense of achievement.

Current Practice in Australia

Despite strong evidence, the translation of cognitive interventions into routine care post‐diagnostic pathways in Australia remains limited. Memory clinics are well‐positioned to provide multidisciplinary care from the point of diagnosis, yet a national survey of 60 memory clinics identified only 20% offered cognitive interventions, and of those only 15% offered two or more sessions [9]. Cognitive interventions were offered as part of multidisciplinary reablement packages together with other supportive components in only 9% of clinics. The most common cognitive interventions offered included memory strategy training (20%) and group‐based programs with a cognitive focus (13%), while approaches using technology such as telehealth memory rehabilitation (4%) and computerised cognitive training (2%) were uncommon [9]. This is despite survey findings showing staff recognise cognitive interventions as essential to post‐diagnostic support [9]. Recently, a panel of experts, including geriatricians, neurologists and allied health professionals, identified cognitive interventions to be a highly desirable component of post‐diagnostic services offered within memory clinics [10]. Despite this level of interest and perceived importance, memory clinics are primarily funded for dementia diagnosis. Several systemic barriers and structural constraints limit the introduction of cognitive interventions within memory clinics, including service structure, workforce and resource demands [9, 10]. Furthermore, although memory clinic guidelines strongly recommend onward referral where a component of care or support is not available within the clinic setting [1], this referral process may be challenging where there is a scarcity of suitable services and providers in the community [17].

Although findings from memory clinics alone are unlikely to capture the full extent of delivery in community‐based settings, given the central role of memory clinics as a source of post‐diagnostic support, the limited availability in this setting likely reflects broader implementation challenges. Findings from a recent scoping review examining implementation of cognitive interventions described variations in delivery settings, with CST often offered within day programs or aged care, while cognitive rehabilitation or memory strategy training were more commonly found in community centres [27]. Other community settings where cognitive interventions of various types could be realistically delivered include primary care clinics, hospital outpatient services, allied health clinics and other non‐government and allied health provider services. Delivery modalities could include telehealth or in‐home‐based services. As a group intervention, CST may also be more cost‐effective [28].

Although cognitive interventions can be delivered in various community settings, including a person's home, by individual clinicians, not‐for‐profit organisations or community groups, the scale of implementation and the consistency of delivery across settings remain unclear. Regardless of the setting, cognitive interventions should be tailored to the individual, ideally through a biopsychosocial approach, and should consider individual preferences, goals and expected outcomes. Individual characteristics, such as age, dementia type and symptom severity, also influence the suitability of each intervention [17].

Neuropsychologists often play a central role in assessment, diagnosis and delivery of interventions for cognitive disorders, including provision of expert post‐assessment feedback and psychoeducation, which can help reduce distress, improve self‐efficacy and improve insight among people newly diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, and in turn can support engagement in other cognitive interventions [17, 29]. However, ongoing involvement of neuropsychologists in the delivery of post‐diagnostic support is often limited due to resource constraints within the public health system and cost‐related barriers when accessing private providers. This is compounded by limited Medicare funding for neuropsychology‐led services. Misconceptions about the role and benefits of rehabilitation for people with dementia also persist [30]. Findings from a survey of healthcare professionals indicated limited awareness of the benefits of cognitive rehabilitation for people with dementia, and many clinicians are prone to nihilistic attitudes [17]. Improving awareness via targeted education to community providers about the benefits of cognitive interventions in dementia as a component of comprehensive rehabilitation and post‐diagnostic support is therefore crucial.

Improving Access to Cognitive Interventions

Improving access to a range of cognitive interventions for people with dementia in community settings requires identifying and addressing the barriers to broader implementation. Resource constraints, particularly insufficient funding and staffing, are often cited as a key barrier to the provision of post‐diagnostic support in routine care [10, 27]. Pike et al. [27] identified common characteristics of successful implementation of cognitive interventions, such as strong stakeholder engagement at all levels (end users, clinicians and organisation), trained facilitators and the availability of adaptable manuals. Integrating cognitive interventions within a range of existing community‐based services, not just to memory clinics, may also broaden access. Findings from an implementation study of the GREAT cognitive rehabilitation program indicated it could be successfully integrated into existing home‐based community health services delivered by England's National Health Service when carefully implemented [23]. Furthermore, the newly integrated cognitive rehabilitation program was well received by practitioners and required only modest additional funds [23].

Group‐based programs that reduce staff workload and costs are generally well accepted by service providers, with strong evidence that CST group programs are cost‐effective, scalable and beneficial across a range of outcomes for people with dementia living in the community [8, 28]. Access to CST, for example, has practical benefits as it is a manualised program that can be facilitated by a range of health and aged care workers.

The Support at Home funding model, introduced in Australia in November 2025, provides an opportunity to expand access to cognitive interventions through its independence and restorative care pathways. Care delivery should reflect the preferences and priorities of people with dementia, including cognitive support and personalised goals [9, 15, 17]. However, achieving this requires workforce capacity building and practical supports, such as delivery resources, fidelity tools and structured supervision, to ensure quality and sustainability.

To address resource constraints within services and shortages of allied health providers, upskilling existing providers and training facilitators will be necessary [31]. Capacity building of both healthcare providers, hospital outpatient services [21] and community organisations via targeted education and training should also improve availability of cognitive intervention services. Resources such as the Cognitive Treatments Article Library and Evaluation (CogTale) website, developed by researchers and clinicians specialising in cognitive interventions, can support this upskilling process by providing evidence‐based information and user‐friendly summaries to inform clinical decision‐making and implementation planning [32].

The use of technology and alternative modes of delivery may also enhance access to cognitive interventions by reducing geographical and cost‐related barriers [27]. Adaptations to the modality of service delivery have been increasingly examined, including the use of virtual CST groups [8]. For instance, there is growing evidence supporting the benefits of digital and virtual reality‐based modes of cognitive training for people with dementia, which has the potential to lower staff costs associated with program delivery [4]. Further evaluation is warranted to assess alternative delivery approaches, technology‐enhanced and virtual delivery modes, and tailoring to meet individual or community needs. There is also evidence that some interventions can be delivered by trained informal carers and family members, such as individual CST. These adaptations are especially important for regional and rural communities, where workforce shortages often create greater barriers to post‐diagnostic care and support, resulting in inequitable access to necessary care [33]. Furthermore, it is important that interventions are tailored to the needs of First Nations people of Australia with dementia [34] and other diverse and marginalised groups to promote improved access to culturally sensitive and appropriate health and social care [10].

Misconceptions related to the aims and outcomes of the use of cognitive interventions for people with dementia may contribute to their underutilisation in the context of post‐diagnostic services and may undermine rehabilitation efforts. Clear and accurate information for clinicians, people with dementia and care partners should outline the benefits and limits of cognitive interventions. In addition to cognitive function, people with dementia and care partners often identify the importance of improvements in areas, such as daily functioning, relationships and quality of life [14]. Although cognitive outcomes are often a primary target of cognitive interventions, a holistic, person‐centred approach to rehabilitation that considers personal goals and individual needs is essential and consistent with the objectives of the National Dementia Action Plan 2024–2034 [13].

Conclusions

Cognitive interventions are a crucial component of post‐diagnostic care for people with dementia, offering benefits across cognitive function, quality of life, social engagement, everyday functioning and goal attainment. Expanding access in Australian community settings would bridge the gap between evidence and practice, aligning with best practice guidelines. Importantly, cognitive interventions reflect the priorities of people with dementia and care partners. By embedding cognitive interventions within routine community‐based care, Australia can move towards more equitable, person‐centred care that supports living well with dementia.
