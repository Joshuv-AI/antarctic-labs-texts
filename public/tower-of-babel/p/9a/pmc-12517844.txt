Machine learning and molecular dynamics reveal anti-osteoporosis potential of dietary flavonoids
Authors: Baihe Zhang, Lixia Zhao, Yutong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis (OP) has imposed a heavy burden on global health. Current treatments often come with side effects, which has spurred interest in dietary bioactive compounds with preventive potential. Flavonoids, typical representatives of both medicine and food, possess nutritional and pharmacological properties related to bone health. However, the key active components and mechanisms of action of their anti-OP effects still require systematic research. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies were integrated. The flavonoid intake and OP status of 5789 American adults (aged ≥50) were analyzed. Five machine-learning algorithms (Boruta, LassoCV, RFECV, mRMR, and ReliefF) were employed to screen flavonoid sub-classes, followed by ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity) analysis. The XGBoost model was used to predict the risk of OP and was validated through the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis. Network pharmacology was used to identify the common targets of OP and flavonoids, and the binding stability was verified by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation. Eleven key flavonoids were identified by machine learning. Five compounds with high bioavailability and low toxicity were prioritized through ADMET screening: daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol. Five core targets related to the pathogenesis of OP were identified through network pharmacology and protein-protein interaction network: signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, estrogen receptor 1, carbonic anhydrase 2, androgen receptor (AR), and estrogen receptor 2. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding between the 5 screened flavonoids and the core targets, especially the binding between apigenin and AR. Molecular dynamics simulation further indicated excellent stability of the AR-apigenin complex. Daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol may inhibit bone resorption and promote bone formation by regulating signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, estrogen receptor 1, carbonic anhydrase 2, AR, and estrogen receptor 2. The high stability of the AR-apigenin complex supports the potential of flavonoids as therapeutic agents for OP through a multi-target mechanism and their value in nutritional intervention.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disease characterized by reduced bone mass, destruction of bone microstructure, and increased bone fragility. The fragility fractures caused by it, as one of the most serious complications of OP, not only lead to limited mobility and increased pain in patients but may also cause secondary lesions such as kyphosis of the spine, seriously affecting the quality of life and increasing the risk of death.[1,2] It has become an important global public health problem threatening the health of the middle-aged and elderly population.

Currently, clinical treatments mainly focus on anti-resorptive drugs and synthetic calcium preparations. However, long-term use is often accompanied by side effects such as digestive tract reactions and cardiovascular risks,[3,4] which prompts researchers to turn their attention to dietary active ingredients with the potential of “preventing diseases before they occur.” In this context, the theory of homology between medicine and food provides a unique medical and philosophical perspective for exploring the diet-health relationship, emphasizing the homology of food and medicine and their synergistic effects in disease prevention.[5] Modern epidemiological studies have shown that substances with homology between medicine and food, represented by flavonoids, can have both nutritional and pharmacological properties.[6]

Flavonoids, as the largest family of plant secondary metabolites, are typical components with homology between medicine and food. They include 6 major sub-classes such as flavanols and flavonols and are widely present in dietary matrices such as fruits, vegetables, and tea.[7] Their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties have been confirmed to be closely related to bone metabolism regulation.[8,9] For example, a recent study showed that the main active flavanone components such as neohesperidin, naringin, and naringenin extracted from dry wolfberry can alleviate retinoic acid-induced OP in rats, improve bone microstructure, inhibit bone resorption, and do not cause obvious liver or kidney toxicity. In particular, naringenin and naringin show potential benefits for treating OP.[10] However, most of the existing evidence is limited to in vitro experiments of single sub-classes or small-sample observational studies. There is a lack of population-based evidence for systematically evaluating the effects of different flavonoid sub-classes on OP, and the key active ingredients and molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

To break through the above research bottlenecks, this study innovatively constructs a 3-dimensional analysis system of “database mining-network pharmacology-computational simulation.” First, based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS), a large-sample dietary flavonoid exposure profile is constructed, and machine-learning algorithms and ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity) screening are used to identify the flavonoid sub-classes most relevant to OP. Second, network pharmacology techniques are used to identify the core target group of flavonoid-OP interactions through protein-protein interaction (PPI) network topology analysis. Molecular docking simulation is used to verify the binding affinity and structure-activity relationship between key components and targets, and molecular dynamics simulation further determines the stability of the complex through dynamic trajectory analysis. This strategy will achieve cross-scale analysis from population epidemiological evidence to molecular mechanisms, providing new candidate molecules and theoretical basis for precise nutritional intervention in OP. This research strategy not only breaks through the analysis dimension of traditional nutritional epidemiology but also interprets the essence of the role of substances with homology between medicine and food at the molecular interaction level, providing a theoretical basis for developing dietary-based prevention and control strategies for OP.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

NHANES is an ongoing cross-sectional study in the United States that assesses the health and nutritional status of American adults and children. It mainly consists of demographic, physical examination, laboratory, dietary, and questionnaire surveys.[11] The data in NHANES were collected after obtaining informed consent from all participants and were approved by the ethics committee of the National Center for Health Statistics (CDC). This study used the survey data of a total of 29,940 participants from 3 cycles in 2007 to 2008, 2009 to 2010, and 2017 to 2018. Figure 1 summarizes the participant flow: after removing individuals with missing dietary flavonoid data, bone mineral density (BMD) values, or any key covariates, and restricting the sample to adults aged ≥50 years, 5789 participants remained for analysis.

Participant screening flowchart. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Assessment of variables

The intake information of flavonoids and their sub-classes was sourced from the FNDDS database linked to the NHANES database. All participants in the NHANES database were subjected to 2 rounds of 24-hour dietary recall interviews. The first interview was conducted face to face, and the second was carried out via telephone 3 days later. Since there were numerous omissions in the data from the second interview in the publicly available data provided by the NHANES database, the food intake data from the first interview were used in this study. The intakes of 6 flavonoid sub-classes and 29 flavonoid compounds were mainly extracted. OP was mainly defined by BMD measurements of the femoral neck and lumbar spine.[12] When the BMD of the femoral neck or lumbar spine was below 2.5, OP was defined.

2.3. Screening of flavonoids

Five machine-learning algorithms, including Boruta, LassoCV, RFECV, mRMR, and ReliefF, were used to predict the core role of flavonoid intake in the OP prediction model.[13,14] The SMILES format of the compounds was collected from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Then, the compounds were screened based on gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, oral bioavailability, drug-likeness, and toxicity using the SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) and admetlab3 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) databases.[15,16] The GI absorption rate reflects the decomposition efficiency of drug molecules in the digestive tract via the oral route and the intensity of their entry into the systemic circulation. The drug-likeness score predicts the potential of a molecule to be developed into a drug by evaluating its structural similarity to known drugs. Oral bioavailability is defined as the proportion of the active pharmaceutical ingredient that enters the systemic circulation relative to the total administered dose after oral administration. While the risk of drug-induced toxicity is used to quantify the degree of cell damage or organ dysfunction that a compound may induce. Therefore, only compounds showing high GI absorption, ideal bioavailability, excellent drug-likeness scores, and low toxicity thresholds were considered as potential candidates for further analysis.

2.4. Construction, validation, and interpretability analysis of an OP prediction model based on flavonoid intake

The predictive efficacy of individual flavonoid intake for OP was initially assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Subsequently, an XGBoost-based predictive model was implemented using Python 3.9 and the xgboost package (v2.0.1), with hyperparameter optimization via grid search (learning rate: 0.01; max depth: 5; subsample ratio: 0.8; n-estimators: 500). Model performance was rigorously evaluated through 10-fold stratified cross-validation (70% training/30% testing) using key metrics including area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and calibration curves. To enhance interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were computed via Python shap library (v0.43.0), determine the correlation between each factor and the model prediction results, and enhance the interpretability of the model.

2.5. Acquisition of disease and compound targets

The targets of flavonoid compounds were obtained from the Drugbank database[17] (https://go.drugbank.com/), and the relevant targets of OP were obtained from the GeneCards database[18] (https://www.genecards.org/). The common targets between flavonoid-related targets and OP-related targets were identified by drawing Venn diagrams and taking the intersection. The common targets were subjected to gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) and the annotation database org.Hs.eg.db (v3.17.0). The significance of enrichment was evaluated using thresholds of P-value <.05 and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P-value <.05. The results were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.4.4). In the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org), the target PPI network was drawn with a minimum required interaction score >0.4 to screen the core targets.[19]

2.6. Molecular docking studies to verify potential candidates

The 2D structures of small molecule ligands were obtained from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These 2D structures were then input into Chem Office software to generate their 3D structures, which were saved as mol2 files. Subsequently, crystal structures of protein targets with high resolution were screened from the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) and used as molecular docking acceptors. The PyMOL software (Schrödinger, Inc., New York) was employed to perform operations such as removing water molecules and phosphate groups from the proteins, and the results were saved as PDB files. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina 1.5.6 software (The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla) to explore the interactions between proteins and ligands. The AutoDock software was used to process the structures of proteins and small molecules. Hydrogen atoms were added to both proteins and small molecules, water molecules were removed from proteins, and torsional forces were determined for small molecule ligands. Then, the coordinates of the docking box were determined. By comparing the scores of the docking results, the optimal conformation of the molecular simulation was finally obtained. The pymol and Discovery Studio 2019 software (BIOVIA, Vélizy-Villacoublay, Paris, France) were used to visualize the 2D and 3D interaction diagrams between the test compounds and key residues. Generally, a binding energy less than −5.0 kcal/mol indicates good binding activity between the 2, while a value less than −7.0 kcal/mol indicates strong binding activity. Lower binding energy implies stronger binding activity, higher affinity, and greater conformational stability.

2.7. Molecular dynamics

The ligand topology files were generated via the CGenFF online server (PARAMCHEM) with a penalty score of <50 to ensure compatibility with the CHARMM force field. The topology of the receptor protein was generated using the pdb2gmx tool in GROMACS (CHARMM36 force field).[20] The system was solvated by adding a 1 nm TIP3P-type cubic water box around it. Ions were added to the system using the gmx genion tool to achieve electrical neutrality. Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled by the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a cutoff distance set at 1 nm. All bond constraints were accomplished by the SHAKE algorithm, and the integration time step in the molecular dynamics simulation was set at 1 fs using the Verlet leapfrog algorithm. Prior to the molecular dynamics simulation, the system underwent energy optimization. The energy minimization process included 3000 steps of steepest descent optimization followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient optimization. The optimization steps were as follows: first, the solute was constrained, and energy minimization was performed on water molecules; then, counterions were constrained for energy minimization; finally, the entire system was energy-minimized without constraints. The simulation was run under the conditions of a 310 K temperature and a constant-pressure NPT system for a duration of 100 ns. During the simulation, tools such as g-rmsd, g-rmsf, g-hbond, g-Rg, and g-sasa were used to calculate the root-mean-square deviation, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds (HBonds), radius of gyration, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), respectively.

2.8. Statistical analysis

Considering the complex sampling design of NHANES, the weight “wtdrd1” was used for weighting to ensure the representativeness of the sample in the general population. For continuous variables with a missing rate of less than 5%, multiple imputation was employed for processing, while variables with a missing rate of 5% or higher were directly excluded. Prior to analysis, continuous variables were standardized using the Z-score method, and categorical variables were subjected to 1-hot encoding. For continuous variables in the baseline data, 1-way analysis of variance was used to compare the differences between different groups, and the results were expressed as weighted means ± standard errors. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was used for comparison, and the results were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of participant

A total of 5789 subjects aged 50 years and above were ultimately included in this study. This sample was representative, representing 84,749,868 non-hospitalized individuals in the United States. The baseline characteristics of the participants were presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q218 according to their OP status. In our study, a total of 314 cases of OP were observed. There were statistically significant differences in age, body mass index (BMI), sex, and Eth between the 2 groups (P < .05). The average age of participants with normal bone density was 64.16 years, while that of participants with OP was 70.96 years. A higher proportion of females (77.07%) developed OP compared to males. Participants with OP had a lower BMI. Non-Hispanic Whites had a higher incidence of OP (60.51%) compared to other ethnic groups. There were no statistically significant differences in the intakes of various flavonoids (P > .05).

3.2. Screening of compounds by machine-learning algorithms

In this study, 5 machine-learning algorithms were employed to screen and compare the predictors. First, the top 10 key variables selected by the Boruta algorithm were myricetin, quercetin, catechin, epicatechin, epigallocatechin, apigenin, kaempferol, peonidin, cyanidin, and daidzein (Fig. 2A). Two compounds were screened out by Lasso regression, namely thearubigins and subtotal catechins, with an optimal regularization parameter of 0.275. One factor, peonidin, was selected through the XGBoost-RFE method. Two factors, luteolin and daidzein, were selected by the SVM-RFE method. Eleven factors were selected by the ReliefF method, namely glycitein, theaflavin3gallate, theaflavin3qgallate, theaflavin33digallate, thearubigins, theaflavin, eriodictyol, malvidin, daidzein, petunidin, and hesperetin. Based on the 5 machine-learning algorithms and the feature importance ranking graph (Fig. 2B), 11 compounds were finally screened out, namely daidzein, myricetin, quercetin, catechin, epicatechin, epigallocatechin, apigenin, kaempferol, peonidin, cyanidin, and luteolin.

3.3. Screening of compounds based on ADMET

In this study, the multi-dimensional pharmaceutical properties of 11 flavonoids were systematically analyzed. The physicochemical parameters showed that the molecular weight ranged from 254.24 to 336.72 g/mol, and the topological polar surface area was between 70.67 and 151.59 Å2. Quercetin (131.36 Å2) and myricetin (151.59 Å2) had relatively high polarities. In terms of lipophilicity, daidzein (log P 2.64) and apigenin (2.11) exhibited significant hydrophobicity, while epigallocatechin (0.42) and cyanidin (0.32) showed strong hydrophilicity. The water-solubility data indicated that all compounds were soluble, except for daidzein and peonidin, which were moderately soluble.

Pharmacokinetic evaluation revealed that all compounds had high GI absorption characteristics, and only daidzein showed blood-brain barrier permeability (BBB permeant). Cytochrome P450 inhibition analysis indicated that daidzein, quercetin, apigenin, luteolin, and kaempferol had broad-spectrum inhibitory activities against CYP1A2 and CYP3A4, and quercetin also inhibited CYP2D6. Drug-likeness evaluation confirmed that all compounds met Lipinski Rule of Five, and among the 5 evaluation systems such as Ghose and Veber, only myricetin did not meet the criteria in 4 rules. The synthetic feasibility index ranged from 2.89 to 3.53. Toxicological characteristics showed that quercetin, myricetin, and epicatechin had potential hepatotoxicity risks, and catechins and epicatechins showed positive reactions in the AMES test. Notably, quercetin and luteolin had moderate cardiotoxicity risks, and none of the compounds showed tendencies of nephrotoxicity or neurotoxicity. In the acute toxicity assessment, the predicted median lethal doses of quercetin and myricetin were significantly lower than those of other compounds. See Table 1 for details. Finally, 5 flavonoids, namely daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol, were selected as the final candidates.

3.4. ROC curve evaluation of the predictive efficacy of flavonoid intake for OP

ROC curves for daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol were separately plotted (Fig. 3A) to evaluate the predictive efficacy of the intakes of the 5 selected flavonoids for OP. As shown in the results (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q218), the AUC values of each compound were all close to 0.5, indicating that when used alone, their ability to discriminate OP was similar to random guessing, and thus their clinical application value was limited. Therefore, a combined model incorporating multiple features needs to be constructed to enhance the discriminatory power.

(B) ROC curve of the model training set. (C) ROC curve of the model validation set. (D) ROC curve of the model test set. AUC = area under the curve, ROC = receiver operating characteristic.

3.5. Construction and evaluation of an OP prediction model based on XGBoost and flavonoid intake

In this study, daidzein, catechin, apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin were incorporated into the XGBoost model. The data were randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3. The training set was used to construct an OP prediction model, and the test set was used to evaluate the model’s performance. Five-fold cross-validation and grid search were employed for parameter tuning to enhance the model’s generalization ability and stability. The specific model effect evaluation is presented in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q218. The AUC of the training set was 0.951 (Fig. 3B), the AUC of the validation set was 0.5222 (Fig. 3C), and the AUC of the test set was 0.558 (Fig. 3D), with an accuracy of 0.8489. According to relevant judgment criteria, the model was considered to have been successfully fitted, indicating that the XGBoost model is suitable for the classification modeling task of this dataset. However, the overall AUC below 0.7 suggests that the model’s discriminative efficacy is limited, indicating that the current flavonoid combination has limited ability to predict and distinguish OP, which may be affected by severe sample imbalance or insufficient feature-disease association strength.

In a specific single prediction sample, positive contributions to the results are made by catechin and quercetin, while a negative contribution is made by apigenin. These results jointly reveal the relative importance and specific contributions of each compound in the model prediction, providing strong support for the interpretability and reliability of the model.

3.9. Molecular dynamics simulation

The AR with the strongest binding energy was selected to conduct molecular dynamics simulations with apigenin. As shown in Figure 7A, the complex system reached equilibrium after 40 ns and finally fluctuated around 1 Å. Therefore, a high stability was exhibited when AR bound to apigenin. The fluctuations of the complex during the movement were relatively stable, indicating that no significant expansion or contraction occurred in the AR-apigenin complex during the movement (Fig. 7B). The SASA between the target protein and the small molecule was calculated in this simulation (Fig. 7C). The results showed that there was no significant change in the SASA of the complex after the binding of the receptor and the ligand, suggesting that the binding of the ligand had little influence on the protein structure. The number of HBonds between the small molecule and the target protein during the kinetic process is shown in Figure 7D. The number of HBonds between the small molecule and the target protein ranged from 0 to 3, and in most cases, the complex had approximately 2 HBonds. This indicates that the ligand had a good hydrogen-bond interaction with the target protein. As shown in Figure 7E, the RMSF value of this complex was relatively low (mostly below 2 Å), so it had low flexibility and high stability. In summary, the complex system of AR and Apigenin was stably bound and had a good hydrogen-bond interaction, indicating a favorable binding between AR and apigenin.

4. Discussion

The NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey project conducted by the CDC in the United States. It is aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized population in the United States. Its rigorous multi-stage probability sampling design ensures a high degree of representativeness, allowing the research findings to be generalized to a broader US population. Data collection is carried out using standardized protocols (including face-to-face interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests) and is supervised by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, ensuring the reliability of the data and the rigor of the methods. By integrating detailed dietary flavonoid data from the FNDDS with the clinical data from NHANES, a unique and powerful platform can be provided for researching the relationship between diet and diseases. Therefore, it constitutes the core dataset of this study.

First, baseline data indicated that OP patients were characterized by advanced age, low BMI, and being predominantly female, which was consistent with the pathophysiological mechanisms of accelerated bone resorption due to age-related decline in bone density, estrogen deficiency, and racial genetic differences.[21] However, there was no statistically significant difference in the intake of each flavonoid between the 2 groups, suggesting that it is difficult to directly determine the association between flavonoids and OP from the perspective of general intake alone, and more precise analytical methods and models are needed to identify potential key compounds.

During the screening process of flavonoids, the differences in feature selection among different machine-learning algorithms reflected the multi-dimensional nature of the biological characteristics of the compounds. Flavonol components such as myricetin and quercetin identified by the Boruta algorithm, with their 3-OH and 4-carbonyl structures, could enhance hydrogen-bonding with bone morphogenetic protein receptors.[22,23] Peonidin, selected by XGBoost-RFE, might contribute to improving advanced glycation end products-mediated inflammation and diabetes-mediated cartilage degradation by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway.[24] Notably, although luteolin selected by the SVM-RFE method showed an effect of promoting bone formation in in vitro experiments,[25] its low predictive efficacy in the cohort might be due to differences in bioavailability caused by intestinal metabolism,[26] suggesting that future research should integrate metabolomics data to optimize algorithm weights.

ADMET property analysis reveals that the relatively high polarity of quercetin and myricetin may influence their distribution and action targets in the body. Differences in lipophilicity and hydrophilicity characteristics indicate that each compound varies in terms of biological membrane penetration, absorption, and dissolution state in body fluids, which can affect their pharmacokinetic behaviors. Pharmacokinetic evaluations show that all compounds exhibit high GI absorption properties, which are crucial for the bioavailability of oral drugs. Analysis of cytochrome P450 inhibition reveals that some compounds have inhibitory activities against key metabolic enzymes, which may lead to drug-drug interactions and affect the metabolism of themselves and other drugs. For example, quercetin inhibits CYP1A2, CYP3A4, and CYP2D6,[27] a fact that requires attention in clinical applications. Among the 11 flavonoids screened, only daidzein was found to exhibit BBB permeability. This characteristic may be associated with the brain-bone pathological axis.[28] For example, in Alzheimer disease, BBB dysfunction leads to the leakage of brain-derived vesicles carrying miR-483-5p (AD-B-EVs) into bone tissue. This process inhibits the expression of Igf2 in bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), driving an imbalance in osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation and directly causing OP.[29] This phenomenon not only explains the high incidence of bone loss in some Alzheimer disease patients but also highlights the potential of miR-483-5p as a therapeutic target for OP comorbidity. Drug-likeness evaluations suggest that most compounds conform to the basic principles of drug design. Analyses of synthetic feasibility indices and toxicological characteristics provide important safety references for subsequent drug development. For instance, quercetin and myricetin have potential hepatotoxicity risks,[30,31] catechin and epicatechin show positive AMES test results,[32] and quercetin and luteolin present moderate cardiotoxicity risks.[33] These findings suggest that in the development process, a balance between efficacy and safety should be struck, and in-depth toxicological studies should be conducted. Ultimately, daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol were selected as the final candidates, as they demonstrate good comprehensive pharmaceutical properties, with both certain biological activities and relative safety and feasibility.

However, ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC values of individual compounds were close to 0.5, indicating limited predictive efficacy as independent biomarkers and an inability to effectively distinguish OP risk. This reflects the multifactorial nature of OP, where relying solely on flavonoid intake is insufficient to capture the complexity of the disease. When constructing the XGBoost prediction model, after integrating 5 compounds, the AUC of the training set reached 0.951, while that of the test set was only 0.558, with an accuracy of 0.8489. Although this result confirms the model’s suitability for classification tasks, the overall discriminative efficacy was inadequate (AUC < 0.7), potentially associated with sample imbalance or weak feature-disease association strength. SHAP interpretation plots further revealed that kaempferol and catechin had the most significant and stable impacts on the prediction results, while the contributions of quercetin and apigenin fluctuated considerably. This emphasizes the importance of prioritizing highly stable compounds in the combined model to enhance reliability.

GO enrichment analysis indicates that the target genes play important roles in estrogen regulation, reproductive system development, and hormone signal transduction. KEGG enrichment analysis results show that the target genes play important roles in metabolic processes, hormone signal transduction, and disease occurrence and development, suggesting that they may serve as potential therapeutic targets or biomarkers. By intersecting the targets of OP and flavonoids, a PPI network was constructed to screen hub genes. Combined with the functional verification of the targets in bone metabolism from existing literature, core target genes such as STAT3, ESR1, CA2, AR, and ESR2 were screened out. These genes all play important roles in bone metabolism. For example, AR was reported to regulate the PPARγ/Wnt signaling axis by inhibiting MEK1 phosphorylation, down-regulating PPARγ expression, and promoting β-catenin nuclear accumulation. Thereby, it can achieve the dual effects of inhibiting adipogenic differentiation and promoting osteogenesis of BMSCs.[34] Estrogen was found to regulate bone cell function and affect bone formation and resorption through binding to 2 different receptors, estrogen receptor α (ESR1) and estrogen receptor β (ESR2).[35] Compared with ESR1, ESR2 was more widely distributed in bone-microenvironment-related tissues such as bone marrow and adipose tissue, and was closely related to the pathological process of post-menopausal OP.[36] In BMSCs, estrogen signaling mediated by ESR2 was shown to maintain cell pluripotency and delay the aging of osteoblast precursors.[37] CA2, an early characteristic of osteoclast differentiation, was found to play an important role in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption and was related to bone-differentiation-associated proteins.[38] A study by Wang et al demonstrated that the differentiation and formation of osteoclasts could be inhibited by suppressing CA2 expression and alleviating OP symptoms in OVX rats.[39] Activation of STAT3 was reported to improve bone malformation, while inhibition would exacerbate bone loss. STAT3 and MSX1 were shown to synergistically promote Dlx5 transcription and drive osteogenic differentiation, thus maintaining bone homeostasis.[40,41]

Subsequent molecular docking verified that 5 candidate compounds exhibited good binding efficacy with core target genes such as STAT3, ESR1, CA2, AR, and ESR2. The binding energies from molecular docking were generally lower than −7.0 kcal/mol, and in particular, the binding energy between AR and Apigenin was the strongest. Therefore, it was further confirmed through molecular dynamics simulations that the AR-apigenin complex reached a stable state after 40 ns, with the number of HBonds maintained at approximately 2 and the RMSF value below 2 Å, which supported its structural stability and potential therapeutic value. These findings provided molecular-level evidence for the anti-OP mechanism of flavonoids, suggesting that dietary intake of flavonoids could influence bone metabolism through a multi-component, multi-target regulatory network.

Although a variety of methods were employed in this study to screen flavonoids with potential therapeutic value and the underlying mechanisms of their prevention and treatment of OP were revealed, there were still some limitations in the research process. First, the 24-hour recall method adopted by the NHANES limited the accuracy of dietary exposure assessment. This method was difficult to capture long-term intake patterns and individual metabolic differences, which might weaken the association between flavonoids and OP. In the future, plasma flavonoid metabolite concentrations should be integrated as biomarkers to improve the accuracy of exposure assessment. Second, as OP cases accounted for only 5.4%, the serious imbalance of the sample might compromise the reliability of the traditional AUC index. This analytical limitation substantially weakens the generalizability of our model’s performance and suggests that any statements regarding the clinical translation or population-level predictive power of these flavonoid intake patterns for OP prevention should be made with extreme caution. The lack of sensitivity analysis (such as Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique oversampling or cost-sensitive learning) and robust indicators (such as balanced accuracy and macro F1 score) limited the generalizability of the model’s performance at the population level. Third, host factors influencing the bioavailability of flavonoids (including genetic heterogeneity and gut microbiota variation) were not incorporated. Future Mendelian randomization or metagenome-wide association studies are necessary to clarify gene-environment interactions. Finally, this study lacked in vitro cell experiments and in vivo models for validation. The causal relationship among flavonoid exposure, target regulation, and the pathogenesis of OP still awaits validation through in vitro and in vivo experiments before its prospects for clinical translation can be evaluated.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically elucidated the potential targets, molecular mechanisms, and the homology characteristics of medicine and food of dietary flavonoids in the prevention and treatment of OP by integrating NHANES, FNDDS, multiple machine-learning algorithms, ADMET evaluation, as well as molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation techniques. Based on machine-learning screening, daidzein, quercetin, catechin, apigenin, and kaempferol were first identified as key flavonoids. Their excellent ADMET properties of high oral bioavailability and low toxicity risk support their use as safe and effective bone protectors. Network pharmacology analysis revealed that these flavonoids exert effects of inhibiting bone resorption and promoting bone formation by targeting and regulating STAT3, ESR1, CA2, AR, and ESR2 genes. Molecular docking confirmed the good binding of the compounds to the target genes. Among them, the AR-apigenin complex with the highest binding energy was further verified for its structural stability and potential therapeutic value after molecular dynamics simulation. These findings highlight the unique advantages of flavonoids as substances with homology of medicine and food. Their dual nutritional-pharmacological properties will provide new approaches for the prevention and treatment of OP.
