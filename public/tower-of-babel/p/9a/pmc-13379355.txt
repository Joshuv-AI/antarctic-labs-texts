Introduction

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has emerged as a physiological pacing strategy that better preserves ventricular synchrony than traditional right ventricular pacing (RVP).1,2 By engaging the native conduction system, LBBAP is associated with narrower QRS duration and improved hemodynamic performance.3,4 Although generally safe, the transseptal lead approach carries procedure-specific risks,5, 6, 7 including septal injury, coronary trauma, and rare stress-related cardiomyopathy. Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) has been reported after pacemaker implantation,8 including LBBAP.9, 10, 11 Left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction is a recognized complication of TTS and may lead to significant hemodynamic instability if not promptly identified and appropriately managed.12,13 We report a case of TTS complicated by dynamic LVOT obstruction after uncomplicated de novo LBBAP implantation in an 81-year-old hypertensive woman with basal septal hypertrophy and a sigmoid septum. After initial clinical improvement, delayed hemodynamic deterioration occurred on day 5, unresponsive to fluid administration but improved with gradual uptitration of low-dose beta-blocker therapy. This case highlights the importance of recognizing predisposing anatomic substrates and underscores the need for early identification and targeted management of dynamic LVOT obstruction in patients who develop hemodynamic instability after conduction system pacing.

Case report

An 81-year-old woman with a history of complete right bundle branch block was admitted for evaluation of symptomatic second-degree 2:1 atrioventricular block, after 2 weeks of progressive fatigue (Figure 1). The patient had a medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and a previous ischemic stroke. Home medications did not include beta-blocker therapy. On admission, her blood pressure was 139/68 mm Hg and her heart rate was 46 beats/min; the physical examination was otherwise normal. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a sigmoid-shaped basal interventricular septum with mild septal hypertrophy (13.7 mm), normal transaortic gradients, and no LVOT obstruction. Regional wall motion and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were preserved (LVEF 60%). On day 2, the patient underwent an uncomplicated and successful LBBAP procedure with septal lead placement using a Biotronik Solia S 60 active-fixation lead. A dual-chamber pacemaker (Evity 8 DR-T; Biotronik, Berlin, Germany) was implanted, with a Biotronik Solia JT 53 passive-fixation atrial lead positioned in the right atrial appendage. The device was programmed in DDD mode (40–130 beats/min). Procedural sedation was achieved with low doses of morphine and midazolam, and the patient’s vital signs remained stable throughout the procedure. The lead was accurately positioned within the interventricular septum on the first attempt, with appropriate depth and orientation confirmed by contrast injection. Particular care was taken to avoid an excessively anterior position, thereby minimizing the risk of engaging septal branches of the left anterior descending artery. The patient reported no chest pain during or after the procedure. 2 hours after implantation, she developed progressively worsening dyspnea. On physical examination, she was tachypneic, with bilateral basal inspiratory crackles and a new grade 3/6 systolic ejection murmur at the left lower sternal border, not previously present. Her blood pressure was 145/60 mm Hg, heart rate 81 beats/min (regular), respiratory rate 29 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 86% on room air. The electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevations most prominent in leads V4–V6 (Figure 2), evident despite the underlying ventricular paced rhythm. Chest radiography showed pulmonary venous congestion (Figure 3). Bedside TTE revealed apical and para-apical hypokinesia with apical ballooning, associated with hyperkinesia of the basal segments, resulting in a severely reduced LVEF of 25% (Supplemental Videos 1 and 2). In addition, dynamic LVOT obstruction (peak gradient 39.7 mm Hg) was observed, accompanied by systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve and mild-to-moderate mitral regurgitation (Figure 4, Supplemental Videos 3 and 4). On laboratory investigations, B-type natriuretic peptide was 1527 pg/mL (normal <100), peak high-sensitivity troponin I 15,413 ng/L (normal 2.3–10.5), and hemoglobin 12.8 g/dL (normal 12–16). Device interrogation demonstrated stable electrical parameters. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and initially treated with intravenous diuretics and noninvasive ventilation, avoiding inotropes to prevent exacerbation of the LVOT obstruction. Coronary angiography was performed the same day and revealed normal coronary anatomy, consistent with a diagnosis of TTS (Figure 5, Supplemental Videos 5–7). On the next day (day 3), the ECG demonstrated complete resolution of the ST-segment elevations, accompanied by the development of diffuse T-wave inversions in the anterior leads (Figure 6). 2 days later (on day 5), the patient experienced acute hemodynamic deterioration, presenting with dyspnea, hypotension (80/50 mm Hg), and diaphoresis triggered by a Valsalva maneuver. Echocardiography at this time showed unchanged findings, particularly the LVOT gradient, hypokinetic apical ballooning associated with hyperkinesia of the basal segments, and the severely reduced LVEF. Intravenous fluid resuscitation was attempted; however, hypotension persisted despite a 500-mL fluid challenge (85/54 mm Hg). Therefore, low-dose beta-blocker therapy was initiated and gradually uptitrated, resulting in reduction of basal hypercontractility and the intraventricular gradient, with subsequent hemodynamic stabilization and no progression to overt cardiogenic shock. Mechanical circulatory support or mechanical ventilation were not required. Over the subsequent days, brief self-terminating atrial flutter episodes occurred, appropriately managed by automatic mode switch (DDIR). Oral anticoagulation with rivaroxaban (15 mg once daily) and oral amiodarone were initiated. A transient worsening of renal function occurred (creatinine increased from 1.3 mg/dL to 1.9 mg/dL), which resolved after reduction of diuretic therapy and discontinuation of ramipril. The patient was successfully weaned off supplemental oxygen and discharged in stable condition on beta-blocker therapy (bisoprolol 2.5 mg twice daily). At discharge (day 13), TTE showed near-complete recovery of left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 49%) and resolution of the LVOT obstruction. The ECG demonstrated less pronounced T-wave inversions in the precordial leads (Figure 7), along with marked improvement in paced QRS morphology, and a significant reduction in peak left ventricular activation time. These electrical changes likely reflect the resolution of TTS and normalization of left ventricular dimensions after recovery from acute dilation. At 1-month follow-up, TTE confirmed complete recovery (LVEF 60%) of left ventricular systolic function (Supplemental Videos 8 and 9). The ECG, performed with the output configuration switched to unipolar for optimal recording, demonstrated complete normalization of ventricular depolarization during paced rhythm (Figure 8).

Discussion

LBBAP has emerged as a novel pacing strategy that offers significant advantages over conventional RVP, including a more physiological ventricular activation pattern, narrower QRS duration, and improved clinical outcomes—such as reduced rates of heart failure hospitalizations and overall mortality.1, 2, 3, 4 Despite a favorable safety profile, the transseptal lead implantation technique carries procedure-specific risks,5 including septal injury, septal hematoma, coronary artery trauma, and rare acute coronary events.6 These events are relatively rare (approximately 5% of cases) and typically do not cause persistent structural or functional compromise of the interventricular septum.7 An even rarer complication of LBBAP is TTS. Although infrequently reported after RVP—with a prevalence of 0.5% in a large single-center retrospective analysis8—3 cases have more recently been documented after LBBAP.9, 10, 11 Although TTS is typically triggered by emotional or physiological stress, these reports suggest that it may also occur after uncomplicated pacemaker implantation. Thaitirarot et al10 described a case of TTS induced by de novo LBBAP in which symptom onset occurred approximately 2 hours after implantation, and the procedure itself was considered the only identifiable stressor. In our patient, symptom onset occurred within a similar timeframe. Stable intraprocedural vital signs and minimal sedation further support the hypothesis that the procedure itself—particularly lead placement—may have precipitated TTS. LVOT obstruction is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication of TTS, reported in approximately 7%–25% of cases,12,13 but, to the best of our knowledge, it has not been reported after traditional pacemaker implantation.8 Notably, only 1 previous case of TTS complicated by LVOT obstruction after LBBAP has been reported.11 In that report described by Scuotto et al,11 a 93-year-old man developed severe TTS with dynamic LVOT obstruction (intraventricular pressure gradient 83 mm Hg) after surgical extraction under general anesthesia of a 14-year-old RVP lead and implantation of a new LBBAP lead. Symptom onset occurred immediately after withdrawal of general anesthesia, and LVOT obstruction was initially managed conservatively without beta-blocker therapy. However, hemodynamic deterioration progressed to overt cardiogenic shock requiring intra-aortic balloon pump support and prolonged mechanical ventilation; the patient ultimately died of septic shock secondary to ventilator-associated pneumonia after 5 weeks. In contrast, our patient underwent uncomplicated de novo LBBAP implantation under minimal sedation (subcutaneous lidocaine with low-dose intravenous midazolam and morphine), presented with a lower LVOT gradient (39.7 mm Hg), and achieved complete hemodynamic stabilization with beta-blocker therapy alone, without requiring mechanical circulatory or ventilatory support. This case underscores several important clinical considerations. First, TTS with dynamic LVOT obstruction after LBBAP is rare but potentially life-threatening. Dynamic LVOT obstruction should be systematically assessed in patients who develop acute heart failure or hypotension after conduction system pacing. The presence of a new systolic murmur may provide an early bedside clue. Close collaboration between electrophysiologists and clinicians involved in postimplantation care is essential, particularly because symptom onset may occur immediately after implantation, with most cases developing within the first 24 hours.8 Second, management differs fundamentally from other forms of cardiogenic shock.13 Careful hemodynamic assessment is required when balancing fluid administration and diuretic therapy, given that excessive preload reduction may worsen the intraventricular gradient and mitral regurgitation. Inotropic agents and afterload-reducing mechanical support such as intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation should be avoided, given that they may exacerbate LVOT obstruction by increasing basal hypercontractility or reducing afterload. Conversely, beta1-selective beta-blockers reduce basal contractility, decrease the intraventricular gradient, and improve ventricular filling, facilitating hemodynamic stabilization even in hypotensive patients. When mechanical support is required, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation may be preferable to intra-aortic balloon pump counterpulsation, as it provides circulatory support while increasing afterload. The Impella® device may also be considered, given that it maintains systemic perfusion by directly unloading the left ventricle and can mechanically interfere with systolic anterior motion, thereby potentially reducing the LVOT gradient. Third, preexisting anatomic substrate seems relevant. Our patient had hypertension, basal septal hypertrophy (13.7 mm), and a sigmoid septum—features not described in previously reported LBBAP-related TTS cases.9, 10, 11 Because LBBAP preserves myocardial work performance more effectively than traditional RVP1,2 and initiates septal activation in a physiological left-to-right sequence, it may, in the presence of hypertrophic anatomy, accentuate basal hypercontractility during stress-induced apical dysfunction, thereby contributing to or exacerbating dynamic LVOT obstruction. Although causality cannot be established from a single case, careful preprocedural echocardiographic assessment of septal morphology may help identify patients at increased risk. Finally, the delayed deterioration on day 5 emphasizes the need for continued hemodynamic vigilance even after apparent stabilization. Notably, LVOT obstruction was already evident on the initial TTE, suggesting that earlier initiation of beta-blocker therapy might have facilitated more rapid stabilization and potentially prevented subsequent decompensation.

Conclusion

TTS complicated by dynamic LVOT obstruction is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of de novo LBBAP implantation. In patients with basal septal hypertrophy, LBBAP may not only act as a trigger for stress cardiomyopathy but may also accentuate basal hypercontractility, thereby increasing susceptibility to dynamic LVOT obstruction. Early recognition of this hemodynamic pattern and prompt initiation of targeted therapy are essential when clinical instability develops after conduction system pacing.
