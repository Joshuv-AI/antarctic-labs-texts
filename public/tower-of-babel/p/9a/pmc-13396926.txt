Effects of supercooling, sub-freezing, and deep-freezing pre-cooling on freshness, flavor, water-holding capacity, and microstructure of beef during EPS-based distribution
Authors: Yuqing Tang, Baide Mu, Depeng Sun, Qian Li, Jinghe Zhang, Hongmei Li, Tingyu Li, Chunxiang Piao, Guanhao Li, Juan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined how pre-cooling temperature affects beef quality and temperature fluctuations during expanded polystyrene (EPS)-based distribution, simulated as 48 h at 25 °C followed by chilled storage at 4 °C. Beef steaks were pre-cooled to −0.5 °C (supercooling), −5 °C (sub-freezing), or −18 °C (deep-freezing) and compared with a 4 °C refrigerated control. Quality was evaluated by freshness indicators (total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), total viable count (TVC)), water-holding capacity, flavor (electronic tongue, electronic nose, gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry), and microstructure. Pre-cooling temperature markedly affected thermal behavior, temperature fluctuation, and quality changes during EPS-based distribution. Deep-freezing (−18 °C) was associated with the largest temperature rise (∼16 °C) and more pronounced indicators of freeze–thaw damage. Sub-freezing (−5 °C) resulted in microstructural features indicative of freezing-related structural disruption and greater drip loss. Supercooling (−0.5 °C) showed the smallest temperature increase (∼4 °C), better preserved microstructure, and maintained relatively higher freshness, flavor, and water-holding capacity. At the end of the 48 h distribution simulation, all pre-cooled groups remained below 15 mg/100 g TVB-N, while the −18 °C group had the highest TBARS and purge loss. During subsequent chilled storage, the −0.5 °C group reached the spoilage limit (20 mg/100 g TVB-N) after 96 h, compared with 72 h for the −5 °C and −18 °C groups. Overall, supercooling at −0.5 °C provided the best beef quality preservation during short-term EPS-based distribution, highlighting the importance of appropriate pre-cooling state selection for e-commerce logistics.Keywords: Pre-cooling, EPS-based distribution, Temperature fluctuation, Microstructure, Beef quality

Introduction

In recent years, with the rise of large e-commerce platforms, platform-based e-commerce has transformed traditional fresh produce sales through its convenience and accessibility, driving rapid growth in the fresh food e-commerce industry (Xu, Wang, & Cao, 2023). In China, major producing regions such as the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region handle large volumes of beef and mutton shipments annually, highlighting the scale of fresh meat logistics. Beef is a key category of fresh produce, rich in nutrients like amino acids and protein. However, because of its composition, it is highly susceptible to spoilage caused by enzymes, oxidation, and microorganisms, requiring strict processing and storage standards (Nychas, Skandamis, Tassou, & Koutsoumanis, 2008).

To accommodate small individual orders and reduce costs in e-commerce logistics, the expanded polystyrene (EPS) box distribution model, which utilizes “EPS box + ice packs,” is widely employed, typically with distribution times of 24–48 h (J. Guo et al., 2025). This approach has intrinsic flaws: it's difficult to maintain stable temperatures inside EPS boxes, and ice packs melt easily. Therefore, managing the distribution environment in e-commerce and limiting temperature shifts have become essential challenges to maintaining the quality of fresh beef.

In e-commerce logistics, pre-cooling treatment before distribution is a vital technology. By lowering the product's temperature in advance, a more stable thermal environment in EPS box is created for the subsequent EPS box delivery, effectively buffering against unavoidable temperature fluctuations during transit. Existing studies have confirmed the effectiveness of pre-cooling treatment. In Navaranjan's study, pre-cooling Tarakihi (a kind of fish) to −1 °C before packaging and simulating air transport resulted in a 1.4 °C lower peak body temperature compared to the non-precooled group, significantly delaying quality deterioration (Navaranjan, Fletcher, Summers, Parr, & Anderson, 2013). Similarly, Guo's research (J. Guo et al., 2025) found that pre-cooling lychees from 15 °C to 5 °C slowed quality loss over 48 h at ambient temperature, and it reduced browning index by 0.39 points, fungal load by 0.66 log (CFU/g), and slowed solids content decline by 23%. Pork pretreated to 0 ± 2 °C and placed in an EPS box with ice packs on all four sides can keep the pork temperature below 0 °C for 24 h, reducing temperature fluctuations during distribution and significantly delaying microbial growth and quality decline compared to that with ice packs on one side (Kim et al., 2025).

In the fresh beef sector, while this low-temperature pre-cooling technology (typically pre-cooling to −18 °C) has been practically applied in e-commerce “EPS box + ice pack” delivery models, comprehensive research on how the initial low-temperature pre-treatment influences quality stability during subsequent distribution, particularly through its effect on beef's freezing characteristics, such as ice crystal formation, is still lacking. The different temperature states that beef undergoes during pre-cooling, such as “supercooling,” “sub-freezing,” and “deep-freezing,” can be defined by its freezing process. Kiani and Sun (Kiani & Sun, 2011) divided this process into three characteristic temperature points: from cooling to the freezing point, to the phase transition stage where ice crystals form, and finally to complete freezing. These three characteristic temperature points systematically capture the changes in myofibrillar lattice structure and water content as they occur from ice crystal nucleation and phase transition to complete freezing (Qiu et al., 2025). Microstructural analyses conducted by Lin and Xie have shown that ice crystal formation at low temperatures compromises the structural integrity of beef, resulting in quality deterioration (Lin et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2023).

Previous studies have mainly focused on the effects of conventional freezing or supercooling on meat quality during storage (Chen et al., 2022; Lagerstedt, Enfält, Johansson, & Lundström, 2008; Leygonie, Britz, & Hoffman, 2012). Some work has examined supercooling or slurry ice for improving chilling efficiency and quality (Cao et al., 2022), and the influence of repeated freeze–thaw cycles has been investigated (Cheng et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022). However, most of these studies were conducted under controlled storage conditions and did not explicitly consider the EPS box + ice pack distribution environment with significant temperature fluctuations. In particular, there is limited information on how different pre-cooling endpoints corresponding to distinct freezing stages (supercooling, sub-freezing, and deep-freezing) affect ice crystal behavior, microstructural integrity and subsequent quality changes during simulated e-commerce distribution. Yet, no previous study has directly examined beef pre-cooling endpoints located at different stages of the freezing curve under a realistic EPS-box distribution scenario.

Therefore, we hypothesized that (i) different pre-cooling endpoints along the freezing curve would lead to distinct freezing-related structural damage, and (ii) these differences, together with temperature fluctuations during EPS-based distribution, would translate into measurable variations in freshness, flavor, water-holding capacity and microstructure. To test this hypothesis, three characteristic temperatures representing distinct freezing stages were selected: −0.5 °C (supercooling), −5 °C (sub-freezing) and −18 °C (deep-freezing). These were systematically compared with a 4 °C refrigerated control to evaluate their combined effects on beef quality under a simulated 48-h “EPS box + ice pack” distribution at 25 °C.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Three carcasses from 36-month-old male cattle (same ranch and slaughter batch; Mule Co., Ltd., Yanji, China) were used as biological replicates (n = 3). From each carcass, 32 adjacent rump steaks (250 ± 20 g, 3 cm thick) were cut and vacuum-packed with a blood-absorbing pad, randomly assigned to four treatment groups: CK (4 °C, non-frozen control), −0.5 °C (supercooling), −5 °C (sub-freezing), and −18 °C (deep-freezing). Steaks in the CK group were stored directly at 4 °C. Steaks in the −0.5, −5 and −18 °C groups were placed in a −30 °C freezer until their core temperatures reached the respective targets (−0.5, −5 or −18 °C), monitored in real time by a K-type thermocouple (TA612A, TASI, China) inserted into the geometric centre of a reference steak. When the target core temperature was reached, the corresponding steaks from each carcass were removed to EPS insulated boxes.

For each treatment, three EPS insulated boxes (30 cm × 25 cm × 20 cm; one per carcass) were prepared. Each box contained eight designated steaks from that carcass and six gel ice packs (500 ± 10 g, pre-frozen at −20 °C for >48 h) (in an insulating thermal bag), arranged in alternating layers and supplemented with filler material to fully occupy the internal space. The box was placed into a corrugated cardboard carton and held in a constant-temperature chamber at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C for 48 h to simulate short-term distribution. Core temperature in each box was recorded every 5 min. Sampling was conducted at three stages: (i) fresh (before any cooling), (ii) freezing stage (FS, immediately after reaching the target core temperature) and (iii) delivery (DA, after 48 h of simulated distribution).

Methods

Reagents

Analytical-grade reagents were used unless otherwise stated. General chemical reagents, including trichloroacetic acid and common solvents, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Paraformaldehyde fixative and haematoxylin-eosin staining kits were obtained from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Plate Count Agar and saline used for microbiological analysis were supplied by Hopebio (Qingdao, China). Ultrapure water was used throughout all experiments.

Beef freezing point determination

The beef freezing process was measured with a K-type thermocouple thermometer (TA612A, TASI, China). The probe was inserted into the center of the sample, which was placed in a −30 °C freezer. Temperature was recorded every 5 min to plot a freezing curve, and the phase-transition plateau was used to approximate the characteristic freezing point.

Flavor analysis

Electronic tongue (E-tongue) analysis

Taste characteristics were measured using an electronic tongue system (SA-402B, Insent, Japan). Approximately 3 g of meat was mixed with 60 mL of distilled water, homogenized, and then centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was filtered. The standard measurement program (including a two-step wash) was used to determine five basic tastes: umami, saltiness, sourness, bitterness, and astringency. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate; within each replicate, four parallel sensor readings were collected from the same extract and averaged as technical replicates for statistical analysis.

Electronic-nose (E-nose) analysis

For electronic nose analysis (PEN3, Airsense, Germany), five grams of minced meat were weighed into a 20 mL vial, sealed, equilibrated for 15 min, and then incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. Detection parameters were as follows: purge 120 s, pre-sampling 5 s, detection 60 s, and carrier gas flow 200 mL/min. The device comprised ten metal oxide sensors: W1C, W5S, W3C, W6S, W5C, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, and W3S (see Supplementary Material Table S1). Three independent samples were prepared and analyzed per treatment group. For each sample, three parallel measurements were performed, and the resulting sensor signals were averaged to obtain a single value for statistical analysis.

Gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS)

A GC-IMS system (FlavourSpec, Haineng Instrument Co., Shandong, China) analyzed volatile organic compounds in samples. Two grams of minced meat were placed in a 20 mL vial, heated at 60 °C for 20 min with shaking at 500 rpm. The headspace injection needle, at 85 °C, injected 1 mL in splitless mode. The IMS device was kept at 45 °C. The drift gas flow was 150 mL/min using 99.999% pure nitrogen, with flow rates of 2 mL/min (0–2 min), 2 mL/min (2–10 min), and 10 mL/min (10–20 min). Qualitative analysis used the NIST and IMS databases with software version 0.4.10. Visualizations like difference maps and fingerprint plots were generated using plugins in the Laboratory Analysis Viewer software. Compounds with PLS-DA VIP > 1 were initially screened as potentially discriminant variables and were further examined by one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) to confirm significant differences between treatments.

Microstructure

For light microscopy, beef samples of approximately 1 cm3 were excised parallel to the muscle fibers and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; G1101) for 24 h at 4 °C. Tissues were then processed according to the manufacturer's standard paraffin-embedding procedure. Briefly, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin using an automatic tissue processor and embedding centre. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4–6 μm with a rotary microtome (RM2016, Leica, Shanghai, China) and mounted on adhesive slides (G6012–1, Servicebio). Sections were dried and stained with a commercial haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) kit (G1076, Servicebio) following the supplier's protocol: dewaxing with Environmental Friendly Dewaxing Transparent Liquid I and II (20 min each), rehydration through graded ethanol to 75% ethanol, haematoxylin staining for 3–5 min, differentiation and bluing, rinsing in tap water, 95% ethanol for 1 min, and eosin staining for 15 s. Finally, sections were dehydrated in absolute ethanol, cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral balsam. Stained sections were examined with an upright optical microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE E100, Nikon, Japan) equipped with a digital imaging system (NIKON DS-U3), and representative cross-sections were captured for qualitative morphological analysis.

Beef samples were cut into 5 mm3 cubes and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS at 4 °C for 2 days. Wash with PBS, dehydrate through ethanol (50% to 100%), then substitute with isopropanol for 1 h. Freeze-dry at −40 °C, gold coat, and observe with SEM (ZEISS EVO 18, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Data analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates, each measured in triplicate). One-way ANOVA was performed at each sampling stage, and Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was applied when significant effects were detected (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Pre-cooling strategy and temperature fluctuation assessment based on freezing curve

Fig. 1A shows the temperature curve during rapid freezing of fresh beef at −30 °C, with three characteristic temperature points: −0.5 °C (supercooling temperature), which initiates the ice crystal formation plateau. In this study, −0.5 °C was operationally referred to as “supercooling” because it represents the onset of the phase-transition plateau before the bulk of latent heat is released, although it is close to the initial freezing point rather than a deep metastable state; −5 °C (sub-freezing temperature), at which ice crystal formation is essentially complete; and − 18 °C, the deep-freezing temperature. These three characteristic temperatures were used as pre-cooling temperatures in this study. At the FS stage, the final core temperatures of the pre-cooled samples were −0.5 °C, −5 °C and −18 °C for the supercooling, sub-freezing and deep-freezing groups, respectively.

(A) Freezing curve of beef in a −30 °C freezer. In the pre-cooling treatments, steaks were removed at core temperatures of −0.5, −5, or −18 °C; (B) Changes in temperature profiles of beef after different pre-cooling treatments combined with EPS box delivery.

Beef samples with initial core temperatures of −0.5 °C, −5 °C, and −18 °C were packed in an EPS box with gel ice packs and exposed to 25 °C to simulate distribution for 48 h. As shown in Fig. 1B, the groups exhibited distinct thermal behaviors: the −18 °C group (PC-T-18°C) showed the largest temperature increase, reaching 16.3 °C, with a rapid initial rise followed by a gradual slowdown and a renewed acceleration toward the end, attaining a final temperature of −1.7 °C; the −5 °C group (PC-T-5°C) experienced a moderate temperature rise of 4.3 °C, also characterized by a rapid early increase that gradually slowed, finishing at −0.7 °C; the −0.5 °C group (PC-T-0.5°C) displayed the smallest temperature increase of 3.8 °C, remaining stable initially before rising almost linearly in the later stage, reaching a final temperature of 3.3 °C. In Fig. 1B, the −0.5 °C group showed a clear temperature stagnation phase in the first 6 h, with temperature staying near −0.5 °C due to thermal equilibrium. Later, as the temperature difference increased, warming accelerated. The −5 °C and −18 °C groups, with larger initial differences, warmed continuously without stagnation. The initial treatment temperature exerted a strong influence on the thermal behavior of beef during distribution, including the temperature fluctuation range, temperature fluctuation pattern, and final temperature. Supercooling better maintained beef temperature during short-term distribution, with the final temperature approximately 4 °C.

Quality deterioration indices

TVB-N refers to the organic amines and nitrogen-containing compounds generated by the decomposition of animal proteins. It is thus widely used as a key indicator for evaluating freshness. Generally, 15 mg/100 g is used as the threshold to distinguish fresh meat from sub fresh meat, and 20 mg/100 g is regarded as the criterion indicating the beginning of spoilage (Huang et al., 2019). The results of this study (Fig. 2A) show that under different pre-cooling conditions, the TVB-N value of beef exhibited a significant upward trend throughout the entire storage period (p < 0.05). The initial TVB-N value of fresh beef was 2.48 mg/100 g. The TVB-N values of all pre-cooling treatment groups were higher than those of the fresh beef (p < 0.05). This corroborates the findings of Chen (X. Chen et al., 2022), who reported that beef pre-cooled to 0 °C and −3 °C exhibited higher TVB-N values than fresh meat. After 48 h of simulated distribution, the TVB-N levels consistently increased, but all of the experimental groups remained below 15 mg/100 g, indicating that the beef retained freshness upon delivery, the TVB-N values of all pre-cooling groups were higher than that of the control group (CK), among which the PC-T-18°C group had the highest value and the PC-T-5°C group had the lowest among the pre-cooled groups (4.3 mg/100 g), although this was still about 73% higher than the fresh beef value (2.48 mg/100 g). During distribution, the PC-T-5°C group underwent a small temperature fluctuation (4.3 °C) and remained in a near-supercooled state for most of the time; existing studies have shown that supercooling helps maintain meat freshness (Cao et al., 2022). Despite the differences, the TVB-N values of all groups at delivery were still significantly below 15 mg/100 g, confirming their fresh state. During the subsequent storage at 4 °C, the patterns of TVB-N change for different pre-cooling treatments diverged. The PC-T-5°C and PC-T-0.5°C groups reached the 15 mg/100 g threshold almost simultaneously, but the former rose at a faster rate and reached the spoilage limit of 20 mg/100 g about one day earlier; moreover, during the same storage period, the TVB-N values of these two groups remained lower than those of the PC-T-18°C group. Although the PC-T-5°C group had a slightly lower TVB-N value than the PC-T-0.5°C group at the end of distribution, the PC-T-0.5°C group showed a slower subsequent increase during chilled storage and therefore achieved a longer overall shelf life.The PC-T-18°C and PC-T-5°C groups exceeded 20 mg/100 g after 72 h of storage, the shelf life of the PC-T-0.5°C group was prolonged by about one day compared with the other two, while the CK group reached this limit only after 120 h of storage. In summary, compared with short-term distribution, storage at constant 4 °C best preserved freshness; among the pre-cooling groups, upon delivery, all groups maintained freshness; however the PC-T-0.5°C group had the longest shelf life, which was 96 h.

Lowercase letters a-g indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) between different treatment methods at the same sampling point; capital letters A-G indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) for the same treatment method across different sampling points (n = 3).

TBARS values, commonly used to measure secondary oxidation products such as malondialdehyde, serve as a key indicator of food oxidation (Tikk, Haugen, Andersen, & Aaslyng, 2008). In this experiment, as shown in Fig. 2B, TBARS values significantly increased over time (p < 0.05) across all treatment groups, indicating ongoing lipid oxidation. The accumulation of peroxides during storage and the free radicals generated by their decomposition further promoted the continuous oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (Ghani et al., 2017). Different pre-cooling treatments significantly influenced the TBARS changes. Compared with the fresh group, pre-cooling at different temperatures caused immediate increases in TBARS values, and the lower the treatment temperature, the higher the TBARS value. At the end of distribution, the TBARS value of the −18 °C group remained the highest, and the TBARS values of the −0.5 °C and −5 °C groups did not differ significantly, indicating that these two groups delayed the increase in TBARS values compared with the −18 °C group. During the subsequent refrigerated storage, TBARS values continued to rise in all groups, and the −5 °C group showed a faster increase than the −0.5 °C group in the later storage period.

TVC is a key indicator for assessing the hygiene status of beef and predicting spoilage risk. As shown in Fig. 2C, the TVC value of the CK group continuously increased throughout storage and was higher than that of the pre-cooling groups. This is expected because the CK group remained at 4 °C without any prior lethal or sub-lethal temperature reduction, whereas pre-cooled samples experienced lower temperatures that suppressed the initial microbial load. All low-temperature pretreatment groups significantly inhibited initial microbial growth. Among these, the PC-T-0.5°C group maintained relatively stable TVC values, while the PC-T-5°C and PC-T-18°C treatment groups exhibited more pronounced inhibitory effects on microbial proliferation. Upon delivery, the TVC of the PC-T-5°C and PC-T-18°C groups remained at relatively low levels of 4.09 ± 0.09 lg CFU/g and 4.15 ± 0.21 lg CFU/g, respectively. This demonstrates that low-temperature pretreatment effectively suppresses microbial growth, sustaining stable microbial control even during the subsequent distribution. Low-Temperature treatment could reduce microbial proliferation in meat products (X. Zhang, Na, Zhang, & Yang, 2025), even under temperature fluctuations during logistics in this study.

Studies have shown that microbial secretion of proteases, which accelerates amino acid degradation, is one of the major pathways contributing to TVB-N accumulation (Bekhit, Holman, Giteru, & Hopkins, 2021). In parallel, reactive oxygen species and free radicals generated during lipid oxidation can promote protein oxidation and breakdown, further elevating TVB-N levels (Geng, Liu, & Zhang, 2023). Thus, increases in TVC can enhance protein decomposition directly through microbial metabolism and may also generate pro-oxidative metabolites that intensify lipid oxidation and indirectly influence TBARS. Accordingly, TVB-N, TBARS and TVC are interconnected indicators that collectively reflect beef quality deterioration. In this study, although pre-cooling suppressed microbial growth, the TVB-N and TBARS values of the pre-cooled groups remained consistently higher than those of the CK group during pre-cooling, delivery and subsequent storage, and continued to rise (Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, TVB-N and TBARS exhibited similar changing patterns and correlations across treatments (e.g., r > 0.89; Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating comparable trends over time. These results suggest that physicochemical factors—such as oxidative stress and freeze–thaw-related structural injury—likely played an important role alongside microbial activity, although our data do not allow the relative contributions of these pathways to be rigorously partitioned.

Flavor analysis

In this study, electronic tongue, electronic nose, and GC-IMS were employed in combination to analyze flavor variations in beef before and after distribution.

The E-tongue simulates the human tongue to analyze, identify, and evaluate tested samples (Fig. 3). Fig. 3A displays the E-tongue radar charts for beef samples subjected to different low-temperature treatments and distribution processes. Pre-cooling treatment exhibits pronounced sourness, bitterness, and astringency, with astringency being most prominent. Its richness is significantly lower than fresh samples. The −0.5 °C group maintains stable flavor profiles before and after treatment, closely resembling fresh beef. After freezing, beef's sourness and astringency increase to the highest level. Fig. 3B presents the PCA plot showing the overall relationships among beef stages. PC1 and PC2 collectively accounted for 86.7% of total variance, exceeding 85%, with PC1 and PC2 contributing 63.0% and 23.7%, respectively. This indicates PC1 and PC2 explain the majority of beef flavor profiles, effectively illustrating taste differences across stages. The −18 °C treatment groups are positioned on opposite sides of the PCA score plot, visualising clear separation between them. The short-term distribution of beef significantly affects its overall flavor profile and pre-cooling at −18 °C is the least favorable treatment for preserving taste. PCA was used only to visualise overall taste differences and does not by itself imply statistical significance.

(A) Electronic tongue radar map of beef; (B) PCA of the E-tongue of beef after different pre-cooling treatments combined with EPS box delivery.

The E-nose can identify sample odors, with subtle volatile compound variations potentially causing sensor response differences (He et al., 2025). Data were collected via sample detection, and PCA results from 118 to 120 s were selected to show inter-sample differences (Fig. 4). The figure shows significant signal intensity changes in W2W, W2S, W1W, W1S, and W5S sensors (response values >1.0), indicating altered beef odor profiles after freezing and delivery. These changes mainly stemmed from shifts in substances related to lipid oxidation, such as alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, and from substances derived from protein degradation, such as sulfides and nitrogen oxides. Sensors sensitive to these compounds, including W2S for alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, W1W and W2W for sulfides and W5S fornitrogen oxides, showed strong responses. In contrast, other sensors had minor signal variations, implying smaller impacts of their target substances on overall odor. Combined with the types of sensitive compounds detected by the sensors, this suggests that characteristic flavors during beef freezing and distribution may be attributed to compounds such as sulfides, nitrogen oxides, alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones. Conversely, no significant differences were observed in ammonia content, certain aromatics, hydrides, or long-chain alkanes. Fig. 4B shows that the first PC accounts for 75.3% and the second PC for 23.4%, collectively explaining 98.7% of the total variance (exceeding 95%). This indicates PC1 and PC2 capture most of the original information in the samples. The high proportion of variance explained by PC1 and PC2 reflects the overall similarity of raw beef odor profiles, and in this context PCA was used only as a qualitative visualization tool to display relative differences among treatments; no cross-validated predictive model was constructed. The scatter plot reveals a clear separation of samples across different processing stages, demonstrating the E-nose's effective differentiation of beef odors at these stages. Results indicate that during the FS, beef odors were broadly similar among treatments at FS, but all differed from fresh beef. At the DA stage, the −18 °C-DA group difference peaked, characterized by significantly enhanced sulfide, nitrogen oxides, alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones. The −0.5 °C treatment group exhibited the most stable odor changes before and after distribution, with its performance at the DA stage closely resembling that of the control group (CK). Deeper pre-cooling (i.e., −5 °C and −18 °C) caused greater deviation from the control group upon delivery, whereas the −0.5 °C treatment group remained the closest to CK in odor profile.

This study employed headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) to identify volatile compounds in beef during distribution. Results revealed 34 volatile compounds identified across beef samples from both stages, including 8 alcohols, 8 esters, 5 aldehydes, 4 ketones, 3 alkenes, and 6 heterocyclic compounds (Fig. 5). Aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones are primary products of beef fat oxidation, with aldehydes being the most prevalent (T. Zhang et al., 2025). Due to their high concentrations and low odor detection thresholds, aldehydes significantly contribute to meat aroma, imparting grassy, floral, and fatty notes. However, excessively high concentrations produce pungent rancid odors that disrupt flavor harmony (Chen et al., 2024). Thus, aldehydes serve as key indicators of odor changes in stored beef. Alcohols primarily originate from the oxidative decomposition and reduction of fatty acids and possess intense aromas that enhance overall meatiness (Domínguez et al., 2019). Ketones impart butter-like and spice-like aromas, while esters primarily contribute floral and fruity notes, each exerting unique effects on beef flavor(Wang et al., 2025).

Fig. 5A and B display two-dimensional and three-dimensional topological spectra, respectively, while Fig. 5C presents a differential spectrum obtained through dimensionality reduction. The Y-axis represents retention time, the X-axis denotes relative drift time, and the Z-axis (or color intensity) indicates signal strength. The brightness of the peak color reflects the concentration level of that compound.

By analyzing the overall flavor fingerprint in Fig. 5C, significant changes in the characteristic volatile flavor compounds of beef during distribution can be further confirmed. Further analysis using Variable Importance Projection (VIP) identified 13 volatile compounds (not considering ethyl acrylate) with VIP values >1, which were used to better characterize flavor changes in frozen beef during distribution, as shown in Fig. 5D.Compared to fresh samples, volatile compounds associated with fatty aromas generally decreased as freezing temperatures dropped. This indicates that freezing weakens the overall flavor profile of beef, and the extent of flavor weakening becomes more pronounced with decreasing freezing temperature. Similar phenomena have been reported in studies on frozen lamb (Bueno et al., 2011). During distribution, temperature fluctuations furtherly altered the flavor profile, and the −18 °C treatment group showed an overall increase in lipid‑oxidation‑related substances. However, whether after pre-cooling and during delivery, the −0.5 °C treatment group showed the most stable odor changes and its odor profile at delivery (DA) was closest to that of the control (CK), as a similarity in flavor change relative to fresh samples. This result is consistent with the findings obtained by E-tongue and E-nose.

Further investigation into the relationship between TBARS and these 13 volatile flavor compounds revealed positive correlations with several flavor compounds, among them, 2‑formylpyrrole was significantly positively correlated with TBARS (r = 0.714, p < 0.05) (see the supplementary materials Fig. S2). These findings suggest that lipid oxidation is likely a major biochemical pathway contributing to flavor deterioration and freshness loss in this study, which is consistent with the findings of Guo (S. Guo et al., 2026). During post-mortem storage, phospholipids in cell membranes undergo enzymatic hydrolysis, releasing large amounts of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Duan et al., 2024). These fatty acids are subsequently oxidized. On one hand, this oxidation generates small-molecule volatile compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, which contribute to flavor formation. On the other hand, the accumulation of MDA is directly reflected in the increase of TBARS value (Yu et al., 2025). Therefore, the strong correlation between TBARS and these flavor compounds essentially represents different expressions of the same oxidation process. Furthermore, reactive aldehydes produced from lipid oxidation can promote protein oxidation and degradation, thereby increasing the TVB-N value. More importantly, these aldehydes, acting as substrates, can further participate in the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation with free amino acids, generating heterocyclic compounds (Zhou et al., 2024). This explains well why 2-formylpyrrole, together with TVB-N showed positive correlations with TBARS in this study.

Taken together, the E-tongue, E-nose and GC-IMS results consistently indicated that pre-cooling at −0.5 °C best preserved a flavor profile closest to fresh and continuously refrigerated beef, whereas −5 and −18 °C pre-cooling promoted lipid-oxidation-derived aldehydes and other flavor-related volatiles. The sensory-relevant trends in taste scores, sensor responses and volatile profiles were therefore mutually supportive.

Water-holding capacity (WHC)

Throughout delivery and storage, lower freezing temperatures led to greater declines in beef water-holding capacity (Fig. 6). Furthermore, storage duration and low-temperature treatment exhibited a significant interaction effect on water-holding capacity (p < 0.05). Trends in moisture content (Fig. 6A) and purge loss (Fig. 6B) of beef samples indicate that the CK and PC-T-0.5°C groups performed similarly, while the PC-T-5°C and PC-T-18°C groups showed comparable trends. Both treatments markedly reduced beef water-holding capacity, with the PC-T-18°C group causing the most severe impairment. This change primarily relates to alterations in ice crystal morphology and protein stability at different temperatures. At −18 °C deep-freezing, complete intracellular freezing in beef forms the most ice crystals, which readily puncture cellular structures, causing mechanical damage. Subsequently, during distribution, temperature fluctuations further exacerbate protein oxidation. This leads to the exposure of hydrophobic groups, reduced water-holding capacity, and increased cross-linking between protein molecules. Collectively, these factors exert a significant negative impact on water-holding capacity (Bao & Ertbjerg, 2019). A plausible explanation is that the higher purge loss from low-temperature processing primarily stems from ice crystal morphology and its physical damage to cellular structures: during freezing, water preferentially forms ice crystals outside cells. To balance osmotic pressure, intracellular water leaks out, promoting continuous ice crystal growth and forming large extracellular ice crystals, thereby exacerbating mechanical damage to muscle fibers (D. Li, Zhu, & Sun, 2018). In summary, PC-T-5°C and PC-T-18°C exhibited the highest purge loss and lowest moisture content due to the synergistic effects of ice crystal formation and oxidative damage. The PC-T-0.5°C group maintained lower exudation loss, favoring moisture retention and exhibiting water-holding capacity closer to that of fresh meat. However, these mechanisms were not directly measured in this study and should be regarded as hypothetical.

Changes in WHC of beef after different pre-cooling treatments combined with EPS box delivery: (A) Moisture Content; (B) Purge loss; (C) Cooking loss. Lowercase letters a-g indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) between different treatment methods at the same sampling point; Capital letters A-G indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) for the same treatment method across different sampling points (n = 3).

Cooking loss is a key indicator for evaluating meat water-holding capacity. Results showed (Fig. 6C) that during low-temperature processing and delivery phases, higher cooking loss occurred with lower treatment temperatures. Notably, during the later storage phase, the PC-T-18°C group exhibited an opposite trend in cooking loss compared to the other three groups. This discrepancy likely stems from severe moisture loss during the initial freezing (FS) and at delivery (DA) stages under −18 °C treatment, significantly reducing the beef's water-holding capacity during subsequent storage. The primary mechanism relates to ice crystal formation characteristics: when beef is within the freezing temperature range of −0.5 °C to −5 °C, extensive intact ice crystals have not yet formed within muscle cells (Leygonie et al., 2012). In contrast, the PC-T-18°C group rapidly traversed this temperature range during rapid freezing, promoting nearly simultaneous formation of numerous fine, uniform ice crystals both inside and outside cells (Lagerstedt et al., 2008). However, during subsequent distribution, the PC-T-18°C group experienced the greatest temperature fluctuation (16.3 °C), indicating it also endured the most significant freeze-thaw cycles. Similar findings have been reported for meats subjected to repeated freezing and thawing, where cumulative freeze–thaw cycles reduce water-holding capacity despite initial structural preservation (Cheng et al., 2019; Vieira, Diaz, Martínez, & García-Cachán, 2009). Although cellular structure was better preserved during initial deep-freezing, repeated freeze-thaw cycles during distribution still led to reduced water-holding capacity in the muscle (Vieira et al., 2009). Although sub-freezing at −5 °C increased purge loss during storage, the subsequent cooking process did not further amplify this difference; the PC-T-5 °C group showed cooking losses closer to those of the CK group under our conditions. In summary, temperature fluctuations during distribution trigger repeated freeze-thaw cycles, accelerating moisture loss. Therefore, maintaining stable temperatures throughout the process is crucial for controlling meat water-holding capacity under similar distribution conditions.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

LF-NMR is an effective tool for assessing water distribution and mobility within muscle (Bertram, Straadt, Jensen, & Dall Aaslyng, 2007). It can measure three distinct states of water within muscle: T2b (0–2 ms), which represents water tightly bound to macromolecules; T21 (10–100 ms), indicating poorly mobile water within the myofibrillar network; and T22 (100–1000 ms), denoting free water outside the muscle (den Hertog-Meischke, van Laack, & Smulders, 1997). T₂ relaxation times measured by LF-NMR in this study (Fig. 7) revealed significant effects of different low-temperature treatments on the water status and muscle structure of beef. Results indicate that low-temperature treatment significantly influences water status, with changes in relaxation times reflecting alterations in water form and interactions with muscle tissue. During the freezing stage T2b, Fig. 7B, the CK group exhibited a slight increase in T2b values compared to fresh beef, while PC-T-0.5°C and PC-T-18°C treatments showed significant decreases. The PC-T-5°C group exhibited no noticeable change. A reduction in T2b typically indicates enhanced binding water retention capacity (L. Li, Zhang, & Yang, 2019). During delivery, T2b values in the PC-T-0.5°C and PC-T-18°C groups rebounded significantly, while CK and PC-T-5°C groups showed decreases upon delivery. Regarding T21, compared to fresh beef, the CK group showed a significant increase during freezing, while the PC-T-0.5°C and PC-T-5°C groups exhibited slight increases. Only the PC-T-18°C group demonstrated a marked decrease. After delivery, CK and PC-T-5°C groups showed decreasing trends in T21 relative to freezing stages, while PC-T-0.5°C and PC-T-18°C groups exhibited increasing trends. Elevated T21 indicates more free movement of immobile water, which signifies weakened water binding and lower water-holding capacity (Cheng et al., 2019). Regarding T22, T22 decreased significantly across all groups from CK to −18 °C as cooling progressed, with no significant difference between PC-T–5°C and PC-T-18°C. On delivery, mirroring the trends observed for T2b and T21, T2 increased in both the PC-T-0.5°C and PC-T-18°C groups while decreasing in the CK and PC-T-5°C groups. Notably, these moisture changes during delivery reflect the interconversion between free and bound water (Gan, Zhang, Mujumdar, & Jiang, 2022). Among the low-temperature treatment groups, the −5 °C treatment showed the best stability across multiple indicators, similar to the CK.

Different capital letters (A–G) indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) among treatments.

Regarding changes in water content (Fig. 7C), the PC-T-18°C group showed the highest proportion of bound water (P2b %) (3.98 ± 0.07%) after the freezing stage, with no significant difference compared to fresh samples (4.03 ± 0.21%) (p < 0.05). However, upon delivery, the bound water proportion in all treatment groups was lower than in fresh samples. Under distribution conditions, the PC-T-0.5°C group had the highest P2b % (3.84 ± 0.19%), indicating that the −0.5 °C treatment better maintained bound water stability during distribution. Regarding non-mobile water (P21%), both during freezing and upon delivery, the CK group stored at a constant 4 °C showed a significant increase in this proportion compared to fresh meat, exhibiting the highest non-mobile water content among all groups. Low-temperature treatment groups also showed a slight increase in non-mobile water, with the −0.5 °C group displaying a higher proportion than other low-temperature groups after delivery. Changes in free water (P₂₂ %) were particularly notable: the CK group exhibited a decrease in the proportion of free water during refrigeration, mainly due to the rearrangement of water molecules, which converted free water into immobile water. The PC-T-5°C group had a higher free water proportion after freezing, likely because −5 °C is at the tail end of the maximum ice crystal formation zone, where ice crystals are not fully formed. This incomplete formation caused damage to cell structures, increasing free water content. This finding aligns with the trend observed in purge loss. Upon delivery, the PC-T-18°C group had the highest P₂₂ % (3.18 ± 0.01%), indicating that both bound and free water are susceptible to temperature and pressure fluctuations, which may lead to loss as exudate or dripping water (Huff-Lonergan & Lonergan, 2005), reducing water-holding capacity during distribution.

While the −5 °C treatment showed relatively stable relaxation times on some parameters, the −0.5 °C treatment achieved a more balanced performance when relaxation time stability and water distribution were considered together, which was consistent with its better WHC. The −0.5 °C treatment achieved the highest proportion of bound water and the lowest proportion of free water (1.76 ± 0.06%). According to research by Li et al. (D. Li et al., 2018), high free water content generally indicates poor water retention capacity. These LF-NMR findings were in good agreement with the purge and cooking loss results in Section 3.4, confirming that during practical distribution processes the −0.5 °C treatment demonstrated superior water retention, making it more suitable for extending storage life and maintaining meat quality stability.

Muscle Fiber morphology

Ice crystal formation and growth are core determinants of the microstructure and final quality of frozen meat (R. Li et al., 2022). HE and SEM images (Fig. 8) reveal a two-stage progressive deterioration mechanism: the pre-cooling temperature dictates the initial freezing damage by regulating the initial ice crystal morphology, and temperature fluctuations during transport exacerbate cumulative damage by driving ice crystal recrystallization.

Changes in ice crystals observed by of beef after different pre-cooling treatments combined with EPS box delivery. (A) HE staining (100 μm); (B) SEM (100 μm).

Histological observation (H&E staining, Fig. 8A) indicates that the pre-cooling temperature governed the initial degree of structural injury. In the freezing stage, the −0.5 °C (supercooling) group showed intact myofilber bundles with uniform interstitial spaces, consistent with mild mechanical compression and slight structural loosening. The −5 °C (sub-freezing) group presented an intermediate pattern with slightswelling and widened gaps. The −18 °C (deep-freezing) group exhibited significant enlargement of interfibrillar spaces, showing obvious hollowing and loose bundles. At delivery stage, the structural damage was amplified by temperature fluctuations: the −18 °C group, which experienced a temperature rise of 16 °C during transport, showed a further aggravated structural loosening and breakage; the fiber separation in the −5 °C group also became more pronounced; in contrast, the −0.5 °C group, which underwent only a 3.8 °C rise, best preserved its structural integrity. SEM images provide further insights into the evolution of ice crystal morphology across the two stages from a microstructural perspective. In the freezing stage, the longitudinal section (Fig. 8B) shows that the −0.5 °C group maintained continuous fibrous texture with minimal iregularities, while the −18 °C group exhibited structural collapse and detachment and tearing along the fiber direction. The transverse section (Fig. 8C) clearly shows that the −0.5 °C group remained compact with negligible porosity, whereas the −18 °C group presented both fine pores and irregular large cavities with severe disruption of the fiber matrix network. At the delivery stage, the pore patterns were associated with coalescence-like changes in ice-crystal-related structures, consistent with recrystallisation-like processes. In the −18 °C group, the original small pores in both the longitudinal and transverse sections (Fig. 8B, C) underwent obvious coalescence and coarsening, leading to tissue fragmentation and the formation of adherent masses that obscured the underlying cavities. This pattern suggests that the pores in the −18 °C group were more sensitive to temperature fluctuations, which may have promoted melting and re-growth during distribution. In contrast, the relatively intact structure of the −0.5 °C group appeared less affected by temperature fluctuations, suggesting relatively more stable underlying structures. Its pore and fiber structure remained stable before and after transport (Fig. 8B, C), showing minimal recrystallization and better preservation of fiber integrity. Microstructural damage was reflected in quality deterioration. In the −18 °C and − 5 °C groups, enlarged inter-fiber gaps (as shown in Fig. 8A) allowed intracellular water to escape as free water (P₂₂% increased), while the disrupted tight fiber structure enlarged the contact interface between lipids and oxygen, accelerating lipid oxidation as reflected by significantly higher TBARS values. The high mobility of free water, no longer retained by the protein network, resulted in a sharp decline in water-holding capacity. The −0.5 °C group, benefiting from minor initial damage by ice crystals and negligible recrystallization during transport, retained a compact fiber structure, with water mostly present as bound water, thereby retaining moisture, yielding optimal water-holding capacity and the slowest oxidation rate. We note that these microstructural observations are qualitative and based on representative HE and SEM images; quantitative morphometric analysis of pore size or shape was not performed. The terms ‘recrystallisation’ and ‘melting and re-growth’ are therefore used as plausible mechanistic interpretations rather than as direct measurements of ice-crystal dynamics.

Conclusions

This study systematically compared the effects of three pre-cooling temperatures corresponding to different stages of the freezing curve (−0.5 °C, −5 °C and − 18 °C) on beef quality during simulated EPS-based e-commerce distribution, using 4 °C refrigeration as a control. Overall, supercooling at −0.5 °C provided the most balanced preservation of freshness, flavor, water-holding capacity and microstructure under the tested conditions.

Compared with −5 and − 18 °C pre-cooling, the −0.5 °C treatment delayed the increase of TVB-N and TBARS and maintained lower values throughout subsequent refrigerated storage. Based on the TVB-N limit of 20 mg/100 g, the shelf-life of the −0.5 °C group was about 24 h longer than that of the −5 and − 18 °C groups under the present experimental conditions. Flavor analysis showed that −0.5 °C pre-cooling better preserved a flavor profile similar to fresh and continuously refrigerated beef, whereas −5 and − 18 °C pre-cooling led to greater accumulation of lipid-oxidation-derived aldehydes. In terms of water-holding capacity, the −0.5 °C group exhibited lower purge and cooking losses, consistent with LF-NMR results showing a higher proportion of bound water and lower free water compared with the other pre-cooling treatments. Microstructural observations confirmed that −0.5 °C pre-cooling caused relatively minor ice crystal damage and preserved muscle fiber integrity more effectively than −5 and − 18 °C.

Within the limitations of a single distribution scenario (48 h at 25 °C in EPS boxes with a fixed ice pack configuration), our findings suggest that supercooling at −0.5 °C can be a promising pre-cooling strategy for short-term EPS-based e-commerce distribution of beef. Future work should validate these results under different ambient temperatures, distribution durations and packaging configurations, and should further investigate the stability and practical feasibility of maintaining supercooled states in commercial logistics systems.
