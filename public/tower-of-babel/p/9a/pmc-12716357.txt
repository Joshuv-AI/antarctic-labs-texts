Temporal Regulation of Signal Recognition Particle During Translation
Authors: Ruilin Qian, Radoslaw J Gora, Sowmya Chandrasekar, Shu-ou Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignal recognition particle (SRP) is a universally conserved protein targeting machine that directs newly synthesized proteins to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). SRP recognizes signal sequences on nascent ER proteins as they emerge from the ribosome and, in response, activates interaction with the SRP receptor (SR) at the ER membrane. Early work suggested that SRP loses targeting competence as the nascent chain elongates; however, the underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. Here we address this question using a combination of steady-state and single-molecule fluorescence spectroscopy measurements. A Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay revealed increased dynamic excursions of the signal sequence from SRP on ribosomes bearing longer nascent chains, leading to a suboptimal conformation of SRP and its impaired interaction kinetics with SR. In addition, the nascent polypeptide associated complex (NAC) amplifies the effects of longer nascent chains to further exclude SRP from ER targeting. Our findings reveal the profound effects of an elongating nascent polypeptide on the conformation and activity of SRP and a key role of NAC in the temporal regulation of SRP, which together impose a limited window for cotranslational ER protein targeting during protein synthesis.Keywords: protein targeting, ribosome, signal recognition particle, signal sequence, single molecule fluorescence

Introduction

Protein biogenesis is a prerequisite for the proper functioning of all cells and requires the correct folding, localization, modification, and quality control of the newly synthesized proteome [1]. Many protein biogenesis pathways initiate during translation, mediated by a collection of molecular chaperones, protein targeting and translocation machineries, and modification enzymes that bind near the polypeptide exit tunnel of the ribosome [2–7]. A constantly evolving nascent polypeptide during translation elongation presents a unique challenge to these ribosome-associated protein biogenesis factors (RPBs), which must recognize molecular signals on the nascent chain while also maintaining contacts with specific docking sites on the ribosome surface. These molecular constraints might impose a limited window of action for each RPB during translation elongation. How the activity of the individual RPBs is regulated by elongation of the nascent protein is not well understood.

Protein targeting to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), mediated by signal recognition particle (SRP), provides a paradigm for the coupling between protein synthesis and localization [5,8,9]. Membrane and secretory proteins account for over 30% of the newly synthesized proteome in eukaryotic cells and are initially delivered to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) for residency in the ER or further trafficking through the endomembrane system. Cotranslational targeting by SRP provides a mechanism to minimize the misfolding and aggregation of these proteins in the cytosol [10]. Loss of SRP leads to mislocalization of ER proteins to mitochondria and proteostatic stress in both the cytosol and ER, highlighting its essential role in organelle biogenesis and protein homeostasis in the cell [11,12]. Multiple severe diseases related to SRP dysfunction have been reported, including neutropenia and growth retardation [13–15].

Eukaryotic SRP is a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising the 7SL SRP RNA tightly bound to six protein subunits (SRP9, SRP14, SRP19, SRP54, SRP68, and SRP72) [16]. The methionine-rich M-domain of SRP54 is positioned at the polypeptide exit tunnel of the ribosome and recognizes ER targeting signals, in the form of either a transmembrane domain (TMD) or a hydrophobic signal sequence, in a nascent protein as it emerges from the ribosome [17–19]. In addition, interactions of SRP68/72 and SRP9/14 with the ribosome further stabilize the binding of SRP with the Ribosome-Nascent chain Complex (RNC), achieving a dissociation constant (Kd) in the subnanomolar range [20]. Eukaryotic SRP receptor (SR) is a heterodimer of SRα and SRβ subunits, anchored at the ER membrane via the N-terminal TMD of SRβ [21,22]. Both SRP54 and SRα contain a specialized GTPase domain, termed NG, whose GTP-dependent dimerization mediates the delivery of SRP-bound ribosomes to the ER membrane [22–26]. A series of conformational rearrangements [27–31] in SRP and SR ensues, leading to the handover of RNC from SRP to the Sec61p translocon or other protein insertion machineries, via which the nascent chain is either inserted into the ER lumen or integrated into the membrane [32–35]. Finally, activated GTP hydrolysis in the SRP-SR complex drives their dissociation from one another, allowing the recycling of SRP and SR.

SRP enjoys kinetic privilege among the protein biogenesis factors that act cotranslationally. Ribosome profiling experiments in bacteria showed that, on average, SRP binds RNCs when the nascent chains are ~50 amino acids (aa) long, when the N-terminal signal sequence or TMD have completely emerged from the ribosome exit tunnel, and leaves RNC after an additional ~60 aa have been synthesized [36]. While the short duration of SRP-RNC interaction during translation could be attributed to the rapid transfer of RNC from SRP to translocation machineries [37], there was also evidence that SRP loses targeting competence during translation elongation, as RNCs with nascent chains exceeding 130 aa are inefficiently targeted to ER microsomes [38]. However, the molecular basis of the length-dependent targeting defects is unclear. Early studies in translation-competent lysate suggested that this defect results from weakened SRP-RNC binding, as the defect could be overcome by increasing SRP concentration [38]. In support of this notion, a single-molecule study [39] suggested that the bacterial SRP displays slower association and faster dissociation rates with RNCs bearing long nascent chains, which results in an up to 43-fold lower equilibrium affinity. However, the Kd values were still as low as 5 nM even at the longest nascent chain length examined. Given the in vivo concentrations of SRP (400–500 nM) [40] and RNC (2–5 µM) [41], the reduced binding affinity is insufficient to account for the loss of SRP targeting competence. Contrary to the conclusion of these works, Flanagan et al. showed that mammalian SRP binds tightly to RNCs, with Kd values in the sub-nanomolar range, at nascent chain lengths ranging from 86 to 220 aa [20]. These observations suggest that weakened RNC binding by SRP is insufficient to account for the inhibition of SRP-dependent translocation at long nascent chain lengths.

On the other hand, recent studies uncovered extensive allosteric regulations of mammalian SRP by the ribosome, signal sequence, and additional RPBs [9,22,27,42]. Ribosome displaying a signal sequence induces SRP into a Proximal conformation, in which the SRP54 NG-domain docks at uL23 near the exit site in vicinity of the M-domain that engages the signal sequence. In this conformation, a Molecular Recognition Feature (MoRF) in the SR linker connecting its NG- and membrane proximal-domains contacts both the M- and NG-domains of SRP54, providing a transient tether that stabilizes early SRP-SR association and thus strongly accelerates formation of the GTP-stabilized SRP-SR NG-heterodimer [21,22,43,44]. This RNC-induced conformational activation of SRP, which initiates ER targeting, is further regulated by the nascent polypeptide-associated complex (NAC), an abundant ribosome-associated chaperone [45,46]. NAC is bound to ribosomes with nanomolar affinity and recruits SRP to the ribosome to initiate scanning through an interaction between the C-terminal UBA domain of NACα and the NG-domain of SRP54 [47]. NAC further gates the engagement of SRP M-domain with nascent chains lacking an exposed ER targeting signal using its central globular domain docked at the ribosome exit tunnel [47–49]; this prevents the mis-activation of SRP and slows SRP-SR association on signalless ribosomes, thus ensuring the fidelity of protein targeting to the ER [42,50].

Considering the recent advances in the understanding of SRP, here we re-examined the regulation of cotranslational protein translocation by nascent chain length. We developed a sensitive and robust Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay that specifically monitors the engagement of signal sequence with the SRP M-domain. Steady-state and single-molecule FRET measurements showed that, while nascent chain elongation had only a modest effect on SRP-RNC binding affinity, it disrupts both the docking of the signal sequence in the SRP54 M-domain and the adoption of the Proximal conformation of SRP induced by signal sequence docking. As the Proximal conformation is essential for SR recruitment, we observed a corresponding decrease in SRP-SR association rates on RNCs with longer nascent chains. NAC further inhibits the Proximal SRP conformation and SRP-SR interaction at long nascent chain lengths. Based on these results, we propose a molecular model for how SRP activity is regulated during translation elongation.

Results

A sensitive and robust FRET assay for signal sequence docking in the SRP M-domain

To specifically monitor the interaction of SRP with the signal sequence on the RNC, SRP54 was labeled at an engineered single cysteine (G363C) in the M-domain with Cy3B-maleimide as the donor dye (Figure 1A) and assembled into holo-SRP (termed SRPM-Cy3B). The RNC bearing the model SRP substrate preprolactin (pPL) was labeled with an acceptor dye ATTO647N (termed RNCATTO647N) at residue 12 preceding the signal sequence using amber suppression and click chemistry. The estimated distance of the labeled residues is 35 Å in the structure of the RNC-SRP complex (pdb 7QWQ) [47], compared to the Förster radius of ~65 Å for this dye pair. Hence, efficient FRET is anticipated.

To test the function of fluorescently labeled SRP, we used an established, reconstituted in vitro translocation assay [44,51]. Full length pPL was translated in wheat germ lysate, which lacks endogenous SRP. The ability of purified SRP and SR to target pPL to the ER was examined using trypsinized, high salt-washed canine microsomes (TKRM) devoid of endogenous SRP and SR. As the N-terminal TMD of SRβ renders the protein aggregation-prone, a soluble SR complex lacking this TMD (SRαβΔTM) was used in all assays [22]. Successful translocation of pPL across the microsomal membrane results in the cleavage of its signal sequence, allowing translocation efficiency to be quantified. This assay showed that SRPM-Cy3B mediated the targeting and translocation of pPL with efficiencies that are within 80% of that of wildtype SRP, indicating that labeling did not substantially impair SRP activity (Figure S1).

To measure FRET efficiency between the dye pair in the RNC-SRP complex, we began with an RNC bearing the N-terminal 91 amino acids of pPL, an optimal length at which SRP binds strongly to RNCpPL91, adopts the Proximal conformation, and undergoes rapid assembly with SR [22,42]. A ~50% decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed when SRPM-Cy3B was incubated with RNCpPL91ATTO647N compared to that with unlabeled RNCpPL91 (Figure 1B), indicating efficient FRET. The FRET signal was competed away by a 10-fold excess of unlabeled RNCpPL91 (Figure 1B), suggesting that fluorescence labeling did not substantially enhance binding affinity.

Equilibrium titrations based on this FRET assay yielded a Kd value of 0.14 nM for SRP-RNC binding (Figure 1C), in agreement with the values determined previously [20,42]. We further measured the kinetic stability of the RNC-SRP complex using a pulse-chase experiment. A preformed SRPM-Cy3B-RNCpPL91ATTO647N complex was challenged with a 10-fold excess of unlabeled SRP, and the loss of FRET during complex dissociation was followed in real time. The observed dissociation kinetics exhibited both slow and fast phases, with koff values of 0.0039 s−1 and 0.0158 s−1, respectively, at a 2:1 ratio (Figure 1D). Thus, the new FRET assay allowed us to directly monitor the signal sequence interaction of SRP and provided a robust method to determine the equilibrium and kinetic stabilities of SRP-RNC interaction.

Nascent chain elongation impairs signal sequence docking in the SRP M-domain

Using this FRET assay, we asked how increased nascent chain length impacts RNC-SRP interaction. To this end, we purified RNCs bearing the pPL nascent chain at varying lengths in 10–20 aa increments (Figure 2A). Unexpectedly, the FRET efficiency between the dye pair peaked at nascent chain lengths of 91–101 aa and decreased afterwards, reducing to <10% when the nascent chain exceeded 131 aa in length (Figure 2A and Figure S2). This suggests that signal sequence docking at the SRP M-domain becomes less efficient as the nascent chain elongates.

As the reduced FRET efficiency at longer nascent chain lengths precluded us from measuring SRP-RNC binding affinity via equilibrium titrations, we instead used an assay based on competition of the FRET signal between SRPM-Cy3B and RNCpPL91ATTO647N by unlabeled RNCs (Figure 2B). We observed dose-dependent reduction in the FRET signal by RNCpPL at all the nascent chain lengths tested (Figure 2B). Competition with unlabeled RNCpPL91 yielded an inhibition constant (Ki) of 0.16 nM (Figure 2B, C), the same, within error, as the Kd value measured by FRET-based titrations (Figure 1C). RNCpPL with longer nascent chain lengths, ranging from 131 aa to 171 aa, yielded Ki values of 0.43–0.47 nM, approximately 3-fold higher than that of RNCpPL91 (Figure 2B, C). As a negative control, the 80S ribosome was much less effective in competing with the FRET signal (Figure 2B, C). These results show that SRP-RNC binding affinity weakens during elongation of the nascent chain, in agreement with previous studies. Nevertheless, SRP still binds with subnanomolar affinity to RNCs even at the longest nascent chain length tested. Given the in vivo concentrations of SRP and RNC, the reduced binding is insufficient to account for the loss of SRP targeting competence.

To further probe the effect of nascent chain length on the dynamics of signal sequence interaction with SRP, we carried out FRET measurements at single molecule (sm) resolution using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM) [42]. RNCATTO647N bearing the pPL nascent chain at lengths of 91, 131, 151, and 171 aa were immobilized on a neutravidin-coated quartz coverslip surface via a biotin tag at the 5’-end of mRNA (Figure 3A). Under TIRFM, fluorescently labeled SRP was excited and detected only when it interacts with the surface-immobilized RNC (Figure 3A). We observed colocalization of SRPM-Cy3B with RNCpPLATTO647N across all nascent chain lengths, providing independent evidence for strong SRP-RNC interaction.

We recorded movies of immobilized SRPM-Cy3B-RNCpPL91ATTO647N complexes with continuous excitation of Cy3B and extracted fluorescence time traces for both the donor and acceptor dyes to determine FRET efficiency. Hidden Markov Modeling (HMM) of the time traces gave the number of FRET states and mean FRET efficiency of each state (Figure S3). The FRET efficiency distribution of the complex was visualized by smFRET histograms (Figure 3C). These analyses showed that, at a nascent chain length of 91 aa, the RNC-SRP complex was dominated by a high FRET population (Figure 3B, C, High) with a mean FRET efficiency of ~0.8, consistent with previous structural analyses [22,42]. Approximately 10% and 5% of the complex sampled alternative conformations with medium and low FRET efficiencies, respectively, at this length (Figure 3B, C, Medium and Low). The medium- and low-FRET populations became increasingly prevalent as the nascent chain elongates. At nascent chain lengths of 151–171 aa, 70–75% of the complex are in the medium- and low-FRET populations (Figure 3B, C). In addition, the dwell time of the signal sequence in the high FRET state progressively decreased as the nascent chain elongates (Figure 3D). These smTIRFM data provided direct evidence for increased dynamics of longer nascent chains, in which the signal sequence more frequently undocks from the SRP54 M-domain and samples alternative conformations.

A longer nascent chain disrupts the activation of SRP for SR recruitment

We previously showed that, upon binding to ribosomes without an ER targeting signal, SRP samples a variety of alternative conformations [22]. The presence of a signal sequence drives most of the SRP into the Proximal conformation, which is required for efficient SR recruitment [22]. We therefore hypothesized that impaired signal sequence docking in the SRP54 M-domain during translation elongation reduces the formation of the targeting-active Proximal conformation in SRP, thereby impairing its association with SR.

To test this hypothesis, we used a previously developed smFRET dye pair that specifically monitors the Proximal conformation [22]. SRP was doubly labeled with a donor dye (ATTO550) in SRP19 and an acceptor dye (ATTO647N) in the NG-domain of SRP54 (termed SRPProx; Figure 4A). In the cryo-EM structure [17], the SRP54 NG-domain is positioned adjacent to SRP19 in this conformation with a 44 Å distance between this dye pair, giving rise to an expected high FRET population. We immobilized unlabeled RNCs bearing varying lengths of the pPL nascent chain, which recruits SRPProx to the quartz coverslip surface for fluorescence detection of the conformational landscape of SRP on RNCs (Figure 4A, B, Figure S4).

As observed previously [22,42], the smFRET histogram of SRPProx bound to RNCpPL91 was dominated by the high FRET population, indicating that the majority of SRP is in the Proximal conformation (Figure 4B–D). As the nascent chain elongates, however, the population in the Proximal conformation decreased successively. At nascent chain lengths of 151 aa and 171 aa, less than 30% of SRP was in the Proximal state, with corresponding rises of the population in medium- and low-FRET states that were previously observed with SRPs bound to ribosomes displaying mutated signal sequences [42] (Figure 4B–D and Figure S4A).

Since the Proximal conformation is essential for rapid SRP association with SR, we next tested the effect of nascent chain length on the kinetics of SRP-SR binding. To this end, we used a previously developed FRET assay based on a donor dye (Cy3B) labeled at the SRP54 NG-domain in assembled SRP (termed SRPNG-Cy3B) and an acceptor dye (ATTO647N) labeled at the C-terminus of the SR NG-domain (termed SRATTO647N) [22]. To prevent rapid SRP-SR dissociation triggered by GTP hydrolysis, the GTPase-deficient mutation SRα-R458A was introduced [22] in addition to the nonhydrolyzable GTP analog GMPPNP. Efficient FRET was detected upon GMPPNP-dependent assembly of the SRP-SR NG-heterodimer, and assembly kinetics was monitored in real time on the stopped-flow apparatus (Figure S5). Previous studies showed that SRP-SR association was strongly accelerated when the pPL nascent chain reaches 61–91 aa in length, after the signal sequence fully emerges from the ribosome tunnel [42]. However, we found that further elongation of the nascent chain led to reduced SRP-SR assembly rates, with kon reduced by over 2-fold at 171 aa (Figure 5A, B). The decrease in assembly rates is consistent with the reduced SRP population in the Proximal conformation observed in smFRET measurements (Figure 4). On the other hand, the dissociation rate constant (koff) of the SRP-SR complex remained unaffected by changes in nascent chain length (Figure 5C). Together, these findings show that a long nascent chain disrupts the conformational activation of SRP that is required for its efficient assembly with SR, which likely contributes to its impaired targeting competence.

NAC blocks the targeting of long-chain RNCs

Given the complexity of the cellular environment, we hypothesized that additional factors might amplify the effect of nascent chain length on the conformation and activity of SRP. NAC, which is highly abundant, binds ribosome with high affinity [45], and has been shown to prevent the SRP-dependent targeting of signalless ribosomes [42,46,47], is a strong candidate for this additional layer of regulation. We therefore investigated whether NAC plays a role in rejecting RNCs with a long nascent chain.

We first examined whether NAC regulates the conformation of SRP when it is bound to RNC with a long nascent chain. To this end, we used smTIRFM to measure the intramolecular FRET of SRPProx bound to RNCpPL with either a 91 aa or 171 aa long nascent chain (Figure S6A). The presence of NAC had <15% effects on the conformational distribution of SRP when the nascent chain was 91 aa long (Figure 6A top panel, Figure 6B), whereas with the 171 aa long pPL nascent chain, NAC further reduced the Proximal population of SRP over 2-fold (Figure 6A lower panel, Figure 6B). In agreement with these observations, stopped-flow measurements showed that NAC significantly slowed SRP-SR association kinetics with RNCpPL171, reducing the value of kon nearly 10-fold, whereas it affected SRP-SR association rate by less than 3-fold with RNCpPL91 (Figure 6C, D and Figure S6B, C). Thus, NAC amplified the reductions in SRP activity caused by a long nascent chain.

To further test the role of NAC in SRP-dependent targeting to the ER, we measured the localization of 35S-labeled RNCpPL with varying nascent chain lengths to salt-washed rough ER microsome (KRM), monitored by the co-sedimentation of RNC with KRM [52–54]. We first measured the targeting of the RNC during translation in rabbit reticulocyte lysate (RRL) containing endogenous SRP and NAC, which confirmed the reduced ER targeting of RNCpPL171 compared to RNCpPL91 (Figure 6E, lanes 1–6). To dissect the roles of SRP and NAC, we partially purified the RNCs after translation using a high-salt sucrose cushion that removes most ribosome-associated proteins including SRP and NAC. We then supplemented purified SRP, NAC, or both during further incubation of the RNCs with KRM. We observed a low background level (~15%) of ER association with both RNCpPL91 and RNCpPL171 in the absence of supplemented SRP and regardless of the presence of NAC (Figure 6E, lanes 19–30, and Figs. 6F). When only SRP was supplemented, the targeting of RNCpPL91 increased significantly over the background level, whereas the targeting of RNCpPL171 increased modestly (Figure 6E, lanes 7–12, and Figure 6F, G). The additional supplementation of NAC modestly reduced the targeting of RNCpPL91 but abolished the targeting of RNCpPL171 (Figure 6E, lanes 13–18, and Figure 6F, G).

Together, these results show that NAC amplifies the defects in the conformation and activity of SRP on RNCs with a long nascent chain, further contributing to the rejection of SRP-dependent ER targeting as the nascent chain extends beyond a critical length.

Discussion

In this work, we examined how cotranslational protein targeting by SRP, a universally conserved targeting machine, is regulated by an elongating nascent chain during translation. Our results define a restricted window of SRP action during the synthesis of a nascent ER protein, elucidate the molecular basis underlying this regulation, and provide a paradigm for the temporal regulation of cotranslational protein biogenesis machineries during translation elongation.

Biochemical assays [38] and in vivo ribosome profiling [12,36] data show that SRP binds nascent polypeptides at an early stage during translation, as soon as the N-terminal signal sequence or TMD fully emerges from the exit tunnel. Ribosome profiling data further indicate that SRP disengages from ribosomes after an additional ~60 aa of the nascent chain has been synthesized. While these observations could be attributed to completion of the targeting cycle [5], our results further show that SRP activity is exquisitely sensitive to the length of the nascent chain, peaking for up to ~70 aa after the ER targeting signal emerges from the ribosome (from 61 aa to 131 aa) and declining to background levels ~40 aa later. This provides definitive evidence for a restricted window of SRP-dependent ER targeting during translation. Notably, eukaryotic SRP contains an Alu-domain, which can compete with elongation factors at the ribosome subunit interface and was proposed to reduce translation elongation rates [55]. It is intriguing to speculate that elongation pauses could extend the time window for SRP during translation, potentially enhancing the targeting efficiency of a subset of proteins in vivo. This hypothesis remains to be tested.

By developing a new, high-resolution assay that directly monitors the dynamics of signal sequence docking at the SRP M-domain and leveraging previously developed biophysical probes on SRP and SR [22], our study further revealed a multilayered mechanism by which the elongating nascent chain restricts SRP activity. Firstly, RNC-SRP binding affinity is weakened ~3-fold when the pPL nascent chain exceeds 131 aa (Figure 2). While this is consistent with previous observations [39], the weakening is modest and gave a subnanomolar Kd for SRP bound to the long-chain RNCs. However, the interaction of the signal sequence with SRP becomes progressively disrupted at the longer nascent chain lengths, such that the signal sequence undocks from the SRP54 M-domain >70% of the time when the nascent chain exceeds 150 aa in length (Figure 3). These dynamic excursions of the signal sequence from the M-domain correlated with less effective conformational activation of SRP on long-chain RNCs (Figure 4). As the Proximal conformation of SRP is required for rapid SRP-SR assembly [22], this further leads to corresponding reductions in SR recruitment rates as the nascent protein elongates (Figure 5). Finally, the cotranslational chaperone NAC further disrupts the Proximal SRP conformation and slows SRP-SR association specifically on long-chain RNCs (Figure 6). Thus, in addition to weakened RNC binding, elongation of the nascent chain impairs multiple signal-sequence induced allosteric regulatory steps in the SRP cycle, both in cis and in trans. Although the effect on each step is modest, their cumulative effects led to the loss of SRP targeting competence.

We previously showed that SRP displays extensive premature targeting activity in the absence of NAC: even before the signal sequence emerged from the ribosome exit tunnel, SRP is activated for assembly with SR, with rates that are ~two-fold slower compared to that on ribosomes displaying the signal sequence at optimal nascent chain lengths [42]. The presence of NAC reduced the SRP-SR assembly rates on short-chain RNCs by over 20-fold, thus blocking premature targeting [42]. In this work, we found that NAC also strongly and selectively suppresses the targeting of ribosomes when the nascent chain exceeds the optimal length range for SRP. Collectively, these observations strongly suggest a key role of NAC in imposing the time window of cotranslational protein targeting. The role of NAC in the temporal regulation of cotranslational protein biogenesis could conceivably extend to other RPBs.

Our results here, coupled with previous findings [22,42,47], lead to a more complete model for the SRP pathway during translation elongation (Figure 7). The highly abundant NAC engages translating ribosomes early, even before an ER targeting signal emerges from the exit tunnel [48]. NAC uses its UBA domain to recruit SRP to the ribosome, while its globular domain, positioned at the exit tunnel, blocks SRP from accessing nascent chains lacking an exposed ER targeting signal (Figure 7, dashed left arrows in blue) [47]. Once a signal sequence fully emerges from the exit tunnel, the ribosome interaction of the NAC globular domain is destabilized [47], allowing the SRP54 M-domain to engage the signal sequence. This information is transmitted, through the linker connecting the M- and NG-domains, to induce the formation of the targeting active Proximal conformation (Figure 7, red arrows and box) [22]. When the nascent chain exceeds 131 aa, however, increased excursions of the signal sequence from the SRP54 M-domain led to progressively less effective conformational activation of SRP, an effect that is strongly amplified by NAC (Figure 7, blue arrows and box). It is likely that the NAC UBA domain, which docks at the SRP NG-domain, competes with the binding of SR NG-domain to further impair SRP-SR association at this stage [47]. The combination of these mechanisms restricts SRP-dependent protein targeting to a defined window during translation, after an ER targeting signal fully emerges from the ribosome but before an additional ~100 aa of the nascent chain has been synthesized. Although this study focuses on the early stages of the SRP pathway, we speculate that the disruption of signal sequence interaction with the SRP M-domain during translation elongation could also help facilitate handover of the nascent chain from SRP to translocation machineries at the ER membrane, a hypothesis that remains to be tested.

Model for the regulation of SRP by nascent chain length and NAC. Signal sequence recognition and conformational activation of SRP is optimal when it interacts with RNCs bearing a nascent chain of 60–130 aa in length (red arrows and shades). In the absence of an exposed signal sequence, NAC prevents SRP from engaging the nascent chain (blue arrows on the left). When the nascent chain exceeds the optimal length range, signal sequence increasingly samples alternative conformations, disrupting the adoption of the Proximal SRP conformation (blue arrows and shades on the right). The globular domain of NAC further disrupts the formation of the Proximal SRP conformation on long-chain RNCs, while the interaction of its UBA domain with the SRP NG-domain competes with SR binding (inset).

Multiple ribosome-associated protein biogenesis machineries dock at defined sites near the exit tunnel and recognize specific sequence motifs on the nascent chain [6,9,10]. However, translation elongation increases the conformational space that can be sampled by the nascent polypeptide and thus raises the entropic penalty for engagement of the nascent chain with these RPBs. Our results here demonstrate how such a trade-off defines an optimal and restricted window for the action of SRP during translation and the multiple layers of allosteric mechanisms, in cis and in trans, that underlie such regulation. Analogous temporal regulation can be envisioned for other RPBs, including modification enzymes that process the N-terminus of the nascent chain and cotranslational chaperones that recognize specific sequence or structural features on the nascent protein.

Experimental Procedures

Protein and RNA

SRP proteins (SRP19, SRP9/14, SRP68/72 and SRP54), SR (heterodimer of SRα and SRβΔTM) [22], NAC, and engineered MmPylRS (Methanosarcina mazei pyrrolysyl-tRNA synthetase) [42] were expressed and purified as described before. SRP 7SL RNA was transcribed by T7 polymerase and purified by acid phenol extraction and isopropanol precipitation as described before [22] with the following modifications: after solubilizing the RNA pellet, the gel purification step was replaced by dialysis in SRP Assembly Buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM KOAc, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2) for 2 h.

mRNAs for in vitro translation and engineered MmPyltRNA (cognate tRNA of MmPylRS) for amber codon suppression were transcribed using T7 polymerase as described before [42]. To immobilize RNCs on neutravidin-coated surface in the TIRF assay, a 4:5 ratio of GTP and 5’-Biotin-GMP (GLPBIO) was used during transcription to generate mRNA with a biotinylated 5’ end. For translocation assays, full length pPL mRNA was transcribed using the pSPBP4 vector and the SP6 Megascript kit (Ambion) [44].

RNC preparation

To generate RNCs with different nascent chain lengths, PCR amplicons encoding, from 5’ to 3’, the EMCV IRES, 3xFLAG tag, the HRV 3C protease cleavage site, and the coding sequences for specified N-terminal residues of pPL were transcribed in vitro using T7 polymerase. mRNAs were in vitro translated in rabbit reticulocyte lysate (RRL) for 30 min at 32 °C as described [42]. RNCs were purified by sedimentation in high salt cushion (50 mM KHEPES pH 7.5, 1 M KOAc, 15 mM Mg (OAc)2, 0.5 M Sucrose, 0.1% Triton, 2 mM DTT), immunopurified using anti-FLAG agarose, sedimentation in high salt cushion, and resuspended in SRP Assay Buffer (50 mM KHEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTT, and 0.04% Nikkol (octaethylene glycol monododecyl ether)). To fluorescently label the RNC, mRNAs with amber codon at R12 were translated in the presence of TCOK (axial-trans-cyclooct-2-en-L-Lysine), PyltRNA, and MmPylRS. For TIRFM assays, biotin-conjugated mRNA was translated for immobilization on passivated coverslip surface.

SRP assembly

SRP was assembled from purified protein subunits and 7SL RNA as described before [22], except that the refolding step of 7SL RNA was omitted, as 7SL RNA was folded during in vitro transcription.

Targeting assays

In vitro translocation.

mRNA encoding full length pPL was translated in wheat germ lysate containing [35S]-methionine for 2 min, followed by addition of specified concentrations of assembled SRP (both wildtype and SRP54M-Cy3B), 70 nM SR, and 0.5 eq/µL trypsin-digested, salt-washed rough ER microsome (TKRM). The reactions were incubated at 25 °C for 40 min and quenched with 2 × SDS sample buffer. Translation and translocation products were separated on SDS-PAGE and visualized by autoradiography [44,51].

RNC targeting.

pPL RNCs with 91 and 171 aa-long nascent chains were in vitro translated in RRL in 40 µL reactions for 30 min in 32 °C, then incubated with 4 eq salt-washed rough microsome (KRM) for 20 min in RT. The translation mixture was layered onto a 50 µL low salt cushion (50 mM KHEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM KOAc, 2 mM Mg (OAc)2, 0.5 M Sucrose), and ultracentrifuged at 40,000 RPM for 5 min (TLA 100; Beckman Coulter) [52]. The pellet containing KRM and cosedimented RNC was resuspended in 20 µL 2.5xSDS Sample loading buffer. The targeting reaction sample remained above the cushion, which was thoroughly mixed and collected as a combined supernatant fraction.

To test the effects of SRP and NAC, 40 µL translation mixtures containing 91 or 171 aa-long RNCs were sedimented over a 100 µL high salt cushion (as described above) to remove endogenous SRP and NAC. Partially purified RNCs were resuspended in 30 µL SRP Assay Buffer and supplemented with energy regeneration mix (1 mM ATP, 0.1 mM GTP, 6 mM creatine phosphate, 0.1 mg/ml creatine phosphokinase) [53]. Where indicated, 50 nM SRP and/or 250 nM NAC were added to the resuspension, and the mixture was incubated with KRM for 20 min at 25 °C. Co-sedimentation of RNC with KRM was analyzed as described above.

Fluorescence labeling

To monitor the proximal conformation of SRP, cyslite SRP54 was labeled at an engineered C47 with Cy3B-maleimide, and SRP19 was labeled at an engineered C64 with ATTO550-maleimide via thiol-maleimide chemistry [22]. Labeled SRP subunits were assembled into holo-SRP using the same protocol as for the wildtype SRP. To monitor signal sequence interaction with the SRP M-domain, RNC was labeled at R12 in the pPL nascent chain using amber suppression followed by click-on reaction between the incorporated TCOK unnatural amino acid and tetrazine-conjugated ATTO647N (RNCpPLATTO647N), with close to 100% labeling efficiency [42]. An engineered C363 in the SRP54 M-domain was labeled with Cy3B-maleimide (SRP54M-Cy3B) with 80–90% efficiency, following the same protocol as for the C47-labeling. To measure SRP-SR association, SRαβΔTM was labeled at the C-terminus of SRα NG-domain with ATTO647N via sortase-mediated ligation [22], and cyslite SRP54 was labeled at C47 with Cy3B-maleimide.

TIRF-based smFRET measurement

Surface Preparation and Sample Immobilization.

Quartz imaging slides and coverslips were aminosilanized using Vectabond (Vector Laboratories) and subsequently coated with biotin-PEG (Laysan Bio). The slides were passivated in Passivation Buffer (5% Tween-20, 1 mg/mL BSA) for at least 1 h at 25 °C, followed by washing with 50 mM KHEPES, pH 7.5. NeutrAvidin (ThermoFisher) was then pipetted into each slide channel at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and incubated inside the slide for 10 min. Excess NeutrAvidin was removed by washing with Imaging Buffer (Assay Buffer supplemented with 1 mg/mL BSA, 100 µM GMPPNP, and an oxygen scavenging system consisting of 4 mM Trolox, 2.5 mM protocatechuic acid (PCA), and 50 nM protocatechuate-3,4-dioxygenase (PCD, Sigma)) [56].

Biotinylated ribosome-nascent chain complexes (RNCs) were diluted to 1.5 nM in Imaging Buffer, pipetted onto the neutravidin-functionalized slide surface and incubated for 10 min at 25 °C to attach to the slide. Subsequently, the slides were flushed with 2 nM SRP, previously diluted in the Imaging Buffer. When indicated, NAC was diluted together with SRP to a final concentration of 20 nM. Fluorescence images were acquired using MicroManager software on a custom-built TIRF microscopy setup. ATTO647N-labeled RNCs were verified by excitation at 635 nm, and single-molecule fluorescence measurements were performed with donor excitation at 532 nm (Cy3B) in single-excitation mode. Both donor and acceptor fluorescence intensities were recorded simultaneously at a temporal resolution of 50 ms.

Each data point represents a single FRET event, limited by temporal resolution of the image acquisition system and equals 50 ms. Individual single FRET events were pooled over all trajectories in each experimental group to contain at least 3000 events, in order to obtain a macroscopic equilibrium distribution of the molecule.

The FRET traces collected for the same experimental group across all experiments were extracted from iSMS, truncated at the photobleaching events, and pooled for Hidden Markov Modeling (HMM) modeling [58] using the hmmlearn algorithm package available in Python. The following parameters were used: number of states = 1–5, covariance structure = full, number of interactions for model fitting = 10,000, convergence tolerance = 1e–5, parameters updated during training = stmc (start probabilities, transition matrix, means of Gaussian emissions, covariances of Gaussian emissions), inference algorithm = Vite rbi). The number of FRET states was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), by using functions bic() and aic() from the hmmlearn package. The BIC and AIC values were calculated for individual models with 1–5 FRET states. As a principle, the model with the smallest number of states with lowest BIC and AIC values was selected to prevent overfitting. In all cases throughout the manuscript, the optimal number of states is three.

The HMM analysis was then repeated with the number of states set to 3 and the number of iterations increased to 100 000 for a more refined fitting (HMM parameters: number of states = 3, covariance structure = full, number of interactions for model fitting = 100 000, convergence tolerance = 1e–5, parameters updated during training = stmc, inference algorithm = Viterbi).
