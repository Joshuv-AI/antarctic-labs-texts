Global research landscape on the genetics of congenital heart disease: A bibliometric and visualized analysis via VOSviewer and CiteSpace
Authors: Fan Zhang, Lei Qi, Mingxue Zhao, Shuming Han, Haoran Zhang, Guangxin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Genetic factors play a significant role in the development of congenital heart disease (CHD). Many studies on the genetics of CHD have been published worldwide; however, no research has assessed and mapped the global research landscape of these studies. This bibliometric and visualized study aimed to delineate research hotspots and trends in the field of CHD genetics. Scientific papers on the genetics of CHD from January 1, 1950, to December 31, 2023, were obtained by searching the Web of Science Core Collection. The bibliometric metadata of each chosen research paper were extracted, analyzed, and visualized using tools such as Microsoft Excel 2021, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace. The final analysis included 5317 papers discussing the genetics of CHD. The countries and journals that published the highest number of papers were the United States (n = 2118), and American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A (n = 332), respectively. In addition to CHD and genetics, keywords such as tetralogy of Fallot, ventricular septal defect, and atrial septal defect appeared most frequently among 8365 keywords. Eight clusters were formed to categorize the keywords. Keywords such as case–control study, whole genome sequencing, and whole exome sequencing in clusters 6, 7, and 8, respectively, had the latest average publication year among all clusters. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first bibliometric analysis of CHD genetics studies. Tetralogy of Fallot, ventricular septal defect, and atrial septal defect are global research topics. The interactions between environmental and genetic factors in the pathogenesis of CHD, genetic etiology of CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension, and molecular genetics of CHD via high-throughput genomic technology are possible areas of future research on the genetics of CHD.

1. Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) refers to a group of anomalies characterized by structural and functional abnormalities in the heart and large blood vessels that occur during embryonic development. Notably, CHD is the most common congenital anomaly in newborn babies, affecting approximately 1% of neonates, and severe CHD, if left untreated, results in death within a short period after birth.[1,2] The prevalence of common types of CHD is as follows: ventricular septal defect (VSD; 35.6%), atrial septal defect (ASD; 15.4%), patent ductus arteriosus (10.2%), pulmonary valve stenosis (6.2%), and tetralogy of Fallot (TOF; 4.4%).[3] The etiology of CHD is multifactorial, with environmental, genetic, and epigenetic factors playing a crucial role. Genetic factors are considered the primary contributors to CHD, with approximately 40% of cases attributed to known genetic risk factors, including chromosomal aneuploidies, copy number variation (CNV), and pathogenic mutations in specific genes.[4,5]

Scientists from all over the globe have published numerous significant research papers on the genetics of CHD. The initial genetic causes of CHD include chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 21 and trisomy 18,[5,6] with heart abnormalities found in 45.4% of infants diagnosed with trisomy 18 syndrome.[7] Genetic causes of CHD also include diverse CNVs, such as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, cat’s eye syndrome, Jacobsen syndrome, and 8p23.1 deletion syndrome. Advanced technologies, such as whole genome sequencing and whole exome sequencing, have been employed to identify genetic mutations associated with CHD.[6,8] Approximately 400 genes are associated with both syndromic and non-syndromic CHD; however, the genetic explanation remains elusive in approximately 60% of CHD cases.[9–12] Contemporary research predominantly aims to uncover novel mechanisms, such as noncoding elements and genetic mosaicism, which might help explain most CHD cases that currently lack a clear cause.[13–21]

Although many papers have been published on the genetics of CHD, it is still a challenge to learn about the knowledge structure, track developmental trends, and forecast future research directions on this topic. The rise of bibliometric analysis offers an indispensable tool for studying the global research landscape of CHD genetics.[22] This method, which is distinct from the traditional literature review, visualizes information to comprehensively understand current research domains and their evolution, and to predict future research directions within a specific domain.[23–25] Therefore, the aim of this study was to conduct a bibliometric and visualized analysis of scientific papers on CHD genetics and explore the current research landscape and directions for future research.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Source of the data and search strategy

Papers related to the genetics of CHD were searched from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC, Thomson Reuters, Philadelphia, PA) on January 10, 2024, and updated on May 7, 2024. We accessed WoSCC by logging into the institutional account of Shandong University. The search strategies were as follows: {Topic = (congenital heart disease* OR congenital heart defect* OR heart abnormalit* OR malformation of heart* OR aortic coarctation OR aortico-ventricular tunnel OR arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia OR transposition of great vessel* OR cor triatriatum OR coronary vessel anomal* OR crisscross heart OR ventricular septal defect OR bicuspid aortic valve disease OR patent ductus arteriosus OR Ebstein anomal* OR Eisenmenger complex OR heart septal defect* OR atrial septal defect OR pulmonary valve stenosis OR ectopia cordis OR hypoplastic left heart syndrome OR isolated noncompaction of the ventricular myocardium OR Levocardia OR quadricuspid aortic valve OR tetralogy of Fallot OR tricuspid atresia OR trilogy of Fallot OR univentricular heart) AND Topic = (genetic* OR heredity OR epigenetic* OR genom* OR gene*)}. Since the first report specifically addressing the genetics of CHD was published in the 1950s,[26] the time span for this study was from January 1, 1950, to December 31, 2023. This study focused exclusively on “articles” and “reviews” written in English. According to Web of Science, “articles” are original research papers that present new findings or data, including empirical studies, experiments, or theoretical analyses. In contrast, “reviews” summarize, analyze, and synthesize existing research on a particular topic. A flowchart (Fig. 1) was developed to outline the search strategy employed. This approach resulted in the inclusion of 5317 papers in the analysis. The full records of these studies were exported and stored in Microsoft Excel 2021 and EndNote Desktop. Bibliographic metadata, such as authors’ names, affiliations, and nations, along with keywords, were standardized to a uniform format.

As this study was a retrospective analysis of preexisting scholarly literature, it was exempt from Ethics Committee approval.

2.2. Data analysis and visualization

Microsoft Excel 2021 was utilized to generate tables highlighting the top 10 authors and institutions based on publication output, while VOSviewer (version 1.6.19) was employed for data visualization in bibliometric mapping, including co-authorship and keyword co-occurrence analyses. Co-authorship analysis evaluates the relationships among authors, institutions, and countries through the number of coauthored papers, serving as an indicator of collaboration trends and leading entities. In VOSviewer’s visualization maps, nodes represent authors, institutions, or countries, with node size indicating the number of published papers and lines between nodes reflecting collaborations, where line thickness represents link strength (LS)—the number of coauthored papers. The total link strength (TLS) sums all LS values for a given entity, indicating overall connectivity, while the distance of connecting curves illustrates the relevance of their co-authorship. Keyword co-occurrence analysis involves the study of the basic properties of keywords, including their frequency and evolution. There are 3 types of visual maps for keyword co-occurrence analysis: the network map, the density map, and the overlay map.

Additionally, CiteSpace (version 6.3.2) facilitated the identification of rapidly emerging keywords through its burstiness function, offering insights into evolving trends within CHD genetics research.

3. Results

3.1. Annual number of published papers and their corresponding citations

A total of 5317 scholarly papers focusing on the genetics of CHD were identified in the WoSCC. Among these, 4609 papers (86.7%) were categorized as “articles,” while the remaining 708 papers (13.3%) were classified as “reviews.” The earliest study on the topics was published in 1950. Figure 2 displays the annual distribution of these papers along with their citations. There was a modest annual increase in the number of publications until 2018. However, a notable acceleration in the number of papers began in 2019, culminating in 453 papers by 2022, a more than 1.7-fold increase compared to the output in 2018. Papers published over the last 5 years constituted 36.2% of the total, indicating a concentrated surge in research activity. 4. Discussion

4.1. General information

In the present study, a bibliometric analysis was conducted on 5317 papers pertaining to the genetics of CHD. By assessing the source journals, authors/institutions/countries, and their collaborations, the overall status of global research on CHD genetics was ascertained. The American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A contributed the highest number of papers on the genetics of CHD (6.2% of all papers), followed by Pediatric Cardiology (2.1% of all papers).

Elizabeth Goldmuntz contributed the highest number of papers, followed by Maria Cristina Digilio and Bruno Marino. The strongest collaboration was between Bruce D Gelb and Wendy K Chung. They coauthored an article titled “De novo mutations in histone-modifying genes in congenital heart disease,” published in Nature, with 661 citations. Their study compared the frequency of de novo mutations between 362 cases of severe CHD and 264 control subjects using exome sequencing data from parent–offspring trios. Their findings indicated that de novo point mutations in numerous genes collectively account for approximately 10% of severe CHD cases.

The institutions that published papers regarding the genetics of CHD were mainly based in the United States. The University of California System contributed the highest number of papers on the genetics of CHD, followed by Harvard University. However, the total number of citations of papers published by Harvard University was higher than that of papers published by the University of California System. Notably, the top 10 most productive institutions in the field of CHD genetics are also leading in other areas of cardiology, each with unique strengths and specializations. For example, Baylor College of Medicine excels in pediatric cardiology, heart transplantation, and genetic cardiovascular disorders, and has a strong emphasis on clinical and translational research. The United States ranked first in the field of CHD genetics in terms of both the number of papers and total number of citations, indicating that it is in the leading position in this field. China ranked second among the world’s top 10 most productive countries. However, China has limited collaboration with other countries, primarily working with the United States and Canada, and needs to enhance its cooperation in this area.

4.2. Top 10 most-cited papers on the genetics of CHD

This study identified the top 10 most-cited papers on the genetics of CHD, including 4 original articles and 6 literature reviews. The first most-cited paper identified novel heterozygous mutations in the genes responsible for encoding either type I or type II transforming growth factor β receptor in ten families exhibiting a newly characterized human phenotype that included CHD.[27] The second most-cited paper identified 3 distinct NKX2-5 mutations in individuals with CHD.[28] The third most-cited paper discovered new spontaneous mutations in many genes that together account for approximately 10% of severe CHD cases.[29] In the fifth most-cited paper, Hove et al highlighted the significance of investigating the interaction between genetic and epigenetic elements, specifically fluid forces, in the study of embryonic vascular abnormalities and cardiovascular conditions, such as CHD.[31]

The 4th, 6th, 7th, 8th, 9th, and 10th most-cited papers were reviews. The fourth most-cited paper thoroughly reviewed gene regulatory networks involved in the evolutionary and developmental processes of the heart, emphasizing that mutations in elements of the cardiac gene network are implicated in CHD.[30] In their sixth most-cited paper titled “22q11.2 deletion syndrome,” McDonald-McGinn et al conducted a comprehensive analysis of the phenotype and genetic basis of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, a condition that ranks as the second most prevalent cause of CHD.[32] This paper was the most cited paper in the last decade. The seventh most-cited paper compared the occurrence of new mutations in 362 severe CHD cases and 264 controls.[33] The eighth most-cited paper reviewed the genetics, pathobiology, and clinical progression of bicuspid aortic valve disease.[34] The ninth most-cited paper summarized the genetic aspects of CHD and proposed an interdisciplinary approach for children and adults with CHD.[35] The papers that were cited the 10th most frequently discussed genetic research into CHD, emphasizing that the haploinsufficiency of transcription factors like NKX2-5, which plays a vital role in the development of the heart, is an underlying cause of CHD and may account for the typical dominant pattern of its heredity.[36]

4.3. Research hotspots and future directions

In this study, keywords were classified into 8 clusters, with each cluster representing a distinct aspect of CHD genetics. Cluster 1 represents a highly significant field of research in CHD genetics, focusing on chromosomal abnormalities in CHD. Chromosomal abnormalities, such as trisomy 21, 18, 13, and monosomy X, are frequently associated with CHD. Although traditional karyotype analysis can identify aneuploidies and chromosomal abnormalities >5 to 10 Mb, its resolution is insufficient for detecting CNVs. Wang et al suggested the adoption of chromosomal microarray analysis for more refined detection of submicroscopic abnormalities, especially when karyotyping or fluorescence in situ hybridization results appear normal. This approach is particularly valuable for diagnosing non-isolated CHD and septal defects.[37,38]

The epigenetics of CHD is discussed in cluster 2. Epigenetic modifications, which are extra-genomic mechanisms that do not entail changes in the DNA sequence but can regulate gene expression by affecting transcription or inhibiting translation, have increasingly been linked to cardiac development and CHD pathogenesis.[39] A study investigating aberrant DNA methylation patterns in the cardiac tissue of fetuses with syndromic and nonsyndromic CHD identified numerous hypermethylated CpG sites within the GATA4 gene. Additionally, elevated levels of GATA4 transcripts were observed in fetuses with both syndromic and nonsyndromic CHD compared to healthy controls, indicating a potential role of this epigenetic alteration in the pathogenesis of cardiac malformation.[40] In another study, Huang et al discovered a significant downregulation of miR-184 in individuals with cyanotic CHD compared to that in control subjects. Furthermore, in vitro investigations demonstrated that the inhibition of miR-184 substantially reduced cell viability and triggered apoptosis under hypoxic conditions, indicating a potential role of miRNA-184 in the pathogenesis of cyanotic CHD through its effects on cardiomyocyte proliferation and apoptosis.[41]

Cluster 3 deals with the genetics of adult CHD. In recent decades, there has been a growing population of adults with CHD requiring specialized care, many of whom have not undergone genetic testing or counseling, particularly those with simple or moderate forms of CHD.[42] The significance of genetics in the field of adult CHD is steadily increasing.[43] Silversides et al conducted a study on rare CNVs in a cohort of 433 unrelated adults with TOF and/or pulmonary atresia, using high-resolution microarrays. Their findings revealed that adults with TOF exhibited a higher prevalence of large rare genetic CNVs than individuals without CHD. These results suggest that although individually rare, CNVs collectively play a significant role in the genetic predisposition to TOF.[44] Cluster 4 primarily involved genetic syndromes associated with CHD. Certain genetic syndromes, such as Down syndrome and CHARGE syndrome, may present with clinical manifestations, including CHD. Down syndrome is the most common type of CHD syndrome. Approximately 50% of newborn babies with Down syndrome have CHD at birth.[45] The exact mechanisms controlling the occurrence and structure of CHD in individuals with Down syndrome remain unclear. Several genes found in the CHD critical region on chromosome 21 have been deemed to be pathogenetically associated with atrioventricular canal defects, such as Down syndrome cell adhesion molecule (DSCAM), collagen type VI alpha 1 (COL6A1), and regulator of calcineurin 1 (RCAN1).[46]

Cluster 5 focused on the genotype–phenotype correlation in CHD. A detailed examination of the cardiac anatomy has revealed that distinct morphological subtypes of CHD are associated with genetic disorders. The association between specific cardiac anatomical patterns and certain genetic abnormalities, such as trisomy, deletion, and mutation, implies that unique morphogenetic processes influenced by genetic factors can lead to the manifestation of specific cardiac phenotypes. Sun et al investigated the relationship between genotypes and phenotypes in individuals with Noonan syndrome-associated CHD. Seven genes harbored disease-associated mutations, and each genotype showed distinct phenotypic characteristics. Specifically, RIT1, SOS1, PTPN11, and SOS2 were associated with pulmonary valve stenosis on echocardiography, whereas RAF1 was associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Notably, patients with BRAF variants exhibit both hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and pulmonary valve stenosis.[47] Cluster 6 emphasized the interactions between environmental and genetic factors in the pathogenesis of CHD. Interactions between environmental teratogens and genetic predispositions can significantly influence the manifestation of CHD phenotypes.[5,48–50] Chowdhury et al conducted a case–control study of genome-wide maternal DNA methylation and presented initial proof of a connection between maternal gene-specific DNA methylation and CHD. Recently, Chen et al conducted a study of 585 patients with CHD and 600 controls to investigate the associations between maternal intake of folic acid and 9 single nucleotide polymorphisms of the GATA4 gene in patients with nonchromosomal CHD. The study revealed that maternal folic acid supplementation, specific genetic variations of the GATA4 gene, and the interaction between genes and the environment were all strongly associated with CHD in the offspring.[51] Further research should prioritize the identification of genetic abnormalities in individuals who have been exposed to environmental hazards to ascertain the role of gene–environment interactions in the development of CHD.

Cluster 7 revealed a different area of research interest that involved the etiology of CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension. CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension is a rapidly progressive condition with an uncertain physiological course. Therefore, there is a growing need to elucidate the precise mechanisms of molecular alterations, which is essential for the identification of novel therapeutic approaches.[52] Advancements in high-throughput sequencing and tandem mass tag-based proteomics have led to significant developments in our understanding of the signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms underlying CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension. In a recent study, Chang et al identified significant expression of transpulmonary exosomal miR-21, particularly in patients with CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension, using next-generation sequencing analysis.[53] Cluster 8 emphasizes the molecular genetics of CHD via high-throughput genomic technology, reflecting the surge in studies prompted by advancements in next-generation sequencing technologies. These advancements have facilitated the identification of minute submicroscopic genomic alterations within the coding exons of genes implicated in CHD.[8] Germline mutations have been discovered in transcription factor genes, such as NKX2-5, TBX5, and GATA4 in patients with CHD. A notable study by Zhao et al involved a detailed genetic exploration of a consanguineous family spanning 4 generations affected by CHD in an autosomal dominant manner. This investigation revealed a novel heterozygous mutation, c.553G>T, in SOX17.[54] Nonetheless, the concept of monogenic causation is challenged by the complexity and diversity of CHD manifestations, indicating that a single genetic perspective may not fully elucidate the heterogeneity observed in CHD phenotypes.[55,56]

To anticipate future research directions, we utilized the VOSviewer software for keyword overlay mapping and the CiteSpace software for keyword burstiness analysis. The overlay map provides a visual depiction of the temporal evolution of keywords within each cluster, starting with the research emphasis on “chromosomal abnormalities in CHD” and transitioning to the current hotspots of “the interactions between environmental and genetic factors in the pathogenesis of CHD,” “genetic etiology of CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension,” and “molecular genetics of CHD via high-throughput genomic technology.” Based on this visualization, it can be inferred that the latter 3 clusters have the potential to emerge as popular areas of future research. Furthermore, the identification of citation bursts revealed that the keywords “whole exome sequencing,” “whole genome sequencing,” and “case–control study” began gaining prominence in 2017, 2018, and 2020, respectively, implying that these areas of research have consistently garnered attention in recent years and can potentially serve as future avenues for studying the genetics of CHD. Clusters 6, 7, and 8 from the network map generated by VOSviewer contain these 3 keywords.

4.4. Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the research hotspots and trends in the genetics of CHD. It also highlights authors, countries, and institutions with the highest productivity, emphasizing their potential for collaboration. Nevertheless, it is crucial to consider the different limitations when analyzing the results. First, studies on the genetics of CHD were retrieved from the WoSCC database. However, the inclusions of papers from alternative databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar were not considered. Furthermore, although we made every effort to include a wide variety of terms in our search criteria, there is still a chance that some terms were excluded. Hence, it is possible that the search results did not encompass all the documents regarding the genetics of CHD. Additionally, the limitations imposed by the WOS classification system regarding authors, articles, and institutions may lead to inconsistencies, such as comparing the University of California system as a whole with individual institutions. Second, bibliographic metadata are subject to change over time, suggesting that future interpretations may yield different outcomes. Additional revisions are required to enhance the accuracy of this study. Third, no papers written in languages other than English were included in this study. Hence, there is a chance that we may have missed significant research in other languages. For example, the impact of Chinese researchers’ findings may be underestimated because of their frequent publications in domestic journals.

5. Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first bibliometric analysis of papers concerning the genetics of CHD. The number of research papers on the genetics of CHD is increasing annually overall, reflecting a growing interest among researchers in this field. The United States is a pioneering country, while the American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A emerged as the most productive journal vis-à-vis research on the genetics of CHD. TOF, VSD, and ASD are global research topics. The interactions between environmental and genetic factors in the pathogenesis of CHD, genetic etiology of CHD-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension, and molecular genetics of CHD via high-throughput genomic technology, are possible areas of future research into CHD genetics.

In summary, this study illustrates research hotspots, trends, and potential future directions in the field of CHD genetics using VOSviewer- and CiteSpace-based bibliometric analysis. The findings of this study will facilitate readers’ prompt and effective access to valuable information in this field. Moreover, the study identifies leading researchers and institutions, and assists scholars in identifying potential research collaborators.
