Baseline serum folic acid as a prognostic biomarker in metastatic renal cell carcinoma treated with targeted therapy
Authors: Oktay Halit Aktepe, Rezan Berkay Izgor, Tugce Ulasli, Ilkay Tugba Unek, Elif Atag, Huseyin Salih Semiz, Aziz Karaoglu, Mustafa Erman, Suayib Yalcin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerum folic acid (SFA) is a key component of one-carbon metabolism. Its prognostic significance in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) has not been established. Thus, this study investigated the association between SFA levels and survival outcomes in patients with mRCC receiving targeted therapy.MethodsThis retrospective dual-center cohort included 220 mRCC patients. Baseline SFA levels measured within three months before treatment initiation were recorded. Receiver operating characteristic analysis determined an optimal SFA cutoff of 5.5 ng/mL for overall survival (OS), categorizing patients into high (> 5.5 ng/mL) and low (≤ 5.5 ng/mL) SFA groups. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models identified independent prognostic factors.ResultsThe median follow-up was 34.4 months. The median progression-free survival (PFS) and OS for the entire cohort were 10 and 24.0 months, respectively. Patients with high SFA had longer PFS (11.1 vs. 9.8 months; p = 0.034) and OS (31.3 vs. 20.8 months; p < 0.001) compared to those with low SFA. In multivariate analysis, SFA was not an independent predictor of PFS. However, low SFA independently predicted inferior OS (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.04, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.40–2.99; p < 0.001), along with IMDC intermediate- (HR: 2.19, 95% CI 1.11–4.30, p = 0.023) and poor-risk categories (HR: 5.96, 95% CI 2.90-12.25, p < 0.001 ) and presence of brain metastasis (HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.10–3.37; p = 0.020).ConclusionsBaseline SFA was independently associated with OS and may serve as a readily available host-related prognostic marker; further studies are warranted to validate these findings and to determine whether SFA adds prognostic value beyond established risk models.Keywords: Folic acid, Metastatic renal cell carcinoma, Prognostic biomarker, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a common malignancy of the kidney and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Approximately 30% of patients are diagnosed with metastatic disease, and a substantial fraction of those initially treated for localized RCC eventually develop metastases [2]. Although targeted therapies (TT) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have significantly improved survival outcomes in metastatic RCC (mRCC), the prognosis for mRCC remains heterogeneous, with an approximate 5-year survival rate of 15% [3]. The International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium (IMDC) prognostic model incorporates clinical performance and key laboratory parameters to estimate prognosis and assign risk categories [4]. However, new biomarkers are necessary to improve prognostic precision and provide better individualized treatment.

Serum folic acid (SFA), a key component of one-carbon metabolism, takes part in the synthesis of nucleotides and methyl groups required for DNA replication and repair [5]. Because of its fundamental role in cellular proliferation, its biological significance has been widely examined in the context of cancer biology [6, 7]. Both low and high SFA levels have been implicated in tumor biology, with deficiency potentially suppressing early growth but inducing genomic instability, while excess may enhance tumor progression [8, 9], indicating a bidirectional influence that depends on timing and exposure level.

In RCC, limited research has explored the relationship between folate metabolism and clinical outcomes. A large prospective study showed that SFA was not significantly associated with survival outcomes [10]. However, recent analyses suggest a potential influence of folate pathways on RCC biology. For example, Qin et al. demonstrated that elevated expression of specific folate-metabolism enzymes is linked to more advanced tumor stages and poorer prognosis in RCC, whereas higher SFA levels appear linked to better cancer-specific survival [11]. Moreover, a recent prospective cohort study identified an L-shaped association between SFA levels and mortality in cancer patients, with lower SFA levels associated with increased risk of cancer-related death [12]. Taken together, SFA deficiency may indicate an unfavorable prognosis in mRCC, possibly reflecting aggressive tumor characteristics or impaired nutritional status. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between SFA levels and survival outcomes in mRCC patients receiving TT.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This retrospective, dual-center cohort study was conducted in the Departments of Medical Oncology at Hacettepe University (Ankara, Turkey) and Dokuz Eylül University (Izmir, Turkey). Demographic and clinicopathological data, including age, sex, histologic subtype, tumor grade, metastatic sites, and IMDC risk category, were retrieved from the electronic medical systems of both centers. The study population comprised patients with histologically confirmed mRCC who received TT, including sunitinib, pazopanib, or cabozantinib between December 2015 and December 2025. Patients were eligible if baseline SFA measurements were available within three months prior to the initiation of first-line TT. SFA measurements were performed in the central laboratories of each participating institution using standardized electrochemiluminescence immunoassay platforms. Both centers operate under routine internal and external quality-control programs and follow nationally accredited laboratory standards. The reference ranges and calibration procedures were comparable across centers, minimizing potential inter-laboratory variability.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and categorical data as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between low and high SFA categories were made using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from start of first-line systemic therapy to disease progression or death, whichever occurred first, censored at date of last disease evaluation for patients without progression. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment start to death from any cause, with living patients censored at last follow-up. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cut-off value of SFA for predicting OS. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were performed to assess associations between clinical variables and survival outcomes. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariate Cox regression models. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 220 patients with mRCC were included in the analysis. The baseline patient characteristics stratified according to SFA levels are presented in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 60 years (IQR 54–67), and 28.6% of the patients were female. The majority of tumors were of clear-cell histology (77.3%), and high-grade (grade III–IV) morphology was present in 70.9% of the cases. The lung was the most frequent metastatic site (73.2%), followed by the liver (23.2%), bone (30%), and brain (8.2%). According to the IMDC risk classification, 11.8% of patients were categorized as favorable, 64.1% as intermediate, and 24.1% as poor-risk.

As shown in Fig. 1, ROC analysis indicated that the optimal SFA cut-off for predicting OS was 5.5 ng/mL (area under the curve [AUC] 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.82, p < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 75%. Accordingly, patients were stratified into low (≤ 5.5 ng/mL) and high (> 5.5 ng/mL) SFA groups. Patients in the high SFA group (> 5.5 ng/mL; n = 88, 40%) and the low SFA group (≤ 5.5 ng/mL; n = 132, 60%) had similar age distributions (60 vs. 61 years, p = 0.780) and comparable sex ratios (female 30.7% vs. 27.3%, p = 0.584). Clear-cell histology predominated in both groups (75.0% vs. 78.8%, p = 0.511), and the proportion of high-grade (III–IV) tumors was nearly identical (69.3% vs. 72.0%, p = 0.671). Regarding metastatic distribution, the lung was the most frequent site in both groups (68.2% vs. 76.5%, p = 0.172), followed by the liver (21.6% vs. 24.2%, p = 0.648), bone (28.4% vs. 31.1%, p = 0.674), and brain (4.5% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.108). Patients with high SFA levels more frequently presented with a single metastatic site compared with those with low SFA (79.5% vs. 64.4%), whereas involvement of two or more metastatic sites was more common in the low-SFA group (35.6% vs. 20.5%; p = 0.016). IMDC risk classification also showed no statistically significant difference between patients with high and low SFA levels: favorable 13.6% vs. 10.6%, intermediate 62.5% vs. 65.2%, and poor 23.9% vs. 24.2% (p = 0.790).

The distribution of systemic therapies according to SFA levels is presented in Table 2. In the first-line setting, pazopanib was administered to 44.1% of the overall cohort, followed by sunitinib (36.8%) and cabozantinib (19.1%). Treatment distribution differed significantly between SFA groups (p = 0.004). Among patients with high SFA (> 5.5 ng/mL), pazopanib was more frequently used (47.7%), whereas sunitinib was more common in the low-SFA group (44.7%). Cabozantinib was administered to 27.3% of patients in the high-SFA group and 13.6% in the low-SFA group. In the second-line setting, nivolumab was the most commonly administered agent (43.9%), followed by axitinib (29.0%), everolimus (23.4%), and cabozantinib (3.7%), with no significant difference between SFA groups (p = 0.568). Similarly, third-line treatment distribution did not differ significantly according to SFA status (p = 0.269), with axitinib being the most frequently used agent (56.5%), followed by nivolumab (23.9%) and everolimus (17.4%).

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up time for the cohort was 34.4 months (95% CI 29.1–39.6), during which 150 of the 220 patients (68.2%) died. The median PFS and OS for the entire cohort were 10 months (95% CI 8.6–11.6) and 24 months (95% CI 19.6–28.4), respectively. Across IMDC groups, median PFS was 16.8 months (95% CI 9.4–24.2) in the favorable IMDC group, 10 months (95% CI 8.1–12.0) in the intermediate group, and 7.5 months (95% CI 6.7–8.2) in the poor group (p < 0.001), and median OS was 78 months (95% CI 13.1–142.9), 24.7 months (95% CI 19.4–30.0), and 10 months (95% CI 8.4–11.6) in the respective IMDC categories (p < 0.001). Patients with high SFA had longer median PFS than those with low SFA (11.1 months vs. 9.8 months; p = 0.034, respectively, Fig. 2A) and also demonstrated superior median OS (31.3 months vs. 20.8 months; p < 0.001, respectively, Fig. 3A). In the clear-cell subgroup (Fig. 2B), median PFS was longer in patients with high SFA (11.8 months, 95% CI 8.2–15.4) than in those with low SFA (10.3 months, 95% CI 8.8–11.7) (p = 0.041), whereas in non–clear-cell mRCC (Fig. 2C), median PFS values of 7.9 months (95% CI 5.9–9.9) and 7.4 months (95% CI 5.1–9.7) for the high and low SFA groups, respectively (p = 0.555). In the clear-cell subgroup (Fig. 3B), median OS was significantly longer in patients with high SFA (31.3 months, 95% CI 28.4–34.3) than in those with low SFA (21.5 months, 95% CI 17.4–25.6) (p = 0.002). However, in non–clear-cell mRCC (Fig. 3C), median OS did not differ significantly between high and low SFA groups (22.3 vs. 19.0 months, p = 0.149).

Across IMDC risk categories, in the IMDC favorable-risk group, patients with high SFA had a longer median OS than those with low SFA (36.3 months, 95% CI 20.6–51.9 vs. 20.6 months, 95% CI 3.9–37.3; p = 0.042, respectively). In the IMDC intermediate-risk group, patients with low SFA had a median OS of 29.0 months (95% CI 25–32.9), whereas the median OS could not be estimated for the high-SFA group (p < 0.001). However, in the poor-risk group, median OS was comparable between low- and high-SFA patients (21.3 months, 95% CI 9.47–33.1 vs. 21 months, 95% CI 10.7–31.2, p = 0.642, respectively).

In the univariate Cox analysis (Table 3), low baseline SFA was associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.02–1.89; p = 0.034). The other variables significantly associated with PFS included non-clear-cell histology (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.02–2.06; p = 0.038) and IMDC risk category, with intermediate- and poor-risk groups showing inferior PFS compared with the favorable group (HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.02–2.77; p = 0.039 and HR 3.20, 95% CI 1.88–5.58; p < 0.001, respectively). Lung (p = 0.164), bone (p = 0.187), and brain metastases (p = 0.141) had p-values ≤ 0.20 and were therefore included in the multivariate analysis. In the multivariate Cox analysis (Table 3), low baseline SFA was not independently associated with PFS after adjustment (HR 1.26, 95% CI 0.92–1.72; p = 0.242). The independent variables in predicting PFS were non-clear-cell histology (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.01–2.08; p = 0.043) and IMDC risk category, with intermediate- and poor-risk groups showing inferior PFS compared with the favorable group (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.17–3.13; p = 0.009 and HR 3.08, 95% CI 1.74–5.47; p < 0.001, respectively).

In the univariate Cox analysis (Table 4), low baseline SFA was associated with inferior OS (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.27–2.68; p = 0.001). The other variables associated with OS included bone metastasis (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.05–2.07; p = 0.023), brain metastasis (HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.25–3.68; p = 0.005), and IMDC risk category, with intermediate- and poor-risk groups showing inferior OS compared with the favorable-risk group (HR 2.26, 95% CI 1.17–4.37; p = 0.015 and HR 5.21, 95% CI 2.58–10.54; p < 0.001, respectively). In the multivariate analysis (Table 4), low SFA remained an independent prognostic marker for OS (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.40–2.99, p < 0.001). The other independent predictors of OS were IMDC intermediate-risk (HR 2.19, 95% CI 1.11–4.30; p = 0.023), IMDC poor-risk (HR 5.96, 95% CI 2.90–12.25; p < 0.001), and the presence of brain metastasis (HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.10–3.37; p = 0.020).

Discussion

This study was conducted to explore the prognostic significance of SFA levels in patients with mRCC, addressing a clinically underexplored aspect of folate metabolism despite its key role in tumor biology and nutrition. We showed that low baseline SFA remained an independent predictor of poor OS after adjustment for established prognostic variables, including IMDC risk category and metastatic burden. Although SFA was not associated with PFS, its association with OS suggests that systemic folate status may serve as an indicator of overall patient resilience, nutritional status, and treatment tolerance. Collectively, our findings indicate that low SFA may serve as a simple, clinically accessible biomarker reflecting metabolic and systemic host factors influencing OS in mRCC. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first clinical studies to demonstrate a relationship between SFA and survival outcomes in mRCC treated with TT.

The OS–PFS discordance in our cohort—SFA independently associated with OS but not PFS—is clinically plausible because OS reflects not only radiographic progression but also host-related factors such as competing non–cancer mortality, treatment tolerability, and post-progression survival (PPS) [13, 14]. This distinction is particularly relevant in mRCC treated with targeted agents, where PFS is a recognized imperfect surrogate for OS because subsequent treatment strategies and variability in PPS can meaningfully influence survival even among patients with comparable initial disease control [15]. In this context, low baseline SFA may reflect reduced physiologic reserve and an adverse nutritional or inflammatory milieu, predisposing patients to treatment-related toxicities, dose interruptions, and other clinical complications that worsen OS without necessarily shortening PFS. Taken together, low baseline SFA may be linked to poorer OS by reflecting host vulnerability and competing risks rather than faster disease progression, which could explain the absence of a parallel association with PFS.

Our findings are in general agreement with recent literature that links low SFA status to worse cancer prognosis. In a prospective cohort of 4,535 patients, Wu et al. reported that low SFA levels were significantly associated with worsened survival outcomes, with an L-shaped relationship between SFA levels and mortality [12]. Additionally, in a bioinformatic study focused on RCC, increased SFA levels have been linked to improved cancer-specific survival [11]. In the same study, the authors proposed an integrated role of folate and glutamine metabolism in RCC progression, particularly noting that the folate-cycle enzyme methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase/cyclohydrolase (MTHFD2) was markedly linked to aggressive pathological features and poor prognosis [11]. MTHFD2, a mitochondrial enzyme upregulated in many cancers including RCC, takes part in one-carbon metabolism and is associated with advanced disease and unfavorable prognosis [16]. Additionally, folate receptor alpha (FRα) is overexpressed in numerous cancers, including RCC, facilitating more efficient folate uptake [17, 18]. It is conceivable that overexpression of MTHFD2 and FRα could lower circulating folate levels, worsening any existing dietary deficiency in RCC.

Beyond direct tumor cell effects, low SFA likely reflects the overall nutritional and physiologic status of the host, which can influence cancer outcomes. Folate deficiency is often associated with other markers of malnutrition and systemic inflammation [19, 20]. Such patients may tolerate systemic treatments poorly, require dose reductions, or have impaired immunity, all of which could contribute to inferior survival [21, 22]. The importance of maintaining adequate folate for patient well-being is highlighted by a clinical observation that supplementing folic acid can ameliorate treatment-related toxicities. Fristrup et al. reported that adding folic acid (1–5 mg daily) for mRCC patients treated with TT reduced the severity of oral mucositis [23]. Therefore, it can be suggested that enhanced tolerability may allow patients to maintain therapy for a longer duration, indirectly contributing to superior survival outcomes. In the present study, although folate supplementation was not standardized, it is plausible that low SFA levels might predispose to treatment-related toxicity and dose delays, consequently resulting in worse OS.

Although ICI–based combinations have become the preferred first-line therapy in mRCC, TTs remain an essential component of current treatment algorithms [24]. Single-agent TT continue to be used in patients who are ineligible for ICI, including those with autoimmune disease, organ transplantation, significant frailty, or prior severe immune-related toxicity [25]. Additionally, they continue to play a pivotal role in subsequent-line therapy following failure of ICI-containing regimens [26]. Therefore, despite the widespread adoption of ICI–TT combinations, TT monotherapy continues to have therapeutic relevance, highlighting the importance of identifying prognostic biomarkers in TT-treated mRCC patients.

SFA should be interpreted primarily as a host-related biomarker, because it is influenced by non–tumor-related factors and responds to recent dietary intake, such that a single baseline measurement may not reflect a patient’s usual nutritional milieu [27]. However, red blood cell (RBC) folate is considered a more robust indicator of long-term folate status [27]. In addition, the systemic inflammatory response may reduce circulating levels of many micronutrients regardless of underlying nutritional state [28], introducing residual confounding when serum micronutrient biomarkers are interpreted in observational cancer cohorts. Therefore, the association between baseline SFA and outcomes in our study is best interpreted as host-related prognostic information reflecting the patient’s nutritional and inflammatory milieu, rather than as evidence of treatment-mediated or causal effects. Formal evaluation of whether SFA provides incremental prognostic value beyond established risk models will require further analyses and external validation.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective, dual-center design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounders. Second, we did not have serial SFA assessments; therefore, temporal changes in SFA during treatment and the potential effects of dietary counseling or supplementation could not be evaluated. Third, although the 5.5 ng/mL cut-off derived from ROC analysis is clinically reasonable, it may not accurately capture the underlying continuous association between SFA levels and survival outcomes. Therefore, we additionally modeled SFA as a continuous variable (Table 5), yet the ROC-derived threshold may still be subject to optimism bias and requires external validation in larger, independent cohorts. Fourth, information on dietary folate intake, supplementation, treatment duration, dose modifications, and toxicity profiles was not systematically recorded, preventing evaluation of potential mediators linking folate biology to clinical outcomes. Finally, mechanistic biomarkers including, tumor expression of MTHFD2 or FRα, circulating homocysteine, and DNA methylation parameters, were not available, limiting biological inference regarding folate metabolism and RCC progression.

Conclusion

Low baseline SFA remained independently associated with OS in patients with mRCC treated with TT after adjustment for established clinical risk factors, suggesting prognostic information related to the host nutritional–inflammatory milieu. As an inexpensive and routinely obtainable laboratory marker, SFA may refine current stratification tools by identifying patients with impaired metabolic or nutritional reserve who are predisposed to poorer survival outcomes. Thus, integrating SFA measurement into baseline evaluation may enhance prognostic precision and help guide timely supportive strategies. Consequently, prospective studies incorporating serial SFA and RBC folate measurements, alongside mechanistic biomarkers, are needed to validate these findings and to assess the impact of folate restoration on treatment tolerance and survival outcomes.
