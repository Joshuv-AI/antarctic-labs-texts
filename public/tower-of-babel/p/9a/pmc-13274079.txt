TASK PACT: a framework for exploring opportunities to expand pharmacists’ roles in supporting equitable cancer control in low-income countries in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Otuto Amarauche Chukwu, Beverley M Essue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer is a complex global health issue that continues to overburden healthcare systems worldwide. Estimates suggest that the burden of cancer will double in the next decade, further straining healthcare systems if effective interventions are not scaled up. One critical barrier to scaling up cancer interventions is the shortage of the cancer workforce, which is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in sub-Saharan Africa, many of which do not have a single oncologist. Task shifting offers a potential strategy to expand the cancer workforce. However, there is limited evidence on task shifting to advanced clinical professionals, such as pharmacists, who could help improve access to cancer control services. Pharmacists are integral members of the healthcare team and possess advanced clinical knowledge that can be leveraged to enhance cancer care. In many LMICs, pharmacists serve as the first point of contact for health complaints and minor ailments, positioning them to aid in early diagnosis and intervention for cancer.The objective of this study is to develop a conceptual framework, TASK PACT (Task Shifting Concepts for Pharmacists to Advance Cancer Control and Treatment), to systematically assess the feasibility of task shifting selected cancer control services to pharmacists. The framework was developed through an integrative review of the literature on task shifting, and is grounded in a synthesis of intellectual capital, organizational readiness for change, motivation, and change theories, and the theoretical domains framework. It provides a structured approach to explore the perspectives of pharmacists, oncologists, policymakers, and patients regarding the acceptability, readiness, and practical implementation of expanding pharmacists’ roles in cancer control.The TASK PACT framework could serve as a practical tool for research that informs policy, guides implementation research, and supports the design of scalable, context-specific collaborative models of cancer care in sub-Saharan Africa. Its application could strengthen the oncology workforce, improve access to cancer control services, and generate evidence to inform broader health system strategies in LMICs.Keywords: Cancer control, Pharmacists, Sub-Saharan Africa, Conceptual framework

Background

Cancer is the second leading cause of death globally, accounting for about 10 million deaths annually; the highest proportion of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with as much as 70% of global cancer deaths [1]. Specifically, in sub-Saharan Africa, cancer mortality has been projected to get worse as the incidence of cancer could almost double by 2040 [2]. This high burden of cancer is driven by issues such as poor access to diagnosis and treatment, which leads to late disease presentation, hence poor prognosis [3]. This is compounded by a critical shortage of oncologists [4]. For instance, Nigeria, which is the most populated country and one of the largest economies in sub-Saharan Africa, has an annual cancer incidence and mortality of 102,000 and 72,000, respectively, giving a mortality to incidence ratio of 0.71 [4]. Despite this high incidence and mortality rate, there are less than 30 clinical oncologists to cater for a population of over 200 million people, according to a 2018 study [4]. Worse still, some other LMICs in the region such as Togo, South Sudan and Sierra Leone have no oncologists [4]. In contrast, high-income countries such as Canada and Australia, with far lesser populations have 517 and 448 clinical oncologists, respectively. [4] In addition, multidisciplinary cancer care teams have been mainstreamed into established models of care in most high-income countries [5, 6]. However, in LMICs, there is limited data to inform the development of policies and models of care which are critical to addressing the high burden of cancer in these countries. [3]

Effective prevention and control of cancer require policy interventions to reduce inequities in access to cancer care, including policies aimed at building collaborative cancer control and care teams [7]. These collaborations bring together health professionals and various organizations for cancer prevention activities at the population level and develop multidisciplinary approaches to improve access to cancer control services [7]. These strategies have been implemented in high-income countries and have been shown to be useful in addressing inequities and improving access to cancer prevention, control, and care. [8]

Task shifting offers a strategic option for efficient use of human resources for health to address critical shortages in health workforce for diseases with high burden like cancer. It involves redistributing tasks amongst health workers, from highly specialized health workers to those with shorter training, to improve access to care [9]. Task shifting has proven to be helpful in expanding access to care for various noncommunicable diseases in low resource settings [10, 11]. A meta-analysis of task shifting for the management of high blood pressure in LMICs showed that training and collaborating with pharmacists, nurses, dietitians, and community health workers to initiate lifestyle interventions, therapy and blood pressure monitoring was effective in managing high blood pressure [10]. Evidence also shows that task shifting to nonphysician health workers for diabetes management was effective in engaging, screening, examining, referring and following up patients in the community and that the quality of interventions were comparable to standard care and led to early recognition of complications, decreased unnecessary physician consultations and better glycaemic control [11].

Therefore, task shifting could be worth considering in expanding access to cancer control services in LMICs, given the shortage of cancer care specialists and the high burden of cancer in these countries. The focus on cancer is because of its rising burden which has been projected to more than double in less than two decades leading to more burden on health systems. Also, the high mortality ratio especially in LMICs requires attention, in addition to the significant cost burden of the disease and poor prognosis due to late presentation in these settings [2, 3].

Literature on task shifting for cancer control

A systematic literature search on four databases (Pubmed, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL) from inception to June 2023 returned no hits for task shifting of cancer care and control services to pharmacists. This suggests a paucity of literature on this issue. There are, however, some available but sparse evidence on task shifting for cancer control in general. One of the few evidences available was published recently in a systematic review that sought to better understand the scope of task shifting to improve access to cancer control [12]. The findings suggest that task shifting to nonphysician health workers could be effective in improving access to certain areas of cancer control such as education and screening [12]. Most of these tasks were shifted to less clinically advanced health workers such as community health workers [12]. None of the studies in this review reported task shifting to pharmacists or other advanced clinical practice health professionals. Also, there was limited evidence on task shifting for other critical cancer control services such as diagnosis, therapy management, palliative care, and survivorship. This suggests the need for further research on how access to these critical elements of cancer care can be improved through inter-professional collaboration involving advanced clinical practice professionals like pharmacists. This could help generate evidence for exploring nonspecialist or community models of care in expanding access to cancer control services in LMICs beyond training new oncologists which requires considerable time.

The rationale for pharmacist’s role in cancer control

The focus on the role of pharmacists in an expanded model of care for cancer in LMICs is based on emerging evidence. Pharmacists are considered to be amongst the most trusted health professionals in LMICs and are often the first port of call for health complaints and minor ailments [13]. As the Doctor of Pharmacy degree programme gains traction in LMICs, newly trained and some existing community pharmacists are now being exposed to more advanced clinical knowledge of various noncommunicable diseases including cancer [14, 15].

Some LMICs are beginning to make provisions for pharmacists’ inclusion in cancer control plans. For example, Nigeria’s National Strategic Cancer Control Plan [16], is comprehensive in its scope and supports multidisciplinary workforce approaches to cancer control, including supporting the training and involvements of pharmacists.

Additionally, the ever-increasing rate of health worker migration in many LMICs continues to deplete the cancer workforce and pharmacists can help fill some of the gaps. This is because pharmacists are less likely to migrate compared to physicians because they face more licensing and practice requirements while migrating compared to physicians [17, 18]. Evidence suggests that over the past decade, pharmacists’ migration have been on the decline in some LMICs [17], and many foreign trained pharmacists from some of these countries continue to return home to practise [18]. Furthermore, an analysis of the pharmacy workforce in Africa shows a growing pharmacy workforce [14]. However, the opposite is the case for physicians in some of these settings. For instance, between 2016 and 2018, over 9000 physicians emigrated from Nigeria to other high-income countries [19] including consultant physicians like oncologists. Furthermore, 90% of recent consultant physicians in Nigeria have indicated an intent to emigrate [19]. Similar trends of physician migration has also been reported across sub-Saharan Africa with the highest volumes observed in countries like South Africa, Ghana, Ethiopia, Sudan, and Uganda [20].

Therefore, pharmacists potentially offer a viable option for improving access to cancer control services. There is an emerging body of evidence on how community pharmacists have supported improved access to cancer control services. For example, in Denmark and Portugal, pharmacists administer vaccines against Hepatitis B and Human Papillomavirus to prevent liver cirrhosis and cervical cancer [21]. In Switzerland, Spain, and France, pharmacists contribute to colorectal cancer screening, either by performing the tests directly or distributing screening kits to patients [21].

Similarly, evidence from Norway shows that community pharmacists can play a role in skin cancer screening [22]. Beyond prevention and early detection, pharmacists in Belgium and the Netherlands provide medication therapy management and palliative care through the Farmabuddy model, which reached over 600,000 patients in its first year [21]. In addition, pharmacists can support patients undergoing treatment by using Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) to monitor quality of life and tailor supportive care [23]. These examples suggest that pharmacists could play valuable roles across prevention, detection, therapy management, and supportive care, and highlight the importance of exploring their capacity within collaborative cancer control models.

Thus, there is a need to explore interventions that could leverage their capacity and skills in expanding access to cancer control services. However, before venturing into this, it is important to lay the critical groundwork to hinge such interventions on. This could involve assessing cancer care specialists and their willingness to participate in task shifting programmes, assessing the readiness of pharmacists for task shifting in cancer control, assessing the current policy landscape, and determining if task shifting interventions could be supported, and understanding patient perspectives and acceptance of task shifting. To inform health workforce planning efforts and respond to the pressing shortage of cancer workforce in LMICs [4], this article focuses on the development of a conceptual framework for examining the feasibility of expanding the scope of practice of community pharmacists. This could lead to more expansive models of cancer control that entail task shifting of' specific cancer control services in addressing the growing burden of cancer in sub-Saharan Africa.

Development of a conceptual framework for assessing feasibility of task shifting of cancer control services to pharmacists

Conceptual frameworks help researchers to better understand issues related to their intended study. They are based on theories, beliefs, personal experiences, and findings from previous research. They are a system of assumptions, theories, expectations, and concepts that informs research and explains, either graphically or in narrative terms or both, the key issues that a piece of research seeks to study and the relationship between them [24].

This article aims to develop a conceptual framework to guide the assessment of oncologists, pharmacists, policymakers, and patients with a view to understanding the feasibility of developing task shifting strategies for improving access to cancer control services in sub-Saharan Africa. The conceptual framework was drawn from critical theories that are relevant to task shifting which were identified by systematically searching and reviewing relevant literature. To identify relevant theories, a systematic search was conducted from inception across four databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL) using a combination of search terms such as “task shifting”, “task sharing”, “task reallocation”, “scope of practice”, “pharmacists”, “health workforce”, “low- and middle-income countries”, and various noncommunicable diseases. To capture conceptual and theoretical work, additional keywords such as “conceptual framework”, “theoretical framework”, “implementation science”, “behaviour change theory”, “organizational readiness”, “intellectual capital”, “motivation theory”, “change management”, and “health systems strengthening” were included. Articles were eligible if they reported on theoretical or conceptual approaches to task shifting or task sharing in cancer control or other noncommunicable diseases.

The theories and framework included in developing the framework are the intellectual capital theory, organizational readiness for change theory, the theoretical domains framework, motivation theory, and change theory.

Intellectual capital theory

Intellectual capital theory suggests that relationships exist between knowledge stocks and the performance of an organization, in that an increase in the knowledge of individuals or groups improves performance [25]. This theory emerged in response to advances in knowledge, applied experience, professional skills, and customer relationships and how these lay a foundation for competitive advantage [26]. This theory encompasses three types of capital: human capital, structural capital, and customer capital, and creating a balance between these three leads to the creation of value [26]. The focus of human capital is that improvement in skills, ability, and knowledge leads to an increase in productivity and generation of new ideas and techniques, which in turn becomes an asset to an organization [26]. Structural capital provides an efficient channel for flow of communication by linking people with information. With respect to intellectual capital theory, structural capital provides a framework for transmission of knowledge and for knowledge sharing to be managed effectively [26]. Customer capital is seen as the most valuable component, yet the worst managed. Customer capital is the reason why the organization exists and the relationship with the customer is central to the worth of an organization, and their opinion should count in any decision an organization takes [26].

With respect to the issue of task shifting to pharmacists to improve access to cancer control services, pharmacists seem to have improved, and continue to improve, on their intellectual capital. With the Doctor of Pharmacy programme, pharmacists are now equipped with the clinical knowledge, skills, and expertise to improve health outcomes for patients for conditions such as cancer [14, 15]. This has improved their ability to handle complex clinical problems and get more involved in directly providing clinical care to patients in conjunction with and guidance from physicians [27]. In high-income countries like the United States, pharmacists have been incorporated in cancer care through oncology residence programmes which enable them to contribute to oncology patient visits, and therapy management [28]. Also, pharmacists contribute to managing the customer capital in health service delivery, even in LMICs, since they are amongst the most trusted health professionals and that most patients contact them first for health complaints and medical-related advice [13]. What seems to be lacking is the structural capital to serve as a framework for flow of knowledge and information between pharmacists and oncologists. This could be critical in creating models of care or practice agreements to utilize pharmacists in expanding access to cancer control services. However, for this to happen, there needs to be a readiness for change by all stakeholders, including oncologists, policymakers, and patients.

Organizational readiness for change theory

This theory proposes that readiness for change in an organization is team-based and psychological and draws upon the commitment of members of an organization in implementing change and trusting their collective ability to implement that change [29]. It proposes that collective and coordinated behaviour change is critical for effective implementation of change that produces anticipated results. In healthcare settings, establishing that there is organizational readiness for change is critical for the successful implementation of change. The theory further establishes the circumstances that suggest a shared readiness for change amongst members of an organization, and these include consistent leadership actions, and sharing of information and experiences. Therefore, there must be shared resolve and collective action amongst all members to follow the procedures that necessitate change implementation, and that the change must be valued by all members for the implementation to be effective [29]. Therefore for change to occur, there must be commitment by all members to work towards that change, and there must be shared belief by all members in their collective capacity to organize and execute the actions that will yield that change [29].

Therefore, oncologists, pharmacists, patients, and policymakers (including other nonphysician health worker stakeholders like nurses, and community health workers) need to be committed to work towards improving access to cancer control services and must trust each other’s capacity to execute the actions to bring about this change using task shifting as a change mechanism. In implementing task shifting in healthcare settings, the professionals involved, the beneficiaries, and acceptability at the policymaking level should be considered [30]. Thus, the readiness for task shifting could be influenced by the professionals involved, in this case, oncologists, pharmacists and policymakers, and their commitment to bringing about task shifting in cancer control services. It could also be influenced by their knowledge, skills, acceptability by patients, the policy landscape, and the acceptability (including affordability and viability) of task shifting by policymakers in a country’s health system.

These influences could independently, or jointly, affect readiness for task shifting. For instance, the view of oncologists on professional boundaries and the knowledge, skill, capability, and preferences of pharmacists, including compensation for these new roles would determine the acceptability of task shifting. Additionally, patients might have reservations about receiving certain types of care from pharmacists or might embrace the idea of receiving care from pharmacists based on already-established trust, previous experiences, and frustration from increased wait times before seeing an oncologist. Furthermore, the level of support policymakers will give to task shifting and their views on health worker shortages and the best approach in addressing it are also factors to consider.

Theoretical domains framework

The theoretical domains framework provides a theoretical lens for viewing the cognitive, affective, social, and environmental influences on behaviour. It helps to identify the effect of these influences on the behaviour of health professionals in accepting and implementing evidence-based recommendations [31]. This framework is particularly useful because changing existing practices, or implementing new practices, which is the case for task shifting in cancer control services, require changes in individual and collective behaviour of concerned stakeholders. This framework offers the benefits of providing a robust theoretical basis to hinge implementation studies on, providing good coverage of potential barriers to implementation and, in combination with other methods, can provide information necessary to facilitate progress from investigation to intervention [32]. This framework contains three key domains that are relevant to task shifting—capability, motivation, and opportunity [33]. With respect to capability for task shifting, pharmacists have the requisite knowledge and skills and have established their professional role and identity in providing clinical services that are beneficial for patients and the healthcare system. Through their experiences in pharmaceutical and clinical care, and the coherent set of behaviours that has made them an integral part of the healthcare team, their knowledge, skills, and identity can be utilized in expanding access to cancer control services through task shifting. Achieving this might require building their capacity and enhancing their capability for implementing particular tasks with additional training, education, and enablement which can be provided to them in collaboration with oncologists. Furthermore, given that these domains in the theoretical domains framework have a strong focus on behaviour change, it is also critical to understanding task shifting in LMICs given the complexities in the healthcare systems in these settings.

Motivation

Improving access to cancer care is a critical health system need, and motivation theory holds that people are often motivated to act when they see the impact of meeting such an important need [34]. Addressing important and complex needs stems from intrinsic motivation and a combination of five key features of a job—skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback. These five key features which are specified in the Job Characteristics Theory of motivation [35] operate within the psychological pathways of work meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results. These, in turn, drive performance and boosts creativity, commitment, satisfaction and retention [35].

The skill variety of pharmacists, the significance of the task that could be shifted to them, the psychological pathway of the meaningfulness of these tasks, and the knowledge of the results of these tasks in improving cancer control could further drive their creativity and commitment in improving access to cancer control. All of these, in addition to the satisfaction they could get in addressing this need, could certainly increase their motivation, and spur their retention within the health workforce, thus addressing the wider problem of health workforce shortage.

Task shifting to pharmacists could further positively affect their intrinsic motivation through experienced meaningfulness of these tasks and the shared responsibility in addressing a significant global health problem. However, this will greatly rely on supervisory relationships with oncologists to create models of care that could improve access to cancer control services. This is because evidence from motivation theory research suggests that while all the other factors mentioned are critical, building a sense of shared responsibility and experiencing supportive collaboration and supervision are just as important [34].

Pharmacists’ capabilities in team-based care and improving access to care is established in the literature [36, 37]. The efficiency and quality of outcomes of services rendered by pharmacists have been shown to improve their job satisfaction. Furthermore, the growing organizational culture of the pharmacy profession and the optimism emanating from these have been shown to motivate pharmacists to do more in improving access to care [38]. However, this motivation can be hampered by lack of support from other members of the healthcare team and their refusal to acknowledge and utilize the emerging role of pharmacists in improving access to care [39]. With respect to task shifting for cancer control, the right support from relevant stakeholders like oncologists and policymakers, the capabilities pharmacists have built from the current advancement of their training in clinical care, in addition to improved quality and efficiency of their services, might contribute to increased motivation to participate in task shifting interventions.

This combination of capacity and motivation has provided an opportunity for health systems to utilize pharmacists in expanding access to cancer control services. The relevance of utilizing this opportunity is buttressed by the evidence that pharmacists are closer to the community, are amongst the most trusted health professionals and are the first port of call for patients especially in LMIC health systems [13]. This evidence points to social influences that pharmacists have and could also be a contributing factor in possibly increased patient preferences for pharmacists’ services in cancer control.

Change theory

The Change theory was developed by Kurt Lewin who theorized a model of change in which change occurs in three stages: unfreezing, change, and refreezing. In this model, Lewin also factors in three major concepts that are critical for change—driving forces, restraining forces, and equilibrium. The driving forces facilitate change by causing a shift in equilibrium. The restraining forces are those that counter change by pushing back against the driving forces. The balance between these forces results in equilibrium. The driving forces need to shift this equilibrium to create change by seeking out methods and avenues to unfreeze a situation that they consider to be ineffective or counterproductive [40, 41].

Lewin posits that this can be done in three ways. It is either there is an increase in driving forces, a decrease in restraining forces, or a combination of both. Successful application of any of these leads to unfreezing. This increases the possibility of movement to a new level where changes in behaviour, opinions, or feeling occur, leading to a more productive policy or action. This is then followed by the refreezing stage where the change is established as the new standard [41].

Change is precipitated by many factors, even at the health systems level. A decline in standards, the discovery of new strategies, or the response to an increase in the burden of a disease such as cancer can draw attention to the need for change. However, as shown by Lewin’s theory, change is subject to resistance. Therefore, it requires the involvement of relevant stakeholders to build consensus that indeed, change is needed. Building this consensus also requires an understanding of what that change entails and the mix of power, information, knowledge and skill, and rewards required to institute and maintain that change [40, 42].

The leaders in the area that requires change need to co-opt, educate, communicate, and task support other members of the team required for that change to materialize. This is followed by knowledge sharing and transfer, empowerment, delegation of authority and responsibility, and reward [40].

With respect to task shifting of cancer control services, there is need to build consensus that it is imperative to explore possible changes to the current cancer strategy since the burden of the disease continues to rise. It is also necessary to build consensus to understand what change entails, and what is needed to support change. This is important for any unfreezing to happen and for resistance to change to be minimal. This is also important for understanding the power mix and level of delegation of authority and responsibility required, what knowledge to share and transfer, how to structure the change and the vision for change.

To achieve this, it could be necessary to draw lessons from other settings where similar change has occurred with respect to improving access to cancer control services by amplifying the role of other healthcare professionals. This could clarify potential driving and restraining forces, and how to successfully unfreeze, implement change and refreeze.

A combination of these theories and framework due to their relevance in task shifting, can yield a conceptual framework for assessing pharmacists, oncologists, and policymakers’ views on task shifting of some cancer control services to pharmacists.

A conceptual framework for assessing cancer control task shifting to pharmacists

Intellectual capital theory, organizational readiness for change theory, the theoretical domains framework, motivation theory and change theory are useful in understanding and developing task shifting interventions. Testing these theories and framework is particularly useful in LMICs in sub-Saharan Africa that continue to grapple with critical shortages of oncology workforce. As highlighted earlier, all the countries in this region are facing a critical shortage of oncologists. This means that many cancer patients do not receive the care they require. Furthermore, many cancer cases go undetected. Even for those that are detected, there is the issue of late presentation which severely affects prognosis [43]. There could also be implications for oncologists themselves in terms of severe burnout, and the social and psychological effects of having to cater to numerous patients [4]. Furthermore, as the cancer burden has been projected to double in the next two decades, there is a strong case to build a cancer workforce that is embedded in communities and leverages the expertise of the existing health workforce. Task shifting offers an additional approach in utilizing the present health workforce to expand access to cancer control services.

However, task shifting interventions are not and should not be implemented in siloes. Bringing all relevant stakeholders is paramount for the strategy to be effective and successful. This is even more important for health systems in sub-Saharan Africa where there are age-long rivalries amongst various categories of physician and nonphysician health workers [44]. Therefore, there is need to establish capacity, cooperation, collaboration, and coordination for task shifting in cancer control services amongst pharmacists, oncologists, and policymakers (including policy stakeholders for other nonphysician health workers like nurses and community health workers). This will require assessment and recognition of the technical capacity of pharmacists to contribute to cancer control services, readiness for change by pharmacists, oncologists, and policy makers, and the motivation and opportunities available to all these stakeholders to implement task shifting.

The proposed framework is referred to as the Task Shifting Concepts for Pharmacists to Advance Cancer Control and Treatment (TASK PACT) framework. This framework synthesizes the key concepts from the intellectual capital, organizational readiness for change and motivation theories, and the theoretical domains framework to assess oncologists, pharmacists, policymakers and patients readiness for task shifting in sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 1).

TASK PACT framework for task shifting to pharmacists for improved access to cancer control services

The TASK PACT framework illustrates the required connections between education, professional experience, and practice settings of pharmacists to support the establishment of customer capital. This capital manifests from their proximity and accessibility to communities, and patient trust in pharmacist services. Additionally, clinical knowledge and professional skills show their capacity and human capital in patient care. With adequate utilization and support (including through remuneration), their optimism, belief in their capacity, and job satisfaction could help keep them motivated. These qualities and factors enhance their professional identity and role in clinical care, suggesting their readiness for task shifting as part of a collaborative model in cancer care. To harness these qualities and translate their readiness into a model of care that includes pharmacists in cancer control, positive individual and collective behaviour change is needed from all stakeholders, including oncologists, policy makers, and other health workers. Positive behaviour change could unfreeze the current non-inclusion of pharmacists in cancer control which could support structural capital where knowledge is shared between stakeholders, such as transmission of necessary skills and knowledge from oncologists to pharmacists. This coordination and commitment from stakeholders, belief and trust in collective ability, and policy support could signal readiness for change. This could establish support for pharmacists to take on certain tasks in cancer control. Refreezing this established support would require clear professional role boundaries, oncologist-led training and coordinationt, supportive policy frameworks, and formal practice agreements that define how pharmacists can contribute to cancer control within multidisciplinary care teams.

Studies have explored or advocated for roles for pharmacists in cancer care [45, 46]. However, there is a dearth of frameworks exploring health system readiness to adopt task shifting models that engage pharmacists in cancer control in LMICs. There is also a paucity of research on task shifting to pharmacists in cancer control [12]. The TASK PACT framework combines workforce capacity, behavioural motivation, readiness for system-wide change, and practical strategies for embedding change. This could offer a more comprehensive and context-specific lens to inform practice guidelines for preparing pharmacists for task shifting, define their expanded roles, and assess the acceptability and practicality of collaborative models of care involving pharmacists. In addition, it can generate evidence on settings most suited for implementation, appropriate practice agreements, remuneration models, and strategies to ensure sustainability.

This framework could also be applicable to other healthcare professionals and practitioners, other diseases beyond cancer, and other settings where there are severe workforce shortages. It could also further advance research that explores the readiness of healthcare systems in sub-Saharan Africa in involving community pharmacists in expanding access to cancer control services through task shifting. Potential research areas, results, and impact of the TASK PACT framework

Evaluating the TASK PACT framework could combine both qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative methods such as interviews and focus groups with pharmacists, oncologists, policymakers, and patients, could explore perceptions, acceptability, and readiness for task shifting. This could be followed by quantitative approaches, including a pilot implementation and assessment using pre–post or quasi-experimental approaches, to evaluate changes in service delivery, patient outcomes, and health system efficiency. Additionally, implementation science methods can further identify contextual factors, facilitators, and barriers. These could generate actionable evidence to guide policy and scalable, collaborative task shifting strategies in sub-Saharan Africa.

Operationalizing task shifting in cancer control requires understanding key stakeholders’ roles, perceptions, and potential resistance. Using the TASK PACT framework, research can examine oncologists’ willingness to delegate tasks, pharmacists’ readiness and competencies, policymakers’ support for enabling regulations, and patients’ acceptance. It can also identify regulatory, inter-professional, and systemic barriers such as limited infrastructure and training gaps. Evidence generated through this research could be critical for designing context-sensitive, multidisciplinary strategies that are feasible, acceptable, and sustainable, ensuring effective implementation of task shifting of cancer control services in LMIC health systems.

Discussion

The proposed TASK PACT framework could help guide research to improve access to cancer control services by exploring opportunities for greater inclusion of pharmacists in the model of care. While the framework focuses on pharmacists, it is important to recognize that other healthcare professionals such as nurses and community health workers, have also been engaged in task shifting strategies for noncommunicable diseases, including cancer. Nurses, for example, play central roles in oncology care, from patient navigation and symptom management to palliative and survivorship support [47]. They have strong professional identity and widely integrated into health systems [48]. However, effective task shifting should not be viewed as a zero-sum approach between professional groups but rather as a collaborative strategy that leverages the comparative advantages of each cadre [9]. Pharmacists offer some unique qualities that could support effective cancer control in LMICs. They work within communities as trusted health professionals, often serving as a primary healthcare gateway as they are often the first port of call for health issues in various communities in LMICS [13]. Additionally, pharmacists also possess advanced clinical knowledge and have been shown to be valuable in cancer control in other settings [21, 22]. These unique qualities can be leveraged to complement and not replace existing roles in cancer control.

Furthermore, pharmacists increasingly contribute to oncology care in hospital and clinical settings, providing medication therapy management, optimizing chemotherapy, managing adverse effects, and ensuring safe handling of high-risk medications [49]. These roles support improved patient safety, treatment outcomes, and reduce oncologist workload [49]. By bringing these skills into their community practice, pharmacists could support the broader cancer workforce and expand access to cancer control services. Recognizing these capabilities is essential for task shifting to pharmacists in LMICs, ensuring interventions align with pharmacists’ competencies and local health system realities.

Beyond these roles, inclusion of pharmacists within a collaborative model could also unlock additional benefits such as improving access to medicines within communities [50]. They could also support in addressing financial barriers to cancer care through cost counseling and facilitating access to affordable generics, which could ease the economic burden of cancer and advance equity in cancer control [50]. Additionally, their involvement can reduce geographic and informational barriers, ensuring that underserved populations receive timely and appropriate cancer care [51]. Integrating pharmacists into multidisciplinary care teams thus strengthens the health system’s capacity to deliver oncology services more equitably.

While pharmacists hold these unique advantages, more effort is needed to ensure their effective engagement in cancer control, including deliberate investments to improve training, supportive supervision, and structured collaboration with oncologists and nurses. This will support improvement and reinforcement of their oncology-specific expertise needed to address increasing patient load and cancer burden. Additionally, broader health system strengthening and reforms are needed to address structural and foundational barriers which also affect pharmacist services provision in LMICs such as limited infrastructure and weak supply chains [52], and health workforce rivalry with physicians [53]. This will help strengthen the enabling environment for effective oncology service delivery at the community pharmacy level.

Pharmacists should enhance their oncology competencies, engage in medication therapy management, and collaborate with oncologists within multidisciplinary teams. Health systems in sub-Saharan Africa should provide supportive regulations, clear scopes of practice, training, supervision, and inter-professional collaboration. Prioritizing community-based integration of pharmacists could strengthen early detection, expand access, and address workforce shortages. Together, these actions can guide context-sensitive, feasible, and sustainable task shifting strategies, operationalizing the TASK PACT framework across LMICs.

Conclusion

A conceptual framework informed by the intellectual capacity, motivation, change, and organizational readiness for change theories, and the theoretical domains framework was developed. This framework could be useful for assessing task shifting to pharmacists for improving access to cancer control services in LMICs. This framework could support research that explores the possibility of using pharmacists to build up the weak and depleting cancer workforce in LMICs to improve access to cancer control services. This has become paramount as the incidence of cancer has been projected to double in these countries in less than a decade, thus, further burdening and constraining healthcare systems that are already severely overburdened and resource-constrained with corresponding increases in cancer morbidity and mortality.
