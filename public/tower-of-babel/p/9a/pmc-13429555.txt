Identification and validation of a prognostic risk-scoring model in drug-resistant ALL and evaluation of immune micro-environment
Authors: Yun-yao Li, Hong-xi Huang, Jian-wei Guan, Wang-jing Cai, Jian-pei Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ALL is the commonest childhood hematological malignancy; modern multi-agent chemotherapy cures about 80% of patients, yet roughly 20% experience primary or secondary chemotherapy resistance that sharply reduces long-term survival. The molecular circuits driving this resistance remain poorly defined, and validated biomarkers for early risk stratification are lacking. Growing data implicate reprogramming of the immune microenvironment as a critical determinant of treatment response and relapse risk. We therefore interrogated transcriptomic profiles from GEO and TARGET datasets to identify genes differentially expressed between chemosensitive and chemoresistant ALL, distilled a 13-gene resistance-associated prognostic index (RAPI-13) by LASSO regression, and validated the signature in independent cohorts. Functional enrichment, immune-infiltration deconvolution and single-cell RNA-seq revealed that high-risk patients harbor activated oncogenic signaling (IL-2/STAT5, NOTCH, PI3K/AKT/mTOR) together with immune-evasion signatures. Mechanistic studies showed that CD40 agonist treatment polarizes tumor-associated macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 state, restores anti-leukemic immunity and re-sensitizes resistant blasts to chemotherapy. In cell-line-derived xenografts and humanized mice, triple therapy combining CD40 agonist, daunorubicin and PD-L1 blockade achieved superior leukemia control, prolonged survival and fostered immune memory formation. These findings provide a clinically applicable prognostic tool and pre-clinical rationale for integrating CD40-centered immunotherapy into frontline protocols to overcome chemotherapy resistance in childhood ALL.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07080-3.

Introduction

ALL is the commonest pediatric hematological malignancy, accounting for about 80% of childhood leukemia. The disease originates in the lymphoid lineage and is defined by the clonal expansion of developmentally arrested B- or T-cell precursors that accumulate in bone marrow and peripheral blood [1]. Contemporary multi-agent chemotherapy protocols achieve a CR for over 90% of patients and deliver 5-year EFS rates of 80–85% [2, 3]. Nevertheless, approximately 20% of children develop primary or secondary chemotherapy resistance, once resistance emerges, the likelihood of cure drops precipitously. Chemical tolerance is the leading cause of persistent MRD after second transplantation and underpins ultimate clinical relapse, profoundly compromising long-term outcome [4, 5]. Therefore, a systematic dissection of the molecular pathways that drive chemotherapy resistance and formulating appropriate intervention strategies have become urgent clinical demands that have yet to be fulfilled.

Chemotherapy resistance in ALL is mediated by a heterogeneous repertoire of mechanisms, including aberrant apoptosis signaling, altered drug metabolism and quantitative or qualitative modulation of drug targets. For example, even modest reductions in CD22 surface density are sufficient to blunt the efficacy of anti-CD22 CAR T-cell therapy [6], whereas up-regulation of MRPs potentiates efflux of diverse cytotoxic agents and confers cross-class resistance [7]. High-resolution omics technologies coupled with robust bioinformatic workflows now allow unbiased identification of master regulatory genes and construction of quantifiable, reproducible prognostic indices that can objectify risk stratification and guide precision medicine in real time [8].

The leukemia microenvironment, composed of infiltrating immune cells, stromal elements and soluble mediators, critically shapes chemosensitivity, immunotherapy responsiveness and ultimately clinical outcome [9, 10]. The density and functional polarity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes correlate tightly with expression of immune checkpoints such as PD-1/PD-L1 and immune co-stimulatory molecules such as CD40, implying that targeted re-programming of the microenvironment can restore drug sensitivity [11, 12]. Elucidating the molecular determinants that link ALL blasts to immune infiltration and anti-leukemic immunity is therefore essential for identifying rational combination targets and refining therapeutic algorithms.

Accordingly, we mined the GEO repository and retrieved 278 differentially expressed genes that discriminate chemo-resistant from chemo-sensitive disease. LASSO regression was employed to distil a RAPI-13 and an accompanying risk-score algorithm that demonstrated strong discriminatory performance in time-dependent ROC curve analysis. This model not only provides a novel molecular tool for clinical risk stratification but also reveals an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by M2 macrophage enrichment, prompting us to investigate CD40, which is a key macrophage-activating receptor, as a potential therapeutic node to reverse chemotherapy resistance, thereby laying a biological foundation for patient selection in subsequent immunotherapy strategies. The gene exerting the highest positive weight was validated at protein level both in normal peripheral-blood mononuclear cells and in ALL cell lines, and the robustness of the signature was independently confirmed with MSKCC and GEO cohort data. The risk score allowed allocation of patients into high-risk and low-risk strata and constituted an independent predictor of outcome after adjustment for established clinical covariates. Mechanistically, the score captured biologically relevant variance in immune infiltration and in oncogenic signaling cascades that propel leukemic progression. Finally, we show, at both cellular and organismal levels, that CD40 agonist therapy polarises TAMs toward the pro-inflammatory, anti-tumoral M1 state and exerts direct cytotoxicity against T-ALL cells. Combination of CD40 agonist with PD-L1 blockade further amplified anti-leukemic immunity, providing pre-clinical proof-of-concept for CD40-centred combination regimens, while the RAPI-13 model is expected to be used in the future to identify high-risk patients most likely to benefit from such immunotherapy, thereby realizing a translational application framework from molecular subtyping to precision intervention.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Anti-C1QTNF (ab36871) and anti-β-actin (ab6276) were obtained from Abcam. Enhanced CCK-8 (E-CK-A362) and Granzyme B ELISA Kit (E-EL-M0594) were obtained from Elabscience. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, HY-18739), Daunorubicin hydrochloride (HY-13062), anti-mouse CD40 antibody (FGK45, HY-P990680) and anti-Human PD-L1 (HY-108730) were obtained from MedchemExpress. CD45 Monoclonal Antibody (58-0459-42), CCR7 Monoclonal Antibody (12-1971-82), CD62L Monoclonal Antibody (17-0621-82) were obtained from ThermoFisher.

Human samples, cell lines and animals

PBMCs were obtained from individuals who had signed the consent form, and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. The leukemia cell lines NALM-6, REH, CEM-C1, Molt-4, Jurkat and THP-1 were provided by the Hematological Oncology Laboratory of the Children’s Medical Center of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. All cells underwent STR testing. The huHSC-NCG mice were purchased from Guangdong Laidi Biomedical Research Co., Ltd., and this experimental protocol has been approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

Data download

The Series Matrix File of dataset GSE32962 (platform GPL570, 43 patients) and the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE130116 (24 pediatric with complete profiles) were downloaded from the GEO database. Processed raw expression data from 661 ALL patients were obtained from the TARGET database. Expression profile data from 140 patients were downloaded from the MSKCC database. None of the above datasets included T-ALL patients; therefore, bioinformatic analyses in this study were restricted to B-ALL cohorts exclusively.

Differential expression analysis

Genes distinguishing disease samples from matched controls (p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.585) were identified using the limma package, followed by volcano plot and heat map visualization.

Differential gene functional enrichment analysis

Functional annotation of the differential genes was performed with the clusterProfiler package. GO and KEGG analyses were used to evaluate relevant functional categories, with significance defined as p-value < 0.05 and q < 0.05.

Model construction and prognosis

Differentially expressed genes were selected and a prognosis-related model was constructed via LASSO regression. In our LASSO regression analysis, we employed 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal penalty parameter (λ) and minimize overfitting. LASSO inherently handles multicollinearity by selecting one variable from a group of correlated predictors and shrinking others to zero, which is a key reason we chose this method. A risk-score formula was generated by incorporating the expression values of the selected genes, weighted by their respective LASSO coefficients. LASSO coefficients were estimated with cross-validation to ensure stability. Using the median risk score calculated from this formula as the threshold, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups. Outcomes between the two groups were compared using Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test. The predictive performance of the risk score was assessed through stratified analyses, and its accuracy was evaluated using time-dependent ROC analysis.

Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to analyze the processed patient data, to infer the relative proportions of 22 types of immune-infiltrating cells, and to conduct correlation analysis of gene expression levels and immune cell contents, thereby constructing an immune infiltration heatmap.

GSVA analysis and GSEA analysis

The GSVA algorithm was applied to score the gene sets downloaded from MSigDB to evaluate potential biological function changes across different samples. Based on the constructed model, patients were assigned to high- or low-risk groups, and GSEA was applied to identify pathways differing between the groups. After differential expression analysis on the subtype pathway gene sets downloaded from MSigDB, gene sets exhibiting significant enrichment were determined using the consistency score (adjusted p-value < 0.05).

Nomogram model construction

Using multivariable regression analysis, we constructed a nomogram that graphically integrates the risk score and clinical symptoms to visualize the interrelationships among variables in the predictive model. In this model, the contribution of each factor, represented by the regression coefficient, was assigned a score. these scores were summed to yield a total score, from which the predicted outcome was calculated.

MiRNA network construction and the regulatory network analysis of important genes

To determine whether specific miRNAs post-transcriptionally regulate some dangerous genes within the model genes, we interrogated the miRcode database for model gene-related miRNAs and visualized the network in Cytoscape. We inferred TFs with the RcisTarget package by ranking motifs via NES computed from AUC values, followed by the expanded annotation that maps motifs to TFs using sequence similarity and genomic context.

Single cells analysis

After the data were processed with the Seurat package, the spatial relationships among clusters were analyzed using the UMAP algorithm. Clusters were annotated as several cell subsets implicated in disease pathogenesis via the cellsdex package. Differential expression analysis (FindAllMarkers, logFC threshold = 1) was subsequently applied to the single-cell expression matrix to extract marker genes for each annotated cell subtype.

Western Blot

PBMCs and ALL cell lines (NALM-6, REH, CEM-C1 and MOLT-4) were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer, quantified by BCA assay, separated on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary mouse polyclonal antibodies against C1QTNF4 or β-actin (1:1,000), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:3,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized with ECL and normalized to β-actin.

Cytotoxicity assay

After different treatments, the cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a concentration of 100 µl (2 × 104 cells/ml), and CCK-8 was added to each well. After incubating in the dark for 2 h, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme reader.

Generation of daunorubicin-resistant Jurkat cells

Using high-dose pulse therapy, cells that developed resistance to DNR were selected. After treating the cells with 50nM DNR for 48 h, the drug treatment was stopped and the cells were allowed to recover in complete medium for 24–48 h. This cycle was repeated until the IC50 of the selected population exceeded that of the parental cells by at least 5 times. The resistant subline was retained and expanded, and was named Jurkat/DNR cells. After establishment, CCK-8 assays were performed again at 2, 4, and 8 weeks to confirm the stability of the resistance phenotype.

Preparation of cytokine-enriched CM

The CM was prepared from M0 type macrophages derived from THP-1 cells. The specific steps are as follows: THP-1 cells were seeded in a complete medium containing PMA but without antibiotics for 48 h to achieve M0 differentiation. Subsequently, the cells were further cultured in a complete medium without PMA for 24–48 h for subsequent experiments. After 24 h of polarization stimulation with CD40 agonist antibodies (FGK45), the cells were cultured in a serum-free medium for 24–48 h. Collect the supernatant.

Granzyme ELISA

Samples were added to antibody-coated 96-well plates, sealed and incubated for 3 h. After aspiration, 100 µl biotinylated detection antibody were added and incubated for another 1 h. The wells were washed 3 times, and then 100 µl HRP-conjugated streptavidin were added and incubated in dark for 15–30 min. The entire process was carried out at 37℃, with gentle shaking on a shaker. Finally, 50 µl stop solution were added to stop the reaction. Absorbance was read at 450 nm.

CDX model of resistant T-ALL

The huHSC-NSG mice were received a 100 µl single-cell suspension containing 5 × 106 Jurkat/DNR cells via lateral tail-vein injection. On the 30th day, blood was collected from the orbital vein of the mice and the CD45 + CD3 + CD7 + leukemia cells were d elected by using flow cytometry. When the proportion of leukemia cells reaches 5% or higher, it is considered that the model has been successfully established.

In-vivo therapy protocol

Leukemic mice were randomly divided into the following eight treatment groups: (A) Control group. (B) IgG. (C) FGK45. (D) DNR. (E) FGK45 + DNR. (F) anti-PD-L1. (G) anti-PD-L1 + FGK45. (H) Triple combination: anti-PD-L1 + FGK45 + DNR. All the above medications were administered by intraperitoneal injection, once every 4 weeks. Each arm was further split into two cohorts: (a) Mechanistic cohort: Retro-orbital blood was drawn 24 h after the first dose. leukocyte-derived cDNA was analysed by qRT-PCR for murine inflammatory transcripts (IL-1β: F: 5′-ATGCCACCTTTTGACAGTGATG-3′, R: 5′-TGTGCTGCTGCGAGATTTGA-3′. TNF-α: F: 5′-CCCTCACACTCACAAACCAC-3′, R: 5′-ACAAGGTACAACCCATCGGC-3′. IL-6: F: 5′-AGCCAGAGTCCTTCAGAGAGA-3′, R: 5′-GCCACTGGTTCTGTGACTCC-3′). Mice were euthanized 72 h after the last dose. spleens were excised and weighed. (b) Re-challenge cohort: Three days after the completion of the treatment, Jurkat/DNR cells were injected into the tail vein of the mice again. Peripheral blood was collected 24 h later. CD45⁺CCR7⁺CD62L⁺ cells were enumerated by flow cytometry. The Survival status was monitored every day until ethical end-points were reached.

Statistical analysis

Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Multivariable analyses were performed with Cox proportional-hazards models. Prior to integrative analysis, batch effect correction was applied to expression profile data from different datasets using the ComBat method to eliminate technical variation from non-biological sources; simultaneously, data harmonization and integration were achieved by standardizing gene expression values across datasets to comparable scales. All statistical computations were executed in R and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association analysis of risk score with tumor immune microenvironment and immune therapy response

The TME significantly affects diagnosis, survival outcomes and clinical treatment sensitivity. immune infiltration is one of its key components. We therefore analyzed the association between risk score and tumor immune infiltration to explore how the risk score drives ALL progression. The proportions of immune infiltration between high and low-risk groups were shown in the figure (Fig. 10.A). immune infiltration was quantified across risk strata. Compared with the low-risk cohort, the high-risk group exhibited marked depletion of resting dendritic cells, activated CD4 + memory T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, and T-follicular helper cells, whereas naive B cells, eosinophils, resting mast cells, monocytes, and plasma cells were significantly expanded (Fig. 10.B). Correlation analysis further revealed that the risk score was positively correlated with naive B cells, M2 macrophages, monocytes, resting mast cells, M0 macrophages, resting CD4 + memory T cells, activated dendritic cells, activated NK cells, plasma cells, eosinophils, CD8 + T cells, memory B cells, neutrophils, and resting NK cells, while it was inversely correlated with naive CD4 + T cells, resting dendritic cells, T-follicular helper cells, activated mast cells, activated CD4 + memory T cells, γδ T cells, and M1 macrophages (Fig. 10.C). Discussion

ALL is the commonest childhood malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related death in children [1, 3]. Chemotherapy remains the backbone of curative therapy, yet early relapse driven by primary chemotherapy resistance and the difficulty of achieving second remission constitutes an unmet clinical need [4]. To address this challenge, we constructed a chemotherapy resistance-associated gene signature model (RAPI-13) for molecular risk stratification in pediatric ALL. By delineating the immune microenvironment landscape of high-risk versus low-risk cases, we provide the first rationale for integrating CD40 agonist therapy into frontline treatment to enable precision medicine.

The RAPI-13 signature was established based on 278 differentially expressed genes selected via LASSO regression, among which C1QTNF4 showed the strongest correlation with poor prognosis, whereas EID3 exhibited a protective effect. Interrogation of ALL-related genes revealed significant co-regulatory relationships with RAPI genes: EID3 expression was negatively correlated with PAX5 (r = − 0.524), while C1QTNF4 was positively correlated with PAX5 (r = 0.683). PAX5 is indispensable for B-cell differentiation, and its haploinsufficiency or mutation arrests terminal maturation while potentiating proliferative and pro-survival signaling, thereby initiating and sustaining leukemogenesis [13–15]. EID3 antagonizes differentiation and maintains stemness, in colorectal carcinoma and osteosarcoma it promotes a cancer-stem-cell phenotype and confers chemoresistance [16, 17]. C1QTNF4 behaves as an oncogene that activates STAT3 and NF-κB signaling to enhance tumor cell survival and impart apoptosis resistance [18]. These data confirm the involvement of C1QTNF4 and EID3 in chemotherapy resistance in ALL. Within the RAPI-13 model constructed in this study, the 13 genes function as an integrated synergistic network collectively contributing to risk stratification, wherein their regression coefficients reflect the weighted balance derived from multi-gene cooperative interactions rather than the independent oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions of individual genes. The collinearity among variables concentrates the “resistance contribution” onto key driver genes (such as C1QTNF4), while assigning opposing coefficients to other genes as statistical compensation. This weight allocation mechanism quantifies the overall equilibrium between oncogenic and tumor-suppressive forces in ALL; however, it does not imply that genes with smaller absolute coefficient values necessarily exert independent pro-tumorigenic or anti-tumorigenic effects. The specific functional roles of individual genes were not extensively investigated in this study, which is an inherent characteristic of multi-gene models functioning as holistic tools. Given that the primary objective of this research was to establish a prognostic model and validate a CD40-centered combinatorial therapeutic strategy, the validation of C1QTNF4 expression levels served to confirm model reliability rather than to elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of all 13 genes. The core value of this model lies in its utility as a tool for identifying high-risk patients, particularly those exhibiting immunosuppressive microenvironment characteristics, thereby guiding the precise selection of candidates for subsequent CD40-targeted immunotherapy.

Patients in the high-risk group stratified by the median risk score had significantly poorer overall survival (P < 0.001), with time-dependent area under the curve exceeding 0.70 at 1, 3, and 5 years. Cox regression analysis confirmed that the risk score is an independent prognostic factor, and a nomogram model integrating clinical variables validated the clinical net benefit of the model.

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying chemotherapy resistance, we performed GSVA analysis. High-risk samples showed selective enrichment of IL-2/STAT5, NOTCH, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways. The IL-2/STAT5 axis is a pan-cancer oncogenic pathway that sustains survival of leukemic blasts [19]. NOTCH is evolutionarily conserved and indispensable for embryonic development and B-lineage specification. Ligand-independent NOTCH1 activation or gain-of-function receptor mutations are initiating events in T- and B-ALL [20, 21]. Targeted inhibition of NOTCH signaling has therapeutic potential: lncRNA LINC00514, for example, activates NOTCH to polarize macrophages toward an M2, immunosuppressive phenotype, thereby blunting immunotherapy responses, whereas NOTCH blockade induces leukemic cell apoptosis [22, 23]. PI3K/AKT/mTOR controls metabolic reprogramming, proliferation and migration, and constitutive activation impairs phosphatase function in T lymphoblasts and is linked to early treatment failure and chemoresistance [24, 25]. The feasibility of dual NOTCH1 and PI3K/AKT inhibition as a novel therapeutic strategy for T-ALL is currently under investigation [26]. Complementary GSEA using MSigDB canonical pathway sets highlighted JAK/STAT signaling, cell adhesion molecules, and cytosolic DNA sensing pathways. JAK/STAT is central to hematopoiesis and lymphoid maturation, mutations or fusions in JAK1/2/3, STAT3/5B or CRLF2 drive leukemic transformation and accelerate progression from myeloproliferative neoplasms to secondary ALL [27, 28]. Cell adhesion molecules modulate leukemia/microenvironment interactions. High integrin-α6 expression, for instance, enhances anchorage-independent survival and confers chemotherapy resistance [29]. The cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway exerts context-dependent effects: acute activation triggers IFN-β release and a senescence-associated secretory phenotype that suppresses tumor initiation, whereas chronic stimulation fosters an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that facilitates immune evasion [30]. Thus, the gene modules captured by our predictive model converge on signaling cascades associated with leukemogenesis, chemotherapy resistance, and immune escape.

Beyond chemotherapy, immunotherapy has become a standard treatment strategy for malignancies. Early efforts focused on immune checkpoints expressed by tumor cells themselves, leading to the development of blockers of the CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 axes. These agents have reshaped the therapeutic landscape for melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma and other entities [31, 32]. However, leukemia blasts shape the immune microenvironment to promote immune evasion. Targeting the TME rather than malignant clones alone may represent the next frontier in immunotherapy. TME remodeling is known to exert dual effects: on one hand, reduction of immunosuppressive TAMs and expansion of CD8 + T cells, Treg 1 cells, and granzyme B + cytotoxic lymphocytes can promote anti-leukemia immunity [33]. On the other hand, accumulation of B cells and memory B cells accompanied by loss of T cells and dendritic cells can promote chemoresistance and immune evasion, ultimately resulting in shortened survival [34]. We applied the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify immune infiltration in chemotherapy resistant high- versus low-risk ALL as defined by our signature model. Notably, TAM polarization was closely associated with disease risk, underscoring its central role in leukemia progression, metastasis, and immune evasion [35]. In this study, we selected a T-ALL cell line (Jurkat/DNR) to establish an in vivo chemotherapy resistance model and evaluate the efficacy of CD40 agonist, based on the following considerations. We chose a T-ALL cell line (Jurkat/DNR) to establish an in vivo resistance model. There were two main reasons for this choice: Firstly, in order to clearly analyze the mechanism of action of CD40 agonists, since T-ALL leukemia cells do not express CD40, this excluded the direct effect on the tumor itself and enabled us to isolate the contribution of macrophage reprogramming; Secondly, to meet the urgent clinical needs of the high-risk T-ALL group, which is characterized by particularly poor prognosis. CD40, a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily expressed on antigen-presenting cells including macrophages, constitutes a master switch linking innate and adaptive immunity [36, 37]. Chemotherapy resistant high-risk cases displayed significantly elevated CD40 levels, prompting the hypothesis that CD40 agonist therapy might reverse chemoresistance.

At the mechanistic level, prior studies have established that CD40 signaling orchestrates antitumor immunity through multi-layered molecular mechanisms. Macrophages occupy the central hub position within the tumor microenvironment cellular communication network, and CD40 agonists provide a direct driving force for macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory antitumor M1 phenotype through activation of the CD40/CD40L signaling axis [38, 39]. The specific pathways are as follows: CD40 agonists engage CD40 receptors on macrophages, initiating downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades that drive macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, manifested by marked upregulation of M1 markers including CD86, TNF-α, and iNOS, alongside enhanced antigen-presenting capacity and tumor cell phagocytosis [40, 41]. Our experimental results further corroborate that CD40 agonists substantially potentiate antitumor effects through functional reprogramming of macrophages: conditioned medium derived from FGK45-treated macrophages directly kills T-ALL cells and reverses chemotherapeutic resistance; in vivo combination therapy demonstrates coordinated upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) and granzyme B, induces systemic antitumor immune memory, and significantly prolongs survival, suggesting that macrophages serve as pivotal microenvironmental regulatory nodes mediating the multi-dimensional antitumor immune responses driven by CD40 activation.

In our preceding studies, we have demonstrated through in vitro experiments that CD40 agonist treatment of M0 macrophages exerts cytotoxic effects against T-ALL cells, and subsequently validated through in vivo experiments that this effect is mediated by the induction of a spectrum of inflammatory cytokines. In the in vivo setting, CD40 agonist combined with chemotherapy or PD-L1 blockade achieved superior leukemia control, with the triple therapy showing the most pronounced effect. Chemotherapy-induced cell death releases abundant tumor antigens; CD40 ligation enables antigen-presenting cells to mount effective T cell responses, while PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition sustains T cell function, thereby promoting durable immunological memory formation. Previous studies have shown that CD40 agonist therapy triggers TAMs secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and, via NF-κB and STAT3 signaling, up-regulates PD-L1 on tumor cells while simultaneously inducing PD-1 on activated T cells, providing a mechanistic rationale for combined CD40/PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [42–44]. However, triple therapy in our T-ALL model provoked massive blast death, initiating a sequential cascade that progressed from “innate immune alarm” (pro-inflammatory cytokine burst) to “adaptive immune killing” (granzyme-mediated cytotoxicity). This potent activation carried the attendant risk of fulminant cytokine-release syndrome and potential progression to multi-organ failure [45]. Therefore, in future clinical translation, the potential risk of CRS must be addressed with the highest priority. To balance efficacy and safety, the following mitigation strategies are recommended: a graded dosing schedule to progressively assess patient tolerability, pre-treatment with IL-6 receptor antagonists (e.g., tocilizumab) to block the core inflammatory pathway of CRS, and careful patient selection based on immune status and disease burden. Rigorous longitudinal monitoring and precise dosing strategies will be essential components of future clinical studies.

From a translational medicine perspective, the findings of this study have clear clinical applicability. First, the RAPI-13 model not only serves as an independent prognostic tool but also identifies high-risk patient populations characterized by a distinct immunosuppressive microenvironment, featuring an imbalanced receptor-ligand pattern: upregulation of CD40 receptor accompanied by downregulation of its ligand CD40L, along with enrichment of M2 macrophages and an imbalanced M1/M2 ratio. This signature suggests that high-risk patients may be ideal candidates for CD40 agonist immunotherapy. Thus, the RAPI-13 model has the potential to guide patient selection in future clinical practice, identifying chemotherapy-resistant pediatric ALL patients most likely to benefit from CD40 agonist-based combination therapy, thereby achieving a closed loop from molecular subtyping to precision intervention. Second, regarding clinical implementation of the model, the 13-gene signature encompassed by RAPI-13 can be assessed via multiplex RT-PCR or targeted RNA sequencing, both of which are technically feasible in routine clinical laboratories with manageable turnaround times and costs. Although upfront testing costs are higher than single-gene assays, the comprehensive risk stratification provided by this model may help reduce unnecessary overtreatment, optimize healthcare resource allocation, and improve overall treatment benefit by precisely selecting patients suitable for CD40 immunotherapy. Notably, compared with MRD, a well-established strong prognostic factor in ALL that reflects remission status only at a single time point after treatment and lacks prospective guidance for intervention at diagnosis or treatment initiation, the RAPI-13 model constructed in this study can identify high-risk children harboring seeds of relapse within the genome at the time of initial diagnosis, facilitating earlier implementation of intensified therapy and close monitoring. Therefore, it can serve as an important complement to MRD, providing a new molecular dimension for precise risk stratification. Last but not least, this study demonstrates for the first time in hematologic malignancies that the triple combination of CD40 agonist, chemotherapy, and PD-L1 blockade exerts synergistic antitumor efficacy, providing direct preclinical evidence for subsequent clinical trial design.

Accordingly, we acknowledge certain limitations of this study. First, bioinformatics analyses and prognostic modeling were primarily based on B-ALL datasets, whereas in vivo validation employed a T-ALL xenograft model. The selection of the T-ALL model was deliberate: the absence of CD40 expression on T-ALL cells enables unambiguous mechanistic dissection of macrophage-mediated, CD40-dependent antileukemia effects, which constitutes the central proof-of-concept for our proposed therapeutic strategy. Nevertheless, this model discrepancy necessitates future validation in B-ALL-specific in vivo systems to confirm the broader translational potential of CD40-centered combination therapy. Second, while the 13 genes in the RAPI-13 model collectively contribute to risk stratification, only C1QTNF4, the gene with the highest weight, was validated at the protein level; functional characterization of the remaining genes requires further investigation. Additionally, due to the lack of public datasets containing both RAPI-13 and MRD data, a direct comparison between the two could not be performed; however, the model’s potential to identify high-risk patients at diagnosis serves as a valuable complement to MRD. Finally, although no overt signs of CRS were observed in our murine model with triple therapy, this risk must be vigilantly monitored in clinical translation, with the possibility of CRS occurrence not excluded, and mitigation strategies including graded dosing and pre-treatment with IL-6 receptor antagonists implemented.

In conclusion, we constructed and independently validated the RAPI-13 model, which discriminates overall survival in pediatric ALL and links it to dysregulated signaling networks, pro-leukemic immune infiltration, and targetable immune checkpoints. Preclinical models demonstrate that CD40 agonist therapy reprograms tumor-associated macrophages, reverses chemoresistance, and synergizes with chemotherapy and PD-L1 blockade, while emphasizing the necessity of precision dosing to manage CRS risk. These data provide a clinically applicable framework for risk stratification and individualized intervention, and nominate CD40-centered combination regimens for next-generation precision oncology trials aimed at eradicating high-risk chemoresistant disease and improving long-term outcomes.
