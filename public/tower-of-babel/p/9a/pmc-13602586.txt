Spontaneous gastric perforation in a 12-year-old adolescent girl: a case report and literature review
Authors: Lisong Shao, Weijun Li, Yuanlong Han, Lian’en Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) is a rare but life-threatening acute abdominal emergency. It is more commonly reported in neonates, whereas cases in older children and adolescents are uncommon. Because of its nonspecific clinical manifestations, SGP is prone to misdiagnosis or delayed treatment. We report a successfully treated case of SGP in a 12-year-old girl, with the perforation located in the relatively concealed posterior prepyloric region of the stomach. Combined with a literature review, this report discusses the clinical features, potential pathogenesis, imaging findings, and diagnostic and therapeutic strategies of pediatric SGP, and evaluates the clinical value of laparoscopic exploration in early diagnosis and treatment.Case presentationA 12-year-old adolescent girl was admitted with a 5-h history of abdominal pain. She presented with sudden severe epigastric pain, a forced posture, nausea, and vomiting. Abdominal radiography showed a large amount of free gas beneath both hemidiaphragms, suggesting gastrointestinal perforation. Abdominal computed tomography further supported the preliminary diagnosis of gastric perforation with acute peritonitis. Emergency laparoscopic exploration revealed an approximately 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm round perforation in the posterior prepyloric wall of the stomach, with no obvious abnormalities in the remaining gastric wall. Laparoscopic two-layer primary suture repair was performed. Postoperatively, the patient received anti-infective and supportive treatment, recovered well, and experienced no related complications during follow-up.ConclusionsAlthough SGP is rare in older children and adolescents, it should be included in the differential diagnosis of pediatric acute abdomen. Perforation of the posterior prepyloric wall is relatively concealed, and early clinical manifestations and inflammatory responses may be atypical, increasing the risk of missed diagnosis. Timely imaging evaluation is essential for early diagnosis. In children with a short disease course, stable vital signs, limited intra-abdominal contamination, and a small perforation, laparoscopic exploration can help identify the perforation site and assess the degree of contamination, while allowing simultaneous minimally invasive repair. This case suggests that early recognition, prompt imaging assessment, and individualized surgical decision-making are key to improving clinical outcomes in pediatric SGP.

Introduction

Spontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) is a rare but life-threatening surgical acute abdomen. It is more commonly reported in neonates, occasionally described in adults, and relatively uncommon in older children and adolescents [1]. Owing to its abrupt onset and nonspecific clinical manifestations, early SGP is often confused with other causes of acute abdomen, which may lead to delayed diagnosis. If not promptly recognized and treated, leakage of gastric contents can rapidly progress to diffuse peritonitis, sepsis, shock, and multiple organ dysfunction, seriously affecting treatment outcomes and prognosis in children [2–6]. Current evidence suggests that neonatal SGP has been widely reported and is often associated with congenital gastrointestinal anomalies, abnormal development of the gastric wall, or other perinatal factors [7, 8]. In contrast, most cases of gastric perforation beyond the neonatal period can be traced to identifiable triggers or underlying causes, such as trauma, peptic ulcer disease, corrosive ingestion, accidental ingestion of magnetic beads, medication use including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids, or stress-related factors [9, 10]. By comparison, pediatric SGP cases without a definite cause remain very limited, and their pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated. Most reported pediatric SGP cases involve perforation along the greater or lesser curvature of the stomach, with prominent early symptoms, rapid disease progression, and high mortality [2, 3, 5]. Here, we report a successfully treated case of SGP in a 12-year-old adolescent girl. The perforation was a small, round defect located in the posterior prepyloric wall of the stomach, a relatively concealed anatomical site, and both the early clinical manifestations and inflammatory response were atypical. Based on a review of the existing literature, this study focuses on the clinical features and possible underlying mechanisms associated with this unusual perforation site, and further analyzes the combined value of imaging evaluation and laparoscopic exploration in early diagnosis and individualized minimally invasive treatment. This case may provide a useful reference for the clinical recognition and therapeutic decision-making of pediatric SGP.

Case presentation

A 12-year- and 6-month-old girl was brought to the emergency department of our hospital by her parents on October 17, 2025, with a 5-h history of abdominal pain. She reported the sudden onset of marked epigastric pain, accompanied by fatigue and poor appetite. The pain subsequently became diffuse and involved the entire abdomen, and she assumed an antalgic posture. She also experienced multiple episodes of non-projectile vomiting consisting mainly of gastric contents without bile. She had no diarrhea, urinary frequency, or urinary urgency. On physical examination, her body temperature was 37.6 °C. Abdominal distension was present, with mild abdominal muscle guarding. No abnormal mass was palpable. Deep tenderness was noted throughout the abdomen, while rebound tenderness was not elicited in the right lower quadrant. Bowel sounds were decreased. Detailed history taking from both the patient and her guardians confirmed that she had previously been in good health, with no relevant family history, no prior episodes of abdominal pain, and no recent history of trauma, foreign body ingestion, medication use, or corrosive substance ingestion. There was also no history of binge eating before symptom onset. An upright abdominal radiograph obtained in the emergency department showed a large amount of free air beneath both hemidiaphragms, raising strong suspicion of gastrointestinal perforation (Fig. 1). To further clarify the diagnosis, abdominal computed tomography was performed immediately and demonstrated a large volume of free intraperitoneal air (Fig. 2), consistent with gastrointestinal perforation with diffuse peritonitis. Following admission, the patient immediately underwent laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, coagulation profile, liver and renal function tests, and screening for infectious diseases. However, the white blood cell count and C-reactive protein level were only mildly elevated and not markedly abnormal. No contraindications to surgery were identified. One hour after emergency admission, she underwent laparoscopic exploration under general anesthesia. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered 30 min before surgery. Pneumoperitoneum was established through one 5-mm periumbilical incision and two 3-mm working ports. Intraoperatively, a 0.5 × 0.5 cm round perforation was identified in the prepyloric region of the posterior gastric wall. No ulcer scar was seen around the perforation, and no abnormalities were found in the remainder of the stomach or in other intra-abdominal organs. A small amount of purulent exudate was present in the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 3). The perforation was repaired laparoscopically with a two-layer suture closure, and the repair was reinforced by coverage with the greater omentum. After peritoneal irrigation, a nasogastric tube was placed and the abdomen was closed. The procedure was completed uneventfully, with an operative time of 90 min and an estimated blood loss of approximately 3 mL.Fig. 3Intraoperative laparoscopic view showing a round perforation in the prepyloric region of the posterior gastric wall (arrow)

On postoperative day 1, a ^13C urea breath test for Helicobacter pylori was performed and was negative. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient received ceftriaxone for anti-infective therapy and omeprazole for acid suppression and gastric protection. She was kept nil per os for 2 days with intravenous fluid support, resumed a liquid diet on postoperative day 3, and had the nasogastric tube removed the same day. She was discharged on postoperative day 5. Based on the clinical presentation, medical history, and examination findings, the final postoperative diagnosis was spontaneous gastric perforation in a child. At follow-up 1 week, 1 month, and 2 months after discharge, she remained well, with no abdominal pain or other gastrointestinal symptoms, and had returned to school and normal physical activity.

Discussion

Rarity and challenges in clinical recognition

Compared with the neonatal period, spontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) is relatively uncommon in post-school-age children and adolescents [2–5]. Our literature review (Table 1) showed that reported cases of SGP in children older than 1 year remain very limited, are mainly concentrated in toddlers and preschool-aged children, and include a considerable proportion of fatal cases [3, 5, 6]. Although adolescent SGP is not unprecedented [2, 4], its rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation may still lead to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis, with rapid progression to diffuse peritonitis, septic shock, or even death [3, 5]. The uniqueness of the present case does not lie solely in the rarity of SGP in this pediatric age group, but mainly in the unusual perforation site. Previously reported adolescent SGP cases have mostly involved the greater or lesser curvature of the stomach [2, 4, 5]. In contrast, the perforation in this case was located in the posterior prepyloric wall, a relatively concealed site, making this a rare case of posterior gastric wall perforation in an adolescent. Notably, the perforation site may directly influence early clinical manifestations and the difficulty of recognition. Anterior gastric wall perforation, because of the larger peritoneal space and relatively poor omental coverage in children, can readily lead to leakage of gastric contents and gas, resulting in obvious pneumoperitoneum on radiography and prominent signs of peritonitis, which facilitates early recognition. In contrast, perforation of the posterior wall near the pylorus may be partially covered by the lesser sac, limiting the early leakage of gastric contents and gas. As a result, peritoneal irritation and inflammatory responses may be atypical, increasing the risk of missed or delayed diagnosis. Potential pathogenesis

The epidemiological characteristics and pathogenesis of pediatric spontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) have not yet been fully clarified. Spontaneous neonatal gastric perforation (SNGP) is often associated with perinatal asphyxia, excessive mechanical ventilation, low birth weight, prolonged corticosteroid use, embryonic developmental defects, structural abnormalities of the gastric wall, and a reduced number of interstitial cells of Cajal (ICCs) [7, 8, 23]. In contrast, gastric perforation beyond the neonatal period in children is usually secondary to identifiable causes, such as trauma, corrosive ingestion, gastric ulcer, gastric tuberculosis, Helicobacter pylori infection, or Burkitt lymphoma treated with chemotherapy [10, 24]. Current evidence suggests that pediatric SGP may be related to increased intragastric pressure, reduced gastric wall perfusion, and local ischemic injury [1, 5]. When intragastric pressure persistently exceeds 30 cm H₂O, venous return from the gastric wall may be impaired, leading to local ischemia, necrosis, and even perforation [25]. Wang et al. [5], in an analysis of 20 non-neonatal pediatric SGP cases, found that most perforations occurred along the greater curvature, which may be explained by this region being the most elastic part of the gastric wall and therefore more susceptible to distension and ischemic injury. In addition, seizures, neurological disorders, and gastric motility dysfunction may contribute to the disease process through gastric wall injury or abnormal motility [4, 20]. The case reported by Watanabe et al. [4] was associated with gastric motility dysfunction and malnutrition, although the perforation site was not emphasized. In the present case, the perforation was located in the posterior prepyloric wall, a transitional area where the gastric lumen gradually narrows toward the pylorus. This region may be exposed to relatively pronounced local mechanical stress and gastric peristaltic pressure; therefore, transient pressure-related injury or local ischemia cannot be completely excluded. Prabhu et al. [15] previously reported spontaneous posterior gastric wall perforation in a non-neonatal child, indicating that posterior wall perforation, although rare, can occur. This patient had no family history, history of trauma, chronic abdominal pain, or medication use, and no history of foreign body or corrosive ingestion. H. pylori testing was negative, and no peptic ulcer or neoplastic lesion was observed intraoperatively. In addition, the patient had no stress-related factors such as binge eating and showed no clinical features of Prader–Willi syndrome. Based on the clinical data and previous literature, and after excluding other definite causes, the final diagnosis was SGP. However, because the patient’s family did not consent to further histopathological examination, our ability to further clarify the underlying mechanism was limited, which represents one limitation of this study. Overall, this case not only expands current clinical understanding of unusual perforation sites in adolescent SGP, but also suggests that spontaneous gastric perforation may occur in children even in the absence of typical high-risk factors. It may therefore provide a useful reference for improving clinical recognition and further exploring the pathogenesis of pediatric SGP.

Imaging findings and early diagnosis

The clinical manifestations of pediatric spontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) are nonspecific and commonly include abdominal pain, abdominal distension, vomiting, or respiratory distress [5, 6, 20]. The typical presentation is sudden severe abdominal pain with signs of peritonitis. However, children, especially younger patients, often have limited ability to describe or localize pain accurately. When the perforation site is relatively concealed and early peritoneal irritation and inflammatory markers are atypical, SGP may be more easily confused with other causes of acute abdomen. Imaging evaluation is essential for early recognition. Ultrasonography is sensitive for detecting even small amounts of free gas and may provide early clues to gastrointestinal perforation in neonates [26]. However, in children with suspected hollow viscus perforation, abdominal radiography remains of important diagnostic value [27, 28]. In the present case, the detection of subdiaphragmatic free gas on abdominal radiography provided a key basis for early diagnosis. Computed tomography (CT) is the most sensitive imaging modality for evaluating suspected gastrointestinal perforation, with a reported accuracy of 82–90% for localizing the perforation segment [27]. In this case, preoperative abdominal CT confirmed gastrointestinal perforation and provided important information for selecting the surgical approach and planning trocar placement for laparoscopic exploration. The patient’s pain rapidly progressed from sudden epigastric pain to generalized abdominal pain. In children with diffuse abdominal tenderness, conditions such as abdominal-type Henoch–Schönlein purpura and sepsis-related secondary peritonitis should also be considered in the differential diagnosis, in addition to gastrointestinal perforation. However, these conditions generally do not present with a large amount of free intraperitoneal gas and are usually accompanied by clues to the underlying primary disease.

Treatment and prognosis

SGP has an abrupt onset and is associated with high mortality. The mortality rate in neonates has been reported to be as high as 30–70% [29], and that in non-neonatal children may reach 30%. This high mortality is mainly related to chemical peritonitis, the corrosive effects of gastric acid and pepsin, and secondary sepsis or septic shock [5]. Surgical intervention is therefore essential for improving prognosis. Previous pediatric SGP cases have mostly been managed by open surgery, Graham patch repair, or partial gastrectomy [1, 2, 13]. In the present case, although preoperative imaging suggested diffuse peritonitis, laparoscopic exploration was selected first because of the short disease course, hemodynamic stability, and only mild elevations in white blood cell count and C-reactive protein level. The primary purpose of laparoscopy was not simply to perform minimally invasive repair, but to identify the perforation site early, assess the degree of intra-abdominal contamination, and guide subsequent treatment decisions. Intraoperatively, only a small amount of purulent exudate was observed, with no extensive adhesions or obvious gastric wall necrosis. The perforation measured only 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 and was suitable for primary suture repair. Laparoscopic two-layer primary closure was therefore performed successfully, resulting in a favorable clinical outcome. These findings suggest that the key value of laparoscopy lies not only in its minimally invasive nature, but also in its integrated role in etiological diagnosis, contamination assessment, and intraoperative decision-making. However, laparoscopic treatment is not suitable for all pediatric SGP cases. According to relevant consensus on acute abdomen management, laparoscopy is appropriate for patients who are hemodynamically stable, have controllable intra-abdominal contamination, a small perforation, and adequate operative conditions [30]. In carefully selected pediatric SGP patients, laparoscopic exploration may serve as a feasible diagnostic and therapeutic strategy. Nevertheless, current evidence regarding laparoscopic treatment for pediatric SGP is still mainly derived from case reports, and high-quality supporting studies are lacking. If the perforation cannot be identified intraoperatively, or if severe contamination, extensive adhesions, unreliable repair, or obvious gastric wall ischemia or necrosis is encountered, open surgery should be selected or conversion to open surgery should be performed promptly according to the patient’s condition.

Conclusion

Although pediatric spontaneous gastric perforation (SGP) is rare, it has an abrupt onset and can progress rapidly; therefore, it should be included in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with unexplained acute abdominal pain or pneumoperitoneum. This case describes SGP in an adolescent girl, with the perforation located in the posterior prepyloric wall of the stomach, a relatively uncommon anatomical site. The early clinical manifestations and inflammatory response were atypical, creating a risk of missed or delayed diagnosis. The successful management of this case broadens current clinical understanding of unusual perforation sites in pediatric SGP and may provide a reference for future case management and research. In children with suspected SGP, imaging evaluation should be performed as early as possible to establish the diagnosis. With strict case selection, laparoscopic exploration may provide combined value in etiological diagnosis, assessment of intra-abdominal contamination, and minimally invasive treatment. Early recognition and individualized surgical intervention are key to improving the prognosis of pediatric SGP. However, its pathogenesis and optimal diagnostic and therapeutic strategies require further investigation through multicenter case accumulation and long-term follow-up.
