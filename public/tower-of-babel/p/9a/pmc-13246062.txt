The role of visceral obesity surrogate markers in predicting cardiovascular disease: Insights from the CHARLS study
Authors: Pingan Guo, Yuan Liu, Guozhu Chen, Yin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the world, accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths worldwide. Early detection of risk factors and effective preventive measures are crucial for reducing the impact of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases on public health. We conducted a retrospective analysis of data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study between 2011 and 2018. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline modeling were used for analysis. Moreover, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to assess the predictive power of Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), lipid accumulation product (LAP), metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF), triglyceride-glucose (TyG), and triglyceride/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) for risk of CVD. CVAI and METS-VF were significantly associated with CVD risk, with odds ratios calculated per 1 standard deviation (CVAI: odds ratio [OR] = 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.29; METS-VF: OR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.01–1.23). LAP (Q3: OR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.11–1.73) and TG/HDL-C ratio (Q3: OR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.08–1.59) showed significant associations with increased CVD risk. For discrimination, CVAI showed the highest AUC values, 0.5767 (0.5560–0.5974) for HD, 0.6123 (0.5873–0.6373) for stroke, 0.5910 (0.5726–0.6093) for CVD, respectively. CVAI and METS-VF were positively associated with CVD risk and showed slightly better discrimination than other adiposity-related indices. These findings suggest that visceral adiposity and dyslipidemia remain important correlates of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk and may inform prevention strategies when considered alongside established risk factors.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is one of the most common clinical diseases and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for about 1/3 of global deaths. At the same time, CVD is also one of the leading causes of disease burden in China. According to the China Cardiovascular Health and Disease Report 2021, the incidence of CVD in China has continued to increase, with the number of people suffering from the disease now as high as 330 million, of which the elderly population occupies the majority, and the mortality rate of CVD being the highest among urban and rural residents. Ischemic heart disease and stroke, which account for 84.9% of cardiovascular fatalities, are the most prevalent and fatal of cardiovascular disease.[1] Meanwhile, participants diagnosed with heart disease (HD), such as heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or stroke, were also considered patients with CVD based on previous literature.[2] In 2019, there were 3.94 million new stroke cases in China. The incidence rate of stroke increased by 86·0% (73.2–99.0) from 1990, reaching 277 (241–322) per 100 000 population in 2019.[3] With the advancement of the socio-economic landscape and shifts in national lifestyles, particularly the accelerated pace of population aging and urbanization, the prevalence of CVD in China is becoming more and more prominent. This can result in enduring discomfort and a decline in physical functionality, significantly impinging on patients’ daily productivity and overall quality of life. The high cost of medical care, long-term treatment, and rehabilitation also imposes a huge economic burden on patients, their families, and society as a whole. According to the National Mortality Surveillance System, the number of deaths from CVD increased from 3.09 million in 2005 to 4.58 million in 2020.[4] In urban China, Stroke-related medical expenses totaled $1682, with an annual out-of-pocket cost of $489.[5] Therefore, the detection of CVD risk factors, early prevention, diagnosis and treatment are of great public health importance to reduce the risk of CVD to human health.

Metabolic disorders, such as high visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia, have a strong link with the development of CVD.[6–9] These factors increase the risk of CVD by increasing the risk of metabolic diseases, for example, hypertension and diabetes; or by causing vascular endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, inflammatory response, redox imbalance and thrombosis.[10,11] Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), Lipid accumulation product (LAP), and Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) are considered as reliable surrogate markers of visceral adiposity. Among them, CVAI is a body fat index constructed by Xia et al by combining the characteristics of Chinese population, which is considered as a potential marker for evaluating visceral adiposity dysfunction in Chinese.[12] Previous studies have found that CVAI is a reliable predictor for the incidence of myocardial infarction and cardiovascular disease in patients with hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea.[13,14] However, its predictive value in the general population deserves further exploration. LAP contains both anthropometric and blood biochemical data, may be more reflective of the degree of lipid accumulation than single central obesity and are easily accessible.[15] METS-VF is a new visceral fat prediction index created by Mexican scholars in the last 2 years, which is considered to be a valid tool for assessing cardiovascular metabolic risk in primary care and research institutions.[16] Importantly, although triglycerides (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) are known CVD risk factors, they do not fully reflect all potential risks. CVAI and METS-VF consider the impact of fat distribution and body fat proportion, which play a crucial role in cardiovascular risk assessment, especially in Asian populations where abdominal obesity has a more pronounced impact. Therefore, as new indices for assessing visceral fat content, CVAI and METS-VF may help provide a more comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular health, optimizing patient management and prognosis. Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is regarded as a trustworthy predictor for assessing the degree of insulin resistance.[17] The ratio of triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) could evaluate the integrated degree of lipid metabolism, which has been linked to a variety of cardiovascular diseases.[18] All of the above 5 (CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, and TG/HDL-C) have been associated with the development of CVD/HD/stroke, however, the difference in the strength of their correlation with the above diseases, as well as the difference in their performance in terms of predicting the risk of the diseases, is not known.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collects high-quality information on families and individuals of middle-aged and older adults in China. Longitudinal cohort studies based on large samples and large data provide an effective guarantee for the scientific validity of the results. Therefore, this research intends to construct a retrospective cohort study using the CHARLS to investigate the relationship between CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, TG/HDL-C and the risk of CVD/HD/Stroke, and to perform dose-response relationship analyses and visualize and present the results using restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling; at the same time, to assess the baseline CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, and TG/HDL-C levels for their predictive ability of the risk of developing CVD/HD/Stroke. It is anticipated that the results of this investigation will provide a valuable scientific foundation for understanding the relationship between metabolic disorders and CVD, and for improving risk assessment and intervention strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

We retrospectively carried out analysis of records from the CHARLS 2011 to 2018. When CHARLS 2011, there were totally 17,709 respondents in database. For different outcomes of disease events, people who meet the following criteria were included in this study: 1) individuals over the age of 45 in CHARLS 2011; 2) and with regarding recodes of disease status. Also, exclusion criteria were lack of data about disease status among Wave 2–4; lack of age information or ages under 45 years old; persons who had been diagnosed with corresponding diseases when CHARLS 2011; missing data for the calculation of relevant surrogate markers of visceral obesity. Finally, a total of 10,004, 11,291 and 9899 participants were included in the analysis focused on heart disease, stroke and CVD. A flowchart for case selection is shown in Figure 1.

A flowchart of the study population.

2.2. Blood data

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from participants in the morning by professional medical staff. In detail, enzymatic colorimetric test was conducted to measure the levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), Total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and glucose. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography.

2.4. Definition of outcomes

The HD was defined by the previous diagnosis of heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart diseases, and the stroke was assessed by the question “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor.” The respondents diagnosed with HD or stroke were determined as CVD patients.[19] In this analysis, these diseases identified in any waves of 2013, 2015 or 2018 were treated as the outcome event, and those never diagnosed with CVD related diseases were classified as the non-disease group.

2.5. Covariates

CHARLS collected the information about demography (age, sex), vital signs [height, weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP)], lifestyle behavior (alcohol and tobacco use), chronic diseases history (diabetes, high blood pressure and cardiac disease) and multiple biochemical indexes for all the individuals. Therefore, the body mass index (BMI) was estimated by weight/height2 (kg/m2), and developed to identify several weight status classes by national criterion: underweight (BMI < 18.5), health weight (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24), overweight (24 ≤ BMI < 28) and obesity (BMI ≥ 28). Then, except for the hypertension history records, the definition of hypertension was systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg (mean of 3 measurements) or related antihypertensive drug use. Also, the definition of diabetes mellitus (DM) was determined as history of diabetes, fasting glucose beyond 126 mg/dL and/or HbA1c more overs 6.5%, or related antihyperglycemic drug use. Hyperlipidemia was classified by showing any of the following signs when baseline: HDL-C < 40 mg/dl, LDL-C > 160 mg/dl, TC ≥ 240 mg/dl, or TG ≥ 200 mg/dl as well as the responses of dyslipidemia to chronic diseases. The smokers were defined by the history of smoking, with a response “Yes” to the question “Have you ever chewed tobacco, smoked a pipe, smoked self-rolled cigarettes, or smoked cigarettes/cigars,” and the participants who drunk at least once a month was defined as alcohol addicts. As for sociodemographic characteristics, marital status was dichotomized into being married and unmarried (including separated, divorced, widowed or never married). household status was classified as rural or urban, and educational standards was categorized into 4 levels: illiterate, elementary school, secondary school and above.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables in normal distribution were presented as means ± standard deviations, and those unnormal ones were presented as medians with an interquartile range. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Missing covariates were imputed using multiple imputation. Logistic regressions were employed to estimate the unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% CI for the associations between these surrogate markers of visceral obesity and CVD-related diseases. Among the regressions, Model 1 was singular variable regression, and Model 2 and 3 were adjusted by possible covariates. In detail, Model 2 additionally included age, sex and BMI scale first, and the last one consisted of all the confounders including SBP, DBP, smoking status, drinking alcohol, household status, marriage status and education levels. The restricted cubic spline models adjusted with potential confounding factors were applied to estimate nonlinear correlation and dose-response association between 5 surrogate markers of visceral obesity and disease risk. In addition, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex and BMI scales, in order to distinguish the distinct effect in specific population. Moreover, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and its 95% CI was calculated to assess the predictive power of CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG and TG/HDL-C, and combinations of surrogate markers with best performances for CVD-related outcomes. Optimal cutoff and Youden Index were identified as well. In this study, 2-tailed P-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all the analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2).

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

A descriptive summary of the variables for the entire study population categorized by heart diseases, stroke and CVD is provided (Supplementary Tables 1–3). Among 9899 respondents in CVD analysis, 2090 (21.11%) of them reported CVD events. The risk of CVD was significantly related with age, sex, body mass index (BMI), drinking alcohol, sleeping time, household status, marital status, various blood index (GLU, HbA1c, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and TC) and chronic diseases including hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidemia. Moreover, in the baseline description for heart diseases (1541/10,004) and stroke (934/11,291), these characteristics of participants with diseases obviously differed from those without as well.

3.2. Multivariate logistic regression analysis of 5 surrogate markers of visceral obesity and CVD

We carried out logistic regressions to explore the relationship for 5 surrogate markers of visceral obesity with risk of cardiovascular diseases, as well as their quartiles (Table 1). The results from the adjusted model indicated most of the markers were connected with CVD in a nonlinear way, instead of CVAI and METS-VF, for per 1-SD increment in CVAI was associated with 16% higher odds (odds ratio [OR] = 1.16,95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05–1.29) and per 1-SD increment in METS-VF was associated with 11% higher odds (OR = 1.11,95% CI, 1.01–1.23) for CVD. Especially, compared with Q1 group, later quantiles of LAP (Q3: OR = 1.39, 95% CI, 1.11–1.73; Q4: OR = 1.40, 95% CI, 1.10–1.78) and TG/HDL-C ratio (Q3: OR = 1.31, 95% CI, 1.08–1.59) led to higher risk of CVD. When it comes to heart diseases, only several quantiles for CVAI (Q2: OR = 1.29, 95% CI, 1.01–1.65; Q3: OR = 1.34, 95% CI, 1.03–1.75), LAP (Q3: OR = 1.33, 95% CI, 1.04–1.71) and TG/HDL-C ratio (Q3: OR = 1.27, 95% CI, 1.03–1.58) showed significant results. The correlations between the 5 surrogate markers and stroke were close to them with CVD with exception of TyG, which only presented strong connection with risk of stroke (per 1-SD: OR = 1.12, 95% CI, 1.02–1.24). Our results indicated that, after adjusting for covariates, the risk of stroke and CVD gradually increased with higher quartiles of CVAI (P for trend: .000; P for trend: .009), LAP (P for trend: .000; P for trend: .003), and METS-VF (P for trend: .020; P for trend: .034). However, TyG and TG/HDL-C often showed significant effects in the Q2 or Q3 quartiles, suggesting a nonlinear relationship with disease risk.

3.3. Dose-response association between 5 surrogate markers of visceral obesity and the risk of the diseases

The results from the logistic models suggested the nonlinear relationships between surrogate markers and those diseases. Therefore, we visualized the dose-response associations with RCS regression (Figs. 2–4) and discovered U-shaped association between 5 surrogate markers of visceral obesity and CVD risk. The significant nonlinear relationships were found in CVAI (P non-linearity = .011), LAP (P non-linearity = .007) and TG/HDL-C ratio (P non-linearity = .037) with CVD event, as well as them with HD (P non-linearity = .006; P non-linearity = .034; P non-linearity = .050, respectively). However, the linkages for stroke were in a linear way, as higher level of surrogate markers was linked with increased risk of stroke, instead of TyG (P non-linearity = .024). Compared to the results from logistic regressions, RCS more directly depicted the relationship between each surrogate marker and disease risk. For example, we observed nonlinear changes in the OR for CVD as the levels of CVAI and LAP increased, which were not revealed in the logistic regression analyses based on the quartiles.

3.4. Subgroup analyses of the association between 5 surrogate markers and the risk of diseases

4. Discussion

Based on what we currently know, this is the initial research to simultaneously assess the relationship of CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, and TG/HDL-C with CVD, HD, and stroke risk. Our findings provide valuable scientific evidence for understanding the association between metabolic disorders and CVD and offer meaningful insights for clinical practice and prevention strategies. In this study, we have summarized several key findings that highlight the significance of these metabolic indices in predicting cardiovascular risks. We suggested that all 5 indexes were associated with CVD/HD/stroke risk. The risk of CVD gradually increased with increasing levels of CVAI and METS-VF. With increasing levels of CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, and TyG, stroke risk increased significantly. CVAI and METS-VF were more predictive of CVD/HD/stroke than the other 3 indices.

CVAI is an important indicator for evaluating visceral adiposity among the Chinese people, and we found that for every one SD increase in CVAI, there was a 16% increase in the risk of CVD (OR = 1.16, 95% CI:1.05–1.29), and a 27% increase in the risk of Stroke (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.11–1.46). This is in line with the study of Wan et al.,[20] who found that a one standard deviation rise in CVAI level was linked to a higher prevalence of CVD. However, compared to their study, our key difference lies in further exploring the predictive ability of CVAI for CVD, while simultaneously comparing it with other body fat-related indicators.

Our findings are corroborated by a US population-based study that found a relationship between stroke occurrence and elevated levels of visceral adiposity index.[21] It is well known that visceral adipose tissue content is considered to be a reliable indicator of insulin resistance,[22] and insulin resistance and its resulting hyperinsulinemia may contribute to the development of CVD by increasing vascular stiffness.[9] In addition, inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, released from adipose tissue may cause CVD, stroke, and HD by mediating endothelial dysfunction, plaque formation, and plaque destabilization.[23] In addition, we found that CVAI had a strong predictive ability for CVD/ HD/stroke. This may be related to the fact that CVAI covers multiple indicators such as age, BMI, WC, TG, HDL-C, etc, which assesses the status of the organism from different levels.

LAP is an assessment of visceral adiposity levels that combines anatomical and physiological indicators, namely waist circumference and TG. We found that those with LAP at the Q3 level were at a significantly increased risk of developing CVD/HD/stroke compared to Q1. LAP level and HD risk were correlated linearly, while CVD risk was correlated nonlinearly. Similar to our results, Kyrou and colleagues[24] found that baseline LAP level was significantly and positively associated with 10-year CVD incidence, and its predictive efficacy was superior to other obesity-related indicators such as BMI, WC, waist-to-hip, and waist-to-height ratio. This implies that LAP may be an important tool for predicting and evaluating CVD risk in patients. The increased LAP levels imply, to some extent, an increase in visceral adiposity, which may affect various physiological and metabolic processes in the body, including glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, or the function and structure of blood vessels, thereby increasing the risk of CVD.[11,25]

METS-VF is an innovative tool for evaluating intra-abdominal adiposity and cardiometabolic health. Previous studies have shown its association with the risk of various systemic diseases, including chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, asthma, and others,[26–29] but it has been relatively understudied in cardiovascular disease. A recent study[30] found that METS-VF was a marker of the development of coronary artery calcification and the recognition of high-risk obese subjects in daily clinical practice. Moreover, Cai et al found a relationship between METS-IR and the risk of stroke in the patients with hypertension.[31] This is similar to our findings. Compared to their study, we further found that METS-VF is more advantageous than other previous indices for predicting the risk of CVD, stroke, etc. The METS-VF formula takes into account METS-IR as an index, making it the first visceral fat assessment index to incorporate insulin resistance into the formula. This index also incorporates age, a significant risk indicator for CVD events, which may potentially explain its ability to more effectively assess the risk of CVD/HD/stroke. Our findings further extend the clinical utility of the METS-VF.

It has been shown that the TyG index is a reliable replacement predictor of insulin resistance (IR). In recent years, there is substantial evidence that the TyG index is related to the occurrence and prognosis of CVD.[32,33] Our study further confirmed these findings and illustrated that TyG was also strongly associated with stroke and HD. The possible mechanisms are as follows: on the one hand, IR triggers inflammation and oxidative stress by causing an imbalance in glucose metabolism and activating the mitochondrial electron transport chain[34]; on the other hand, IR causes lipid metabolism disorders, leading to atherosclerosis. At the same time, IR may lead to platelet hyperactivity,[35] increase the expression of thromboxane A2, cause thrombosis, and increase the risk of CVD and stroke.

TG/HDL-C has been widely applied to evaluate the degree of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome in individuals.[36,37] Some researchers have discovered a relationship between the TG/HDL-C and the risk of heart disease, stroke and CVD. A study based on UK biobank data found that the risk of CVD tended to increase significantly with increasing quartiles of the TG/HDL-C ratio.[38] Sato and colleagues[39] found that elevated TG/HDL-C was associated with a significantly higher risk of stroke in a healthy BMI population, and was a potential predictor of the risk of stroke in a healthy BMI population. Increased insulin resistance is a significant risk factor for the onset of CVD, and it may be represented in increased TG/HDL-C ratios. In addition, high triglyceride levels may contribute to the formation of atherosclerosis. These may be the reasons why an increased TG/HDL-C ratio is linked to a higher probability of heart disease and stroke.

Our results suggest that the CVAI and METS-VF indices show modest discrimination in identifying individuals at risk for CVD/HD/stroke. These indices may serve as useful adjunct markers in risk stratification, but their ability to predict individual outcomes is limited. The effectiveness of these markers may vary across different populations due to factors such as ethnicity, comorbidities, and genetic background. We did not perform a formal head-to-head comparison with established cardiovascular risk scores (e.g., Framingham, SCORE/ASCVD) in the present study. First, several components required to compute these scores consistently (such as detailed lipid fractions, medication use, or other score-specific variables and definitions) were not uniformly available across CHARLS waves or were not measured in a manner fully compatible with the original derivation cohorts, which could lead to noncomparable or biased estimates if applied directly. Second, many widely used risk scores were developed and calibrated in populations with different baseline risk profiles and outcome adjudication procedures; applying them without appropriate recalibration may conflate model transportability issues with the evaluation of visceral adiposity indices. Consequently, they should be integrated into a comprehensive risk assessment approach alongside established multivariable models for more accurate clinical decision-making. For patients with high CVAI and METS-VF scores, more aggressive management of cardiovascular risk factors, such as lipid levels, blood pressure, and blood glucose, is recommended. While lifestyle modifications, including diet, exercise, and weight management, play a key role, challenges remain in ensuring patient adherence and integrating these indices into routine clinical practice due to the need for specific equipment and trained professionals. Furthermore, regular monitoring of CVAI and METS-VF levels in high-risk individuals is essential for tracking changes in metabolic health and adjusting treatment strategies accordingly. Despite their usefulness, accessibility and patient compliance may pose challenges in resource-limited settings. In conclusion, integrating CVAI and METS-VF into clinical practice could significantly enhance the prevention and management of CVD/HD/stroke, but their implementation should be tailored to individual patient needs and clinical settings, with further research needed to improve their applicability and cost-effectiveness across diverse populations.

5. Advantages and limitations

Our study has certain advantages. First, the CHARLS is a large database based on the Chinese population, which covers a wide range of populations, including both clinical information and biospecimen data. The large sample size of the clinical study increased the generalization ability and reliability of the results. Second, the 7-year longitudinal cohort study design provides some reference for interpreting the association between baseline CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, and TG/HDL-C levels and the risk of prevalence of CVD/HD/stroke in later stages. Further, we adjusted for multiple confounding covariates to ensure the reliability and generalizability of the results. However, there are some limitations of this study. First, the survey was conducted among Chinese adults aged 45 years or older; therefore, whether these findings can be generalized to other ethnic groups or younger populations requires further investigation. Outcomes were ascertained using self-reported physician diagnoses in CHARLS, which may introduce recall bias and outcome misclassification, particularly among older participants. Such misclassification could attenuate true associations and should be addressed in future studies through validation with adjudicated endpoints. Second, although CVAI and METS-VF demonstrated relatively higher AUCs than the other indices, overall discrimination remained modest (AUC ≈ 0.58–0.61) and should not be interpreted as strong stand-alone clinical predictive performance. In addition, residual confounding may persist, including unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors such as diet, physical activity, and time-varying metabolic characteristics.

6. Conclusion

In this study, our findings suggest that CVAI, LAP, METS-VF, TyG, and TG/HDL-C are associated with the risk of CVD/HD/stroke. Of these, CVAI and METS-VF showed relatively higher discriminatory performance, though their AUC values reflect only modest ability to differentiate risk. These findings reinforce the role of high visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia as independent risk factors for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. However, the generalizability of these results is limited by the retrospective nature of this study, which was conducted in middle-aged and elderly individuals in China. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse cohorts and assessing the effectiveness of interventions targeting these markers to reduce CVD risk.
