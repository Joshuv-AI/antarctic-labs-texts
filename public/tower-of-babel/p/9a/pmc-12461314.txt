Time-Dependent Heat Transfer Coefficients of Standard Cans during Pasteurization
Authors: Hussain Albattah Alhusni, Suresh Krishnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Canned food products are pasteurized by heating them in hot water to deactivate enzymes, followed by rapid cooling. We utilized computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to analyze heat transfer in juice cans of 330 mL, 500 mL, and 1000 mL placed in water baths set at 60 °C, 80 °C, and 25 °C. Two methods were employed to study heat transfer: a detailed conjugate heat transfer simulation and a simplified boundary condition approach that applies time-averaged heat transfer coefficients to the can wall without modeling the surrounding bath fluid. The conjugate heat transfer simulation’s time–temperature profile aligned with experimental results within a 2% margin. The three stages of heat transfer during heating and cooling cycles significantly affected the profile inside the sealed container, making this simulation essential for determining effective pasteurization come-up times. Results indicated that the heat transfer coefficients on both the inner and outer surfaces were nearly identical during heating and cooling. The temperature contours inside the container showed spatial patterns similar to those in both cycles. As expected from natural convection effects, a cold spot was consistently found near the bottom and a hot spot was consistently found near the top. Additionally, increasing the water bath temperature from 60 to 80 °C can shorten come-up times by 12–15%.

Introduction

Thermal processing is crucial for the safety of packaged foods. It includes methods like aseptic processing, where sterilization occurs before packaging, and canning, which applies treatments afterward. Assessing the effectiveness of thermal processing involves monitoring the time–temperature profile, linked to the behavior of target microorganisms. Conventional pasteurization processes aim for a microbial reduction of 5–10 log units (Clostridium sporogenes spores ATCC 11437), utilizing thermal resistance parameters such as D-values, which are around 30.20 min at 80 °C.

Measuring temperature and velocity profiles and pinpointing the slowest heating zones are challenging due to probe interference. To overcome this, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is used to simulate transient heat transfer and flow patterns in thermal processing, providing accurate predictions of time–temperature histories and capturing the complex interactions in natural convection. −

Sterilization requires high temperatures, where heating sources like steam increase contact and lower thermal resistance. Studies show that the sterilization of canned foods depends on factors such as the container’s height-to-diameter ratio, orientation, and the food’s rheological properties. Shorter cans improve heat transfer through better circulation, while taller cans can hinder convection. Placing tall cans horizontally promotes circular fluid motion, which enhances heat distribution and impacts quality parameters such as texture, color, and nutritional value. ,− Additionally, the heating rate of liquid foods in a container can be improved through rotational and reciprocal agitation. This method introduces extra inertial forces, resulting in shorter processing times and improved temperature uniformity.

The reviewed pasteurization methods include gentle treatments like hot-air and hot-water pasteurization, which are crucial for processing beverages such as milk, fruit juices, pharmaceutical products, and canned vegetables. Their goals are to inactivate spoilage microorganisms, extend shelf life, and maintain sensory qualities like flavor and color. However, pasteurization encounters challenges from thermal resistance related to the water bath, can thickness, and contents inside. Heat transfer during the process is complex, involving interactions between the liquid food and the surrounding bath fluid, making it more complicated than simple assumptions of a uniform heat transfer coefficient or outer can wall temperature profile. −

Thermal resistances from boundary layers and wall thickness slow heat equilibration. Recent studies show water baths outperform air ovens for thermal inactivation. The external convective heat transfer coefficient is crucial in food thermal processing, especially pasteurization, as it ensures product safety and quality. Despite advancements in computational fluid dynamics (CFD), there are few studies on the thermal resistance of hot bath fluids. This has resulted in gaps in our understanding of heat transfer during the pasteurization of canned food in water baths as well as in determining the average heat transfer coefficient at both the inner and outer surfaces.

This study aims to (1) validate the CFD methodology by comparing simulated results with experimental data from a 330 mL pasteurization container, (2) analyze how transient heat transfer coefficients affect time–temperature history and microbial reduction, and (3) compare the conjugate heat transfer results with those from the time-averaged heat transfer coefficient related to the container wall thickness.

Using the simulated results, we evaluated heat transfer at various surfaces of the container in different bath temperatures for commercially available canned sizes.

Materials and Methods

Physical and Thermal Properties

Fruits and vegetables from different regions often display variations in their physical properties, which can lead to inconsistencies and decreased reproducibility in experimental results. To reduce these variables, we chose grapes from a local market for our experiments. Fresh seedless grapes were manually pressed and filtered to obtain 330 mL of clear grape juice without the addition of any sugars. The filtration process ensured that only dissolved solids remained. A consistent reading of 18°Brix was measured before and after filtration using a hand-held refractometer (SKU 1794481). The physical properties of grape juice were characterized experimentally. Density was measured using a specific gravity bottle, while thermal conductivity and thermal diffusivity were estimated using a fabricated dual-needle line-heat-source (DNL) setup.

Rheological Characterization

The rheological behavior of grape juice was characterized using a rotational rheometer (RM 200 PLUS, Lamy Rheology Instruments, France) fitted with a spindle MS-DIN 11, suitable for low-viscosity fluids. The system has a measuring volume of 27 mL and operates in the viscosity range of 2.5–27,000 mPa·s. Since the viscosity showed negligible variation with temperature and shear rate across the tested range, the average value was 0.0032 Pa·s. Pasteurization Process and CFD Methods

Pasteurization involves heating, followed by cooling. Containers filled with grape juice were immersed in water baths (hot or cold) at various temperatures for this purpose.

Method 1 (Conjugate Heat Transfer/Water Bath Simulation): This method solved the governing equations simultaneously across three conjugate domains: the water bath fluid, the can wall, and grape juice inside the can. Energy transfer between these domains enabled the simulation of conjugate heat transfer.

Method 2 (Boundary Condition Approach/Without Water Bath Simulation): This method utilized time-averaged heat transfer coefficients (derived from Method 1 results) applied as boundary conditions on the outer surface of the can wall. Only the equations for the juice inside the can and the can wall itself were solved in this approach. This method is practically acceptable due to reduced computational resources while maintaining reasonable accuracy for modeling industrial processes and scale-up.

The pasteurization process was modeled using three conjugate domains: the water bath (fluid), the grape juice inside the container (fluid), and the can wall (solid). The governing equations for these domains were solved numerically using ANSYS CFX V17.0. In the fluid domainsthe water bath and the juicecontinuity, momentum, and energy equations were applied, while in the solid domain of the can wall, only the energy equation for heat conduction was considered. This conjugate heat transfer simulation provided velocity and pressure distributions within the fluid regions as well as temperature profiles throughout all domains.

Initial Conditions and Boundary Conditions

Initial State (T = 0)

At the start of the simulation, both the grape juice and the can wall are set to a uniform temperature. For heating scenarios, this initial temperature is 27 °C, while for cooling simulations, the starting temperature is either 60 or 80 °C, depending on the specific conditions being modeled. The water bath, which serves as the external thermal environment, is maintained at a constant temperature throughout the simulation, either 60 or 80 °C for heating or 25 °C for cooling. It is important to note that, at this initial state, all fluid domains, including both the juice and the bath, are stationary, meaning that there is no fluid motion influencing heat transfer.

Boundary Conditions

All surfaces of the can, including the top, bottom, and sides, are subject to a no-slip wall condition. This means that there is no relative motion between the fluid and wall at these boundaries. This assumption is commonly used in computational heat transfer simulations to accurately model the interaction between solid and fluid interfaces.

Solution Methodology

Computational simulations were run on a high-performance CPU with an Intel Xeon Processor E5–2670 v3 at 2.30 GHz and 64 GB of RAM. A high-resolution numerical scheme with a nearly unity blend factor was used to ensure robustness by preventing nonphysical numerical oscillations such as overshoots and undershoots. Variable time-step control was employed to balance accuracy and efficiency.

During heating phases, the time-step was set as 0.05 × exp (0.00085 × ta), while during cooling phases, it was 0.1 × exp (0.0007 × ta), with ta representing cumulative simulation time. These adaptive time step formulas were chosen to keep the Courant number below 0.5 consistently. The RMS convergence criterion for all simulation domains was set at 10–5, ensuring convergence and reliable solutions.

Results and Discussion

Validation

The properties of grape juice (18°Brix) were incorporated into the Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulation. The predicted temperature–time profile at the container’s center was validated with the experimental measurements, as illustrated in Figure . The maximum observed discrepancy between simulation and experimental results was approximately ±2%. This deviation lies within the expected experimental error range as the uncertainty of the K-type thermocouple and Arduino Uno used is ±2 °C, which is ∼3% at 60 °C. Initially, heat transfer predominantly occurred through conduction, which is inherently stable and predictable, resulting in minimal error margins. As the heating continued and convective heat transfer intensified, minor variability increased, reflecting the dynamic nature of the convective heat transfer process.

Estimation of Heat Transfer Rates from Surfaces (Wall Heat Flux)

The wall heat flux (q w) represents the rate of heat transfer per unit surface area across the bottom, side, and top surfaces of a sealed container. This parameter was extracted as a time-dependent variable using the postprocessing capabilities of CFX. 5 qw=k∂T∂r|r=R

The average wall heat fluxes were calculated on each surface ⟨q w⟩, as illustrated in Figure . During the heating and cooling of canned fluids in a pasteurization bath, heat transfer occurs through three primary modes: conduction, convection, and then conduction again, as the system approaches equilibrium. The dominance of each mode shifts over time, whether at the can’s outer or inner surfaces (Figure ).

For the outer surfaces of the can, the process begins with conduction, which is the dominant heat transfer mechanism. When a can is first immersed in a hot bath, heat flows from the bath to the can’s outer surface due to the temperature difference, while the surrounding fluid is mostly stationary (Figures and ).

Temperature contours during heating of grape juice from 27 to 60 °C in cans of different sizes: (a) 330 mL can, (b) 500 mL can, and (c) 1000 mL can.

Temperature contours during cooling of grape juice from 60 to 25 °C in cans of different sizes: (a) 330 mL can, (b) 500 mL can, and (c) 1000 mL can.

As the can’s surface temperature rises, density gradients develop in the bath fluid near the can, particularly at the sides and bottom. These gradients induce natural convection currents, which enhance heat transfer rates, especially at the bottom surface where buoyancy-driven flow is strongest (Figure ). The heat flux at the outer surface initially decreases as the temperature difference narrows but increases locally due to the onset of convection, particularly at the bottom, followed by the side. At the top surface of the can, however, a stagnant layer of less dense, hot fluid often forms, limiting convection and making conduction the primary mode of heat transfer throughout the process (Figure ). As the temperature difference between the bath and the can’s contents diminishes, both natural convection and overall heat flux decrease. Eventually, with minimal temperature gradients, conduction again becomes the dominant mode until thermal equilibrium is achieved, as shown in Figure a–d.

On the inner surfaces of the can, the initial temperature difference between the can wall and the juice is negligible, leading to minimal initial heat fluxes (Figure b,d). As the can wall warms up, the temperature difference with the juice increases, causing the heat flux to rise to a maximum and then decrease. Then, conversion of heavier and lighter fluids occurs inside the can, further boosting heat transfer, especially at the bottom and sides (Figure ). The increase in heat flux at the bottom surface first appears in the 1000 mL volume, followed by the 500 mL volume and then the 350 mL volume (Figure b). This sequence illustrates how increasing volume affects the timing of thermal responses, with larger volumes reacting earlier due to their greater thermal mass. The extent of this enhancement is closely related to the formation of cold spots, which is controlled by the sample’s thermal mass (Figure ). Natural convection significantly influences this process by affecting how cold regions develop and persist within the fluid. Larger sample volumes, possessing greater thermal mass, tend to show more pronounced cold spot effects (Figure ), resulting in a stronger increase in heat flux compared to smaller volumes (Figure b,d). This volume dependence is also seen at the side surface, where heat flux enhancement is delayed in the 1000 mL volume and occurs later in the 500 and 350 mL volumes (Figure ). This pattern is similar to what is observed at the bottom surface, highlighting the important roles of both the volume and thermal mass (Figure ). This absence of a cold spot at the top confirms that buoyancy effects dominate the bottom and side heat transfer pathways. Conversely, no heat flux increase is observed at the top surface, because cold spot buildup does not occur there, removing the driving force for such enhancement (Figures and ). The upper inner surface, where convection is minimal, shows a consistent decrease in heat flux throughout the process. As the process progresses and the temperature difference between the can wall and the juice diminishes, heat flux declines on all of the inner surfaces (Figure ). The trends in heat flux for different volumes of the can and across various surfaces remain consistent for both temperatures of the hot fluid, 60 and 80 °C. However, the heat flux increases with increasing temperature of the hot water bath, as illustrated in Figure d.

Observations regarding the size of the cans revealed that the initial wall heat flux was highest for the 500 mL can compared to the 300 and 1000 mL cans. Notably, the side surface heat flux for the 1000 mL can was significantly lower, as illustrated in Figure . The larger thermal mass and higher volume of the contents in 1000 mL can slow down the internal heating rate. This results in a prolonged temperature gradient between the inner juice and the surrounding hot water, leading to persistent thermal stratification and delayed buoyancy-driven motion (Figure ). Additionally, the size of the can contributes to the formation of thicker boundary layers on both the internal and external surfaces. In contrast, the outer and inner surfaces of smaller cans experience more vigorous external convection and thinner boundary layers (Figure ), resulting in higher heat flux values (Figure ).

Transient Area-Averaged Temperature Profile at the Outer Surface during Heating and Cooling

The temperature profiles of cans with volumes of 1000 mL, 500 mL, and 330 mL were assessed by monitoring the outer surfacesspecifically the top, side, and bottomduring both heating and cooling phases, as shown in Figure . During the heating phase, the rate of temperature increase on the side surfaces was slowest for the 1000 mL can, intermediate for the 500 mL can, and fastest for the 330 mL can. This trend mainly results from the formation of thicker thermal boundary layers on larger cans, which inhibit heat transfer. Conversely, smaller cans experience an earlier onset of natural convection, leading to thinner boundary layers and a quicker temperature increase (Figure ).

Area-average temperatures over time for heating in grape juice containers subjected to water baths at (a) 60 °C and (b) 80 °C and cooling at (c) 60 °C and (d) 80 °C, showing variations by spatial region (bottom, side, top) and juice volumes (1000 mL, 500 mL, and 330 mL).

For the bottom and top surfaces, the temperature initially increases quickly until the cold spot effect becomes apparent. As the cold spot moves downward, a brief decrease in temperature is observed on the bottom and side surfaces, followed by a sharp increase as lighter, warmer fluid rises along the side (Figures and ). Meanwhile, the temperature at the top surface increases more gradually, resulting in a smaller temperature difference (ΔT) overall, mainly governed by heat conduction rather than convection (Figures and ). Notably, in Figure , when the bath temperature is elevated to 80 °C, the rate of temperature increase increases significantly. Under these conditions, the 500 and 330 mL cans reach thermal equilibrium faster than the 1000 mL can, largely due to their lower thermal mass and reduced resistance to heat transfer.

During the cooling phase, the thermal behavior essentially mirrors that observed during heating but in reverse (Figure ). Cooling proceeds more rapidly when the initial juice temperature is higher (80 °C compared to 60 °C), as the greater temperature difference creates a stronger cooling gradient and accelerates heat loss (Figure ). Throughout both heating and cooling, the temperature dynamics are primarily influenced by buoyancy-driven natural convection and the geometry of the containers (Figure ). These factors govern the development of thermal boundary layers, which, in turn, control the rates of heat transfer. Overall, the observed temperature patterns underscore the critical roles of container size, boundary layer formation, and natural convection in determining the thermal response of canned liquids during processing (Figures and ).

The average heat transfer coefficient ⟨h⟩ is determined by relating the area-averaged wall heat flux to the difference between the volume-averaged temperature (⟨T v ⟩) and wall temperature. T w is the wall’s surface temperature (K), and ⟨T v ⟩ is the volume-averaged temperature of the fluid inside the can (K).

The transient average heat transfer coefficient (HTC) profiles observed during grape juice pasteurization demonstrate thermal responses throughout both the heating and cooling processes. Observations were recorded at water bath temperatures of 60 and 80 °C during heating and at 25 °C during cooling. This applies to both the outer and inner surfaces of the can (see Figures and ). As shown in the referenced figures, the HTC increases rapidly during the initial phase and then stabilizes as the system approaches thermal equilibrium. The overall trends in HTC during heating and cooling are remarkably similar to only minor quantitative differences between the two processes (Figures and ).

Container volume greatly affects the HTC distribution. In the water bath simulation, the temperature profile within the can was determined by using a conjugate heat transfer model. This method takes into account time-dependent changes in both the inner and outer heat transfer coefficients. Although this approach offers detailed insights, it is computationally intensive due to the need to model three interacting domains.

To simplify this, a boundary condition approach was developed. In this method, the time-averaged heat transfer coefficient from the water bath simulation is used as a fixed boundary condition, removing the need to explicitly model the water bath.

Comparing the two approaches shows significant differences in the volume-averaged temperature profiles during both the heating and cooling phases, as shown in Figure . The boundary condition method, with its fixed heat transfer coefficient, results in shorter heating and cooling times compared to the water bath simulation, emphasizing how dynamic heat transfer conditions affect process accuracy. Come-up time was calculated to estimate the exact difference between these approaches.

The results demonstrate that both higher bath temperatures and smaller container sizes improve lethality outcomes, significantly reducing pasteurization times.

On average, employing a hot water bath extended the come-up time by about 8–10 min compared to the case without water immersion. This illustrates how dynamic heat transfer influences process accuracy. The 1000 mL setup consistently required the longest come-up time across all scenarios because of its slower thermal response and persistent cooler zones in the core, even at later time points (1000 s), highlighting the impact of higher thermal mass (Figure ). Conversely, the 330 mL container heated up more quickly, showing shorter come-up times. These findings highlight that both container sizes significantly affect the overall heating performance during pasteurization.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the container size, bath temperature, and heating/cooling methods (with or without a water bath) significantly affect the thermal pasteurization of canned grape juice. The computational fluid dynamics (CFD) approach developed in this study allows for precise measurement of how convective heat transfer coefficients change over time on both the inner and outer surfaces of the container.

The 500 mL can achieve the highest convective heat transfer coefficient, effectively balancing quick heating with moderate thermal mass. There were no significant differences in the time-averaged heat transfer coefficients on both the inner and outer surfaces during the heating and cooling phases, nor at water bath temperatures of 60 and 80 °C.

Using a hot water bath added approximately 8 to 10 min to the come-up time compared to scenarios without a water bath or a boundary value approach (which uses a fixed value of the time-averaged heat transfer coefficient as a boundary condition). This highlights the importance of dynamic heat transfer for process accuracy.
