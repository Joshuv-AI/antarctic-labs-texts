Recurrence of allergic fungal rhinosinusitis after endoscopic sinus surgery: A retrospective comparative study between nasal and oral steroids
Authors: Majid Bani-Ata, Mahammed Al-Ameri, Ahmad Bani-Ata, Tareq Bashaireh, Amjad Alnusair, Abdelwahab Aleshawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS) is a highly recurrent form of chronic allergic rhinosinusitis in immunocompetent patients. This study aims to compare the recurrence rates of AFRS between patients who received nasal steroid spray alone and patients who received a combination of nasal and oral steroids after endoscopic sinus surgery. This was a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent endoscopic sinus surgery for AFRS. The selection of the 39 participants has followed the 5 aspects of Bent and Kuhn criteria. Patients were divided into 2 groups: those discharged on both nasal and oral steroids (combination group) and those discharged on nasal steroids alone (local therapy group). All patients were followed for 3 years post operatively, and multiple assessments done for disease recurrence. One patient from 13 patients in the combination therapy group (7.7 %) and 9 patients from the 26 patients in the local therapy group (34.6 %) had recurrence of the condition and underwent functional endoscopic sinus surgery revision surgery (Fisher Exact Test = 3.30, P = .069). AFRS has recurred more frequently among patients who had bilateral disease (33.3%) compared to those who had unilateral disease (0%) (Fisher Exact Test = 4.03, P value = .045). In the postoperative medical management in ARFS, the combination of a short course of oral prednisolone and nasal steroid spray maybe effective to lower the recurrence rate of the disease when compared to nasal steroid spray alone.

1. Introduction

Allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS) is a noninvasive form of highly recurrent chronic allergic hypertrophic rhinosinusitis that is seen in immunocompetent patients. It has clinical, histopathological, and prognostic features that can be readily distinguished from the other forms of chronic fungal rhinosinusitis. It is caused by a Gell and Coombs hypersensitivity reaction, likely a type I IgE-mediated reaction, to an extra-mucosal fungal antigen.[1–3]

The treatment of AFRS entails endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) followed by medical management. The latter includes different regimens that have been explored including allergen immunotherapy, topical and systemic corticosteroids, antifungal therapy, antihistamines and antileukotrienes.[3,4]

The aim of our study was to compare the recurrence rates of AFRS after endoscopic sinus surgery for 2 medical regimens: nasal-spray steroids alone and a combination of nasal-spray and oral steroids. We also compare the recurrence rate among our patients with different demographic and clinical characteristics.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Design

This study has followed a retrospective design to review medical records of patients who underwent ESS for AFRS at our university-based tertiary center between January 2013 and December 2016.

2.2. Sample and setting

This study has reviewed charts of patients who underwent ESS for AFRS at a university-based tertiary center. Using Chi-square test to calculate to calculate sample size based on alpha level = 0.05, power = 0.80, and effect size = 0.50, the minimum needed sample size was 32 cases. The sample size in this study was 39 cases.

2.3. Ethical issues

We were granted permission by the institutional review board of Jordan University of Science and Technology (number: 18/115/2018). All data retrieved from the records kept confidential and none of the patients’ identifiers kept in our record. The consent form was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

2.4. Eligibility criteria

All patients selected for the study were diagnosed to have AFRS and met Bent and Kuhn criteria. Any participants who did not meet all criteria were excluded.

2.5. Procedure

All patients included in this study underwent functional ESS with effective removal of all allergic fungal mucin. The initial diagnosis of AFRS or its recurrence after surgery in all included patients was established by a specialist otolaryngologist and rhinologist based on Bent and Kuhn criteria.[5,6]

The selection of the participants has followed Bent and Kuhn criteria. The well-known criteria include 5 aspects: type 1 hypersensitivity to fungi, nasal polyposis, characteristic radiographic findings of serpiginous areas of high attenuation, eosinophilic mucin and positive fungal stain.[5] All patients treated in the same way by one doctor. Furthermore, all patients did not take any medicine that affects the results of study. All patients were followed for 3 years postoperatively and multiple assessments done for disease recurrence by nasal flexible fiber optic examination. Collected data included patients’ demographics, clinical notes and surgical findings.

Patients in whom remnants of fungal mucin may have remained during surgery due to bleeding, uncontrolled blood pressure or technical issues were excluded from the study. After surgery, all patients were followed up regularly at the clinic and underwent multiple assessments for recurrence of polyps by anterior rhinoscopy and fiberoptic nasolaryngoscopy. Patients with postoperative follow up of <1 year or incomplete or missing medical records were excluded.

Two postoperative regimens were included in our cohort: a combination therapy group and a local therapy group. Both groups received budesonide nasal-spray (64 micrograms twice daily) for 1 year, but the combination group additionally received 16 days course of oral prednisolone. Our oral prednisolone regimen total for 16 days as follow: 40 mg for 4 days, 30 mg for 4 days 20 mg for 4 days and 10 mg for 4 days. None of our patients had any significant side effect of prednisolone.

3. Results

A total of 39 patients who were diagnosed with AFRS and underwent ESS were included in this study. Of them, 21 (53.8%) were females. Mean age of the patients was 36 years, with standard deviation of 13 (range between 18 and 50) years. Mean follow up time was 23 months, with standard deviation of 10 (range between 12 and 40). A total of 13 patients were discharged on both nasal and oral steroids (combination group) and 26 were discharged on nasal steroids alone (local group). 4. Discussion

Patients were divided into comparison groups based on age, gender and smoking status to explore the potential effect of these factors on the recurrence rate. Rhinosinusitis affects about 20% of the population at least once in a lifetime.[7] Currently, AFRS is responsible for 7% to 12% of chronic rhinosinusitis cases undergoing sinus surgery.[8] AFRS is almost exclusively a disease found in areas of high humidity, where mold counts are higher than other regions.[9] In these territories, AFRS can account for up to 32% of chronic rhinosinusitis cases undergoing endoscopic sinus surgery. AFRS is more common and presents at a younger age and in the African American population.[10–12]

The high recurrence rates of AFRS have prompted many physicians to study different postoperative medical regimens used for this condition including immunotherapy, antifungal treatment, and steroids.[13,14]

In the past, allergen immunotherapy was less popular because it was thought that the antigens administered to the patient could provoke a Gel and Coomb type III reaction which may worsen the condition. It was later shown, however, that surgery should be able to remove most of the inciting fungal load from the paranasal sinuses allowing immunotherapy to achieve sufficient immunomodulation to benefit the patient. Therefore, immunotherapy has recently gained more momentum in the postoperative management of ARFS.[10,14] It was also demonstrated that immunotherapy can reduce the reliance on systemic and topical steroids and prevent recurrences up to 17 months after surgery.[15,16] It is clear that a role may exist for immunotherapy in the overall treatment strategy for ARFS although additional supporting studies are warranted.

The role of antifungal therapy is still controversial. Studies have shown that it decreased the invasiveness of the disease and that it can decrease steroid dependence and recurrence rates. Itraconazole is an antifungal drug that is commonly used in a dosage of 200 mg twice daily for 6 weeks. Voriconazole is a newer medication in which cost and drug related morbidity may limit its use. Fluconazole nasal spray has also been used with encouraging results.[17,18]

A recent report from India compared postoperative steroids and itraconazole in the prevention of AFRS.[19] According to the study, patients treated with itraconazole showed a better symptomatic relief and endoscopic clearance of disease. It was concluded that itraconazole is an effective treatment alternative to steroids in the postoperative management of the condition.

The benefits of oral corticosteroids therapy include increased cure rates, increased time to functional endoscopic sinus surgery revision, reduction in the mucosal stage of the disease, and reduced systemic IgE levels. In addition, among patients in whom the condition recurs, a clinically milder form may be encountered.[13] Kuhn and Javer recommended oral prednisolone after ARFS surgery starting with 0.4 mg/kg and slowly tapering to 0.2 mg/kg. After maintaining normal mucosa for 4 months, the dose is reduced to 0.1 mg/kg body weight for another 2 months and then stopped.[18] In our study, we compared combining oral and nasal steroids with nasal steroids alone. Unlike the long tapering course method of oral steroid recommended by Kuhn and Javer, we used a short tapering method over 16 days course of oral prednisolone. We have found that the recurrence rate of AFRS in patients using only nasal steroids was around 35% compared to about 8% in patients using a combination of oral and nasal steroids. Although the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to our small sample size, it is felt clinically to be of consequence. Further limitations to our study include the retrospective nature with its inherent risks and the variable follow up time after surgery. Prospective comparative studies with larger sample size are warranted to further explore the medical regimens used after AFRS surgery in order to fine-tune our postoperative care and lower the recurrence rate in these patients.

Regarding the role of biologics in the treatment of AFRS, dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab shown a promise effect as adjunct treatments for recalcitrant AFRS.[15,20] These biological agents target type 2 inflammatory mediators such as IgE, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 which may prove efficacy in those patients.[21] Furthermore, biologic agents could also help reduce the necessary dose of oral corticosteroids and thus reduce the risk for systemic side effects of corticosteroids. Moreover, biologic agents may slow and possibly even reverse the inflammatory process, reducing the dependency on corticosteroids and antifungal therapies in the treatment of AFRS.[21]

The strength of the study was that all patients were selected based on the diagnosis of AFRS according to Bent and Kuhn criteria. Any participants who did not meet all criteria were excluded. Although this study included participants over 4 years, but the final sample is considered a limitation in its size. However, this could be a strength in the homogeneity of the sample because it followed strictly the 5 aspects of Bent and Kuhn criteria in the inclusion and exclusion procedure.

In conclusion, the combination of a short course of oral prednisolone and nasal steroid spray can be considered as an effective postoperative management strategy to lower recurrence rates in AFRS when compared to nasal steroid spray alone.
