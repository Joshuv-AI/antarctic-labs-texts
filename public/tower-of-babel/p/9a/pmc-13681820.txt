Adherence of healthcare personnel to infection prevention and control program in maternal and child hospitals in Sana’a City, Yemen, cross-sectional study
Authors: Jamal Abdu Sofan, Yahia Ahmed Raja’a, Mohammed Abdullah Al Amad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfection prevention and control (IPC) is essential in maternal and child healthcare settings to reduce healthcare-associated infections. Evidence on IPC program implementation, staff knowledge, and adherence in Yemeni hospitals remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate IPC program implementation, assess healthcare personnel knowledge and adherence, and explore factors associated with IPC adherence in public and private maternal and child hospitals in Sana’a City.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2024 to May 2025 in one public and one private maternal and child hospital in Sana’a. IPC program implementation was assessed using the WHO Assessment Tool on IPC Minimum Requirements for Tertiary Health Care Facilities. Performance was categorized as advanced (≥ 76%), intermediate (51–75%), basic (26–50%), and inadequate (≤ 25%). Healthcare personnel knowledge (37 items) and adherence (15 items) were assessed using structured questionnaires, with scores expressed as percentages of the maximum possible and classified as poor (< 60%), moderate (60–79%), or good (≥ 80%). Differences between hospitals were assessed using t-tests and chi-square tests. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with IPC adherence. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsSeventy-one healthcare personnel participated (66% public, 34% private; median age 30 years; 59% female). Overall IPC adherence was higher in the private hospital than in the public hospital (75.4% vs. 70.6%; p = 0.003). The public hospital had an advanced IPC program (80.0%), while the private hospital was intermediate (68.9%). Knowledge levels were moderate in both hospitals (public: 75.5%, private: 77.9%). In multivariable analysis, healthcare personnel with higher PPE knowledge, medical specialists, and those working in private hospitals had higher adherence scores.ConclusionIPC adherence in maternal and child tertiary hospitals in Sana’a is influenced by individual knowledge and institutional and professional factors. Strengthening PPE-related knowledge and addressing institutional differences between public and private hospitals may improve IPC adherence and reduce infection risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15198-3.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) remain a major global challenge, contributing to substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden [1]. They also accelerate antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which is projected to cause up to 10 million deaths annually by 2050 [2, 3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 1.4 million patients are affected by HAIs at any given time, with risks in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) being 2–20 times higher than in high-income settings [4]. Infection prevention and control (IPC) programs are fundamental to reducing healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), with structured IPC interventions shown to decrease HAI rates by up to 70% [5]. Effective IPC practices are also critical for ensuring patient safety, strengthening outbreak preparedness and response, and improving the overall quality of healthcare services [6].

The challenge is particularly critical in maternal and child hospitals, which represent an important IPC setting because they provide care for immunologically vulnerable populations including pregnant women, newborns, infants, and children, who are at increased risk of healthcare-associated infections, severe complications and death [7, 8].

These facilities routinely perform high-risk procedures, including childbirth, caesarean sections, neonatal intensive care, and other invasive interventions, where failures in IPC can result in severe maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [9]. Ensuring effective IPC in these settings is therefore essential for protecting vulnerable patients and improving the quality and safety of maternal and child healthcare [10, 11].

In Yemen, prolonged conflict and health system disruption have challenged the implementation of infection prevention and control measures and antimicrobial resistance containment programs, potentially increasing the risk of HAIs and antimicrobial resistance [12]. Reported HAI rates reach 65.4 per 100 patients, increasing to 68.2 per 100 patients in intensive care units, with a high level of antimicrobial resistance [13]. Although IPC standards exist in Yemen, their implementation remains suboptimal, with implementation reported in roughly 65% of general hospitals and 40% of maternal and child hospitals [14]. Weak infection control practices have also contributed to the spread of HBV, HCV, and HIV among healthcare workers and medical waste handlers [15–17]. Despite the high vulnerability of maternal and child healthcare settings in Sana’a, evidence on IPC program implementation, healthcare personnel knowledge, and adherence remains limited. Assessing these hospitals is therefore essential to identify implementation gaps and inform targeted interventions to strengthen patient safety and the quality of maternal and child healthcare services.

This study aimed to evaluate IPC program implementation, assess healthcare personnel knowledge and adherence, and explore factors associated with IPC adherence in public and private maternal and child hospitals in Sana’a City.

Materials and methods

Study design setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted between October 1, 2024, and May 31, 2025, in Sana’a City, which hosts the largest network of public and private hospitals in Yemen and serves as a major referral hub. The study focused on specialized tertiary maternal and child health (MCH) hospitals. At the time of the study, maternal and child health services were provided in eight public hospitals and several private hospitals in Sana’a City. However, only two public hospitals and three private hospitals were dedicated specialized MCH hospitals. Two hospitals were purposively selected to represent public and private healthcare models and to ensure comparability between specialized MCH facilities. Selection was based on predefined criteria, including provision of comprehensive obstetric and neonatal emergency care (CEMONC), referral capacity for maternal and paediatric emergencies, availability of specialized healthcare staff, and adequate infrastructure. Palestine Hospital for Maternity and Childhood was selected from the public sector because, among the two specialized governmental MCH hospitals in Sana’a City, it was considered more comparable to private MCH facilities. The other governmental MCH hospital serves as a major national referral center, receiving cases from all Yemeni governorates, and differs substantially in scale and service scope. C-Plas Hospital was selected from the private sector because it met all predefined criteria and functions as an established referral center. The selected hospitals had capacities of 300 and 35 beds, respectively, and together employed 852 staff, including 468 technical staff. Although the selected hospitals may not fully represent all MCH hospitals in Yemen, they reflect important characteristics of specialized tertiary referral MCH facilities and provide a meaningful comparison between public and private healthcare settings.

Study population, eligibility criteria, and sample size

The study population comprised all healthcare personnel working or responsible for quality control and infection prevention and control (IPC) within hospital departments, regardless of academic or professional level.

Staff with designated IPC responsibilities who worked during the study period were included. Those not involved in IPC, with less than six months in their role, or absent during data collection were excluded. Eligibility was confirmed using official IPC and quality assurance records.

From a total workforce of 852 staff across both hospitals, 90 individuals were identified as eligible based on formal assignment to IPC-related roles. Of these, 76 completed the questionnaire (84.4% response rate), and 71 fully completed questionnaires were included in the final analysis; five were excluded due to incomplete key variables. As all eligible IPC-related staff within the selected hospitals were targeted, no formal sample size calculation was performed prior to data collection.

Data collection tools

IPC Program Assessment: The WHO assessment tool on Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) Minimum Requirements for Tertiary Health Care Facilities was used to evaluate hospital IPC programs across eight core components. A structured interview instrument, developed specifically for this study and adapted from the WHO assessment tool, was used to collect data through interviews and observation with five hospital officials and IPC supervisors. The tool comprises 45 indicators covering eight IPC core components. Each indicator was scored as “Yes” (1) or “No” (0), with maximum scores as follows: IPC program (5), IPC guidelines (4), IPC education and training (3), HAI surveillance (7), multimodal strategies (1), monitoring/audit of IPC practices and feedback (4), workload/staffing and bed occupancy (7), and built environment, materials and equipment (14). The total scores for each core as well as the overall scores of the eight cores were summed, converted to percentages, and classified into four implementation levels according to WHO recommendations: Inadequate (0–25%), Basic (26–50%), Intermediate (51–75%), and Advanced (76–100%) [18].

Structured self-administered questionnaires were used to assess knowledge and adherence at the personal level. The tool was originally developed as a validated instrument with a 3-point response format, accredited by the University of Putra Malaysia, translated into Arabic, and previously used in Yemen [19]. In the present study, the response format was expanded to a 5-point Likert scale to improve response discrimination while retaining the original content domains. Knowledge with 5-point Likert responses (37 items, max 185 points) covered six domains: Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) (15 items, max 75 points, aseptic technique (5 items, max 25 points), reprocessing of medical devices (5 items, max 25 points), environmental cleaning (4 items, max 20 points), textiles/laundry processing (4 items, max 20 points), and medical waste management (4 items, max 20 points). Practice adherence (15 items, max 75 points) covered PPE (5 items, max 25 points), aseptic technique (7 items, max 35 points), and isolation precautions (3 items, max 15 points). Reverse scoring was applied to negatively phrased items to ensure that higher scores consistently reflected better knowledge and adherence. Domain and overall scores were calculated for each participant as percentages of the maximum possible scores, and item- and domain-level mean scores were also computed. The modified Bloom’s cut-off criteria were used to classify scores as poor (< 60%), moderate (60–79%), and good (≥ 80%) [20]. Both the interview instrument and self-administered questionnaire were pilot-tested with five experienced healthcare workers involved in IPC activities to assess clarity, relevance, and feasibility in the local context. Minor wording modifications were made based on participant feedback before the main data collection.

The English versions of the IPC program assessment tool and self-administered questionnaire, including details of reverse-scored items, are provided in the Supplementary Data Collection Tool.

Potential source of bias

Several measures were taken to minimize potential sources of bias, including the use of standardized and previously validated instruments and pilot testing to ensure clarity and feasibility. Institutional data were collected through structured interviews along with observation, while knowledge and adherence practices were assessed using anonymous self-administered questionnaires to reduce interviewer bias. Reverse scoring was applied to reduce response pattern bias. However, adherence was self-reported and may be subject to social desirability bias, potentially leading to overestimation of compliance.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was overall IPC adherence, measured using the total adherence score derived from the sum of the 15 practice items and treated as a continuous variable in linear regression analyses. For descriptive purposes, adherence scores were also converted into percentages of the maximum possible score and classified using modified Bloom’s criteria. Potential explanatory variables included continuous variables (age and knowledge domain scores) and categorical variables (hospital type and socio-demographic and professional characteristics), which were evaluated for their associations with IPC adherence.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 22, while linear regression analyses were performed using Stata version 14. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using both the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Differences between public and private hospitals were evaluated using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with overall IPC adherence scores. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Independent variables included socio-demographic and professional characteristics, hospital type, and IPC knowledge domains. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). The WHO IPC core components were excluded from the multivariable model due to collinearity with hospital type, as they reflect institutional IPC system implementation. Model fitness was evaluated using R², adjusted R², and F-statistics. Supporting statistical outputs, including results of normality testing, regression diagnostics, and multicollinearity assessments, are presented in the Statistics Supplementary File (Supplementary Figs. 1–4).

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Seventy-one healthcare personnel participated in the study, with 66% from the public hospital and 34% from the private hospital. The overall median age was 30 years (IQR: 26–35). More than half (59%) were female. Almost two thirds (69%) were married, and 59% held a diploma qualification. Nursing and midwifery were the predominant specialization (72%). Nearly three-quarters (73%) were service Personnel, while 73% had received infection prevention and control (IPC) training, and 72% reported prior IPC experience (Table 1).

Infection prevention and control program

At the institutional level, the public hospital demonstrated a more advanced IPC program than the private hospital (80.0% vs. 68.9%). In the public hospital, most components were rated as advanced, except IPC education and training (33.3%, basic) and IPC guidelines and monitoring/audits (75%, intermediate). In the private hospital, advanced IPC education and training (100.0%), lower scores were mainly observed in IPC guidelines (50%) and healthcare-associated infection surveillance (28.6%), while other components showed relatively comparable performance across both hospitals (Table 2).

Knowledge of infection prevention and control among healthcare personnel

Regarding IPC knowledge, healthcare personnel in the private hospital showed slightly higher overall IPC knowledge compared with those in the public hospital [(144.3 ± 10.6; 77.9%) vs. (139.7 ± 10.7; 75.5%), p = 0.086], although both groups demonstrated moderate knowledge levels. A significant difference was observed only in personal protective equipment (PPE) knowledge, where the private hospital scored higher [(60.6 ± 4.5; 80.8%, good) vs. (56.8 ± 4.9; 75.7%, moderate), p = 0.002]. Other IPC domains showed comparable scores between the two hospitals, with no statistically significant differences (Table 3).

Healthcare personnel in the private hospital had higher overall IPC adherence scores compared with those in the public hospital [(56.6 ± 4.6; 75.4%) vs. (53.0 ± 4.7; 70.6%), p = 0.003]. The largest difference was observed in aseptic technique [29.9 ± 3.0 (85.5%) versus 27.5 ± 2.7 (78.5%), p = 0.001], followed by isolation precautions [ (12.8 ± 1.7 (85.6%) vs. 11.9 ± 2.0 (79.0%), p = 0.047)]. In contrast, personal protective equipment adherence showed no significant difference between the two hospitals (Table 4).

Factors associate with IPC adherence

IPC adherence was influenced by both individual and institutional factors. Eight variables were identified in the univariable analysis as significantly associated with IPC practice scores. No evidence of multicollinearity was detected among knowledge domains and socio-demographic variables (Statistics Supplementary File Supplementary Fig. 2). The WHO IPC core components were excluded from the multivariable model due to collinearity with hospital type (Statistics Supplementary File Supplementary Fig. 3).

The final multivariable model was statistically significant (F (8,62) = 7.82, p < 0.001) and explained 50.2% of the variance in IPC adherence scores (R² = 0.502; adjusted R² = 0.438). Multicollinearity was not detected (VIF range: 1.15–1.76; mean VIF = 1.39) (Statistics Supplementary File Supplementary Fig. 4).

In the multivariable analysis, higher PPE knowledge scores were associated with higher IPC adherence (β = 0.29, p = 0.007). Medical specialists had significantly higher adherence scores compared with other healthcare personnel (β = 4.93, p = 0.013), while staff working in the private hospital had higher adherence scores than those in the public hospital (β = 2.27, p = 0.037) (Table 5).

Overall, higher PPE knowledge, being a medical specialist, and working in the private hospital were independently associated with higher IPC adherence.

Discussion

This study demonstrated differences in institutional IPC implementation, knowledge, and adherence between public and private maternal and child tertiary hospitals in Sana’a City.

The public hospital showed stronger performance in overall WHO IPC core components, whereas healthcare personnel in private hospitals demonstrated better adherence to IPC practices. Although overall knowledge levels were comparable between facilities, PPE-related knowledge, medical specialty, and hospital type were independently associated with IPC adherence. These findings suggest that both institutional context and individual competencies contribute to IPC performance.

At the institutional level, the public hospital achieved an advanced IPC level according to the WHO IPC core component framework. In contrast, the private hospital demonstrated an intermediate level, indicating variability in institutional IPC capacity. The stronger performance of the public hospital in surveillance and staffing may reflect better alignment with national policies and external support mechanisms. However, IPC education and training remained at a basic level, highlighting a gap between structural IPC capacity and workforce development. In contrast, the intermediate performance of the private hospital was largely driven by weaknesses in IPC guidelines and healthcare-associated infection surveillance systems, consistent with evidence suggesting weaker governance and oversight in private healthcare facilities [2]. Yemen’s prolonged health system challenges, including resource shortages, workforce constraints, and limited institutional support, may further affect the consistency and sustainability of IPC implementation across hospitals [21]. Although training was advanced in the private hospital, it was only basic in the public hospital, reflecting differing institutional priorities. However, training alone, without systematic monitoring and feedback, may have a limited long-term impact [22]. Strengthening structured IPC training, supportive supervisory feedback mechanisms, and collaboration with WHO-supported IPC initiatives may help improve adherence and sustain IPC standards. Both hospitals performed well in multimodal strategies and infrastructure, whereas monitoring and feedback remained only at an intermediate level, indicating an incomplete quality improvement cycle [23].

The variability observed in WHO IPC core component implementation in this study is consistent with findings from other low- and middle-income countries, where surveillance systems, monitoring mechanisms, and IPC training are frequently reported as weaker components of IPC programs [24, 25].

The knowledge profile observed in this study is consistent with findings from Yemen and other LMIC settings. Previous studies assessing nurses’ knowledge of nosocomial infection control in Sana’a reported similar patterns, although those studies focused on nurses rather than dedicated IPC personnel [26]. Knowledge levels in the current study were higher than earlier surveys in Yemen, where only a small proportion of healthcare workers demonstrated good IPC knowledge [27], and where intensive care staff showed low awareness of ventilator-associated pneumonia prevention guidelines [28]. Globally, these findings align with evidence from South Asia and the Middle East, where IPC knowledge levels are generally moderate and only a minority of healthcare workers achieve high proficiency [29].

Regarding moderate IPC adherence, findings are consistent with studies from other low- and middle-income countries where adherence often ranges between 60 and 75% [30–32].

Previous studies from Yemen and similar settings reported inconsistent IPC adherence despite adequate knowledge, reinforcing the persistent knowledge–practice gap; an intervention study in Aza’al Region showed poor pre-training IPC knowledge and practices among nurses [33]. In King Salman bin Abdul-Aziz Medical City, nurses had good knowledge but variable practices [34]; and a systematic review reported suboptimal compliance in developing countries, even when knowledge is adequate [23]. This discrepancy likely reflects contextual and system-level barriers, including limited resources, high workload, and inconsistent supervision [28, 29]. Comparable gaps have also been reported in other LMIC settings, including Juba Teaching Hospital and Addis Ababa hospitals, where adherence to PPE and isolation practices remained suboptimal [30, 35].

The regression findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that IPC-related knowledge, professional role, and institutional factors are important determinants of adherence to standard precautions [32, 36, 37].

The findings suggest that strengthening IPC implementation requires both institutional and individual-level interventions. Healthcare facilities should prioritize targeted PPE training alongside routine supervision, monitoring, and feedback mechanisms. Policymakers should strengthen surveillance systems, guideline implementation, and workforce support to sustain IPC performance [30, 38]. From an ethical perspective, strengthening IPC adherence is essential to protect healthcare workers and patients from preventable healthcare-associated infections and ensure safe healthcare delivery.

A key strength of this study was the integration of institutional IPC assessment using the WHO IPC framework with evaluation of healthcare personnel knowledge, adherence, and associated factors. Future research should consider longitudinal or interventional designs to assess the effect of targeted IPC training and system-strengthening interventions on sustained adherence.

This study has several limitations. As all eligible IPC-related healthcare personnel within the selected hospitals were included, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Consequently, the relatively small sample size and inclusion of only two tertiary hospitals may have limited statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the result should be interpreted cautiously and may be most applicable to similar care settings.

Although the knowledge and adherence questionnaire was adapted from a previously validated instrument and pilot-tested for clarity and feasibility, its internal consistency was not formally reassessed after modifying the response format from a 3-point to a 5-point Likert scale. In addition, IPC adherence data were self-reported, there is potential for reporting and social desirability bias, which may have led to overestimation of adherence practices.

Finally, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and unmeasured factors such as workload, resource availability, and organizational culture may also have influenced the findings.

Conclusion

Maternal and child tertiary hospitals in Sana’a demonstrated intermediate to advanced levels of IPC program implementation, with stronger overall performance in the public hospital. Healthcare personnel showed moderate IPC knowledge and adherence overall, while staff in the private hospital demonstrated significantly higher adherence, particularly in aseptic techniques and isolation precautions. In multivariable analysis, higher PPE knowledge, being a medical specialist, and working in a private hospital were independently associated with higher IPC adherence. These findings suggest that IPC performance is influenced by both individual knowledge and institutional factors. Strengthening PPE-related knowledge, supporting continuous professional development, and addressing institutional differences between public and private hospitals may improve adherence to IPC standards and enhance patient safety in Yemen’s maternal and child healthcare hospitals.
