Large language models and global health equity: a roadmap for equitable adoption in LMICs
Authors: Haichao Chen, Dian Zeng, Yiming Qin, Zeyue Fan, Faye Ng Yu Ci, David C. Klonoff, John S. Ji, Shuyang Zhang, Kwesi Nyan Amissah-Arthur, Michelle María Jiménez de Tavárez, Saleha Masood, Phuoc Van Le, Pearse A. Keane, Bin Sheng, Tien Yin Wong, Yih Chung Tham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLarge language models (LLMs) have been proposed to address global health inequity by providing accessible and high-quality health care, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). However, despite the early enthusiasm following the release of GPT, development and deployment of LLMs have remained heavily concentrated in high-income countries (HIC), raising concerns that such technology may worsen existing health disparities instead of alleviating them. The most recent LLMs, which include features such as lower cost, and open-source framework, show promise in rebalancing LLMs' benefits worldwide. In this viewpoint, we examine the current challenges and imbalance in LLM deployment across global regions, identify the key barriers to adoption in LMICs, assess current LLMs' advances and the new opportunities they bring to global health equity. We also propose a five-dimensional roadmap—focusing on people, products, platforms, processes, and policies—to advance LLMs' equitable adoption in LMIC and improve inclusive progress in global health.Funding10.13039/501100012166National Key R&D Program (Grant No: 2022YFC2502800); National Natural Science Fund of China (Grant No: 82388101); 10.13039/501100004826Beijing Natural Science Foundation (Grant No: IS23096).

Introduction

Global health inequity represents one of the most fundamental challenges in healthcare, characterized by systematic disparities in health outcomes, resource allocation, and access to quality medical care between high-income countries (HICs) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1, 2, 3 In Western Pacific region, such disparity is also severe. For instance, Lao PDR, Cambodia, and Vanuatu have ratios of 0.2–0.5 doctors per 1000 people, compared to 4.0 in Australia. Such gaps in healthcare workforce, compounded by inequitable access to essential medical devices, critical medications, and basic healthcare infrastructure, contribute significantly to an observed life expectancy difference exceeding ten years between these groups of countries.1, 2, 3

Traditional interventions to address global health inequity have operated through established paradigms: enhancing healthcare workforce education in LMICs, increasing LMICs' financial support and technical collaboration with HICs, and systematically developing basic healthcare infrastructure in LMICs.4, 5, 6 For example, WHO supported Ministry of Health in Cambodia to step a Pre-Service Education to improve the quality of local health professionals, thus reducing the regional health disparity.7

Though these approaches have made meaningful contributions, significant inequity remains. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has created a potentially paradigm-shifting opportunity for global health equity, offering sophisticated medical knowledge and reasoning capabilities through accessible digital platforms.8, 9, 10 Current evidence demonstrates that advanced LLMs can achieve expert-level performance on standardized medical examinations, generate clinically accurate diagnostic hypotheses, and streamline administrative tasks that disproportionately burden resource-constrained healthcare systems.11, 12, 13, 14 For LMICs specifically, these capabilities address multiple critical healthcare gaps simultaneously: augmenting the diagnostic capabilities of healthcare workers with limited specialized training, providing continuous medical education in contexts lacking adequate educational infrastructure, and improving clinical operational efficiency amid workforce shortages. In addition, LLMs can offer targeted solution for health challenges specific to LMICs, such as HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and maternal mortality. Early implementations have demonstrated promising applications, with customized-LLM chatbot facilitating maternal care in rural India15 and proprietary LLMs, such as GPT-4o and Gemini, providing tuberculosis-related health consultations in China.16

Current landscape of LLMs in global health is uneven

Despite LLMs' theoretical potential in improving LMICs' health care, their current adoption in LMICs has been markedly limited and raised a concern that LLMs may paradoxically exacerbate existing global health disparities.17, 18, 19 Rather than serving as a “rising tide that lifts all boats” by reducing global health inequities, the current trajectory of LLM development may primarily benefit already resource-rich healthcare systems while remaining inaccessible to populations with the greatest healthcare needs. This disparity is reflected in the uneven global distribution of LLM healthcare research: over 87% of published research in this field is led by institutions in high-income countries.20 In comparison, LMICs only contribute to around 13%, with Africa accounting for a mere 0.31% of global healthcare LLM research, despite Africa's population making up nearly 20% of the world's people. Besides the missing role of LMICs in LLM-related healthcare research, LMICs are also underrepresented within LLMs. While tracing the training data of LLMs and quantifying the share of LMIC content is difficult, this underrepresentation is suggested by the poor performance of LLMs on LMIC-related questions.21,22 Furthermore, most publicly available medical examination benchmarks originate from a limited number of HICs, such as the United States and the United Kingdom, and some upper middle-income countries, including China and India.23 Without benchmarking data from LMICs, adapting and developing LLMs for local use remains a significant challenge. In addition, the cutting-edge well-known medical LLMs are primarily developed by a few leading countries, including the United States (AIME,24 Medpalm25), and China (Huatuo,26 MedFound,12 Baichuan-M127).

The application of LLMs in global health also demonstrated an uneven pattern. The United States alone accounts for 20.6% of global ChatGPT traffic, whereas low-income countries collectively contribute less than 1%. This pattern of adoption is closely correlated with national gross domestic product (GDP).16 One possible explanation is that most advanced LLMs are developed and owned by commercial companies, which prioritize markets with higher economic returns. As a result, LMICs with small market sizes, have received proportionately reduced attention. Additionally, clinical trials, a pre-deployment step for LLMs to be integrated into healthcare, are also overwhelmingly concentrated in a few countries. The majority of these trials have been conducted in China and the United States, followed by Canada and Germany,28 while most LMICs are evidently absent from these early implementation efforts.

Obstacles to LLMs' deployment in LMICs' healthcare system

The low adoption of LLMs in LMICs reflects growing concerns that their original promise to improve global equity is falling short. Three major key factors may contribute to such limited adoption. The first, often overlooked amid the grand visions of AI's potential, is the lack of foundational infrastructure in many LMICs, especially in low-income countries. For instance, access to reliable electricity remains unmet in some regions29; in Sub-Saharan Africa, only about 50% of the population has consistent access to electricity. This alone hinders clinicians to adopt computers, let alone advanced tools like LLMs. Even in areas where electricity is available, healthcare facilities frequently suffer from severe shortages in IT infrastructure, including basic hardware, stable servers, and dependable Internet connectivity.30,31

The second factor is the high cost associated with LLMs, which presents a unique barrier to adoption in LMICs. Different from traditional AI, which typically address a single task with million-scale parameters, as seen in tuberculosis screening in Bangladesh,32 HIV management in South Africa,33, 34, 35 and maternal health monitoring in Nigeria,36 LLMs are capable to handle a wide range of tasks with billion-scale model size. This generalizability comes at the expense of significant computational and financial demands. For instance, training a medical model like Meditron required 128 GPUs, each costing around $10,000.37 Operating an LLM with 70 billion tunable parameters, an 8K token context and 10–100 concurrent inference runs requires approximately 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, resulting in an annual cloud service cost of around $300,000. In some low-income countries, deploying such a model to serve every 100,000 inhabitants could consume up to 15% of the national healthcare expenditure (Fig. 1). Even individual access is costly. The third factor is that LLMs often underperform in LMIC settings. Studies have shown that LLMs make up to three times more errors when retrieving information related to low-income countries—such as Kiribati and Nauru—compared to high-income nations.21 LLMs also struggle with health issues specific to low-income regions.38 Beyond limitations in knowledge coverage, linguistic bias pose a significant challenge. Most cutting-edge LLMs are predominantly trained on widely spoken, data-rich languages, which are typically the mainstream languages of HICs. This training data bias have resulted in a significantly reduced performance in LMIC linguistic contexts, which further limits the practical utility of LLMs in LMICs. As an illustration, GPT-4's accuracy drops from around 80% in English to just 50% in Thai.39

New developments in LLMs supporting equitable adoption in LMICs

Although the current role of LLMs in advancing health equity remains limited, their recent developments indicate promising potential. There are three major developments. The first key advance is the shift toward open-source LLMs. Since the first appearance of OpenAI GPT3.5 in 2022, proprietary models have largely dominated the field. However, beginning in 2024, a notable shift towards open-source LLMs have emerged, with models such as LLaMA, DeepSeek, and Qwen leading the trend (Fig. 2A). These open-source tools create new opportunities for health equity. First, open-source LLMs may be favored over proprietary ones because they enable local deployment. Unlike proprietary models that require sending patient data to external or overseas cloud services, open-source models can be deployed locally with data retained exclusively within the hospital. This local deployment also allows for continuous monitoring to ensure compliance with safety regulation. Second, open-source LLMs are more easily adapted to local healthcare contexts through post-training. This is particularly important for LMICs, where healthcare environments often differ significantly from mainstream data used to train LLMs. Open-source LLMs also offer greater flexibility in adapting to cultural contexts, ranging from languages to values, ensuring better alignment with the expectations and communication styles of target communities. The second advance is the rapidly decreased deployment cost. While training LLMs remains expensive, the cost of running these models has decreased tenfold each year while maintaining comparable performance (Fig. 2B). The AI hardware is also getting 30% cheaper each year.40 The AI hardware is also getting 30% cheaper each year and is likely to remain so in the next few years. Against this backdrop, it is likely that the cost of adopting LLMs at a similar capability level could proportionately decrease, making them significantly more affordable to LMICs. In addition, some post-training technologies, such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and knowledge distillation, have enabled small LLMs to perform nearly as well as large LLMs, while using less than 1% of their compute cost.41, 42, 43, 44 Though the current cost of local LLM deployment still exceeds the threshold for most LMICs, the emergence of cheaper LLMs with satisfactory performance is foreseeable in the near future, lowering the barrier for LLMs' deployment in LMICs.

The third advance is the increased adaptability of LLMs. Recent innovations have made LLMs more flexible and capable of being tailored to diverse languages, cultures, and clinical contexts. Techniques such as prompt tuning, which adjusts the input instructions to influence LLMs' responses, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which improves output by extracting and incorporating relevant external information – would help to enable LLMs' customization even with relatively small datasets in resource-constrained settings.45,46 Furthermore, LLMs' development has focused more on multilingual capacity, reducing the language bias that historically limited the effectiveness of LLMs in LMICs.39

The road ahead for LLMs' equitable adoption in LMICs

Despite the recent advances of LLMs, their potential in global health equity, or more specifically in LMICs, remains unrealized. Realizing this requires two major areas of effort. The first is to strengthen the AI readiness of LMICs and lay the foundation for LLM adoption.47 The second is to strategically prioritize directions for LLM adoption. Different from HICs that can afford substantial investment in the competitive development of LLMs, LMICs should focus on the local adaptation and application of these models. Their limited resources necessitate careful selection of use cases that maximize impact while minimizing unnecessary expenditure. The first ‘p’ stands for process, emphasizing that LLMs' adoption in LMICs should follow a standard evaluation process. The initial step is to identify priority use cases, which should be informed by local healthcare needs, the technical capacity of LLMs, and their comparative advantage over traditional AI algorithms. Local needs can be determined through a thorough investigation of the local context, while the latter two require a comprehensive evaluation approach. The FDA has proposed a seven-step lifecycle for AI healthcare products, including data collection, model development, validation, deployment, monitoring, and performance evaluation.48 This process has been successfully adopted by non-LLM algorithms deployed in LMICs.36,49,50 For example, Mayo developed an AI algorithm to detect low ejection fraction, first validating it on retrospective datasets and later conducting a local clinical trial in Nigeria to detect peripartum cardiomyopathy, a condition with the world's highest incidence there.36 Drawing inspiration from such examples, LLM evaluations can similarly begin with retrospective benchmarking studies and proceed to pilot clinical trials.28,51 The next step is a risk assessment. Specific attention must be given to LLMs' hallucinations and inherent biases,52,53 which should be systematically evaluated and addressed prior to broader implementation. Finally, a continuous monitoring system is demanded to track performance and drive iterative improvements. Whenever adverse effects occur, they should be addressed through a well-designed error compensation mechanism as recommended by the WHO in Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health.54

The second ‘p’ is people. In the AI roadmaps of many LMICs such as the Philippines, Vietnam, and Thailand, a skilled workforce is recognized as a critical element of development. Equitable adoption of LLMs in LMIC healthcare demands expertise across multiple domains, including artificial intelligence, data science, medicine. Most LMICs face a shortage of professionals trained in these interdisciplinary areas.55 Bridging this gap calls for sustained investment in education across these areas. Collaboration is another effective approach to address this shortage. By fostering partnerships, LMICs can not only import knowledge and technology but also strengthen the skills and expertise of local researchers. As these researchers build their capacity, they are better equipped to train and mentor others, creating a multiplier effect that yields long-term benefits for the healthcare workforce in LMICs.

The third ‘p’ stands for products. The health products powered by LLMs must not only be affordable but, more importantly, practically useful in LMICs. One of the most critical early steps in LLM adoption will be local adaptation, which includes tailoring models to regional languages, specific application contexts, and cultural norms. A foundational requirement for this process is the establishment of comprehensive digital training datasets. Establishing electronic medical record systems should be prioritized, as they directly reflect local healthcare contexts and will serve as the primary training and validation corpora for LLMs. The next step is customization. This process involves three key stages: continuous pretraining to incorporate domain-specific medical knowledge; supervised fine-tuning for task-specific optimization; and reinforcement learning from human feedback to align the model with local cultural values and clinical practice standards.56 Ultimately, the development of a user-friendly, contextually relevant LLM-based product will be essential to ensure effective and sustainable implementation within LMIC health systems.

The fourth ‘p’ refers to platform. First, it is essential to establish core digital capabilities, harmonized database and data security platform before deployment of any specific LLM. Moreover, rather than relying on a government-led platform, a collaborative ecosystem that actively engages industry, government, and academia should be fostered. According to World Economic Forum, the healthcare generative AI market was valued at $2.7 billion in 2025 and is estimated to reach over $17 billion by 2034, reflecting its substantial economic potential. An industry-driven strategy for healthcare LLMs can stimulate economic growth, attract investment, and ease pressure of LLM resource demand. It also enables faster and more continuous product improvement through real-time user feedback. A similar approach can be seen in India's Odisha AI Policy launched this year, which emphasizes building an industry-government-research AI ecosystem by funding startups and promoting collaborative research.57 Similar strategies can be adopted by other LMICs to enable sustainable LLM adoption in healthcare.

The fifth ‘p’ refers to policy. Developing a national policy to guide the adoption of LLMs is a critical first step. Many pacific island nations currently lack an AI policy, and fewer LMICs have policies tailored specifically to the healthcare sector.58 It is also essential to create policies that are grounded in local realities. A policy that misplaces its focus risks slowing the AI development with resources wasted. For instance, the supercomputer in Lengau, South Africa, has struggled to operate due to an unreliable electricity supply.59 Context-specific and clearly focused policies are crucial for LMICs to embrace the transformative benefits of LLMs effectively.

Conclusion

LLMs hold immense potential to improve global health equity, addressing one of the major challenges in human civilization. However, the current adoption and deployment of LLMs in LMICs remains limited, mainly due to resource constraints, high costs and suboptimal performance in local contexts. While notable progress has been made to improve technical aspects of LLMs (e.g., cost-efficiency, openness, and adaptivity), further efforts across the domains of people, products, platforms, process and policy are needed to enable practical and equitable adoption in resource-constrained settings.
