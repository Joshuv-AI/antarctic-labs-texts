Evaluating the effect of cigarettes smoking on oesophageal cancer risk: a systematic review and meta-analysis with dose–response relationships
Authors: Ayaka Teshima, Irene Possenti, Daniele Bertacchi, Vincenzo Bagnardi, Claudia Specchia, Cristina Martinez, Esteve Fernández, Silvano Gallus, Alessandra Lugo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOesophageal cancer (OC) affects over 600 000 people annually, with tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption as primary risk factors. We aimed to synthesize existing evidence to quantify the dose–response relationship between smoking and OC risk. A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted, examining case-control and cohort studies published up to April 2026. Pooled relative risks (RR) were estimated using random-effects models. Dose-response relationships by intensity, duration, and time since quitting of smoking were modelled using log-linear models, with linear or spline terms based on model fit. Of 221 eligible articles, 178 original studies were included. Compared with never smokers, the pooled RR of OC was 2.68 (95% confidence interval; CI: 2.37–3.03; n = 66) for current smokers, 1.71 (95% CI: 1.54–1.90; n = 61) for former smokers, and 2.15 (95% CI: 1.98–2.34; n = 144) for ever smokers. For current smokers compared with never smokers, the risk was 3.03 (95% CI: 2.42–3.79; n = 35) for squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and 2.25 (95% CI: 1.80–2.82; n = 14) for adenocarcinoma (OAC). OC risk increased non-linearly with smoking intensity, linearly with smoking duration, and decreased linearly with time since quitting. This meta-analysis reinforces the strong association between smoking and OC, with higher risk for OSCC than OAC. The observed non-linear dose–response relationship indicates that the risk of OC increases sharply even at low levels of smoking intensity, doubling below nine cigarettes and tripling below 17 cigarettes per day. These findings support complete smoking avoidance as the most effective prevention strategy of OC.

Introduction

Oesophageal cancer (OC) is the eighth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, responsible for approximately 600 000 new cases and 550 000 deaths each year [1]. The prognosis for OC is poor, with a 5-year survival rate generally near 20% [2], reflecting late-stage diagnosis and limited treatment options. Geographic variations in the incidence of OC are notable, with high incidence rates observed in East Asia, Southern and Eastern Africa, and Northern Europe [3]. Squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and adenocarcinoma (OAC) represent the two primary histological subtypes of OC, with OSCC being the predominant subtype globally (84% OSCC vs. 15% OAC) [4].

Several distinct risk factors have been identified for these subtypes [5–9]. OAC is mainly associated with Barrett’s oesophagus, history of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), obesity, and tobacco smoking, whereas OSCC is mostly linked with tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption [6, 8, 9]. Particularly, the risk of OAC is approximately two to three-fold higher among current smokers compared to never smokers [6], while the association between tobacco smoking and OSCC has been estimated to be even stronger [9].

Recent epidemiological studies have indicated significant shifts in the incidence patterns of these subtypes. Notably, North America and European regions have experienced a decline in OSCC incidence but an increasing trend in OAC, which is likely attributable to evolving lifestyle factors, such as the rising prevalence of obesity and persistent GERD [10]. Conversely, high-incidence regions in Asia, Africa, and South America continue to report elevated rates of OSCC, largely driven by persistently high levels of smoking and alcohol consumption [8, 10].

Although tobacco smoking is a well-established risk factor for OC, recent reviews and meta-analyses specifically providing an updated global dose–response assessment of cigarette smoking and OC risk remain limited. Several recent reviews have confirmed the importance of tobacco use in relation to oesophageal cancer, particularly OSCC, but their scope has often been restricted to specific geographic settings such as Africa or broader categories of modifiable risk factors. For example, recent African-focused systematic reviews have emphasized the importance of tobacco use, alcohol consumption, dietary factors, environmental exposures, and socioeconomic conditions in explaining the high burden of ESCC in this region [11–13]. However, comprehensive global evidence on cigarette smoking intensity, duration, and smoking cessation in relation to OC risk remains limited [14]. Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies [15] have highlighted the importance of incorporating precise dose–response data, rather than relying solely on smoking status, to accurately estimate population smoking-related cancer burden [11, 13, 14]. In addition, given the particularly poor prognosis of OC, a deeper understanding of its risk factors is essential to refine risk estimates and develop effective prevention strategies.

Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to precisely quantify and update the relationship between cigarette smoking and the risk of OC, both overall and by histological subtype. Through systematic examination of epidemiological data, this study will also explore the impacts of smoking intensity, duration, and time since quitting on the development of this disease. Furthermore, potential subgroup differences according to sex, geographic region, and other key stratifications are explored.

Methods

This meta-analysis is part of a series of systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining the association between cigarette smoking and secondhand smoke (SHS) exposure and cancer risk [16–24]. An original search methodology, including an umbrella review (Supplementary Table S1) and a traditional review [18], was applied to ensure the identification of all the relevant original publications published up to 28 April 2026 [18]. Our study protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42017063991).

Our search string for the traditional review included combinations of MeSH terms and text words related to OC and tobacco or smoking (Supplementary Box 1).

Details of the search strategy, eligibility criteria, and data extraction are reported in Supplementary Annex 1.

By merging the original articles identified in the umbrella review and the traditional review, a total of 218 non-duplicate publications were considered eligible (Supplementary Fig. S1).

We calculated pooled RRs for current, former, and ever smokers compared to never smokers, both overall and stratified by study design (i.e. cohort and case-control studies). We explored both linear and non-linear associations between smoking intensity (for current vs. never smokers), smoking duration (for current vs. never smokers), and time since smoking cessation (for former vs. current smokers) and the log RR of OC. Further methodological details are reported in Annex 1.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.2 (R Development Core Team, 2017), utilizing the ‘meta’ and ‘dosresmeta’ packages [25].

Results

Study selection and description

Of 221 eligible original articles, 43 were excluded because their subjects had already been included in other publications (Supplementary Table S2). Thus, a total of 178 original articles (121 case-control and 57 cohort studies) were included in the present meta-analysis (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). The included studies were published between 1969 and 2025 and described more than 125 000 cases of OC. Of these, 29 studies provided data specifically for OAC and 82 for OSCC.

Sixty-six studies on OC provided a measure of the association (or information to derive it) for current smokers, 61 for former smokers, and 144 forever smokers, as compared with never smokers. Moreover, 69 studies reported RR estimates for smoking intensity (22 among current smokers), 43 for smoking duration (7 among current smokers), and 31 for time since quitting. Publications containing data that were partially excluded from the present meta-analysis, with the corresponding reasons for exclusion, are described in Supplementary Table S5. Study-specific quality scores for case-control and cohort studies are shown in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7, respectively, with 12 high-quality case-control studies and 29 high-quality cohort studies.

Quantitative data synthesis

The pooled RR of OC for current smokers compared with never smokers was 2.68 (95% CI: 2.37–3.03) overall, 2.80 (95% CI: 2.29–3.42) in case-control studies, and 2.56 (95% CI: 2.22–2.95) in cohort studies (Fig. 1). The corresponding RR estimates for former smokers compared with never smokers were 1.71 (95% CI: 1.54–1.90), 1.86 (95% CI: 1.59–2.19) in case-control studies, and 1.53 (95% CI: 1.36–1.72) in cohort studies (Fig. 2). Forever smokers, the pooled RR was 2.15 (95% CI: 1.98–2.34) overall, 2.25 in case-control studies (95% CI: 2.02–2.50), and 1.90 in cohort studies (95% CI: 1.67–2.16; Supplementary Fig. S2). All pooled estimates showed statistically significant between-study heterogeneity, with analyses on current, former, and ever smokers displaying I2 of 88.4%, 85.9%, and 90.2%, respectively.

Regarding histological subtype, the RR of OSCC was 3.03 (95% CI: 2.42–3.79; n = 35; Supplementary Fig. S3) for current smokers, 1.70 (95% CI: 1.42–2.05; n = 33; Supplementary Fig. S4) for former smokers, and 2.14 (95% CI: 1.86–2.45; n = 71; Supplementary Fig. S5) forever smokers. The RR of OAC was 2.25 (95% CI: 1.80–2.82; n = 14; Supplementary Fig. S6) for current smokers, 1.71 (95% CI: 1.44–2.02; n = 13; Supplementary Fig. S7) for former smokers, and 2.05 (95% CI: 1.77–2.39; n = 20; Supplementary Fig. S8) forever smokers.

Other possible sources of heterogeneity were examined through stratified analyses (Table 1). In the analysis of current vs. never smokers, significant differences were observed according to endpoint among cohort studies (RRs were 3.15 for mortality and 2.43 for incidence; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), geographic area (RRs were 4.36 in studies conducted in North America; 3.10 in those conducted in Europe, 2.12 in those conducted in Asia, 2.74 in those conducted in South America, 2.71 in those conducted in Oceania, and 2.19 in those conducted in Africa; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), income (RRs were 3.28 in studies conducted in high-income countries and 1.63 in studies conducted in middle- or low-income countries; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), and study quality (RRs were 3.02 in low-quality studies and 2.23 in high-quality studies; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01). In the analysis of former vs. never smokers, significantly different RR estimates were observed according to endpoint in cohort studies (RRs were 1.91 for mortality and 1.44 for incidence; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), income (RRs were 1.78 in studies conducted in high-income countries and 1.56 in studies conducted in middle- or low-income countries; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), and quality (RRs were 1.87 in low-quality studies and 1.50 in high-quality studies; P value for test for subgroup differences = .04). In the analysis of ever vs. never smokers, statistically significant differences were observed according to study design (RRs were 2.26 in case-control studies and 1.87 in cohort studies; P value for test for subgroup differences = .03), income (RRs were 2.45 in studies conducted in high-income countries and 1.91 in studies conducted in middle- or low-income countries; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01), and quality (RRs were 2.24 in low-quality studies and 1.78 in high-quality studies; P value for test for subgroup differences < .01). No significant differences were observed according to sex, number of cases, adequate adjustments, and year of publication.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plots (Supplementary Fig. S9) and Egger’s test indicated evidence of publication bias for current (P < .01), former (P = .02), and ever smokers (P < .01). Adjusted pooled estimates obtained using the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method are reported in Supplementary Table S8.

Dose–response analysis

Figure 3 shows the dose–response relationships between smoking intensity (for current vs. never smokers; panel a), duration (for current vs. never smokers; panel b), and time since quitting (for former vs. current smokers; panel c) and the risk of OC. We observed a non-linear increase in OC risk with smoking intensity among current smokers. RRs of OC sharply increased with a low number of cigarettes up to 20 cigarettes/day, the relation then levelling off for a higher amount of cigarettes/day. The RRs were 2.00 (95% CI: 1.76–2.29) for 8.2 cigarettes/day, and 3.00 (95% CI: 2.54–3.56) for 16.9 cigarettes/day (estimated using the curve functions reported in Supplementary Box 2). The RR increased linearly with increasing duration of smoking: RRs were 2.00 (95% CI: 1.64–2.45) for 27.5, and 3.00 (95% CI: 2.19–4.13) for 43.6 years of smoking. The risk of OC significantly decreased linearly with increasing time since quitting, the RR for former compared with current smokers being 0.50 (95% CI: 0.42–0.60) after 17.8 years since smoking cessation and reaching the level of never smokers after 26 years since smoking cessation (RR: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.28–0.47).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide the most comprehensive and up-to-date evidence on the association between cigarette smoking and the risk of oesophageal cancer. Based on 221 eligible studies and using data from 178 original articles, it includes over 125 000 OC cases. Our findings show that the risk of OC for current smokers is between two and three times that of never smokers, while former smokers have a 70% increased risk of OC, compared to never smokers. Dose–response analyses revealed that the risk of OC more than doubled at a smoking intensity of just nine cigarettes per day or after 28 years of smoking. Notably, the risk among former smokers was reduced by half after 18 years of cessation.

Compared with previous meta-analyses, our findings are quite consistent with those from the meta-analysis by Ansary-Moghaddam et al. [26], which, to our knowledge, is the most recent comprehensive meta-analysis to provide a RR of OC for current vs. never smokers without geographical or histological subtype restrictions. Their study reported a RR of 3.07 for current vs. never smokers, a slightly higher estimate than ours. However, their analysis was based on only 37 studies, whereas our pooled estimate is derived from almost twice as many studies (66), providing a more robust and comprehensive assessment of the association. For former smokers, the most recent meta-analysis by Miyazaki et al. [27] reported a RR of 0.74 for former vs. current smokers. While our study did not directly compare former to current smokers, this estimate can be derived from the ratio between the RRs for former vs. never smokers and current vs. never smokers, resulting in a value of 0.65. This estimate is based on 61 studies, almost twice as many as those included by Miyazaki et al. [27], thus confirming previous results and providing a more robust estimate.

In our meta-analysis, we found higher RRs for current smokers in OSCC compared to OAC. The biological explanation of this difference lies in the fact that cigarette smoke directly affects the squamous epithelium, potentially leading to genetic and epigenetic mutations and, therefore, to increased carcinogenic susceptibility in squamous cells [28, 29]. Additionally, OAC is established to be strongly related to other risk factors, such as gastroesophageal reflux [7], Barrett’s oesophagus [30], and obesity [31], which may dilute the relative impact of smoking on its development. This difference in the associations between smoking and the risk of different histological subtypes is in line with findings from the literature, although previous meta-analyses have found even more pronounced RRs for OSCC [5, 14]. Conversely from previous findings from the literature, we did not observe the analogous difference between histological subtypes for former smokers, for whom our RRs of OAC and OSCC were nearly identical. This could be partially attributed to the inclusion of recent studies reporting low RRs of OSCC for former smokers [32–34]. A possible biological explanation is that, after smoking cessation, the risk for OSCC may decline more easily than that for OAC, potentially reflecting differences in carcinogenesis pathways and latency periods between histological subtypes.

Among cohort studies, we found significantly higher RRs for mortality than for incidence, particularly for current and former smokers, compared with never smokers. It has been hypothesized that smoking not only induces malignant transformation of normal oesophageal cells, but may also change tumour cell or related metabolic activity, thus accelerating cancer progression and undermining treatment response [35]. Additionally, smoking-related comorbidities, such as cardiovascular or respiratory diseases, could exacerbate health decline, increasing mortality risk.

In our analysis, we found higher RRs for current smokers in North America and Europe compared with other continents. Similarly, the RRs for all smoking statuses were higher in high-income countries compared with middle- and low-income countries. This finding, which is consistent with previous literature [36], is challenging to interpret, particularly given that OSCC, which is more strongly associated with smoking, is more common in Asia and less developed countries [1, 4]. Possible explanations include a time lag between the onset of smoking and the onset of disease. For example, in countries such as China, where smoking only peaked in popularity in the mid-1990s, the full impact of tobacco use may not be fully reflected in published studies [37]. Genetic differences may also play a role, with some populations potentially being more susceptible to the carcinogenic effects of smoking in the oesophagus. In addition, in populations with lower RRs, other factors may influence the incidence of OC, diluting the association with smoking. For example, in less developed countries, poor oral hygiene and viral infections may account for an important proportion of cases [11, 13]. Finally, it is possible that in more developed countries, smokers tend to smoke more intensively or for longer periods compared to those living in less developed countries [36]. Future research should also explore potential interactions between smoking and other risk factors to better understand the multifaceted aetiology of OC and its histological subtypes in different geographic areas. Of note, our estimates for Africa were generally lower than those reported in recent meta-analyses [11–13]. This difference may reflect our decision to include only studies reporting risk estimates, whereas other meta-analyses derived RRs from raw data when not available, thereby incorporating a larger number of unadjusted estimates potentially affected by confounding.

The dose–response analysis carried out with flexible modelling techniques showed a non-linear association for smoking intensity, with a sharp risk increase up to 20 cigarettes per day, and a levelling off thereafter. This highlights the fact that even smoking a small number of cigarettes per day, e.g. nine, is sufficient to double the risk of OC. The risk linearly increased with smoking duration. An inverse linear dose–response association between time since quitting smoking and OC risk emerged; the risk of a former smoker halving after 18 years and reaching that of a never smoker after 26 years since quitting smoking. This result has great relevance from a public health perspective and should encourage people to quit smoking to reduce their risk. Our findings were generally consistent with those emerging from the meta-analyses published in the previous decades [14, 27, 36], which were, however based on a smaller number of studies.

The limitations of this work are those typical of meta-analyses of epidemiological studies, such as recall bias, selection bias, and misclassification of exposure in original studies considered in the meta-analysis. We observed slightly higher risk estimates in case-control studies compared to cohort studies, although the difference was borderline significant. This discrepancy could be explained by higher recall and selection biases in case-control designs, which may lead to an overestimation of smoking exposure among cases [38]. Additionally, exposure misclassification in cohort studies, where smoking status is often assessed only at baseline without updates over time, could attenuate risk estimates. Furthermore, competing risks, such as mortality from smoking-related diseases before OC develops, could contribute to lower observed RRs in cohort studies [39]. Interestingly, we found higher RRs for current smoking in case-control studies using hospital-based controls compared to those with population-based controls. While hospital controls may sometimes have a higher smoking prevalence [40], the observed pattern suggests that in our meta-analysis, other factors may be at play. One possible explanation is that hospitalized patients may quit smoking due to health concerns or cessation programs, even for non-smoking-related conditions, leading to fewer current smokers among hospital controls, which could inflate the risk estimates. Our analysis found higher RRs in low-quality studies, likely due to residual confounding, selection bias, or less rigorous exposure assessment. However, even in high-quality studies, the association between smoking and OC remains strong and statistically significant, with RRs well above unity, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. Notably, we found no significant differences in the estimates when considering studies with appropriate adjustments, including for alcohol consumption, or when comparing studies with different numbers of cases. This further supports the reliability and consistency of our results. Regarding other potential adjustments, only one study explicitly adjusted for Barrett’s oesophagus, precluding a formal assessment. Moreover, as Barrett’s oesophagus may lie on the causal pathway between smoking and oesophageal adenocarcinoma, such adjustment could lead to overadjustment bias and attenuated estimates. Heterogeneity between studies was found for each smoking variable, which may be the result of pooling data from studies conducted with different methodologies, considering different definitions of smoking, and including individuals with various characteristics and background risks. To allow for heterogeneity, we used random-effects models, that give more conservative estimates although they do not resolve heterogeneity. We also investigated possible putative sources of heterogeneity in risk estimates through stratified analyses according to study, population, and cancer characteristics. The consistently high heterogeneity observed across almost all stratified analyses (I2 > 60%) likely reflects differences in the underlying populations rather than purely methodological variability. In particular, geographic variation, differences in smoking patterns (e.g. intensity, duration, and type of tobacco), and the distribution of histological subtypes are likely major contributors. Moreover, the varying prevalence of other risk factors, especially alcohol consumption, diet, and environmental exposures, and comorbidities, such as gastrointestinal diseases and metabolic disorders, may further modify the association between smoking and oesophageal cancer, contributing to residual heterogeneity and confounding even within stratified analyses. Potential publication bias remains a concern and can partially distort the true magnitude of the association between smoking and OC. However, estimates obtained using the trim-and-fill method were generally lower but remained statistically significant, supporting the robustness of the observed associations.

Our meta-analysis has several strengths. The methodology used to identify original articles, based on a combination of umbrella and traditional reviews, allowed us to include data from 218 epidemiological studies investigating the association between cigarette smoking and OC risk, making this meta-analysis the most comprehensive on the issue. A careful screening process of the retrieved publications was carried out to avoid data overlapping from original studies included in pooled-analyses and/or individual-patient meta-analyses. In addition, we modelled the risk functions to describe the best dose–response relationships with smoking intensity, duration, and time since quitting smoking.

This meta-analysis reinforces the strong and statistically significant association between smoking and OC, with risk estimates consistently above unity across different study designs and populations. From a public health perspective, our findings underscore the critical need for comprehensive tobacco control measures to reduce the burden of OC worldwide. Strengthening smoking cessation programs and implementing stricter policies could have a significant impact on reducing incidence rates and associated mortality.
