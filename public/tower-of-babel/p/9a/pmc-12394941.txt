Analysis of noise characteristics in intraoral X-ray sensors using the Noise-Power Spectrum and non-parametric metrics from diagnostic imaging
Authors: Philip Roebers, Ralf Schulze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital X-ray sensors have significantly changed dental radiography, enabling faster image acquisition and reducing radiation doses for patients. Despite the advancements in technology, noise in X-ray imaging remains a challenge. In this study, noise was examined using the Noise-Power Spectrum (NPS) and a non-parametric method. Blank images were taken under different exposure times and voltage settings. The analyses show that noise decreases with longer exposure times. Among the examined sensors, 2 showed distinct NPS peaks, and 1 exhibited no relationship between exposure time and noise levels. These results are discussed on terms of specific sensor structures, artefacts and/or unaccessible post-processing algorithms.

Introduction

In past years, dentistry has experienced a large shift from analogue to digital X-ray technology.1–3 This transition offers many advantages, enabling dentists to diagnose more accurately, work more efficiently, and reduce radiation doses.1–4 However, this new technology also has its shortcomings.5 One persistent issue is noise, the random variations in pixel brightness that can interfere with image clarity and make it harder to diagnose accurately.6–8

Although modern X-ray sensors have made great improvements in reducing noise, until today noise has not been completely eliminated.9 The reason for this lies in the nature of noise itself, which is a combination of sources of noise: the electronics inside the sensor, the way images are captured, and even how photons are distributed across the sensor surface.10,11

The advantages of digital sensors are well known and documented, yet, there is much to learn and improve about how to measure and minimize noise effectively.12 This study describes how noise behaves in digital intraoral X-ray sensors, focusing on how it varies depending on the type of sensor and the settings used. By using Noise-Power Spectrum (NPS) analysis7,13 with a non-parametric noise measurement method,14 the study can provide some insights into these characteristics and how it change under different situations. Understanding how noise characteristics change with varying exposure times may help optimize the trade-off between image quality and patient dose.

Methods

A systematic approach was used to analyse noise in intraoral digital X-ray images by utilizing NPS7,13 calculations and a non-parametric noise measurement method based on the computation of the τ-value.14 The first step in the analysis was exporting DICOM-format blanc X-ray images from 4 intraoral sensors under varying exposure parameters, using blank exposures for consistency, out of the sensor specific software. Each sensor has distinct specifications, detailed in Table 1. Parameters such as pixel size play a big role in changing the performance and characteristics of digital intraoral radiographic systems, making it essential to consider when interpreting the results.15,16

Each sensor was used with exposure durations of 20, 40, 63, 80, 100, 125, and 160 ms, under 2 different tube voltage settings of 60 and 70 kV.

Regions of interest (ROIs) measuring 256 × 256 pixels were defined for each image. To ensure consistency and comparability in the NPS results, the same ROI size was used for all images analysed, as NPS is known to be influenced by the dimensions of the selected ROI size.17,18 These ROIs were randomly selected from the central portion of the image, and a minimum distance to the edge of 100px was selected to avoid edge artefacts, maintaining a minimum distance between them to ensure non-overlapping areas.19 Within the central image portion, the mean pixel intensity was calculated to normalize signal variations and establish a baseline mean grey-level image.

Each ROI was subjected to iterative processing (1000 iterations) to minimize variability and ensure robustness. To get a ROI that only contains noise for further analysis, the mean grey-level was subtracted for the ROI. In each iteration, the Fourier transformation of the subtracted ROI was carried out to calculate the NPS. To make the results easier to interpret, the power spectrum was radially averaged by organizing the NPS values into concentric spatial frequency bands. This approach created a smoother and more intuitive representation of the noise distribution.20

All computations and visualizations were implemented using MATLAB. The τ-value is a non-parametric metric, designed to quantify image noise independent of large-scale structures within the image.14 This method was implemented using a MATLAB package publicly provided (Non-Parametric Noise Measure (Tau)).

In essence, the method compares the intensity of each pixel with its 8 neighbouring pixels across a range of threshold levels. For every threshold, a binary map is created to indicate whether the intensity differences exceed the specified threshold. These differences are then counted and aggregated to form a distribution of intensity variations across the thresholds. The τ-value is derived as a weighted average of these thresholds, resulting in a single scalar value that captures the noise characteristics of the image.14 To minimize the influence of the sensors’ chamfered edges on the outcome, a 100-pixel margin around the image outlines was excluded from all calculations. This method is assumed to be particularly robust against large-scale structural variations in the image, as it focuses on local intensity fluctuations rather than overall brightness patterns, since empty X-Ray images were used is not further important.14 By applying this function to the image, the τ-value was derived as a complementary metric to the NPS, enabling a deeper assessment of noise properties in intraoral digital X-ray images and a validation of the NPS-Results.14 All blank images used for noise analysis are available at https://www.zmk.unibe.ch/ql/blankimages_for_noise_calculation_of_digital_intraoral_sensors.

In addition to the descriptive analysis, statistical tests were applied to control whether the differences observed in the NPS values at different exposure times are significant. For the τ-values, no statistical tests could be performed since the value is calculated once for the image and does not change over multiple calculations. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the NPS distributions deviated from normality across all iterations and exposure conditions, as seen in the Appendix A, Table A1. Nevertheless, we conducted independent sample t tests to compare noise levels between successive exposure times (e.g., 20 vs. 40 ms, 40 vs. 60 ms, etc.). Given the large sample size (n = 1000 per group), the t-test still is a robust and appropriate method despite deviations from normality. With such large groups, the t-test still gives reliable results, even if the data are not perfectly normally distributed.22 A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of P<.008. The results revealed statistically significant differences in noise levels between most exposure time steps for all sensors, as seen in the Appendix A, Table A2, confirming the general trend of decreasing noise with increasing exposure time. Notably, no significant differences were observed between 80 and 100 ms for the Dürr Dental VistaRay 7 sensor, and between both 80 vs. 100 ms and 100 vs. 120 ms for the Sirona XIOS XG Supreme.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed analysis of noise characteristics of 4 selected digital X-ray sensors when used with different exposure times. Even though this study provides a detailed analysis of noise characteristics of the 4 intraoral digital X-ray sensors, it does not directly addresses the impact of noise on diagnostic accuracy, as only blank images were used. In many previous studies within the field of radiology and dental radiology it has been shown that an increase in noise substantially decrease diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, noise also compromises the discrimination ability of fine anatomical structures.6,23–26 As we have—for obvious reasons—used blank images, we cannot answer the question as to which amount of noise impacts real-world diagnostic processes and accuracy. Importantly however, we document that longer exposure times reduce noise. Future studies should aim to investigate this relationship using structured radiographs and observer-based evaluations to assess whether the noise identified in this study translate into measurable differences in diagnostic outcomes.

The analyses of NPS characteristics among the tested intraoral X-ray sensors showed differences resulting from their noise characteristics, operational parameters, and different structural designs.27 The variations in NPS curve shapes observed across sensors can correlate with factors, such as scintillator composition, pixel geometry and size, and electronic architecture, as primary contributors to noise patterns.17,28,29

There were distinct peaks in the NPS results of the Sirona XIOS XG Supreme and Dürr Dental VistaRay 7 sensors (Figure 2), suggesting the presence of fixed patterns noise. Through intense manual stretching of one of those Sirona Xios XG images with NPS, a faint underlying structure, formed like a rectangle with chamfered corners, was revealed, as shown in Figure 3.

For the Dürr Dental VistaRay 7 a more complex pattern was found, as shown in Figure 4.

It resembles a grid wire-like structure, potentially resulting from architectural elements of the sensor itself, like the layout of the readout circuitry, electrode arrangement, or internal shielding components of the sensors. However, the intermittent presence of these fixed noise patterns for both Sensors across all exposure times raises questions about their origins. Given their non-random and structured appearance, they are likely formed by a combination of hardware-related fixed-pattern noise and software-level image corrections. Both aspects are developed by the manufactures to ensure an out of the box good looking radiograph for dentist and are not designed for blank images. Future work using raw image data would be valuable for distinguishing true sensor-induced patterns from algorithmic artefacts. However, manufactures do not freely provide access to unprocessed images or volunteer to give insight into the processing of the radiographs.

The consistent reduction in noise, in both the NPS and τ-calculation, with increasing exposure times, except for the Sirona XIOS XG Supreme sensor, corroborates theoretical expectations of the SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) and the photon statistics.10,30–32 The anomalous behaviour of the Sirona sensor, where noise trends deviate from other tested devices, may be linked to its response to blank exposures. Noise characteristics in blank exposures may differ from those in clinical images because of the presence of anatomical structures and the application of additional image processing algorithms. In clinical settings, anatomical content introduces variations in local contrast and texture that can cover up or interact with noise patterns, making structured noise less apparent or reducing its impact. Moreover, manufacturer software often applies adaptive post-processing steps—such as edge enhancement, noise reduction, or contrast normalization—based on the image content. These context-selective algorithms may behave differently on clinical images compared to blank images, potentially changing or amplifying certain types of noise or artefacts. As a result, noise measurements from blank images, while valuable for baseline analysis, may not fully reflect the complexity of noise behaviour under real-world conditions.

Finally, the study’s findings show the utility of employing both NPS and the non-parametric τ-value for noise characterization.14,33 The use of different approaches provided by these 2 metrics contribute to a deeper understanding of the sensors noise character, offering perspectives on differentiating between random and structural noise contributions.13 Future research should aim to expand on the examination of noise with a multifunctional approach, incorporating more images, set a standardization on how to acquire images for noise analysis and exploring real-world clinical imaging scenarios to validate its applicability.

Conclusion

This study shows the importance of noise analytics and characteristics in digital intraoral radiography and their dependence on sensor-specific properties and operational parameters. By using both the NPS and the non-parametric τ-value, a more comprehensive and deeper understanding of noise behaviour was reached. The results demonstrated that while most of the tested sensors generally follow a reduction in noise with increasing exposure times, distinct noise patterns were observed in the NPS results of the Sirona XIOS XG Supreme and in the Dürr Dental VistaRay 7 sensors. These anomalies, including NPS peaks and inconsistent noise trends, suggest the influence and use of post-processing algorithms and sensor-specific characteristics, highlighting the need for further research into acquisition protocols, software calibration and a back-tracing for image prepossessing. Future research should aim to extend the noise analysis by incorporating larger datasets, looking at clinical imaging scenarios, evaluating the role of advanced noise reduction techniques used by the manufacturer software and a standardization for noise assessments in X-ray images.
