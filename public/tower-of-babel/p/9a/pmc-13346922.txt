3D Electrical Impedance Tomography of Regional Ventilation During Jet Ventilation: A Pilot Study
Authors: Daniel S Fink, Nilton Barbosa da Rosa, Sanghee Cho, Michelle Mellenthin, Andre Vieira Pigatto, Tzu‐Jen Kao, Benjamin Cloyd, Ana Fernandez‐Bustamante, Matthew Clary, Drew Gottman, Ryan Lau, Jennifer L Mueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesLow‐frequency jet ventilation (LFJV) is commonly used in laryngotracheal surgery when intubation is not feasible. However, limited understanding of regional ventilation and distal airway pressures raises safety concerns and restricts broader adoption. This study employed electrical impedance tomography (EIT) to quantify tidal volumes and regional ventilation patterns in patients undergoing airway surgery with LFJV.MethodsAdult patients undergoing microlaryngeal surgery for subglottic stenosis were prospectively recruited from the Laryngology Clinic. A standardized anesthesia protocol was followed. EIT was used to measure tidal volumes and assess regional ventilation during bag‐mask ventilation, laryngeal mask airway (LMA) ventilation, LFJV before and after dilation, and during recovery. Linear mixed‐effects models were applied to compare LFJV with LMA ventilation, accounting for repeated measures within subjects.ResultsSix patients underwent surgery with EIT monitoring, of whom four had complete datasets for analysis. LFJV preferentially ventilated apical lung regions, while basal regions were consistently under‐ventilated. Apical‐to‐basal volume ratios were consistently higher during LFJV compared with LMA (> 100% higher). Similar differences were observed in the global inhomogeneity index (24% higher), left‐to‐right ratio (21% higher), and anterior‐to‐posterior ratio (9.5% higher).ConclusionThis is the first study to apply EIT to assess ventilation distribution during LFJV for microlaryngeal surgery. LFJV was associated with relative hypoventilation of basal and dorsal lung regions and increased ventilation inhomogeneity compared with LMA ventilation. Larger studies are warranted to validate these results and inform optimization of LFJV in clinical practice.Level of Evidence4Keywords: airway surgery, electrical impedance tomography, jet ventilation, regional ventilation, subglottic stenosis

Introduction

Low‐frequency jet ventilation (LFJV) is a form of ventilation used in laryngotracheal surgery when intubation is not possible or would obscure the surgical field [1]. In this technique, ventilation is delivered at high pressure into the trachea through a needle or catheter with passive expiration. LFJV has been used for decades, and multiple series support its overall safety and efficacy [2, 3]. However, LFJV is performed in an open system, unlike standard ventilation through a cuffed endotracheal tube. Thus, delivered tidal volumes and distal airway pressures cannot be directly measured, and airway teams rely on chest rise and oxygenation by pulse oximetry to assess adequacy of ventilation. Applying insufficient pressure can result in hypoventilation, whereas applying excessive pressure can result in overdistension and barotrauma [4, 5]. These effects, potentially compounded by heterogeneity in ventilation between different lung segments, have the potential to cause harm. [6] As such, LFJV remains largely limited to specialized centers.

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) is a noninvasive high temporal, low‐spatial resolution imaging technique that can track changes in regional ventilation and perfusion. In thoracic EIT applications, electrodes are placed circumferentially around the chest, and low‐amplitude alternating current (AC) is injected through the electrodes. The measured voltages are used to compute images showing changes in the electrical properties of biological tissues [7, 8]. The impedance of the lungs changes by up to 300% during normal tidal breathing [9]. The ability of EIT to track changes in regional ventilation makes it well suited for imaging patients receiving LFJV during laryngeal surgery. EIT has previously been used to measure tidal volume in high‐frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) and superimposed high‐frequency jet ventilation (SHFJV) [10].

This pilot study evaluated the ability of EIT to measure and compare tidal volumes and regional ventilation during airway surgery with bag‐mask ventilation, laryngeal mask airway (LMA) ventilation, and LFJV. Understanding the regional effects of LFJV is essential for optimizing airway management and minimizing ventilation‐related injury. EIT offers the opportunity to directly visualize these effects during surgery. The hypothesis was that ventilation would be less homogeneous during LFJV than during bag‐mask or LMA ventilation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective observational pilot study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB #17‐1927). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The objective was to compare regional ventilation during tidal breathing, bag‐mask ventilation, LMA ventilation, and LFJV, and to evaluate the effect of changes in jet pressure and frequency on ventilation distribution.

Participants

Adults aged 18 to 89 years undergoing surgery for subglottic or tracheal stenosis were eligible. Exclusion criteria included body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2, significant cardiopulmonary disease contraindicating jet ventilation, tracheomalacia, glottic or multilevel stenosis, pregnancy, and the presence of a pacemaker or chest metal.

Ventilation Protocol

EIT data were collected continuously throughout the procedure and annotated using nomenclature at the following time points: (1) spontaneous tidal breathing before induction, (2) bag‐mask ventilation, (3) LMA ventilation, (4) LFJV before dilation, (5) LFJV with altered frequency and duration, (6) LFJV after dilation, (7) LMA ventilation after dilation, and (8) bag‐mask ventilation after removal of the laryngoscope.

Bag‐mask and LMA ventilation targeted tidal volumes of 7–8 mL/kg predicted body weight with a respiratory rate of 10 breaths per minute and FiO2 of 1.0. LFJV was delivered through a 14 Ga jet ventilation needle attached to the Pilling 230 mm adult male subglottiscope (model 522245) at an initial pressure of 20 psi with 2‐s insufflations per breath. Measurements were then taken at 25 psi at the same rate. Adjustments to jet settings were made as needed at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist for patient safety. After completion of the procedure, the LMA was replaced and an additional set of EIT measurements was obtained, followed by a final measurement during bag‐mask ventilation after removal of the subglottiscope or LMA.

Data Acquisition Protocol

EIT data were collected using the GENESIS prototype EIT system from GE Healthcare, which employs parallel current drive with simultaneous multisource current injection to enable rapid acquisition of three‐dimensional datasets, GE HealthCare [11]. Two rows of 16 electrodes were placed above and below the breasts, at approximately the 3rd and 7th intercostal spaces, using a wearable electrode interface as shown on a healthy adult male volunteer in Figure 1. A low‐amplitude AC (10 kHz, ≤ 0.22 mA RMS) was applied using the trigonometric current patterns [12], and surface voltages were measured at 20 frames/s.

An example of EIT electrode placement shown on a healthy adult male volunteer using the wearable electrode array. The ground electrode is placed on the shoulder and the ground cable encased in a plastic sleeve is seen in the right‐most figure. The center figure is an example of an EIT ventilatory reconstruction at full inspiration displayed in DICOM orientation, with the lung regions annotated in the figure.

Reconstructions of difference images of the conductivity were computed on a 3‐D Joshua tree mesh using a one‐step iterative reconstruction algorithm based on the ToDLeR algorithm [13] and displayed in two 2‐D cross‐sectional slices corresponding to each row of electrodes. Data were collected continuously and then segmented and analyzed at each ventilation mode.

Upper‐to‐lower volume ratios, left‐to‐right volume ratios, anterior‐to‐posterior volume ratios, and the global inhomogeneity index (GI, %) were computed from the reconstructed conductivity values in the lung regions. The GI index provides a normalized comparison of voxel conductivity values in the lung region relative to the median and is a measure of ventilatory inhomogeneity; higher GI values correspond to greater inhomogeneity [14].

Additional Measurements

Clinical variables included oxygen saturation, jet pressure, respiratory rate, and subjective grading of chest rise using a 0–3 scale by blinded observers. Preoperative data such as pulmonary function testing, computed tomography imaging, and laryngoscopy findings were also collected as part of standard care.

Statistical Analysis

The effect of LFJV compared to LMA ventilation was measured as an average increase (in %) on the metrics compared to LMA ventilation as well as the range of the increase across four subjects. Four ventilation metrics were analyzed: GI (%), upper‐to‐lower volume ratio, left‐to‐right volume ratio, and anterior‐to‐posterior volume ratio. Also, the metrics were assessed using a linear fixed‐effects model that accounts for different subjects. Given the small number of patients analyzed (n = 4) and the model chosen, we acknowledge the limitation of generalizing the results to the general population and the results should be considered preliminary and exploratory, intended to highlight consistent physiological patterns. Analyses were performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 [15] using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Results

Sixteen patients were enrolled for this pilot investigation. Ten patients opted for procedures at a secondary site to reduce wait times and could not be included. Six patients underwent the procedure with EIT measurements. Two patients were excluded due to electrode displacement, leaving four patients with analyzable data. All procedures were completed without adverse events.

All four analyzable patients were women with subglottic stenosis. The mean age was 45 years (range, 30–63 years), and the mean BMI was 21.7 kg/m2 (range, 19–23.5 kg/m2). Stenosis length averaged 19.5 mm (range, 10–31 mm), beginning 8–20 mm from the superior surface of the vocal folds. All patients had grade 1 or grade 2 stenosis. No patient had significant pulmonary disease or other comorbidities contraindicating jet ventilation.

Across all subjects, EIT demonstrated that LFJV preferentially ventilated the upper lung regions, whereas dependent regions were consistently under‐ventilated. In contrast, bag‐mask and LMA ventilation produced more uniform distributions, with greater aeration of dependent zones. These patterns were also evident in the time traces of ventilatory volume. In each set of time traces corresponding to LFJV, ventilatory volume increased markedly when the jet was on compared with when it was off. Superimposed oscillations were due to the pulsatile perfusion signal, which was not removed prior to image reconstruction; this was verified by inspection of reconstructions during jet‐off segments and comparison of the reconstructed pulsatile perfusion with the heart rate.

As shown in Figure 8 quantitative analysis supported these observations. The GI was higher during LFJV than during LMA ventilation (9% ~ 50% higher, 24% on average), indicating greater regional variability in ventilation. The upper‐to‐lower volume ratio was significantly increased during LFJV (23% ~ 400% higher, > 100% higher on average), reflecting preferential aeration of nondependent lung regions. Similarly, both left‐to‐right (2% ~ 39% higher, 21% on average) and anterior‐to‐posterior (−5% ~ 18% higher, 9.5% on average) ratios were elevated during LFJV compared with LMA. Although we recognize the limited generalizability of these findings to the wider population and should consider the results preliminary and exploratory intended to highlight consistent physiological patterns, we observe a consistent pattern across the patients with all findings remaining significant (adjusted p‐values were 9.2e‐10, 3.7e‐9, 1.6e‐05, and 0.0414 respectively), despite variations in jet setting.

Discussion

Despite its relative safety, LFJV is reserved for patients without significant pulmonary disease or elevated BMI due to the inability to adequately measure distal airway pressures. This is further complicated by likely inhomogeneous pressure and volume delivery to the distal airways [16]. The inability to optimize distal airway ventilation likely accounts for the noted increase in need for intubation in patients with morbid obesity, longer stenotic segments, and with longer procedure duration [17, 18]. This pilot study is the first to use EIT to assess tidal volumes and differences in regional ventilation with bag‐mask ventilation, LMA ventilation, and LFJV in patients undergoing airway surgery.

It was initially hypothesized that tidal volumes would be poorer during LFJV compared with LMA or mask ventilation. The data demonstrated relative homogeneity of ventilation during bag‐mask and LMA techniques, characterized by the recruitment of dependent lung regions. In contrast, the initiation of jet ventilation produced a marked increase in inhomogeneity, evidenced by decreased ventilation of basal and posterior regions alongside preferential ventilation of apical, nondependent regions. This pattern persisted following laser incisions and dilation, despite the presumed normalization of tracheal anatomy. Notably, these findings were observed in a cohort of patients with normal BMI and no significant lung disease; consequently, it is presumed that ventilation inhomogeneity would be even more pronounced in patients with elevated BMI.

To optimize ventilation, some centers have adopted HFJV and SHFJV [19]. Leiter and colleagues demonstrated improved end expiratory chest wall volume and tidal volumes using SHFJV, although both HFJV and LFJV provided adequate oxygenation and carbon dioxide elimination [20]. Windpassinger et al. used EIT to compare mask ventilation with subglottic HFJV and supraglottic SHFJV and found no statistically significant or clinically meaningful differences in the center of ventilation between modalities, although supraglottic SHFJV was associated with lower arterial oxygenation and subglottic HFJV improved dependent silent space ventilation [10]. Bialka et al. used EIT to assess the effect of different driving pressures in HFJV and found that lower pressures had similar tidal volumes with lower airway pressures [6]. Given the current study's relatively small sample size, we could not assess the effect of increasing pressures on delivered volumes or homogeneity but lower barotrauma risk is expected with LFJV compared to HFJV.

The present study suggests that, compared with LFJV, LMA ventilation provides more homogeneous ventilation with greater recruitment of dependent lung fields. These findings support the concept that EIT can serve as a real‐time monitoring tool to visualize regional ventilation, guide selection of ventilation strategies, and potentially inform titration of jet settings in higher‐risk patients.

This study has several limitations. The most important is the small sample size; the study was designed as a pilot investigation, and several eligible patients elected to undergo procedures at an alternative location, further reducing enrollment. In addition, there was an early learning curve for the clinical team in applying the EIT system, which contributed to electrode displacement and unusable data in two patients. Finally, the findings are limited to patients with relatively normal BMI and without significant lung disease and may underestimate the degree of inhomogeneity that could occur in obese or critically ill populations.

Conclusion

This pilot study is the first to use EIT to assess ventilation distribution during LFJV for laryngeal surgery. Three‐dimensional difference images of lung conductivity were reconstructed from EIT data, and regional volume ratios and the GI were computed during bag‐mask ventilation, LMA, LFJV, and in some instances, spontaneous breathing. In these four patients studied, LFJV preferentially ventilated apical lung regions, while dependent regions were consistently hypoventilated. These pilot data are preliminary observations that suggest that LFJV may predispose to under‐ventilation of dependent lung zones, even in otherwise healthy patients. Incorporating real‐time EIT could inform optimization of jet settings and potentially improve safety in higher‐risk populations.
