Impact of ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia on all-cause in-hospital mortality in adult patients who were critically ill: a retrospective cohort study in a tertiary grade A hospital
Authors: Fengsheng Cao, Guangyu Qiu, Lu Zhang, Dingxin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the association between acquired blood chloride abnormalities and all-cause in-hospital mortality in adult patients who were critically ill with normal blood chloride at admission to the intensive care unit (ICU).DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingSerum chloride concentration was measured by an indirect potentiometric method.ParticipantsThis study encompassed adult patients who were critically ill and admitted to the ICU, and who exhibited normal blood chloride levels (99–110 mmol/L) at Xiangyang Central Hospital between 1 April 2020 and 30 April 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause during hospitalisation. The secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, the length of ICU stay and the total length of stay in the hospital.ResultsThis study included 1131 patients. The baseline characteristics were comparable among the three groups of patients (all p>0.05). The all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was 11.4% (n=129). The incidence of acquired hyperchloraemia 48 hours after admission was 16.53% (n=187), with a mortality rate of 17.6% (n=33). The incidence of acquired hypochloraemia was 9.37% (n=106), with a mortality rate of 12.3% (n=13). The proportion of patients with persistently normal blood chloride was 74.09% (n=838), with a mortality rate of 9.9% (n=83). ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia was associated with a higher in-hospital mortality rate than patients with persistently normal blood chloride (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.09 to 3.05, p=0.022). Furthermore, for every 1 mmol/L increase in blood chloride levels, there was an 11% increase in the risk of all-cause in-hospital mortality (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.20, p=0.014).ConclusionsAcquired hyperchloraemia 48 hours after ICU admission is independently associated with a higher risk of all-cause in-hospital mortality in adult patients who were critically ill.

Introduction

The all-cause mortality rate in the intensive care unit (ICU) is approximately 16%,1 and mortality in patients with abnormal electrolyte levels is about three times higher than in patients with normal electrolyte levels.2 The chloride ion is the most abundant anion in the extracellular fluid, providing about 1/3 of the extracellular fluid tension,3 and plays a crucial role in homeostasis.4 Hospital-acquired abnormalities of chloride metabolism are common, observed in 25%–45% of the patients in the ICU.47 Acquired hyperchloraemia can be caused by medical intervention8 since saline is often used in patients who are critically ill as a resuscitation fluid, maintenance fluid or drug solvent.

Compared with traditional electrolytes, such as sodium, potassium and calcium, the chloride ion is not being given enough attention.4 The intravenous infusion of large amounts of saline over a short period will cause iatrogenic hyperchloraemia.8 Hyperchloraemia can lead to hyperchloraemic acidosis, renal function injury, haemodynamic instability, impaired cardiovascular function, inflammation, thrombosis and mortality.4 8

Hyperchloraemia and chloride levels are associated with an increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) and AKI-related morbidity and mortality in patients who are critically ill.5 9 10 Increases in plasma chloride during hospitalisation are associated with excess hospital mortality,5 while the association of hypochloraemia with mortality is uncertain.9 These previous studies focused on hyperchloraemia at admission to the ICU, or they examined the changes in chloride levels in all patients (irrespective of the chloride status at admission).5911 No previous studies focused only on patients with normal chloride levels at admission. Indeed, including patients with chloride abnormalities at admission would introduce bias since those patients would already be at risk of poor outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between acquired abnormal blood chloride and all-cause hospital mortality in adult patients who were critically ill with normal blood chloride on admission to the ICU.

Methods

Patient and public involvement in research

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Study design and patients

This single-centre retrospective cohort study included adult patients who were critically ill and admitted to the ICU of Xiangyang Central Hospital between 1 April 2020 and 30 April 2022. The inclusion criteria were (1) age >18 years and (2) normal blood chloride (99–110 mmol/L) at ICU admission. The exclusion criteria were (1) ICU stays of <48 hours, (2) missing daily chloride monitoring results for the first 48 hours, (3) no creatinine monitoring results during the hospital stay or (4) repeated multiple ICU admissions.

Data collection

All data (listed in table 1) were obtained from the hospital’s electronic case information system on 15 August 2022. All blood measurement data were obtained from the hospital laboratory department and were evaluated after admission to the ICU. Serum chloride concentration was measured by an indirect potentiometric method (ADVIA 2400 fully automated biochemical analyser; Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Oakville, ON, Canada). At the study ICU, blood sampling is performed around 06:00 and at other times according to the physician’s bedside judgement. More detailed information has been reported in our previous article.12

Definitions

The initial blood chloride concentration at ICU admission (Cl0) referred to the first serum chloride result measured after ICU admission, which usually was on the same calendar day as ICU admission. Blood chloride concentration 48 hours after ICU admission was referred to as Cl48. ΔCl was defined as the difference between Cl48 and Cl0 (ΔCl=Cl48−Cl0). The patients were classified according to the reference range of the normal chloride values (99–110 mmol/L), with chloride levels of ≥110 mmol/L defined as hyperchloraemia, 99–109 mmol/L as normal blood chloride and ≤98 mmol/L as hypochloraemia.

Acquired hyperchloraemia refers to hyperchloraemia that occurred 48 hours after ICU admission when blood chloride was normal at ICU admission. Acquired hypochloraemia refers to hypochloraemia that occurred 48 hours after ICU admission when blood chloride was normal at ICU admission. Persistent normochloraemia refers to chloride levels that remained within the normal range at ICU admission and 48 hours after ICU admission.

Outcome

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause during hospitalisation. The in-hospital mortality rate was calculated as the number of deaths divided by the number of hospitalised individuals. The secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, the length of ICU stay and the total length of stay in the hospital. The 30-day mortality rate was determined by dividing the number of deaths within 30 days by the total number of hospitalised individuals.

Statistical analysis

SPSS V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The clinical and demographic information was summarised and compared among groups. Medians and IQRs were used to represent non-normally distributed quantitative data, and the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and analysed using the χ2 test. A predictive model for in-hospital mortality was constructed using binary logistic regression to compare the differences between the in-hospital mortality rates of patients with acquired blood chloride abnormalities and those with persistently normal blood chloride. The variables were included stepwise using the change-in-estimate method. Only the covariates that significantly improved the fit of the model were kept. For each variable, the ORs and 95% CIs were calculated. All models passed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Due to the close relationship between some variables, such as chloride and sodium ions, variance inflation factors (VIF) were used to test the covariates for possible covariance. Two-sided p values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 3912 patients were admitted to the ICU during the study period, but 1131 adult patients with normal blood chloride on ICU admission were included (figure 1). The incidence of abnormal blood chloride on admission to the ICU was 44.12%, with hyperchloraemia at 30.87% (n=625) and hypochloraemia at 13.24% (n=268). For patients with normal blood chloride on admission to the ICU, the incidence of acquired hyperchloraemia at 48 hours after admission to the ICU was 16.53% (n=187), the incidence of acquired hypochloraemia was 9.37% (n=106) and the proportion of patients with persistent normal blood chloride was 74.1% (n=838). The median age of all patients was 66 years (IQR 54 to 75), and the proportion of males was 63.7% (n=721). The baseline characteristics were comparable among the three groups of patients, including age, sex, Body Mass Index, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, pH, procalcitonin (PCT), base excess (BE) and underlying diseases such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer, cirrhosis, cardiovascular disease, sepsis or septic shock, traumatic brain injury (TBI) and diabetic ketoacidosis (all p>0.05). However, there were significant differences in lactate, Cl0, Cl48, ΔCl, Na0 (sodium measurements on ICU admission), Na48 (sodium measurements 48 hours after ICU admission), ΔNa (defined as Na48−Na0), need for mechanical ventilation and history of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and chronic lung disease (all p<0.05) (table 1).

The all-cause in-hospital mortality was 11.4% (n=129). There were significant differences in in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality and length of ICU stay in patients with different serum chloride levels at 48 hours after ICU admission (all p<0.05) (table 2).

A restricted cubic spline was constructed to explore the relationship between Cl48 and hospital mortality. A U-shaped relationship was observed (figure 2). Variables at admission were included to construct a multivariable regression model, and the change-in-estimate method was used to screen the independent variables. Although the VIF was 1.472 for sodium and chloride at 48 hours after ICU admission, sodium was not included in the model because of possible covariance between sodium and chloride (as both are given with normal saline) and because the target variable was chloride. In the multivariable logistic regression, ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia was associated with a significant increase in in-hospital mortality compared with patients with persistent normochloraemia (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.09 to 3.05, p=0.022). In contrast, there were no significant differences in in-hospital mortality between the ICU-acquired hypochloraemia and persistent normochloraemia groups (OR 1.38, 95% CI 0.65 to 2.92, p=0.398). When Cl48 was entered into the model as a continuous variable, the conclusion remained unchanged, and it was found that the in-hospital mortality of patients with acquired hyperchloraemia increased by 11% for every 1 mmol/L increase in Cl48 (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.20, p=0.014) (table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

In order to check the robustness of the conclusions, three methods were used for sensitivity analyses. First, multivariable multiple interpolation was used to impute missing data for PCT, pH, BE and lactate (online supplemental table S1). After data filling, ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia still had a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate than patients with persistently normal blood chloride (OR 1.745, 95% CI 1.059 to 2.876, p=0.029). In contrast, there was still no significant difference in in-hospital mortality for the ICU-acquired hypochloraemia group compared with patients with persistently normal blood chloride (OR 1.315, 95% CI 0.647 to 2.675, p=0.449). Second, the results of the multivariable analysis after excluding patients with TBI (OR 1.906, 95% CI 1.106 to 3.286, p=0.020) remained consistent with the previous results, whereas the in-hospital mortality rate of the ICU-acquired hypochloraemia group was still not significantly different compared with the persistently normal blood chloride group (OR 1.417, 95% CI 0.671 to 2.990, p=0.361). Third, after excluding patients with sepsis and septic shock, ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of in-hospital mortality compared with the group with persistently normal blood chloride (OR 2.183, 95% CI 1.109 to 4.297, p=0.024), whereas the effect of ICU-acquired hypochlorhydria on in-hospital mortality was not statistically significant (OR 0.826, 95% CI 0.284 to 2.396, p=0.724). Finally, after excluding patients with renal replacement therapy, ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of in-hospital mortality compared with the group with persistently normal blood chloride (OR 1.710, 95% CI 1.047 to 2.791, p=0.032), whereas the effect of ICU-acquired hypochlorhydria on in-hospital mortality was not statistically significant (OR 1.424, 95% CI 0.697 to 2.908, p=0.332) (table 4).

Discussion

In contrast to previous literature, which has focused mostly on blood chloride abnormalities on admission to the ICU, especially hyperchloraemia, or on changes in blood chloride levels irrespective of the admission chloride status,61316 the present study focused on patients with normal blood chloride at admission but who acquired blood chloride abnormalities at 48 hours after admission to the ICU. Thongprayoon et al9 showed that 41% of all adult patients with normal blood chloride on admission, including ICU and non-ICU patients, developed hyperchloraemia or hypochloraemia during hospitalisation, which is different from the present study population (only ICU patients) and the time point of determining acquired blood chloride abnormality (at 48 hours in the present study). In the present study, the incidence of acquired blood chloride abnormalities 48 hours after admission to the ICU was 25.91%, and there were significant differences among the groups regarding Cl0, chronic lung disease and CKD. It is possible that the chloride concentration and volume of fluid infused affect the incidence of acquired blood chloride abnormalities, or that CKD or chronic lung disease affects the electrolyte and acid-base balance of the body.4 Severe diarrhoea, vomiting, high-dose diuretics, renal replacement therapy, nasogastric tube drainage, congestive heart failure and intracranial hypertension can cause changes in blood chloride levels.17

Although the correlation between abnormal blood chloride and adverse outcomes in patients who are critically ill is still debated, the recent literature supports the association of hyperchloraemia with an increased incidence of mortality141821 and AKI1219 2125 in patients who are critically ill, but those studies did not specifically examine the impact of acquired hyperchloraemia in patients with normal chloride levels at admission. In a 2016 retrospective study of 8830 adult patients in the ICU, Van Regenmortel et al20 found that hyperchloraemia at admission to the ICU was significantly associated with an increase in ICU and in-hospital mortality. A retrospective clinical study by Neyra et al16 in ICU patients with severe sepsis or septic shock examined serum chloride levels at ICU admission and 72 hours after admission and showed that serum chloride was independently associated with in-hospital morbidity and mortality in patients with critical illness and septic shock; the results showed no correlation between chloride concentration at ICU admission and all-cause in-hospital mortality, but chloride levels at 72 hours after ICU admission were associated with increased in-hospital mortality, with a 12% increase in in-hospital mortality for every 5 mEq/L increase in Cl72 (blood chloride concentration 72 hours after ICU admission).16 Increases in ΔCl were also associated with an increase in in-hospital mortality. Each 5 mmol/L increase in ΔCl was associated with a 15% increase in in-hospital mortality.16 Although the present study examined the changes in chloride levels in patients with normal chloride levels at admission, the excluded patients with hyperchloraemia at admission were identified, and it is known that all those patients ultimately had normal chloride levels during ICU stay. Hence, future studies should examine the impact of the changes in chloraemia during hospitalisation in patients with hypochloraemia or hyperchloraemia at admission.

No relevant previous study focused on patients with normal blood chloride levels at admission, and the present study adds evidence regarding ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia. In the present study, ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia was associated with a significant increase in all-cause in-hospital mortality compared with patients with persistently normal blood chloride, which is supported by the previous studies described above but performed in different patient populations.141821 In addition, those previous studies did not exclusively include patients with normal blood chloride at admission.

On the other hand, hypochloraemia was not associated with mortality in the present study. Previous studies reported increased mortality in patients with hypochloraemia and congestive heart failure26 and continuous renal replacement therapy.27 A study showed that community-based hypochloraemia was independently and dose-dependently associated with an increased mortality risk.28 The reasons for the discrepancy could lie in the wide variety of conditions that led to ICU admission in the present study, the fact that all patients were in the ICU and the fact that normal saline is used in virtually all patients in the ICU. Still, this lack of association between hypochloraemia and mortality warrants further analysis since this lack was consistently observed in all sensitivity analyses. Indeed, TBI is associated with electrolyte disturbances due to regulatory mechanisms that try to control intracranial pressure or medical interventions to control that pressure.29 Sepsis and septic shock are also associated with fluid and electrolyte dysregulation.30

The literature supports the idea that hyperchloraemia, around 48–72 hours after ICU admission, clusters with sepsis/septic shock and greater use of mechanical ventilation and is associated with increased all-cause mortality.14 16 31 32 The observation that sodium trends mirror chloride, and that higher cumulative sodium-chloride exposure tracks with mortality, is biologically plausible and consistent with data comparing saline with balanced crystalloids, especially considering that normal saline contains chloride and sodium ions but cannot completely disentangle ion toxicity from confounding by illness severity and fluid volume.163335 Thus, the inference that the hyperchloraemic group likely reflects a sicker or at least distinct diagnostic cohort, despite similar APACHE II scores, is well aligned with published evidence, while a cautious tone regarding causality and residual confounding remains warranted.

This study has some strengths and some weaknesses. One strength is that the study population was all adult patients admitted to the ICU over a short and relatively contemporary period, with a heterogeneous patient population with a median APACHE II score of 22 (16 to 27), providing good generalisability. Still, the study has some limitations. First, it was a single-centre retrospective study, and the characteristics of retrospective studies allow us only to find associations between factors but not to establish causal relationships. In addition, the study was limited to the data available in the charts. Several factors can influence the blood chloride ion levels (eg, severe diarrhoea, vomiting, use of high-dose diuretics, renal replacement therapy, nasogastric tube drainage, congestive heart failure, intracranial hypertension, etc), but they could not be assessed in the present study because many patients were transferred from other hospitals, and the data were missing or inconsistent. The primary data source was the electronic medical record system, which did not allow for a systematic and reliable collection of the precise timing and specific causes of death for all 129 patients who died. The cause of death in patients who were critically ill is often multifactorial and can be challenging to attribute to a single factor based on retrospective chart review alone. Second, the study specifically focused on the impact of early (first 48 hours) acquired chloride abnormalities as an initial investigation into this patient subgroup. Consequently, systematic data on serial chloride measurements beyond the 48-hour mark are not available. Third, some of the patients may have had renal replacement therapy within 48 hours after admission to the ICU, which may have influenced the determination of acquired blood chloride abnormalities, and the present study included all interventions affecting blood chloride without differentiating between them. Acquired chloride abnormalities are common in adult patients who were critically ill with normal blood chloride on admission to the ICU, with a prevalence as high as 25.91%. ICU-acquired hyperchloraemia is significantly correlated with increased hospital mortality, 30-day mortality and extended ICU stays. Fourth, cases with abnormal chloride values at admission were excluded since the aim was to examine patients with initially normal chloride levels. However, it resulted in the exclusion of many patients, and the consideration of confounding factors such as pathologies and management leading to hyperchloraemia was limited. Fifth, differences in chloride levels were observed at admission, but all included patients had normal chloride levels at admission.

Studies have shown the use of crystalloids for fluid therapy (eg, Hartmann’s solution, Ringer’s lactate and recently developed more expensive products),36 and given the theoretical concerns about high cumulative doses of sodium and chloride ions, future studies should examine the risk of ICU-acquired chloride abnormalities in patients who did not receive saline for fluid maintenance. Still, as saline remains the most common fluid replacement or maintenance solution used in the world, especially in developing countries,3739 the present study provides important information regarding the risks of complications related to saline use in the ICU. Much attention should be paid to avoiding iatrogenic hyperchloraemia since the present study implies that the development of hyperchloraemia in patients with normal blood chloride at admission is associated with adverse patient outcomes. Of note, the present study could not determine the exact contribution of fluid replacement to hyperchloraemia occurrence, but care should be taken with fluid replacement/maintenance, including the use of solutions not chloride-based. Blood chloride should also be monitored at relatively short intervals to allow prompt interventions and adjustments.

Conclusions

In conclusion, acquired hyperchloraemia in ICU patients with normal chloride levels at admission was independently associated with higher all-cause in-hospital mortality, higher 30-day mortality and longer ICU stays. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring chloride levels. Avoiding chloride-rich solutions in adult patients in the ICU could, when possible, reduce mortality, which should be examined in future studies.
