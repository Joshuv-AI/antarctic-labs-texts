Sodium butyrate enhances sorafenib-induced ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death by modulating IRF2-Oasl2-cGAS pathway in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ying Xiang, Jinyu Zheng, Xinlu Zhao, Lina Zhou, Qiong Yan, Yichun Ma, Yue Zhou, Ping Jiang, Yi Fang, Wenjun Li, Yinya Pan, Hongji Tao, Wenying Li, Fan Xiang, Yang Hua, Yuyi Li, Chang Zheng, Weihong Ge, Yuhong Li, Simin Yan, Yun Zhu, Guifang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While much attention has been focused on the direct cytotoxic effects of anticancer agents on tumor cells, emerging evidence suggests that the induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) and the consequent remodeling of the tumor microenvironment may critically influence overall therapeutic efficacy. Sorafenib, a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, can induce ferroptosis and thereby trigger ICD, offering the potential to overcome chemoresistance and coordinate systemic antitumor immunity. However, its therapeutic efficacy in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains limited, attributing to its low efficiency in inducing ICD. Herein, this study investigates combined sodium butyrate (NaB) and sorafenib in liposomal co-delivery system (LipNaB@Sor) at an optimal 1:1 M ratio in CRC. LipNaB@Sor synergistically amplifies sorafenib-induced ferroptosis and consequent ICD, concurrently activating antitumor immunity through dendritic cell maturation and cytotoxic CD8+ T cell recruitment. Mechanistically, IRF2-mediated Oasl2 downregulation activates the cGAS-STING pathway, augmenting DNA damage signaling cascades. In vivo validation demonstrates significant tumor suppression and prolonged survival without observable toxicity, establishing LipNaB@Sor as a translatable strategy to overcome therapy resistance in immunologically unresponsive CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent and second most lethal malignancy globally [1]. While current therapeutic paradigms integrate chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), multimodal regimens yield suboptimal response rates (27.1 %–60.9 %) [[2], [3], [4]]. A persistent challenge in cancer therapy is the conversion of a “cold” tumor-characterized by poor infiltration of immune cells-into a “hot” tumor, which is densely infiltrated by immune cells. The activation of the immune system results in a dual mechanism of tumor eradication: first, the direct cytotoxic effect of cancer-killing treatments such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and second, an antitumor immune response mediated by effector lymphocytes-such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells-through the release of cytotoxic molecules including perforins and granzymes, as well as through the engagement of death receptors via surface ligands such as Fas ligand and TRAIL [5,6]. For instance, the strategy of converting immunologically "cold" tumors to "hot" states via chemotherapy-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) underpins numerous clinical trials combining ICD-inducing chemotherapeutics with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [[7], [8], [9]]. These findings underscore the importance of not only achieving direct tumor cell death but also orchestrating a pro-inflammatory milieu that supports durable antitumor immunity. Thus, eliciting effective anticancer immune responses through ICD requires both a T cell-inflamed, antigen-rich tumor microenvironment (TME) and the presence of abundant adjuvants and inflammatory signals.

Certain targeted anticancer agents, particularly tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as crizotinib and cabozantinib, function as potent ICD inducers [10,11]. However, their clinical utility is constrained by significant hepatotoxicity [12]. Sorafenib maintains an irreplaceable position in clinical oncology, particularly as a first-line treatment for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma [13]. Its established multi-kinase inhibitor profile-targeting not only system Xc− to induce ferroptosis but also VEGFR, PDGFR, and RAF signaling pathways-enables a comprehensive therapeutic effect that extends beyond tumor cell death to include anti-angiogenesis and TME modulation [14,15]. Ferroptosis-an iron-dependent lipid peroxidation-driven death modality-represents a promising ICD inducer by remodeling the TMEs [8,[16], [17], [18], [19]]. However, the sorafenib's efficacy in CRC is constrained by immunosuppressive TME-driven resistance mechanisms, including: (i) IFNγ pathway suppression, (ii) impaired antigen presentation, and (iii) ferroptotic immune cells (e.g., CD8+T cells, DCs) [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. While its capacity to elicit ICD via ferroptosis alone may be limited, its well-documented clinical efficacy, manageable safety profile, and broad availability make it an ideal backbone aimed at enhancing ICD and overcoming tumor immune resistance [26]. This necessitates combinatorial strategies pairing ICD inducers with enhancers-agents that augment dendritic cells (DCs) ' capacity to detect ICD-associated signals and cross-present tumor-associated antigens to T lymphocytes-to overcome intrinsic ICD suppression.

Sodium butyrate (NaB), a potential ICD enhancer, has been shown to modulates immune cell activity-including cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and DC differentiation [27,28]. Indirect evidence also suggests that butyrate can potentiate the efficacy of ferroptosis inducers by restoring redox balance and promoting a more robust antitumor immune response [27,[29], [30], [31]]. These observations indicate that combining NaB with sorafenib may enhance ICD and improve immune-mediated tumor elimination, offering a promising approach to overcome chemotherapy resistance. Herein, we utilized a liposome platform for co-delivering butyrate and sorafenib (LipNaB@Sor) at an optimal synergistic concentration ratio to evaluate its treatment efficacy in CRC. The characterization and tumor targeting capability of LipNaB@Sor was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo, and the underlying mechanisms of their synergy to ferroptosis, ICD and immune molecular modulation were investigated. Furthermore, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte flow cytometry analyses were used to assess the regulation of TMEs and activation of specific immune responses in vivo (as illustrated in Scheme 1). Results and discussion

Chemotherapy elicits heterogeneous responses correlated with ICD and tumor microenvironment remodeling

The efficacy of chemotherapeutic regimens exhibits heterogeneity across different cancer types as well as among individual patients. Accumulating evidence supports that, in addition to the direct cytotoxic effects on tumor cells, secondary immune responses play a crucial role in determining treatment outcomes. Therefore, enhancing the subsequent immune activation may represent an important strategy to expand the indications of promising agents in clinical practice [32]. To initially characterize chemotherapy-remodeled TMEs and ICD alterations, we analyzed the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE178318 (GEO), comprising transcriptomic profiles of primary CRC, matched liver metastases, and peripheral blood during preoperative chemotherapy. Following rigorous quality control and batch correction, we profiled the transcriptional landscape of primary CRC tumors, matched liver metastases, and blood at single-cell resolution (Fig. 1A–B). In brief, cell populations clustered into thirteen distinct types, with significant compositional shifts observed in major immune lineages (T/B/NK cells, macrophages) across tissue compartments. The potent anti-tumor efficacy of chemotherapy is precisely delineated by the significant enrichment of specific immune pathways. This coordinated mechanism is initiated by the enhanced "antigen processing and presentation", which serves as the crucial trigger. This robust antigen presentation then logically fuels the broader "immune response" and, more specifically, the "adaptive immune response", reflected by the downstream activation and clonal expansion of antigen-specific lymphocytes. Consequently, this targeted process drives the tumor infiltration and cytotoxic activity of T cells-the direct mediators of tumor cell killing-ultimately resulting in observed tumor regression and sustained efficacy.

We further identified responder-specific immunomodulation: decreased exhausted CD8+ T cells and Tregs alongside increased Th17 cells (Fig. 1D–E), indicative of chemotherapy-potentiated immune activation and enhanced ICD. Nevertheless, the potential association between chemotherapeutic efficacy and the induction of immune responses has not been fully established. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) identified significant enrichment of immune-related pathways-notably MHC class I and class II antigen processing and presentation-in chemoresponsive patients relative to non-responders (GSE 45404) (Fig. 1F). Expression of key immunomodulatory genes, including HLA-DRA, HLA-DQA1, CXCL10, and CXCL9, was positively correlated with therapeutic response, implying potentiated T-cell and DC activation and recruitment in chemosensitive tumors (Fig. 1G–J). In alignment with these observations, analysis of TCGA data established that elevated HMGB1 expression was significantly associated with prolonged overall survival, identifying it as a favorable prognostic marker in CRC (Fig. 1K).

While ICD inducers thus represent promising therapeutic candidates, their efficacy remains constrained by tripartite barriers: suboptimal ICD induction, inadequate tumor targeting, and poor tissue penetration. Consequently, combinatorial regimens pairing ICD inducers with enhancers emerge as strategically viable solutions to overcome intrinsic tumor resistance.

Combinatorial strategies of ICD inducers and enhancers for CRC

While TKIs such as crizotinib and cabozantinib function as potent ICD inducers, their clinical utility is constrained by significant hepatotoxicity [10,11]. In contrast, sorafenib-a multi-targeted TKI with favorable safety profiles-represents a promising alternative. To enhance CRC cell sensitivity and amplify anti-tumor immunity, we synergized sorafenib with NaB, a potential ICD enhancer, thereby potentiating its therapeutic efficacy.

We evaluated the synergistic effects of sorafenib and NaB at various molar ratios (1:0.1 to 1:10 μM:mM) using free drug formulations (Fig. 2A and S1A). Our results indicated a synergistic interaction between the two drugs, particularly at a certain range of molar ratio (Fig. S1B). Subsequently, we formulated these drugs into liposomes, which significantly reduced the IC50 of sorafenib by 2.5-fold at a 1:1 M ratio (Fig. 2B and S1C). Based on these findings, we developed LipNaB@Sor liposomes at a 1:1 M ratio for further investigation. Characterization of the liposomes via dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed average particle sizes of 55.5 ± 2.71 nm (Lip@Sor), 84.3 ± 2.1 nm (LipNaB), and 86.1 ± 2.8 nm (LipNaB@Sor), with corresponding zeta potentials of −13.8 ± 1.7 mV, −12.3 ± 0.9 mV, and −12.3 ± 1.4 mV, respectively (Fig. S2A–2B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the nanoscale structure of the liposomes (Fig. S2C). Stability studies over 96 h in PBS at pH 7.4 showed consistent particle size, indicating favorable temporal stability (Fig. S3A). LipNaB@Sor exhibited a desirable sustained release profile, with over 60 % of the encapsulated drugs released over 72 h (Fig. S3B–3C). Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or danger signals released during ICD initiate antigen-specific adaptive immune responses, characterized by the extracellular release of high-mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1), secretion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and cell-surface exposure of calreticulin (ecto-CRT) [8]. We next explored its potential to enhance ICD. Following co-incubation with various treatment groups, we observed a time-dependent increase in the extracellular secretion of ATP and HMGB1 levels, with notably more pronounced elevations in the LipNaB@Sor-treated samples (Fig. 2C–D). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed a significant increase in ecto-CRT intensity and a concomitant decrease in HMGB1 intensity in the LipNaB@Sor group compared to other treatments (Fig. S4A–S4D). The apparent discrepancy in HMGB1 levels is consistent with its active release during ICD, demonstrating a redistribution of the protein from the intra-to the extracellular space.

To investigate the capacity of LipNaB@Sor to activate the immune response, we co-cultured bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) with cancer cells treated with PBS, Sor, Lip@Sor, LipNaB, and LipNaB@Sor for 24 h. Subsequent assessment of BMDC surface markers showed significant changes in the expression of MHCII, CD80, and CD86 among the different groups (Fig. 2E–J). Specifically, the LipNaB@Sor group exhibited approximately a 1.5-fold increase in the expression levels of MHCII, CD80, and CD86 compared to the control group. Moreover, the LipNaB@Sor treatment significantly increased apoptosis rates by approximately 7.1-fold relative to the control (Fig. S5A–S5C). LipNaB@Sor demonstrated potent cell proliferation suppression, reducing proliferation by 76.9 % relative to the control group (Fig. S5D–S5E). This dual mechanism underscores the therapeutic potential of LipNaB@Sor in cancer therapy, offering a promising strategy for improving treatment efficacy through both direct tumor cell killing and immune system activation.

Mechanistic basis of LipNaB@Sor-Induced ferroptosis and immunogenic remodeling

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the tumoricidal effect of LipNaB@Sor, we conducted transcriptome sequencing and KEGG enrichment analysis comparing LipNaB@Sor with Lip@Sor (Fig. 3A). The analyses revealed significant alterations in gene expression profiles, with key ferroptosis-related genes such as Slc3a2, Ftl1, Gclm, and Acsl5 being downregulated, while Gpx4, Acsl3, Acsl1, Gss, and Gclc were upregulated (Fig. 3B). RT-qPCR validation confirmed these transcriptional changes, demonstrating reduced expression of Slc7a11 and Nfe2l2 (Fig. 3C–D) in the LipNaB@Sor group, alongside significantly elevated levels of Gpx4, Fth1, Lpcat3, and Tfrc (Fig. 3E–H). Protein expression analysis further supported these findings, showing that LipNaB@Sor robustly inhibited the SLC7A11-GPX4 pathway, disrupted the P62-NRF2-KEAP1 pathway, and decreased FTH1 expression (Fig. 3I and Fig. S6A–S6F). As is reported that the molecular basis of ferroptosis involves maintaining a delicate balance between oxidative damage and antioxidant defenses [34]. Iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation are critical triggers of membrane oxidative damage during ferroptosis [35]. To evaluate how LipNaB@Sor affects this balance, we quantified intracellular ROS, GSH, iron (Fe2+), lipid oxidation, and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using specific kits. Flow cytometry analyses after 48-h co-incubation with various liposomal formulations showed that LipNaB@Sor treatment markedly increased intracellular ROS levels by 4.1-fold compared to the control group (Fig. 3J–K). This increase was also significant relative to groups treated with Sor, Lip@Sor, and LipNaB. Additionally, LipNaB@Sor led to elevated lipid oxidation (Fig. 3L–M), intracellular MDA (Fig. 3N) and Fe2+ (Fig. 3O) levels, while concurrently reducing GSH levels (Fig. 3P). Pre-treatment with Fer-1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor, abolished LipNaB@Sor-induced elevations in lipid peroxides, MDA, and Fe2+, and prevented the depletion of GSH (Fig. 3L–P). These results collectively indicate that LipNaB@Sor effectively induces ferroptosis by disrupting the balance between antioxidant defense and oxidative damage, promoting iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, thereby enhancing ICD.

Oasl2 downregulation and its role in CRC progression following LipNaB@Sor treatment

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the effects of LipNaB@Sor, we conducted a comparative transcriptomic analysis across different treatment groups. The analyses revealed distinct patterns of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the LipNaB@Sor and Lip@Sor groups, as visualized by a volcano plot (Fig. 4A). Notably, among the top 10 up-regulated and down-regulated genes, Oasl2 showed a significant reduction in expression (Fig. 4B). Oasl2 functions similarly to OASL, a member of the oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) family that lacks 2′-5′ OAS activity. OASL is known for its role in inducing type I interferon (IFN) responses and stimulating antiviral immunity against RNA viruses while inhibiting IFN responses during DNA virus infections [36,37]. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the STRING database identified 29 genes closely associated with Oasl2 (Fig. 4C). Despite the established role of Oasl2 in innate immunity and viral defense, its contributions to tumorigenesis, treatment, and prognosis have not been extensively studied. To explore the potential function of OASL in cancer after LipNaB@Sor treatment, we conducted a systematic analysis using the GEPIA database. Firstly, our findings revealed significantly elevated OASL expression in CRC patients (Fig. 4E) and an upward trend across various tumor types (Fig. S7). Higher OASL expression was also associated with poorer overall survival, post-progression survival, and recurrence-free survival (Fig. S8A–8C). Furthermore, clinical surgical specimens from the pathology center of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital were analyzed, revealing robust staining for OASL in tumor tissues, particularly in higher-stage malignancies, compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 4F–H). These results collectively suggest that OASL plays a significant role in the malignant progression of CRC, offering new avenues for improving outcomes in CRC patients.

IRF2-mediated transcriptional regulation of Oasl2 modulates cGAS-STING signaling and anti-tumor immunity Post-LipNaB@Sor treatment

To elucidate the effects of LipNaB@Sor on the transcriptional regulation of Oasl2, we integrated data from three transcription factor (TF) prediction databases-TFDB, PROMO, and the UCSC Genome Browser. This analysis identified 13 overlapping TFs, with a particular emphasis on IRF2 (Fig. 5A). IRF2 functions as a transcriptional repressor of IRF-1-driven transcription, thereby modulating IFN-I responses [39]. Although both OASL and IRF2 can inhibit IFN responses during DNA virus infections [37,39], the relationship between these two molecules following LipNaB@Sor treatment has not been fully explored. Data from the GEPIA database indicated a positive correlation between OASL and IRF2 expression, suggesting that IRF2 may act as a positive regulator for Oasl2 (Fig. 5B). Our findings showed that while Lip@Sor did not alter Irf2 mRNA levels, it increased IRF2 protein expression. Motif analysis using the JASPAR database identified two binding motifs for IRF2 within the Oasl2 promoter region (Fig. 5E and Fig. S10). Subsequently, we conducted a CUT&Tag assay to elucidate the interaction between the IRF2 protein and the DNA within the promoter region of Oasl2. qPCR analysis following the CUT&Tag assay demonstrated that LipNaB@Sor and LipNaB inhibited Oasl2 transcription by suppressing IRF2 activity, whereas Lip@Sor enhanced IRF2 activity (Fig. 5F–G). Previous studies have demonstrated that OASL suppresses the activity of the DNA sensor cGAS, thereby restricting interferon production [37]. Accordingly, we examined DNA damage markers, including γH2AX phosphorylation and the release of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), in cancer cells [38,40]. LipNaB@Sor significantly induced histone H2AX phosphorylation (Fig. 5H–J and Fig. S9C) and promoted dsDNA release (Fig. 5K). Co-localization analysis confirmed the interaction between OASL and cGAS, while fluorescence intensity analysis showed that inhibiting OASL enhanced cGAS activation by LipNaB@Sor (Figs. S11A–S11B). Moreover, LipNaB@Sor activated the cGAS-STING pathway in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 5L, Fig. S9D–S9E).

Subsequently, we designed specific siRNAs targeting Oasl2 to elucidate its role in the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway. Both Si#1 and Si#2 effectively inhibited Oasl2 mRNA and OASL protein levels (Figs. S12A–S12B). Notably, Oasl2 knockdown promoted DNA damage and activated the cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. 5M and Fig. S13). Previous research has established that interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) are downstream targets of type I IFN signaling, often activated alongside the cGAS-STING pathway [41]. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that LipNaB@Sor mitigated the inhibition caused by Lip@Sor, leading to elevated ISG expression (Fig. S14A). Interference with Oasl2 also resulted in increased ISG expression (Fig. S14B).

To determine whether Oasl2 acts as the critical mediator, we overexpressed Oasl2 in tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. S12C and S12E). This genetic intervention significantly attenuated LipNaB@Sor-induced cGAS-STING signaling (Fig. 5N), demonstrating that Oasl2 downregulation is necessary for immune activation. To further delineate the upstream regulatory mechanism, we performed Irf2 knockdown experiments (Supplementary Fig. S12D and S12F). Silencing Irf2 effectively suppressed Oasl2 expression and consequently enhanced cGAS-STING pathway activation (Fig. 5O). Importantly, this enhanced signaling in Irf2-deficient cells was reversed by concurrent Oasl2 overexpression (Fig. 5O), confirming that Oasl2 functions downstream of IRF2 in this regulatory cascade.

These results collectively suggest that LipNaB@Sor exerts its effects by modulating the transcriptional regulation of Oasl2 through the suppression of IRF2 activity. This modulation enhances DNA damage signals and activates the cGAS-STING pathway, ultimately leading to increased ISG expression and potentially enhancing the anti-tumor immune response. These findings highlight the complex interplay between transcriptional regulators and DNA-sensing pathways in the context of ferroptosis induction and provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic potential of LipNaB@Sor.

Augmented tumor targeting and therapeutic efficacy of LipNaB@Sor in CRC

Our previous research has demonstrated that butyrate modification of drug delivery systems can significantly enhance the targeting capacity of colonic epithelium [42]. Then, we constructed coumarin 6-loaded liposomes (Lip@C6) containing either 1 % or 10 % NaB for cellular uptake experiments. After a 6-h co-incubation period, the fluorescence intensity of C6 in LipNaB with 10 % NaB showed significant concentration- and time-dependent increases (Fig. 6A–B). Discussion and conclusion

A major impediment to leveraging ferroptosis in cancer therapy is its inconsistent efficacy as an ICD inducer. Although ferroptosis effectively eliminates tumor cells, it often fails to translate this cytotoxicity into a robust anti-tumor immune response, frequently resulting in a persistently "cold" TME [22,23]. This deficiency primarily stems from inadequate DC activation and a consequent blunted T-cell response, directly attributable to impairments in the antigen processing and presentation pathway [44]. It is this fundamental disconnect between tumor cell killing and the induction of productive immunity that severely curtails the therapeutic promise of ferroptosis, underscoring the critical need for strategies that can amplify its immunogenic output.

ICD has garnered attention as a potential therapeutic strategy for overcoming resistance to conventional cancer treatments and enhancing antitumor immunity [5,8,32]. Our study demonstrates a novel combinatorial strategy that effectively converts ferroptosis from a potentially immunosuppressive event into a potent trigger of antitumor immunity by co-delivering sorafenib and NaB. Our research delves into the synergistic effects of butyrate in augmenting sorafenib-induced ICD in CRC, presenting a coherent narrative that underscores the importance of this interaction for tumor therapy. The development of LipNaB@Sor liposomes at an optimal 1:1 M ratio of NaB to sorafenib significantly reduced the IC50 of sorafenib, indicating a potent synergy between the two compounds. This reduction was not only observed in terms of cytotoxicity but also extended to enhanced apoptosis rates and suppression of cell proliferation, highlighting the efficacy of LipNaB@Sor in CRC cells.

Beyond direct cytotoxicity, LipNaB@Sor's ability to induce ICD emerged as a critical factor in its therapeutic potential. ICD triggers the release of DAMPs, such as HMGB1 and ATP, and the ecto-CRT, initiating antigen-specific adaptive immune responses [8]. Our results demonstrated that LipNaB@Sor significantly increased the extracellular secretion of ATP and HMGB1, accompanied by elevated ecto-CRT intensity. This activation of ICD not only eliminated targeted tumor cells but also stimulated a systemic anti-tumor immune response, potentially leading to the destruction of metastatic lesions. The modulation of the TME further supported the immunotherapeutic benefits of LipNaB@Sor. Flow cytometry analyses revealed significant increases in MHCII, CD80, and CD86 markers on DCs, indicative of DC maturation and enhanced antigen presentation. Additionally, there were notable increases in CD8+ T cell populations secreting granzyme B and IFNγ, underscoring the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Conversely, proportions of MDSCs were reduced, effectively lessening immune suppression within the TME. Collectively, these findings suggest that LipNaB@Sor can transform a "cold" TME into a "hot" one, promoting robust antitumor immunity.

The underlying mechanisms driving this synergy were elucidated through transcriptome sequencing and KEGG pathway analysis. Key findings included the inhibition of the SLC7A11-GPX4 pathway and disruption of the P62-NRF2-KEAP1 pathway, which are central to regulating ferroptosis and cellular oxidative stress [29,34]. Importantly, LipNaB@Sor treatment led to increased intracellular ROS, MDA, and Fe2+ levels while reducing GSH levels, effectively disrupting the balance between antioxidant defense and oxidative damage. These alterations triggered ferroptosis, consequently inducing ICD and establishing the mechanistic foundation for the observed antitumor efficacy. Our therapeutic strategy reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment by shifting the early immunosuppressive ferroptotic response into a sustained immunogenic phase. This is evidenced by sustained HMGB1 release and robust DC activation, consistent with the later immunogenic stage of ferroptosis described previously [45].

We unravel a previously unrecognized IRF2-Oasl2-cGAS signaling axis that mechanistically links ferroptotic damage to STING activation and DC maturation, providing a new theoretical framework for immune-oncology. The role of LipNaB@Sor in modulating the transcriptional regulation of Oasl2 via the suppression of IRF2 activity was also confirmed. In brief, Oasl2 is functionally analogous to OASL, which normally inhibits IFN responses during DNA virus infections [37]. By downregulating Oasl2 expression, LipNaB@Sor indirectly activated the cGAS-STING pathway, enhancing DNA damage signals and increasing ISG expression. This modulation likely contributed to the observed improvements in antitumor immunity and ICD. We evaluated the in vivo efficacy of LipNaB@Sor in mouse models of subcutaneous tumors and lung metastasis. The treatment demonstrated potent activity, significantly inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis while extending host survival compared to single-agent liposome controls. Notably, safety evaluations indicated no discernible impairment across organ systems, confirming the favorable biosafety profile of LipNaB@Sor.

In summary, our comprehensive studies provide compelling evidence that LipNaB@Sor efficiently targets the IRF2-Oasl2-cGAS axis, resulting in marked improvements in antitumor immunity by inducing ferroptosis and ICD. The formulation of LipNaB@Sor not only enhances direct tumor cell killing but also activates potent immune responses, transforming the TME from "cold" to "hot". From a translational perspective, we developed a stable liposomal nanoformulation that enables the synergistic delivery of two drugs, presenting a promising new therapeutic option for CRC patients. These findings offer intriguing clinical implications and innovative therapeutic approaches for overcoming ferroptosis resistance in CRC. By strengthening the logical connections between each result and weaving them into a cohesive narrative, we have highlighted the significance of sorafenib-induced ferroptosis-related ICD in cancer treatment. This approach underscores the potential of LipNaB@Sor as a transformative therapy for CRC, offering hope for improved patient outcomes in the face of treatment-resistant tumors.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of liposomes

The ethanol injection method was utilized to prepare Lip@Sor, LipNaB, and LipNaB@Sor. Sorafenib, sodium butyrate (NaB), and lecithin were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sorafenib (0.5 mg) and lecithin (10 mg) were dissolved in 0.75 mL of ethanol at a weight ratio of 1:20. This mixture was subsequently injected into a rapidly vortexed solution of ultrapure water or aqueous NaB (0.3–5.1 %, 9.25 mL) at a molar ratio of 1:0.1–1:10 (Sora: NaB [μM:mM]). The concentration of liposomes was increased using an Amicon Ultra centrifuge (Millipore) at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. Lip@C6, Lip@C6 with 1 % and 10 % NaB, Lip@DiR, LipNaB@DiR, and LipNaB@Sor/DiR were synthesized following the same protocol. The diameters and zeta potentials of the liposomes were measured using DLS. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) capacity for both sorafenib and NaB in different formulations are shown in Table S1.

Calculation formulas:Encapsulationefficiency(EE%)=(Measuredconcentrationindrug−loadednanoparticles×Solutionvolume)/Druginput×100%.Drugloadingcapacity(DL%)=(Measuredconcentrationindrug−loadednanoparticles×Solutionvolume)/Totaldrugamountinthesystem×100%.

The chemicals used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell lines

CT26.WT cells (ATCC, CRL-2638) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Biochannel, Nanjing, China) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (100 IU/mL) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 95 % relative humidity and 5 % CO2.

Mouse model

Female Balb/c mice (5–6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) to establish a subcutaneous tumor transplantation model. For tumor growth and survival analysis in vivo, 1 × 105 CT26 cells were injected subcutaneously into the flank of each Balb/c mouse. Tumor dimensions and body weight were monitored using an electronic caliper and scale every 2 to 3 days. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, the mice were randomized and treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Lip@Sor (Sora: 2 mg/kg), LipNaB (equivalent to NaB in LipNaB@Sor), or LipNaB@Sor (Sora: 2 mg/kg) via intravenous injection every other day. When the tumor volume reached 1000 mm3 or the tumor diameter reached 15 mm, the mice were euthanized for the collection of blood, organs, and tumors. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume = length × width2/2 (mm3). A pulmonary metastasis model was generated by injecting CT26 cells (5 × 105 in 100 μL of PBS) into the tail vein of Balb/c mice. Metastatic burden was evaluated weekly via bioluminescence imaging, employing the same treatment regimen as previously described. All animal procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University (DWSY-24015471).

Cell viability assay

Cancer cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates containing complete DMEM culture medium and incubated overnight. The cells were treated with free sorafenib, free NaB, and liposomes loaded with sorafenib and modified with NaB at various concentration ratios for 24 h. To assess cell viability, the medium was replaced with a 10 % CCK-8 solution. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using the Spark™ 10M multimode microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). The commercial assays are detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Cell apoptosis and colony formation assay

Cancer cells were treated with PBS (control), Sor, Lip@Sor, LipNaB, and LipNaB@Sor (1:1) for 48 h, then stained with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The proportion of apoptotic cells was measured by flow cytometry (BD Accuri™ C6, USA). The Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime) was employed to evaluate the anti-tumor effects of the different liposomes. For the colony formation assay, cells (500 to 1000 per well) were seeded in six-well plates and treated with various liposomes for 24 h; subsequently, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM culture medium for 5–7 days. Cell colonies were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 1 % crystal violet for 30 min. The colonies were imaged using the EVOS™ M7000 Imaging System (Invitrogen, USA).

DCFH-DA staining

Cancer cells were incubated with a DCFH-DA working solution (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA7308) in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C for 30 min. The level of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) was quantified using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri™ C6, USA) with an excitation wavelength of 480 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in lysed cells from different treatment groups were detected using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (BL904A, Biosharp). Each 100 μL of cell lysate supernatant was mixed with 200 μL of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution, and the mixtures were incubated at 95 °C for 60 min. The levels of MDA were measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 532 nm. To assess lipid peroxidation using a flow cytometer, Lipofluor (Dojindo) was added to the experimental medium and incubated with cancer cells at 37 °C for 0.5 h.

Iron assay

Cellular iron concentrations in different treatment groups were measured using the Ferrous Ion Content Assay Kit (BL1147B, Biosharp). Following the manufacturer's protocol, cells were lysed and centrifuged (12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C) to obtain cell lysate supernatants. The supernatants were then homogenized with iron assay buffer and iron reducer, and the mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The levels of Fe2+ were detected using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 562 nm.

GSH assay

Reduced GSH concentrations in various treatment groups were measured using the Reduced Glutathione (GSH) Content Assay Kit (BL874B, Biosharp). According to the manufacturer's instructions, cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed. After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, 20 μL of the cell lysate supernatant was mixed with 160 μL of the GSH working solution and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The resulting yellow product, 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid, was detected using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 412 nm.

ATP release assay

Cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates and treated for 6, 12, and 24 h. The cell supernatants containing extracellular ATP were collected and analyzed using the Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime). Luminescence was measured with a microplate reader.

Release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)

Cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates. After treatment for 6, 12, and 24 h, cell-free supernatants were collected and analyzed for HMGB1 levels using an ELISA Kit (MOSTCELL) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cellular uptake

To assess the uptake efficacy of LipNaB@Sor, the fluorescent probe C6 was used as a substitute for sorafenib in the preparation of Lip@C6 (10 μg/mL), Lip@C6 with 1 % NaB, and Lip@C6 with 10 % NaB (molar ratios of C6: NaB = 1:10 and 1:100). After incubation with cancer cells for 3 and 6 h, the fluorescence intensity of C6 was measured using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri™ C6, USA). Representative fluorescence images were acquired with the Broadband Leica TCS SP5 and the Leica LAS AF application (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Biodistribution of LipNaB@Sor in vivo

To evaluate the tumor-targeting ability and organ distribution of LipNaB@Sor in vivo, the fluorescent probe 1,1′-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) was utilized in place of sorafenib for the production of Lip@DiR, LipNaB@DiR, and LipNaB@Sor/DiR at a concentration of 0.5 % (molar ratios of DiR: NaB = 1:1000). Following intravenous injection of the liposomes, the fluorescence intensity of DiR in the tumor was measured at 4, 8, and 24 h, with representative images captured using the AniView100 imaging system (Biolight Biotechnology Co., Guangzhou, China).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

For immunohistochemistry, representative tissue sections from human CRC and mouse tumor xenografts were collected. OASL (ABclonal, A24529), IRF2 (Selleck, A5374), GPX4 (Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig), SLC7A11 (MCE, HY-P80935), and Ki-67 were stained with 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (brown chromogen). Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The Immunoreactive Score was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity by the proportion of positive cells, with scores as follows: 0 (negative staining or 0–5 %), 1 (weak staining or 6–25 %), 2 (moderate staining or 26–50 %), 3 (strong staining or 51–75 %), and 4 (greater than 75 %).

For immunofluorescence staining, the fixation-permeabilization-washing-blocking process was conducted after 24 or 48 h of liposome incubation. For membrane staining (e.g., ecto-calreticulin [ecto-CRT]), permeabilization was omitted. In other cases, cells were permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100 solution for 15 min. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CRT (Proteintech, 10292-1-AP), HMGB1 (MCE, HY-P80183), γH2AX (Abcam, ab81299), OASL (Abcam, ab229136), and cGAS (Abcam, ab302617). Secondary antibodies, either Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500) (Proteintech, SA00013-2) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500) (Proteintech, SA00013-4), were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Subcutaneous tumors were stained with primary antibodies against CRT (Proteintech, 10292-1-AP), HMGB1 (MCE, HY-P80183), and P-IRF3 (CST, YP0326), with nuclei stained with DAPI for 15 min. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) to evaluate the biological safety of LipNaB@Sor. All slides were visualized and photo-documented using the Broadband Leica TCS SP5 and Leica LAS AF application (Leica Microsystems). Relative semiquantitative analyses were performed using ImageJ software. Details of the primary antibodies can be found in Supplementary Table S4.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Hiscript® III Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Vazyme). The relative expression levels of target genes were quantified via RT-qPCR, normalized to the control gene GAPDH. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S5.

RNA interference

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime) containing complete protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Whole cell lysates were boiled in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample loading buffer, loaded onto SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.2 μm pore size, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5 % milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with a primary antibody (1:1000 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. Membranes were then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Immunoblots were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200) and quantified using ImageJ software. An anti-OASL antibody (ABclonal, A24529), confirmed by the manufacturer to recognize both human OASL and its mouse homolog Oasl2, was used for immunoblotting. Antibodies utilized are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

DCs culture and maturation in vitro

Murine BMDCs were isolated from BALB/c mice following the manufacturer's protocol. RPMI 1640 complete culture media supplemented with 10 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4, PeproTech) and 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, PeproTech) was employed for BMDC culture. After 5 days, DCs were collected and co-cultured with tumor cells treated with PBS, Sor, Lip@Sor, LipNaB, and LipNaB@Sor at a ratio of 5:1 for 24 h. Flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II, USA) and FlowJo software were utilized to analyze the percentages of matured DC populations, specifically CD11c+MHCII+, CD11c+CD80+, and CD11c+CD86+. DC maturation was assessed using FIXABLE VIABILITY DYE EF660 (eBioscience, 65-0864-14), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD86 (eBioscience, 25-0862-82), PE-conjugated anti-CD11c (eBioscience, 12-0114-82), PerCP710-conjugated anti-CD80 (eBioscience, 46-0801-82), and FITC-conjugated anti-MHC-II (eBioscience, 11-5321-82).

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were obtained from subcutaneous tumors on day 14, at which point tumor volumes were comparable among the different experimental groups. Collagenase I (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma) and DNase I (1 μg/mL, Roche) were used for digestion. Leukocytes were labeled with FIXABLE VIABILITY DYE EF780 (eBioscience, 65-0865-14) to distinguish live cells from dead cells. Single-cell suspensions were incubated with Fc block (Miltenyi) and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. For intracellular staining, single-cell suspensions were fixed and permeabilized prior to the staining process. The specific antibodies used for staining are listed in Supplementary Table S4. Tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations were analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer, USA) and FlowJo software.

RNA-seq analysis

Cancer cells were treated with PBS (control), Lip@Sor, LipNaB, and LipNaB@Sor for 24 h, then total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and subjected to RNA library construction and sequencing following the standard protocol provided by Majorbio. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DESeq2 R package. KEGG pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted using the clusterProfiler R Bioconductor package.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag was performed using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit (Vazyme, TD-90) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For each CUT&Tag reaction, 100,000 cells were immobilized onto Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads and incubated overnight with 1 μg of primary antibody against IRF2. Following this incubation, bead-bound cells were treated with secondary antibodies at a 1:50 dilution. After removing unbound antibodies, pA/G-Tnp Pro was added to activate tagmentation. CUT&Tag-enriched DNA was purified for RT-qPCR and agarose gel electrophoresis. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S7.

Plasmid construction

The cDNA of Oasl2 was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pCMV3 vectors with FLAG tag.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1) and R 4.3.2. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Semiquantitative analyses of immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blots were performed using ImageJ software. Co-localization analyses of double fluorescence utilized the Pearson correlation coefficient. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using two-tailed Student's t-tests, while multiple group comparisons were assessed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival analyses were estimated using Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test. Results are indicated as follows: ns (not significant); ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001.
