Comparative effectiveness of total thyroidectomy versus hemithyroidectomy for low-risk differentiated thyroid cancer: a multicenter retrospective cohort study with propensity score matching
Authors: Zhen Ma, Ihab E. Ali, Vivian George Vincent Fernandez, Wei Wang, Jinqiang Pan, Jingliang Gu, Ziyi Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal surgical approach for patients with low-risk differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) remains controversial. Current evidence comparing long-term outcomes between total thyroidectomy and hemithyroidectomy is limited by short follow-up and a lack of comprehensive patient-reported outcomes.ObjectiveTo compare the effectiveness of total thyroidectomy versus hemithyroidectomy in patients with low-risk DTC, evaluating oncologic outcomes, complications, thyroid function, quality of life, and healthcare costs.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of 800 patients with low-risk DTC treated at three tertiary centers between 2014 and 2021, with follow-up through 2023. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed using age, sex, tumor size, histology, and BRAF V600E status. Patients were analyzed according to the operation actually performed (as-treated analysis). Primary outcomes were overall survival and recurrence-free survival. Secondary outcomes included complications, thyroid function, quality of life (HADS, SCL-90), and healthcare costs. Analyses included Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests and Cox proportional-hazards regression, with Bonferroni correction for the two primary endpoints.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 400 patients were included in each group (median follow-up 7.2 years, IQR 5.1–8.9). The 8-year overall survival rates were 97.2% (95% CI 95.6–98.8%) for total thyroidectomy versus 96.1% (95% CI 94.2–97.9%) for hemithyroidectomy (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.44–1.42, p = 0.43). The 8-year recurrence-free survival rates were 96.8% versus 94.2% (HR 1.48, 95% CI 0.73–2.98, p = 0.28). Hemithyroidectomy was associated with a significantly lower rate of permanent hypoparathyroidism (2.0% vs 8.0%, p < 0.001, NNH = 17). Total thyroidectomy entailed a universal requirement for lifelong levothyroxine (100% vs 15% requiring replacement in the hemithyroidectomy group); among hemithyroidectomy patients not requiring replacement, subclinical hypothyroidism was common. At one year, fewer hemithyroidectomy patients reported clinically significant anxiety/depression (18% vs 25%, p = 0.018). Mean total 8-year healthcare costs were lower with hemithyroidectomy (¥28,247 vs ¥55,439, p < 0.001).ConclusionsFor appropriately selected patients with low-risk DTC, hemithyroidectomy provides oncologic outcomes comparable to total thyroidectomy while offering advantages in complications, thyroid-function preservation, quality of life, and healthcare costs. Treatment decisions should be individualized through shared decision-making. These findings support consideration of de-escalated surgery but require validation in prospective randomized trials.

Introduction

Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) represents over 90% of thyroid malignancies, with incidence rates increasing worldwide owing to enhanced diagnostic capabilities and screening [1]. The age-adjusted incidence of thyroid cancer has nearly tripled over the past four decades, from 4.9 to 14.3 per 100,000 [2]. A substantial proportion of these diagnoses are low-risk DTC, defined by the American Thyroid Association (ATA) as intrathyroidal tumors ≤ 4 cm without extrathyroidal extension, lymph-node metastasis, or aggressive histological features [3].

Surgical management of low-risk DTC evolved following the 2015 ATA guidelines, which recommended hemithyroidectomy as sufficient treatment for appropriately selected patients with 1–4 cm unifocal, intrathyroidal DTC [3]. This shift from the traditional preference for total thyroidectomy was based on evidence of comparable oncologic outcomes between the two approaches for low-risk disease [4–6].

Total thyroidectomy theoretically offers complete tumor removal and facilitates postoperative radioactive-iodine therapy and thyroglobulin monitoring [7], but it necessitates lifelong thyroid-hormone replacement and carries higher risks of permanent complications, including hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury [8]. Hemithyroidectomy preserves thyroid function in many patients and reduces complication rates, although concerns remain regarding residual disease and surveillance limitations [9, 10].

Molecular diagnostics, particularly BRAF V600E analysis, have informed DTC risk stratification [11]. The BRAF V600E mutation, present in approximately 60% of papillary thyroid carcinomas, is associated with adverse clinicopathologic features and higher recurrence in some series (17.6% vs 9.6%) [12, 13]. However, its independent prognostic value is attenuated after adjustment for clinicopathologic factors, and current ATA guidelines do not recommend using BRAF status alone to determine the extent of surgery; coexisting alterations such as BRAF/TERT-promoter co-mutation carry greater prognostic weight [3, 13]. The clinical utility of BRAF for surgical decision-making therefore remains limited and should be interpreted within the broader risk profile rather than in isolation.

Despite numerous comparative studies, consensus on the optimal surgical approach remains elusive owing to methodological limitations, including short follow-up, small samples, heterogeneous populations, and inconsistent outcome measures [14–18]. Most studies have emphasized oncologic endpoints while neglecting patient-reported outcomes, quality of life, and economic considerations [19, 20]. Using propensity score matching to reduce selection bias, this multicenter retrospective cohort study comprehensively compares total thyroidectomy and hemithyroidectomy for low-risk DTC across oncologic outcomes, complications, thyroid function, quality of life, and healthcare costs over extended follow-up.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted across three tertiary referral centers: the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, and Shanghai Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. These centers were selected for their comprehensive thyroid-cancer programs, standardized surgical protocols, and high-volume thyroid-surgery practices (> 200 procedures annually).

Participants and eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) adults aged 18–75 years with pathologically confirmed low-risk DTC per the 2015 ATA guidelines (tumor diameter ≤ 4 cm; no extrathyroidal extension; no lymph-node metastasis [clinical or pathological]; no distant metastasis; no aggressive histological variants); (2) primary surgical treatment with total thyroidectomy or hemithyroidectomy; (3) complete clinical, pathological, and follow-up data; and (4) surgery performed between January 2014 and December 2021 (minimum 2-year follow-up).

Exclusion criteria: (1) previous thyroid surgery or neck irradiation; (2) concurrent malignancy; (3) previous radioactive-iodine or systemic therapy; (4) incomplete follow-up (> 2 missed key time points); (5) completion thyroidectomy after an initial hemithyroidectomy; and (6) high-risk histological variants (tall-cell, columnar-cell, hobnail). The potential bias introduced by criterion 5 is addressed in the Limitations.

Sample-size calculation

Based on pilot data and prior literature suggesting a potential 5% difference in 8-year recurrence-free survival (95% vs 90%), with 80% power, α = 0.05, and 10% loss to follow-up, the minimum required sample size was 350 patients per group. We targeted 400 per group to ensure adequate power for secondary analyses.

Surgical procedures and standardization

Procedures were performed by experienced thyroid surgeons stratified by experience (5–10 years vs > 10 years). Total thyroidectomy comprised complete bilateral thyroid removal, systematic parathyroid identification and preservation, recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring and preservation, and central-compartment lymph-node sampling when indicated [21]. Hemithyroidectomy comprised ipsilateral lobe and isthmus removal with parathyroid and recurrent laryngeal nerve preservation and frozen-section analysis when indicated [22]. Quality-assurance measures included mandatory video documentation, standardized operative reports, and regular technique audits.

Propensity score matching

To reduce selection bias, propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including age (continuous), sex, tumor size (continuous), histological type (papillary vs follicular), BRAF V600E status, surgeon experience level, and institution. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit propensity score. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. We acknowledge that several clinically relevant confounders (multifocality, preoperative TSH, thyroid-autoantibody status/Hashimoto thyroiditis, comorbidity burden, year of surgery, and baseline quality of life) were not incorporated into the matching model; their potential residual confounding is discussed in the Limitations.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes were overall survival (time from surgery to death from any cause) and recurrence-free survival (time to locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, or death from any cause). Secondary outcomes were: complications — permanent hypoparathyroidism (hypocalcemia requiring calcium/active-vitamin-D supplementation beyond 12 months, with a documented attempt at withdrawal), recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (persistent voice change > 6 months), and postoperative bleeding; thyroid function (TSH, FT4, FT3; requirement for levothyroxine replacement); quality of life using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS; 14 items; validated Chinese version, Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [23, 24] and the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90; validated Chinese version) [25, 26]; healthcare costs (surgery, hospitalization, medications, follow-up); and lifestyle modifications by standardized questionnaire.

Follow-up protocol

Standardized follow-up was performed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months and annually thereafter, including physical examination and neck ultrasonography, thyroid-function tests, serum thyroglobulin and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (total thyroidectomy group), quality-of-life questionnaires (1, 3, 5 years), and healthcare-utilization tracking. All total thyroidectomy patients received levothyroxine replacement/suppression targeting a TSH of 0.5–2.0 mIU/L in accordance with ATA risk-adapted recommendations [27].

Statistical analysis

Analyses used SPSS 28.0 and R 4.3.0. The primary analysis was performed according to the operation actually performed (as-treated analysis); a per-protocol sensitivity analysis examined the influence of treatment crossover. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) according to distribution (Shapiro–Wilk), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Survival analyses used Kaplan–Meier estimation, log-rank tests, and Cox proportional-hazards regression; the proportional-hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and log–log plots. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction (α = 0.025 for the two primary endpoints). Sensitivity analyses included per-protocol analysis, competing-risk analysis, inverse probability weighting, and stratified analyses by tumor size, age, and institution. Missing data were handled with multiple imputation (m = 5) under a missing-at-random assumption.

Ethics and regulatory approval

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review boards of the three participating centers: the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Protocol No. EX1682022; 15 January 2022); the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Protocol No. EX1682022-A; 10 February 2022); and the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Protocol No. 2022-SH-01; 5 March 2022). The need for individual informed consent to participate was waived by each of these institutional review boards because of the retrospective design, the use of fully de-identified data, and the minimal risk to participants, in accordance with the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Humans (China, 2023) and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Follow-up and loss to follow-up

The median follow-up was 7.2 years (IQR 5.1–8.9; range 2.0–9.8). Overall, 89 patients (11.1%) were lost to follow-up, with no significant difference between groups (10.8% vs 11.5%, p = 0.74). The median time to loss of follow-up was 5.8 years.

Primary outcomes

Overall survival

During the study period, 28 deaths occurred (14 in each group). The 8-year overall survival rates were 97.2% (95% CI 95.6–98.8%) for total thyroidectomy versus 96.1% (95% CI 94.2–97.9%) for hemithyroidectomy. The hazard ratio for death in the hemithyroidectomy group was 0.79 (95% CI 0.44–1.42, p = 0.43), indicating no significant difference (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Kaplan–Meier curves for overall survival. Secondary outcomes

Complications

Postoperative complications are summarized in Table 3 and Fig. 6. Permanent hypoparathyroidism occurred in 32/400 (8.0%) total thyroidectomy versus 8/400 (2.0%) hemithyroidectomy patients (RR 4.00, 95% CI 1.87–8.56, p < 0.001; number needed to harm [NNH] 17, 95% CI 12–28). Under our relatively strict definition (calcium/active-vitamin-D dependence beyond 12 months with a documented withdrawal attempt), the 8.0% rate lies at the higher end of the published range; meta-analyses report 0–3% at high-volume centers and up to 9–14% in population-based registries [28, 29]. Complication rates were inversely correlated with surgeon experience in both groups: surgeons with > 10 years’ experience had significantly lower permanent hypoparathyroidism (3.5% vs 8.2%, p = 0.007) and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (2.1% vs 5.7%, p = 0.011) than those with 5–10 years’ experience.

Thyroid function

Thyroid-function outcomes are presented in Table 4 and Fig. 7. By design, all total thyroidectomy patients were rendered athyreotic and required lifelong levothyroxine (100%); with replacement targeting a TSH of 0.5–2.0 mIU/L, these patients were biochemically euthyroid throughout follow-up. In the hemithyroidectomy group, 60/400 (15.0%) developed overt hypothyroidism requiring levothyroxine by 12 months (median time to onset 8.5 months, IQR 3.2–14.7). Notably, the elevated group-mean TSH in the hemithyroidectomy group (Table 4) indicates that subclinical hypothyroidism (isolated TSH elevation without overt biochemical hypothyroidism) was frequent and is consistent with pooled meta-analytic estimates of post-hemithyroidectomy hypothyroidism of approximately 22–29%, which are higher in Asian populations [30]. Quality of life and economic analysis

Discussion

In this multicenter, propensity-matched cohort of patients with low-risk DTC, hemithyroidectomy achieved oncologic outcomes comparable to total thyroidectomy while conferring advantages in complications, thyroid-function preservation, quality of life, and healthcare costs. We summarize these findings in context below.

Oncologic outcomes in context

The comparable 8-year overall survival (97.2% vs 96.1%) and recurrence-free survival (96.8% vs 94.2%) are consistent with large cohorts and meta-analyses showing that surgical extent does not significantly affect survival in low-risk thyroid cancer [4, 5, 16, 31–33]. Although hemithyroidectomy showed a numerically higher recurrence hazard (HR 1.48), this did not reach statistical or clinical significance, and competing-risk analysis confirmed no difference in disease-specific mortality [34]. Age ≥ 55 years and tumor size > 2.5 cm were associated with worse outcomes, consistent with established prognostic factors; the association of BRAF V600E with overall survival should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events, and aligns with the view that BRAF status alone is insufficient to guide the extent of surgery [3, 12, 13].

Complications, thyroid function and patient-reported outcomes

Hemithyroidectomy reduced permanent hypoparathyroidism four-fold (2.0% vs 8.0%; NNH 17). Permanent hypoparathyroidism substantially impairs quality of life and entails lifelong calcium management [28, 35]. Our 8.0% rate is higher than the 0–3% reported at the most experienced centers and reflects our strict definition and routine central-compartment exploration; the markedly lower rate among the most experienced surgeons (3.5%) underscores the influence of surgical expertise and volume on outcomes [29, 36]. The difference in recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (5.0% vs 3.0%) was not significant. As expected, total thyroidectomy mandated lifelong levothyroxine in all patients, whereas 15% of hemithyroidectomy patients developed overt hypothyroidism requiring replacement; subclinical hypothyroidism was nonetheless common, consistent with the literature [30]. Hemithyroidectomy patients reported less anxiety/depression at one year, and hormone dependency was an independent predictor of psychological distress, although baseline quality of life was not matched and residual confounding cannot be excluded [37]. These patient-reported differences reinforce the value of incorporating quality of life into surgical decisions for a disease with excellent survival [31, 38].

Economic and molecular considerations

The 8-year cost difference of ¥27,192 (approximately US$4,200) mainly reflects hormone-replacement and monitoring expenses and is increasingly relevant in resource-limited settings [39, 40]. Beyond BRAF V600E, molecular markers including RAS mutations, RET/PTC rearrangements, and TERT-promoter co-mutations are increasingly integrated into risk stratification and may refine individualized decisions [11–13, 41]. Different approaches require tailored surveillance: total thyroidectomy permits thyroglobulin monitoring, whereas hemithyroidectomy relies primarily on neck ultrasonography [42]. These considerations support a shared decision-making framework rather than a single default operation [43].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a relatively large, propensity-matched sample, long-term follow-up, comprehensive outcomes including patient-reported measures, multiple concordant sensitivity analyses, and standardized surgical techniques. Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, the retrospective design carries inherent selection bias despite matching, and the propensity model omitted clinically relevant confounders — multifocality, preoperative TSH, thyroid-autoantibody status/Hashimoto thyroiditis, comorbidity burden, year of surgery, and baseline quality of life — so residual confounding may persist. Second, patients who underwent completion thyroidectomy after an initial hemithyroidectomy were excluded from the as-treated cohort; because such patients are typically those upstaged on final pathology, their exclusion may bias outcomes in favour of hemithyroidectomy. Third, thyroid-function testing at fixed intervals may have underestimated subclinical hypothyroidism in the hemithyroidectomy group. Fourth, quality-of-life and economic analyses used surrogate measures and a single national cost framework, limiting generalizability. Fifth, the overall-survival multivariable model was underpowered (28 events), so its estimates are hypothesis-generating. Finally, multiple imputation assumptions may not hold for all variables. Prospective, adequately powered randomized trials with standardized molecular risk stratification and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

For carefully selected patients with low-risk DTC, hemithyroidectomy provides oncologic outcomes comparable to total thyroidectomy while offering advantages in complications, thyroid-function preservation, quality of life, and healthcare costs. Treatment decisions should remain individualized, integrating tumor characteristics, patient preferences, surgical expertise, and institutional capability through shared decision-making. These findings support hemithyroidectomy as a reasonable first-line option for appropriate patients but require validation in prospective randomized trials before they can inform definitive recommendations.
