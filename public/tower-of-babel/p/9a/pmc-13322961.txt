Exploring quality and patient safety competencies and competency frameworks: a scoping review
Authors: Dimuthu Rathnayake, Sahar Hammoud, Veronica Hanlon, Louise Hendrick, Mary Browne, Gemma Moore, John Fitzsimons, Aoife De Brún, Roisin O’Malley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSustaining quality and patient safety (QPS) is a global priority due to risks such as patient harm, poor experiences, inequity, and resource waste. Training and education in key competency areas can support continuous improvement and enhance workforce performance. Clearly defining core QPS competencies for healthcare staff is essential to ensure preparedness to provide quality, safe care. Competency frameworks provide structured support for training and curriculum development, with many developed globally as strategic initiatives, some using evidence-based approaches. However, there is no consensus on core competencies to promote quality and patient safety, definitions of relevant terms can vary, and evidence on framework effectiveness is mixed. To address this research gap, the objective of this review was to systematically explore and collate evidence to provide insight into the current landscape of QPS competencies and frameworks. Additionally, this review sought to identify how competencies and competency frameworks have been designed, developed, implemented and evaluated.MethodsWe searched for studies on QPS competencies and frameworks in PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Cochrane Library from Jan 2010 to April 2023. We also conducted grey literature searches on Google Scholar, Opengrey, and the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE). All studies that described QPS competencies and/or frameworks were included. Since the studies varied in type, a narrative approach was adopted to synthesize the included studies.ResultsOut of the initial 14 144 studies found, 118 were deemed relevant after thoroughly reviewing abstracts and full texts. Of these, 36 studies outlined specific QPS competency frameworks, while the remaining 82 studies assessed various QPS competencies across diverse contexts. Upon analysing data from all 118 studies, we identified 37 QPS topic areas and a variety of competency statements encompassing skills, knowledge, and behaviours deemed relevant for promoting QPS. The most frequent QPS topic areas included communication, patient safety culture, teamwork and collaboration, risk monitoring and management and patient-centred care.ConclusionThe review has highlighted key insights into education and training related to QPS competencies. Core competency topic areas have been identified that are essential for workforce development and the promotion of quality and patient safety in healthcare settings. However, there is a gap in research on strategies to effectively implement and integrate QPS frameworks into practice, leading to insufficient evidence on translating these competencies into real-world application.

Introduction

Healthcare systems are inherently complex and continuously evolving. Sustaining quality and patient safety (QPS) in this environment has emerged as a global health priority. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), one in ten patients experiences an adverse event while receiving hospital treatments in high income countries [1]. In a rapidly changing healthcare landscape, it is essential to maintain a strong focus on ensuring continuous quality and safety improvement in healthcare delivery, regardless of ongoing reforms, disasters, or workforce challenges. To ensure that all healthcare staff feel prepared and empowered to adopt an active role in providing better quality and safer care, the need to clearly articulate the key competencies required of all healthcare staff has been advocated [2]. While healthcare organizations have been making continuous efforts to optimize QPS in their organizations and workforce [3, 4], healthcare staff still demonstrate considerable variation in professional performance and engagement in QPS [5–8]. Greater consistency and clarity are needed across professional disciplines and health training programmes regarding the knowledge, skills, and behaviours essential for fostering a strong QPS culture across all healthcare settings.

Education plays a pivotal role in enhancing QPS through the delivery of training. Accordingly, along with other essential requirements, including technical competencies relevant to specific professional groups, competencies relevant to QPS have been incorporated into training programmes globally. A variety of educational opportunities have been developed to train healthcare staff to develop their skills to support QPS in practice [3, 8, 9], including in Higher and Further Education settings, with efforts to integrate QPS modules into undergraduate and post-graduate curricula [10, 11]. Achieving this through training and education in core, agreed QPS areas of competence can improve the QPS capability of healthcare staff and promote cultures of continuous improvement. This has been advocated to build an agile and resilient health systems workforce that can withstand future challenges [11].

Competency frameworks are developed and organized to provide both theoretical and practical guidance for training and curriculum development, helping to foster the competencies needed for practice. Internationally, there have been many efforts to develop competency frameworks for patient safety and/or quality [12–15] implemented as strategic patient safety and quality initiatives at various systems levels, and some have been rigorously designed and developed using evidence-based approaches [16–18]. Yet, very few frameworks have integrated both quality and patient safety [13, 19, 20]. Quality and patient safety are interdependent dimensions of healthcare practice, where safety is considered a key dimension of quality. Combining them supports a more holistic approach to professional education, ensuring that learners develop the knowledge, skills, and attitudes needed to improve care processes, reduce risk, prevent harm, and enhance patient outcomes within complex healthcare systems [3, 11]. Furthermore, the extent to which competency frameworks developed in one jurisdiction can be effectively adapted to different national and local contexts remains unclear. Context-specific factors such as the working environment and institutional policies influence the consistent improvement of quality and patient safety within healthcare systems and services [21, 22]. Finally, there is a lack of consensus regarding the requisite core competencies for staff [21], variability in definitions of key aspects of QPS and mixed evidence regarding whether these competency frameworks are achieving their stated outcomes and impact. Given this heterogeneity, the aim of this review is to map the existing QPS competencies and competency frameworks relating to QPS. Further, this review sought to collate approaches to designing, developing, implementing, and evaluating of QPS competency frameworks.

Materials and methods

A scoping review [23] was chosen as the methodology for this review because the topic of QPS competencies and competency frameworks has not been consistently reported or extensively reviewed. The literature on this subject was considered likely to be heterogeneous in nature, in terms of definitions and listings, as indicated by initial literature searches. To ensure methodological rigour, this scoping review was conducted in accordance with the updated guidance outlined in the JBI Manual of Evidence Synthesis [24]. The review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework [25].

Search strategy

To identify key literature relevant to QPS competencies, we considered any QPS competency-based approaches and QPS competency frameworks that related to patient safety and quality relevant to the healthcare setting. Searches were conducted in five academic databases in April 2023: PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane library. Two search themes were specified and combined with the Boolean operator ‘AND’ for the final electronic search (‘patient safety’ OR ‘safety’ OR ‘quality’ OR ‘human factors’ OR ‘clinical audit’) AND (‘competenc*’ OR ‘capability’ OR ‘framework’ OR ‘strateg*’ OR ‘polic*’). To enhance our review, we also conducted additional grey literature searches in Google, OpenGrey, and the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) and scanned bibliographies of included studies manually.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all relevant studies, reports, and policy documents, irrespective of the study methods, including qualitative and/or quantitative or review studies published during the period 2010 to April 2023. We limited the search to the most recent publications to ensure the most up to date evidence and competency frameworks. Recognizing that some frameworks may have been updated over time, we were interested in the most recent versions. There was no limitation on the types of healthcare settings or participant group, once the population were working in a healthcare setting. Frameworks and competencies aimed at all clinical or non-clinical healthcare staff, as well as patients and families were considered. Articles were included if they reported QPS competencies and/or competency frameworks, and if they described approaches to the design, development, implementation, or evaluation of QPS competency frameworks.

Study screening

The screening process was conducted using Covidence data management software. In the first step, title and abstract screening was independently undertaken by two reviewers (DR and SH). In the second step, full-text screening was completed independently by the same reviewers. Any disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (ADB).

Data extraction and synthesis

The first author (DR) extracted data from all included studies, and SH performed random checks on 20% of records to verify data extraction. For all included studies, data were extracted related to the title, target population(s), defined levels of competence, competencies, and competency overarching domains, development process of lists/framework, as well as details related to implementation, monitoring, and evaluation details of the competencies/framework. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with the final author (ADB).

Given the broad scope of the review, variability in study types and methods was anticipated. Accordingly, a narrative synthesis [23] of the included studies was undertaken. The synthesis focused on identifying explicit competency statements through identifying common topic areas related to quality and patient safety (e.g. communication) and collating the relevant competency statements under that topic (i.e. the actionable descriptions of what healthcare professionals should know, do or be able to demonstrate), and examining approaches used to design, develop, implement, and evaluate these QPS competency frameworks. The analysis also calculated the frequencies of these topic areas so as to establish those most prominent in the literature on competency frameworks.

Results

The searches across five academic databases yielded a total of 16 645 (Fig. 1). An additional 25 records were identified through grey literature searches and citation searching. After removing duplicates supported by Covidence software, 14 144 records remained, and these were screened against inclusion/exclusion criteria. Following title and abstract screening, 290 articles were retained for full text screening and, ultimately, 118 articles met the criteria for inclusion in the scoping review.

QPS competencies and frameworks

Table 1 provides a summary of the studies that were included in the review. Of the 118 articles, 103 were peer reviewed journal articles. There was increasing trend of publications for the topic over the recent years (47 studies from 2020 to 2023). Thirty-six of the articles had explicit QPS competency frameworks/models (as listed in Supplementary File S1), while the remaining 82 studies focused on assessing and evaluating QPS competencies across various settings (as listed in Supplementary File S1). These frameworks were presented in various formats, including as figures, tables, reports and research articles. More than 60% of the presented frameworks (n = 22) aimed to enhance the QPS competencies of all healthcare staff categories universally. Generic QPS competencies that are fundamental to all healthcare staff (regardless of discipline and role) featured in 47 of the articles. Some articles presented QPS competencies that were specific to different staff and student categories, with most of them focusing on QPS competencies for nursing students (n = 30). Other frameworks tailored to specific professional groups included intensive care nurses, medical officers and laboratory practitioners.

Design, development, and implementation of competency frameworks

The results concerning the approaches adopted to designing, developing, implementing, and evaluating QPS competency frameworks are presented in Supplementary File S3. A targeted literature review was reported in 17 articles at the inception of the framework development. Some studies utilized qualitative discussions, while some used Delphi methods to achieve expert consensus on the competency framework content [14, 26–28]. Many of the listed QPS frameworks were developed based on literature reviews followed by expert opinion and input [27, 29].

There was significant variation in the conduct and reporting of the competency framework development process [30]. Only seven articles discussed their implementation approaches, and five reported multiple evaluations conducted on the framework. Canada [14], Australia [31], New Zealand [29] and the UK [15] have implemented patient safety competency frameworks as part of their national strategic approaches to improving healthcare quality and safety. Several studies assessed QPS competencies using various instruments and tools to explore their effectiveness in supporting competency development throughout the training period (see Supplementary Table S1) [32–35]. The most frequently used tool to evaluate the impact of the implementation of competency frameworks in the selected articles was the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey (H-PEPSS) [36]. As presented in Table 3, five studies provided information on competency statements proportionate to different levels of proficiency to provide an example of how some frameworks were structured to support learning and competency development. Other frameworks defined core competencies based on the requirements of various professional roles/staff categories, rather than having levels of proficiency.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

The primary aim of this study was to collate QPS competencies and competency frameworks and to understand how they have been deployed and evaluated in health systems to develop the QPS performance of healthcare staff over time. The findings provided evidence of the most frequently incorporated QPS competencies and offers some, albeit limited, insight into how these have been developed and deployed. Sufficient capacity and capability in QPS at entry into professional practice was a primary concern in all 118 included articles. The most frequently reported topic areas were communication, patient safety culture, teamwork and collaboration, risk monitoring and management and patient-centred care.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review contributes evolving body of literature to the QPS competencies and competency frameworks by collating the relevant literature on this topic and helping to identify gaps in the literature and areas for future empirical work. The strength of the method lies in its ability to cover a broader scope of QPS competencies and frameworks. This has enabled the identification of the most frequent topics areas in published QPS competency research. However, our approach also has limitations. While we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview, we were not able to delve into in-depth relationships regarding the outcomes of QPS frameworks and competency development in the health workforce. Additionally, while we were interested in the most recent QPS frameworks and competencies in order to reflect more recent developments in QPS research, relevant studies may not be included due to the selected time range for the study (2010–2023). Similarly, the search tried to balance the sensitive and specificity to order to find the most relevant papers; there is a risk that some relevant papers may have been missed. However, we tried to mitigate this though citation searching of included studies.

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

Of the thirty-six articles using explicit QPS-related competency frameworks, a majority of these (60%) aimed to enhance the QPS competencies of all healthcare staff categories universally. For instance, some frameworks were titled as interprofessional QPS competency frameworks and were described as applicable to all staff categories and professional roles [37, 40]. This indicates that many of the QPS competencies frequently cited in the extant literature are not specified for a single professional category, but rather generic in nature and widely relevant across health systems. Non-technical skills, such as interpersonal communication and situational awareness, featured in many QPS frameworks, emphasizing the importance of interprofessional competencies related to topics including communication, teamwork and collaboration in modern healthcare settings. This perhaps reflects the increased recognition and acknowledgement of the importance of human factors in healthcare education and in promoting quality and safe care in practice in recent years [41]. Given that multidisciplinary and interdisciplinary team-based care is becoming the norm in modern healthcare delivery, education and training opportunities to enhance these skills have been strongly advocated. A recent systematic review highlights the strong relationship between interprofessional education interventions and key quality outcomes, including length of stay, patient safety incidents and patient satisfaction [42]. Thus, the development of shared QPS competencies in healthcare team members is associated with enhanced quality and safety of care.

A variety of topics under the umbrella of QPS were identified in the included studies, and often there was variation in the language and definitions used across frameworks. The dataset revealed a list of 37 QPS topic areas, with competencies related to communication, patient safety culture, teamwork and collaboration, risk identification, monitoring, preventing and management, and patient-centred care being the most frequently listed topic areas. These competencies have been consistently presented in many articles [6, 17, 35, 36, 43–45], indicating their importance to the safe and effective practice of healthcare.

The primary aim of introducing QPS frameworks in most of the settings was to enhance the outcomes of educational programmes and facilitate improvements to curricula. However, many studies lacked evidence supporting the mechanisms through which frameworks can best translate relevant QPS knowledge, skills, behaviours and attitudes into practice. We also observed significant variation in the conduct and reporting of the competency framework development process. Many of the listed QPS frameworks were developed based on literature reviews followed by expert opinion and input. In addition to literature reviews, some frameworks were developed using qualitative research methods such as focus group discussions, and Delphi rounds to consult subject expertise, policy makers and healthcare staff to obtain systematic consensus. The use of these robust research designs has contributed to the development of evidence-based, expert-informed competencies and topic areas, that, arguably, aim to be more relevant and feasible to implement in health systems, as QPS competency development requires reflective, participatory learning experiences [46, 47]. Notably, only one study reported using co-design methods [48], reflecting a more engaged research process or participatory action research approach embedded in the methodology [49, 50]. Given the underuse of co-design approaches in the development of competency frameworks to date, we argue that this should be strongly considered in planning future competency frameworks that are sustainable, align to the real-world needs, and that are perceived as practical and relevant to intended end users.

Several post-intervention evaluations were carried out using validated instruments, with a few showing favourable results [16, 51]. The most used tool in the selected articles was the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey (H-PEPSS) [36]. The current evidence on the QPS framework lacks certainty as an intervention, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions about its effectiveness in improving QPS performance [37]. Despite the common presentation of these competencies, there is a need for evidence-based actionable insights on how exactly the QPS competency approach can improve QPS in healthcare and under what conditions. Rigorous research designs and systematic approaches are invaluable in identifying and harnessing learning related to effective and ineffective implementation strategies [52]. It is imperative to recognize the significance of using systematic and well-designed methodologies to gain comprehensive insights into the development and deployment of QPS competency frameworks in healthcare settings.

Implications for research, policy and practice

Integrating QPS competencies and frameworks into educational pathways offers both opportunities and challenges. Successful integration requires collaboration among key stakeholders. At the organizational and policy level, adopting QPS competency frameworks affects workforce development and continuing professional development, helping to create a shared understanding of safety and quality across healthcare roles. However, differences in healthcare contexts mean that QPS frameworks need to be adaptable and tailored to remain relevant across disciplines, settings, and resource levels. Further research is essential to guide the selection of the most effective QPS competencies for practical education and to establish strong evidence regarding the effectiveness of these competency frameworks. Continuously refining and exploring QPS frameworks is necessary for enhancing the competency, capabilities and performance of healthcare staff, which ultimately leads to improved patient care and outcomes.

Conclusions

Through the examination of identified competencies and competency frameworks, this analysis has provided valuable insights into the education and training of QPS by mapping competencies and competency areas. This process has allowed us to identify and link the requisite skills, knowledge, and behaviours required for professional development to support and promote QPS in practice. Developing Quality and Patient Safety (QPS) competencies, through the adoption of competency frameworks, can facilitate their integration into both undergraduate and postgraduate educational programmes and training initiatives and support learners to become competent in skills relevant to quality and patient safety. However, most studies have focused on improving educational programmes and curricula, with little emphasis placed on exploring how to implement and integrate QPS frameworks into diverse contexts. As a result, there is a paucity of evidence to support the mechanisms through which QPS frameworks translates relevant competencies into practice settings.
