Towards a holistic framework: a scoping review protocol to map early childhood development services
Authors: Mehrdad Amir-Behghadami, Jafar-Sadegh Tabrizi, Seifollah Heidarabady, Mohammad Barzegar, Alireza Nikniaz, Saber Azami-Aghdash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionProgrammes based on early childhood development (ECD) services play a vital role in improving child health and developmental outcomes. Across many countries, these programmes target children under 5 years of age—including those who are healthy, at risk or with developmental disorders—and are implemented in governmental and non-governmental organisations in both the health and non-health sectors globally. This protocol outlines a scoping review designed to systematically map and synthesise existing evidence on the components, implementation strategies and delivery mechanisms of ECD services worldwide. The review aims to inform the development of optimised and comprehensive interventions that support holistic child development.Methods and analysisThis review will be conducted by Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and its recent advances. Several databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Science Direct and Web of Science, Scientific Information Database, Magiran, IranMedex and Barakat knowledge network system, will be initially searched for studies up to December 2024. All searches will be done for published or unpublished articles/reports without time and language restrictions. Two researchers will independently carry out screening of the included studies and extraction of data. Any discrepancies will be resolved by consensus. In case no initial consensus is reached, a third researcher will be consulted to make a decision. The findings will be synthesised through the content-analysis method.Ethics and disseminationThe findings of this review may offer a novel perspective for developing a comprehensive package of ECD services within the Iranian health system. Furthermore, it provides a detailed roadmap for researchers and stakeholders aiming to enhance child health and prevent developmental disorders. This study received ethical approval from Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (ID: IR.TBZMED.REC.1404.139), with no ethical concerns as it involves no patient participation or interventions.Keywords: Community child health, Health Services, Child

Introduction

Children constitute the foundation of human development.1 Early childhood development (ECD), encompassing the first 5 years of life, is critical for lifelong health and well-being.2 Scientific advances, including neuroscience, have reinforced the importance of prioritising ECD investment due to its high returns in human development.3 During economic evaluations, it is also known as the cost of the most effective stage of life for investing in the direction of reaching the goals of human development.4 In addition, it is the fastest growing period and the period in which the developing brain is most sensitive to stimulation and nurturing.5 Every experience during this period affects the whole life of individuals as well as society.6 7 The bright future belongs to nations that invest in children, because child development is one of the most important components of health throughout life.7

Development is prone to a series of variations that a human being achieves during life in order to develop physically, mentally, verbally and socially.8 Such variations are influenced by various factors, including hereditary genetic factors, as well as environmental factors, nutrition and social stimuli.8 Since the foundation of the future life of every person depends on the experiences of the early years of life, then insufficient care during this period will lead to a delay in the growth and development of the child.9 Development criteria can be delayed from birth or reduced after a relatively normal period.10 If children’s needs and developmental domains are properly addressed during this period, they will be healthier and have better thinking and reasoning abilities and social and emotional skills.11 12

Approximately 250 million children in low-income and middle-income countries are at risk of suboptimal development, with prevalence increasing in certain regions such as the Middle East.13 14 Despite some improvements, developmental delays remain a significant global health challenge requiring urgent policy attention.2 The prevalence of developmental disorders varies worldwide and accounts for significant numbers even in developed countries.7 A significant proportion of children (13%–17%) suffer from developmental disorders15 and only 16%–18% of children with speech disorders, mental retardation, learning disabilities and behavioural disorders are identified before starting school.16 17 Its prevalence has been reported as 18.7%–19.8% in several cities of Iran.18,20 All this evidence based on valid findings emphasise the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment in order to prevent developmental delay in children.21

Recent decades have seen significant advances in the care and treatment of high-risk infants worldwide.22 Different national and international studies highlight that the implementation of follow-up programmes for high-risk infants and toddlers can play a significant role. It can be mentioned that it leads to the rapid diagnosis of developmental delays and disorders and the beginning of early intervention. In addition, it will help to reduce complications and prevent the continuation of developmental disorders, avoid wasting time and money, reduce future care costs and increase their productivity as members of society.23 24 ECD is often used to describe a wide range of programmes that pursue a common end goal of realising children’s potential capacities for optimal development. These programmes may take place in different levels of exposure (child, family and society) and among different levels (primary, secondary, tertiary healthcare).12

A review of the published literature clearly emphasises that ECD programmes can have positive effects on child development. In Bangladesh, developmental programmes for 3, 4 and 5-year-old children are conducted by various non-governmental organisations. Most of them offer a half-day programme 5–6 days a week with play classes, education and hand washing in cooperation with parents. Outcomes measured in terms of parenting and nutrition are generally not positive, except for parental knowledge presented in Shishu Bikash Kendro for 3–4-year-old children.18 25 However, child outcomes in terms of school readiness, vocabulary and analytical reasoning will be more positive when the programme is of relatively better quality.18 19 25 26 There are few ‘comprehensive’ programmes in Bangladesh that focus on all children’s needs, including cognitive and psychosocial development, health, nutrition and hygiene. The experiences of other countries show that such programmes can be useful by maintaining quality standards in the provision of child development services, especially for children who have a number of other underlying diseases.20 26 The Chicago Parent Child Center and Development Program (CPC) is a recognised example of a comprehensive early childhood intervention that provides educational and family support services to economically disadvantaged children and their parents. Findings indicate that participation in CPCs increases children’s cognitive and language development. Also, the participation of parents in this programme helps to foster the long-term developmental achievements of children.21 27

The Iranian health system offers a potentially strong platform for integrating ECD services; however, gaps remain with no universal system currently established. This underscores the need to synthesise global evidence on developmental care packages to inform national service design.28 29 Iran has a relatively complete health system, which is mainly organised at three levels and can be an important platform for providing ECD services to families with children (figure 1).

Structure of the Iranian health system.

At the first level, public health and primary care services are provided through the nationwide primary healthcare (PHC) network. The network has similar organisational structures and functions and provides PHC services for children in the coverage areas, including vaccinations, regular physical examinations and screening, diagnosis and treatment of common diseases. The secondary level of an independent regional health authority includes the regional health network. Regional public hospitals and specialised polyclinics are located at this level. Iran also has a developed and active private health sector, which is mainly concentrated in urban areas and plays a major role in providing secondary and tertiary care.29 30 However, these developmental services currently are not fully integrated nor comprehensive. Hence, there is a need to prepare a comprehensive ECD services package based on the existing level structure to improve the provision of these services to children.

A sustainable, wide-coverage and cost-effective delivery method can be the integration of interventions into existing service systems.31 As Shah et al stated that PHC facilities can be the closest location to the population covered in the successful implementation of ECD interventions due to their established infrastructure, non-stigmatising location and frequent, well-attended clinic visits during children’s first 3 years of life.32 However, Iran has not yet established a universal ECD service system that covers urban and rural areas. Some ECD programmes have been implemented in poor rural areas, but they are mainly funded by non-governmental organisations with unstable and limited coverage and are not properly structured according to the level of service.

Although many efforts have been made recently to improve children’s health in Iran, executives and experts believe that Iran’s current situation is facing many challenges in terms of children’s natural development and related activities.33 The mentioned problems prevent children from enjoying their rights. One of the most important challenges is the lack of a holistic and comprehensive view of children’s development and the low level of social awareness. In addition, another major problem that hinders childhood development programmes in Iran is the lack of a national programme for this age range, as well as implementation defects that have not been addressed in any of the previous development programmes. Therefore, government organisations did not have plans and instructions in this field and do not currently have them. However, with the cooperation of several ministries in Iran, including the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Education and the Social Welfare Organization, they presented an initial proposal to compile a national document for Integrated Early Childhood Development (IECD) in 2008 (27).33 34 However, this document has not been implemented at the national level after several years and is still viewed as an experimental plan. As a result, the coherence and integration between the policies and programmes of the main players will be questioned, and there will be parallelism and sometimes conflict between these policies. To continue this activity, it is of great importance to sensitise legislative and policy-making institutions to support and officially implement IECD programmes (27).33

In addition to describing Iran’s health system and its approach to providing developmental services for children under five, this review will examine the efforts and programmes implemented by the regional offices of the six WHO regions. These WHO regional offices have developed diverse ECD service programmes and interventions tailored to their regional needs and contexts. For example, the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region advocates scaling up evidence-based, cost-effective ECD interventions integrated within existing child health programmes. Meanwhile, the WHO European Region has developed comprehensive programme and assessment tools aimed at supporting optimal child development through multisectoral collaborations across health, education and social services. By synthesising the actions taken by the WHO regional offices alongside Iran’s experiences, this review aims to identify common components, strategies and gaps in delivering developmental care services globally. Such comparative analysis will provide valuable insights for designing an integrated and comprehensive service package based on the service-level system suitable for children’s developmental needs in diverse settings.

This scoping review aims to comprehensively map and synthesise existing ECD services for children under 5 years—including healthy children, those at developmental risk and those with developmental disorders—focusing on their components, implementation and delivery of developmental services within a holistic framework, addressing the current gap as no review study has yet been conducted on this topic.

Methods and analysis

Review method

The summary of this protocol has been reported by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement.35 36 The proposed scoping review will be reported by the reporting guidance provided in the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).37 It was not possible to register the protocol of this review in PROSPERO because this is a scoping review.

This study was designed based on the methodological framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley,38 the methodology described in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewers’ Manual, which other authors have further refined.39,41 A scoping review is designed to examine the extent, scope and nature of research activity on a topic of interest and to identify research gaps in the available literature. The framework consists of six sequential stages in the process of conducting an excellent scoping review: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) data charting, (5) data analysis and reporting the results and (6) consultation exercise.38

Stage 1: identifying the research question

The main research question/phenomenon of the current review will be ‘What is known about the provision of ECD services across different regions of the world, and what interventions, programmes and service packages have been designed and implemented to address the developmental needs of children under 5 years of age—including those who are healthy, at risk, or with developmental disorders?’ which specifically includes the following questions:

Stage 2: identification of relevant studies

An extensive search will be conducted across national and international electronic databases including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Science Direct and Web of Science, Scientific Information Database, Magiran, IranMedex and Barakat knowledge network system. The search strategy aims to locate published and unpublished studies and will be carried out by an experienced researcher familiar with these databases.

Additionally, official websites will be searched for grey literature, including OpenGrey.eu, WHO.int and Google Scholar. Clinical trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov, the International Standard Randomized Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN), the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform and the UK Clinical Research Network Study Portfolio will also be included. There will be no language or date restrictions on the search up to the end of 2024, with translation assistance sought as needed. Furthermore, prior to final publication, the search will be updated once more. Reference lists and citations of included studies will be reviewed to identify any additional relevant evidence missed during the database searches.

The search strategy will be developed based on our research question to maintain focus and scope. An initial scoping search will be conducted in the Medline database to identify relevant keywords, subject headings and terms by reviewing benchmark articles, titles, abstracts, dictionaries and synonyms. Following this, the search strategy—including keywords and indexing terms—will be finalised and approved by the research team. The strategy will then be adapted with minor modifications for other databases. Boolean operators and proximity commands will be used to combine and refine search terms. A draft search strategy was initially prepared for Medline and then tailored for other databases as appropriate (see online supplemental additional file 1). Complementary search methods will be described in the final review report.

Criteria for considering studies for inclusion

PICo is used as a framework for formulating eligibility criteria in qualitative research studies, which stands for population (P), phenomenon of interest (I) and context (Co)42 (online supplemental additional file 2).

Stage 3: study selection

The study selection process will follow the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram and be presented in the final scoping review.

EndNote software (V.X8) will be used to manage references. Search results from all databases will be imported into a single EndNote software library, where duplicates will be both automatically and manually removed. The screening will occur in three phases: first, titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two researchers (MA-B and SH) based on eligibility criteria; second, full texts of potentially relevant studies will be reviewed in detail by the same researchers (MA-B and SH); third, reference lists and citations of included studies will be handsearched to identify additional relevant publications. Reasons for excluding full-text studies will be documented and reported in the PRISMA flow diagram. For studies with incomplete information, the authors will be contacted for further details. Any disagreements between researchers (MA-B and SH) will be resolved through discussion, and if consensus is not reached, a third researcher (J-ST) will be consulted for adjudication.

Stage 4: data charting

Data extraction tables will be developed in Microsoft Word to systematically capture comprehensive and relevant information from quantitative and qualitative studies. The data charting process will be followed by the guidance provided by the JBI template extraction instrument. Two members of the research team (MA-B and SA-A) will independently extract data from 20% of the included studies for quality control. The lead researcher (MA-B) will then complete data extraction for the remaining studies. Additionally, another researcher (J-ST, SH and MB) will review the extracted data to ensure accuracy and consistency. The primary information related to data extraction was provided in this scoping review (see online supplemental additional file 3). To minimise language bias and ensure accuracy, professional translation and AI-assisted tools with expert verification will be used during data charting. In this study, artificial intelligence-based translation tools, including Google Traslate, DeepL, and ChatGPT, will be employed alongside professional human traslators as translation assistants to enhance the linguistic accuracy and overall quality of non-English academic manuscripts.

Stage 5: data analysis and reporting the results

For expressing and synthesising the results of the included studies, a narrative synthesis of overall evidence will be undertaken by comparing and contrasting the data. The four steps of the narrative synthesis included developing a theory of available evidence, developing an initial synthesis, exploring the relationships in the data and determining the robustness of the synthesis. Evidence theory information was obtained from explicit statements in the available evidence. In developing the initial synthesis, three techniques will be used, including textual descriptions, tabulation and data translation through content analysis. Data will be synthesised manually by the content analysis technique. Content analysis is a systematic and reproducible technique for compressing many text words into fewer content categories based on explicit coding rules. The translation technique will also be used to explore relationships in the data. Although translation has been developed for qualitative research, it can be used for a combination of qualitative and quantitative evidence. Instead of translating words or phrases, translation focuses on finding a common rubric for salient categories of meaning.43

Stage 6: consultation exercise

Preliminary findings will be used by the research team to inform the design of a comprehensive package of ECD services tailored for the Iranian health sector and its collaboration with non-health sectors. Additionally, up-to-date information on child development service provision will be gathered to assist researchers in mapping these processes. In subsequent phases, these findings will be shared with a panel of experts to obtain further insights and validation, which may require ethical approval depending on the nature of those consultations.

Discussion

The side effects caused by childhood development disorders in the later stages of life are often irreparable, irreversible, or their treatment is much more expensive than prevention in early childhood.44 In the last two decades, integrated child development interventions have been recognised as a powerful economic investment.44 As benefit–cost ratios from the USA show that for every dollar invested in ECD services, there will be at least US$2 return to community.45 Economic returns have been estimated at 15%–17% for every dollar focused on crime, education and welfare savings and tax increases due to higher incomes.45 Interventions implemented in early childhood are more cost-effective because providers have many potential contacts with mothers and children during this period.46

Despite these data, there is little evidence on the bundle of child development services in the health system and its effects on improving the quality of childhood neurodevelopmental care. A comprehensive package has not been designed and implemented, and there has been little, if any, opportunity to rigorously test its effectiveness to improve health outcomes for children, especially the most disadvantaged. In the complex health system, many institutional, research, academic and funding organisations have lost sight of the importance of developing a package of ECD services to improve child health outcomes.

This review informs research on the development and implementation of an integrated ECD package and potentially in relation to any domain of child development more broadly. Therefore, the findings of our review can be considered an important resource for policy-makers, programmers, primary care providers and other providers and researchers in a variety of settings in different regions of the world. Our next steps will include the development of integrated child development services in the Iranian health system to fill knowledge gaps on how to provide child development services using process mapping from the first level of service delivery to referral to higher levels. In addition, the requirements of children’s development services will be determined at every level of the Iranian health system. Finally, understanding the resources required for pilot implementation, assessment and evaluation, and implementation at scale.
