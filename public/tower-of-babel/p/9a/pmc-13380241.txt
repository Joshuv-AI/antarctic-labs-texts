Associations of Emotion Regulation Difficulties, Psychological Well-Being, and Life Satisfaction with Suicidal Ideation and Suicidal Behaviour Among University Students in Indonesia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Meidiana Dwidiyanti, Heni Dwi Windarwati, Retno Lestari, Hanik Endang Nihayati, Irmansyah Irmansyah, Asri Maharani, Helen Brooks, Rohman Hikmat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeSuicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students are associated with multiple psychological factors, yet evidence from Indonesia remains limited, particularly studies examining emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction simultaneously. This study aimed to examine the associations of emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students in Indonesia.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from June to September 2024 among 420 university students from three state universities in Central Java and East Java, Indonesia. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. Suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour were assessed using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation and the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised. Emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction were measured using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale-16, Ryff’s Psychological Well-Being Scale, and the Satisfaction with Life Scale, respectively. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed using psychological total scores as continuous variables. The adjusted models controlled for age, sex, education level, GPA score, GPA satisfaction, parental socioeconomic status, place of residence, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history.ResultsAmong the participants, 180 students (42.9%) were identified as having suicidal ideation, and 87 students (20.7%) were identified as being at risk of suicidal behaviour. In the adjusted continuous-variable models, higher emotion regulation difficulty scores were associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation (AOR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10) and suicidal behaviour (AOR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08). Higher life satisfaction scores were associated with lower odds of suicidal ideation (AOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98) and suicidal behaviour (AOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–1.00). Higher psychological well-being scores were associated with lower odds of suicidal behaviour (AOR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99), but were not significantly associated with suicidal ideation after adjustment.ConclusionGreater emotion regulation difficulties and lower life satisfaction were associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour, whereas lower psychological well-being was associated only with suicidal behaviour after adjustment. These findings suggest the importance of assessing multiple psychological factors in campus-based suicide prevention strategies and support the integration of mental health promotion into higher education policies in Indonesia.Keywords: emotion regulation, life satisfaction, psychological well-being, suicidal behaviour, suicidal ideation

Introduction

Suicidal ideation refers to thoughts, considerations, or plans related to ending one’s life.1 Although suicidal ideation does not always progress to suicidal behaviour, it is an important indicator of psychological distress and may signal increased vulnerability to suicide-related outcomes.2 In this study, suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour are examined as two separate but related outcomes. Suicidal ideation refers to the presence of suicidal thoughts, whereas suicidal behaviour refers to behavioural risk related to suicide, including previous suicidal acts, communication of suicidal intent, and perceived likelihood of future suicidal behaviour.3 This distinction is important because the psychological factors associated with suicidal thoughts may not be identical to those associated with behavioural suicide risk.

University students are a population that may be vulnerable to suicide-related outcomes because they often experience academic pressure, social adjustment, financial difficulties, identity development, and uncertainty about future careers.4 These challenges may become more difficult to manage when students have limited coping resources, poor emotional support, or inadequate access to mental health services.5 Therefore, understanding psychological factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students is important for strengthening campus-based mental health promotion and suicide prevention.

Suicide remains a major public health concern globally, particularly among adolescents and young adults.6 Suicide attempts and suicide-related behaviours are commonly reported among young people, including those in the transition from adolescence to adulthood.1 In Indonesia, suicide among young people has also become an important public health concern.7 In 2023, several provinces, including Central Java and East Java, reported high numbers of suicide cases.8 These provincial data are relevant because the present study was conducted among university students in Central Java and East Java. However, empirical evidence on psychological factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among Indonesian university students remains limited.

University students in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) may experience additional challenges related to mental health, including limited access to professional psychological services, stigma toward help-seeking, uneven availability of campus counselling, and limited early detection systems.9,10 In Indonesia, many university students may rely on family, peers, lecturers, or informal support systems when dealing with psychological distress. However, these sources of support may not always be sufficient for students experiencing suicidal thoughts or behavioural suicide risk. Therefore, identifying psychological factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour is necessary to inform prevention strategies that are realistic, culturally appropriate, and feasible within university settings.

Emotion regulation is one psychological factor that may be associated with suicide-related outcomes. Emotion regulation refers to the ability to understand, monitor, and modify emotional responses in order to respond adaptively to internal and external demands.11 According to Gross’s process model of emotion regulation, individuals regulate emotions through several strategies, including situation selection, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation. Difficulties in emotion regulation may make it harder for students to manage intense negative emotions, academic stress, interpersonal conflict, and feelings of failure. Previous studies have shown that difficulties in emotion regulation are associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among young people.12 Therefore, emotion regulation difficulties may be an important psychological factor to examine in relation to suicide-related outcomes among university students.

Psychological well-being is also relevant to suicide-related outcomes. Ryff’s model conceptualizes psychological well-being as a multidimensional construct that includes autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance.13 Students with lower psychological well-being may experience difficulties in maintaining positive relationships, developing a sense of purpose, accepting themselves, or managing academic and social demands.14 These difficulties may be associated with greater vulnerability to suicide-related outcomes. However, psychological well-being may not have the same relationship with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour.15 Because suicidal behaviour may reflect more severe psychological and functional vulnerability than suicidal ideation alone, it is important to examine whether psychological well-being is associated with both outcomes after adjustment for other relevant factors.16

Life satisfaction is another important psychological construct related to student mental health. Life satisfaction refers to an individual’s overall cognitive evaluation of life circumstances.17 It reflects how students evaluate their academic progress, relationships, future expectations, and personal goals. Students with low life satisfaction may perceive their life circumstances as disappointing, stressful, or inconsistent with their expectations.18 This negative overall evaluation of life may be associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Unlike psychological well-being, which reflects broader functioning and personal growth, life satisfaction captures a global judgment of whether one’s life is going well.19 Therefore, examining life satisfaction together with emotion regulation and psychological well-being may provide a more complete understanding of suicide-related outcomes among university students.

Previous studies have examined emotion regulation, psychological well-being, life satisfaction, and suicide-related outcomes among young people.20–22 However, several gaps remain. First, many studies have examined these psychological factors separately, which limits understanding of their associations when considered within the same analytical framework. Second, previous studies have often focused on suicidal ideation alone, although suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour may have different psychological correlates.23 Third, evidence from Indonesia remains limited, particularly among university students in regions where suicide has been reported as a public health concern. These gaps suggest the need for studies that examine multiple psychological factors simultaneously and distinguish suicidal ideation from suicidal behaviour.

Based on the theoretical and empirical literature, this study was guided by three hypotheses. First, higher emotion regulation difficulties would be associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Second, lower psychological well-being would be associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Third, lower life satisfaction would be associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations of emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students in Indonesia. The findings are expected to provide context-specific evidence to support campus-based mental health promotion, early screening, and suicide prevention strategies in Indonesian higher education settings.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study used a cross-sectional design to examine the associations of emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students in Indonesia. The study was conducted from June to September 2024 in three state universities located in Central Java and East Java, Indonesia.

Central Java and East Java were selected because both provinces have reported suicide cases among young people and because the participating universities provided institutional permission for student recruitment. The study population consisted of undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled at the three participating universities.

Participants and Sampling

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were: (1) being an active student at one of the participating universities, (2) being aged 18 years or older, (3) being able to read and understand Indonesian, and (4) being willing to provide written informed consent. Students were excluded if they submitted an incomplete questionnaire with substantial missing responses, withdrew before completing the questionnaire, or submitted duplicate online responses.

The minimum sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 for logistic regression analysis. The calculation was based on a two-tailed test, a significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.95, an estimated odds ratio of 1.80, and an assumed probability of 0.30 for the main outcome among students without the main exposure. These assumptions were selected to represent a small-to-moderate association between psychological factors and suicide-related outcomes. Based on this calculation, the minimum required sample size was 312 participants. To account for potential incomplete responses and non-response, an additional 10% was added, resulting in a minimum target sample of 344 participants.

A total of 465 students were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 438 students returned the questionnaire. After data screening, 18 questionnaires were excluded because of incomplete responses or duplicate entries. Therefore, 420 students were included in the final analysis. The response rate was 94.2% based on returned questionnaires from invited students and 95.9% based on usable questionnaires from returned questionnaires.

Recruitment and Data Collection Procedure

Participant recruitment was conducted with assistance from university staff at each participating university. Study information was distributed through campus bulletin boards, university Email groups, and student social media groups. The study information included the purpose of the study, eligibility criteria, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality procedures, estimated time required to complete the questionnaire, and contact information of the research team.

Interested students were directed to a designated contact person at each university. The contact person provided further information about the study and explained the participation procedure. Before completing the questionnaire, all participants received an information sheet and signed an informed consent form.

Data were collected using both paper-based and online questionnaires. The paper-based questionnaire was distributed during scheduled recruitment sessions on campus, whereas the online questionnaire was distributed using a secure survey link. The content, item order, instructions, and response options were identical in both formats. Before data collection, the research team checked both versions to ensure consistency between the paper-based and online questionnaires.

Participants completed the questionnaire independently. The estimated completion time was approximately 15–20 minutes. Paper-based questionnaires were returned in sealed envelopes to protect confidentiality. Online responses were stored in a password-protected database accessible only to the research team. Duplicate online responses were screened by checking repeated entries, identical demographic patterns, and response timestamps.

Measurements and Instruments

Suicidal Ideation

Suicidal ideation was assessed using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI). The BSSI consists of 19 items that measure the presence and severity of suicidal thoughts. Total scores range from 0 to 38, with higher scores indicating greater suicidal ideation.24

In this study, suicidal ideation was categorized into two groups: absence of suicidal ideation and presence of suicidal ideation. A score of 0 indicated absence of suicidal ideation, whereas a score greater than 0 indicated the presence of suicidal ideation.25 This classification was used as a screening-based categorization and should not be interpreted as a clinical diagnosis. The BSSI showed good internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.

Suicidal Behaviour

Suicidal behaviour was assessed using the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R). The SBQ-R consists of four items assessing lifetime suicidal ideation or attempts, frequency of suicidal ideation, communication of suicidal intent, and self-reported likelihood of future suicidal behaviour.26 Total scores range from 3 to 18, with higher scores indicating greater suicide-related behavioural risk.

A cut-off score of 7 or higher was used to indicate risk of suicidal behaviour. This cut-off has been widely used in non-clinical populations.27 In this study, the SBQ-R was used as a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool. The SBQ-R showed acceptable internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82.

Emotion Regulation Difficulties

Emotion regulation difficulties were assessed using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale-16 (DERS-16). The DERS-16 consists of 16 items that assess difficulties in accepting emotional responses, engaging in goal-directed behaviour, controlling impulses, accessing emotion regulation strategies, and achieving emotional clarity.28 Higher scores indicate greater difficulties in emotion regulation.

For the primary analyses, the DERS-16 total score was treated as a continuous variable.29 Because no universally accepted clinical cut-off is available for the DERS-16 among Indonesian university students, the sample median was used only for sensitivity analyses to facilitate interpretation of higher and lower emotion regulation difficulty groups. In the present study, the median DERS-16 score was 42. Students with scores above 42 were categorized as having higher emotion regulation difficulties, whereas students with scores of 42 or below were categorized as having lower emotion regulation difficulties.30 This categorization was used only for sensitivity analysis and analytical interpretation and should not be interpreted as a clinical diagnosis. The DERS-16 showed high internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91.

Psychological Well-Being

Psychological well-being was assessed using the 18-item version of Ryff’s Psychological Well-Being Scale. This scale measures six dimensions of psychological well-being: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance.31,32 Total scores range from 18 to 108, with higher scores indicating better psychological well-being.

For the primary analyses, the psychological well-being total score was treated as a continuous variable. Because no established clinical cut-off is available for the 18-item Ryff scale among Indonesian university students, the sample median was used only for sensitivity analyses to facilitate interpretation of lower and higher psychological well-being groups. In the present study, the median score was 72. Students with scores below 72 were categorized as having lower psychological well-being, whereas students with scores of 72 or above were categorized as having higher psychological well-being.33,34 This categorization was used only for sensitivity analysis and analytical interpretation and does not indicate clinical impairment. The scale showed acceptable internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84.

Life Satisfaction

Life satisfaction was assessed using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS).35 The SWLS consists of five items that measure an individual’s overall cognitive evaluation of life satisfaction. Total scores range from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction.36

For the primary analyses, the SWLS total score was treated as a continuous variable. Because no population-specific clinical cut-off is available for Indonesian university students, the sample median was used only for sensitivity analyses to facilitate interpretation of lower and higher life satisfaction groups. In the present study, the median SWLS score was 22. Students with scores below 22 were categorized as having lower life satisfaction, whereas students with scores of 22 or above were categorized as having higher life satisfaction.37 This categorization was used only for sensitivity analysis and analytical interpretation and should not be interpreted as a clinical diagnosis. The SWLS showed good internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86.

Covariates

The covariates included age, sex, education level, GPA score, GPA satisfaction, parental socioeconomic status, place of residence, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history.

Age was categorized as <25 years and ≥25 years. Sex was categorized as male or female. Education level was categorized as bachelor’s degree or master’s degree. GPA score was categorized as <3.50 and ≥3.50. GPA satisfaction was categorized as satisfied or not satisfied. Parental socioeconomic status was categorized based on whether parents’ income was below or above the provincial minimum wage. Place of residence was categorized as living in a boarding house, living with relatives, or living with parents. Perceived family harmony was categorized as yes or no. Past physical medical history and past mental health problem history were categorized as yes or no.

These covariates were selected because previous studies have suggested that sociodemographic, academic, family-related, and health-related factors may be associated with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students.2,38,39 Therefore, these variables were included in the adjusted logistic regression models to reduce potential confounding.

Furthermore, students who live apart from their parents may experience adverse psychological outcomes such as depression.40 Thus, the place of residence, whether alone, with relatives, or with parents, was included as a covariate. GPA, including both students’ scores and their satisfaction with those scores, was considered because academic performance can serve as a proxy for lifetime suicide risk.41 Finally, both past physical and mental health histories are factored in, as they are closely linked to elevated levels of suicidal behaviour compared to those without health issues.42 Hence, past medical history was also included as a covariate in this study.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed before group comparison. Because several continuous variables were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare continuous variables between students with and without suicidal ideation and between students with and without suicidal behaviour. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Binary logistic regression was used because both outcome variables were dichotomous: suicidal ideation status and suicidal behaviour risk status. Separate regression models were conducted for suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour.

To address concerns regarding potential information loss from dichotomizing psychological variables, the primary regression analyses were conducted using the psychological measures as continuous total scores. Emotion regulation difficulties were entered as the DERS-16 total score, psychological well-being was entered as the total score of Ryff’s Psychological Well-Being Scale, and life satisfaction was entered as the Satisfaction with Life Scale total score. These continuous-variable models were treated as the main analytic models because they retained the full variability of the psychological measures and avoided reliance on sample-specific median cut-off points.

The regression analysis was conducted in two stages. First, unadjusted logistic regression was performed to examine the crude association between each continuous psychological variable and each suicide-related outcome. Second, adjusted logistic regression was performed to control for age, sex, education level, GPA score, GPA satisfaction, parental socioeconomic status, place of residence, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history. The enter method was used, meaning that all prespecified psychological variables and covariates were entered into the adjusted models simultaneously.

Odds ratios (ORs), adjusted odds ratios (AORs), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and p-values were reported. For continuous psychological variables, ORs and AORs were interpreted as the change in odds of suicidal ideation or suicidal behaviour associated with each one-point increase in the corresponding scale score. Higher DERS-16 scores indicated greater emotion regulation difficulties, whereas higher psychological well-being and life satisfaction scores indicated better psychological well-being and greater life satisfaction.

To facilitate clinical and practical interpretation, median-based categorical analyses were retained only as sensitivity analyses. In these sensitivity analyses, the DERS-16, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction scores were categorized using their sample medians. Students with DERS-16 scores above the median were categorized as having higher emotion regulation difficulties, whereas those with scores at or below the median were categorized as having lower emotion regulation difficulties. Students with psychological well-being and life satisfaction scores below the median were categorized as having lower psychological well-being and lower life satisfaction, respectively. These categorical analyses were not treated as the primary analytic results.

Multicollinearity among independent variables and covariates was assessed using tolerance values and variance inflation factors (VIFs). A tolerance value below 0.20 or a VIF above 5 was considered evidence of problematic multicollinearity. In the present study, tolerance values ranged from 0.41 to 0.93, and VIF values ranged from 1.08 to 2.44, indicating no serious multicollinearity among the variables included in the regression models.

Because several statistical comparisons were performed, the findings were interpreted based on the direction and magnitude of the odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, statistical significance, and theoretical relevance. No causal interpretation was made because of the cross-sectional design.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Commission of the Faculty of Nursing, Universitas Airlangga, Surabaya, Indonesia (Reference No. 3208-KEPK). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participants received written information about the study before participation. The information sheet explained the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw from the study at any time without academic or personal consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the questionnaire. Because the study addressed sensitive topics related to suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour, participants were informed that they could skip any question that made them uncomfortable Participants who experienced distress during or after completing the questionnaire were advised to contact the university counselling service or the designated contact person at each university for further support.

All data were anonymized before analysis. Paper-based questionnaires were stored in a locked cabinet, and online data were stored in a password-protected database accessible only to the research team.

Results

Participant Flow and Characteristics

A total of 465 students were invited to participate in this study. Of these, 438 students returned the questionnaire. After data screening, 18 questionnaires were excluded because of incomplete responses or duplicate entries. Therefore, 420 university students were included in the final analysis.

The mean age of participants was 22.5 years (SD = 5.58). Most participants were female (338/420, 80.5%) and enrolled in bachelor’s degree programs (407/420, 96.9%). The mean GPA score was 3.6 (SD = 0.24), and 270 students (64.3%) reported being satisfied with their GPA. Slightly more than half of the participants reported parental socioeconomic status below the provincial minimum wage (211/420, 50.2%). Most students lived in boarding houses (263/420, 62.6%), and 365 students (86.9%) perceived their family as harmonious. Regarding health history, 47 students (11.2%) reported a past physical medical history, and 15 students (3.6%) reported a past mental health problem history (Table 1).

Based on the suicide-related outcome measures, 180 students (42.9%) were identified as having suicidal ideation, whereas 87 students (20.7%) were identified as being at risk of suicidal behaviour. Suicidal ideation was therefore more frequently reported than suicidal behaviour among the participating university students.

Students with suicidal ideation differed significantly from those without suicidal ideation in terms of GPA score, GPA satisfaction, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history. Students with suicidal ideation had a lower mean GPA score than those without suicidal ideation (3.5 vs 3.6, p = 0.002). A higher proportion of students with suicidal ideation were not satisfied with their GPA (45.6% vs 28.3%, p < 0.001), perceived their family as not harmonious (23.3% vs 5.4%, p < 0.001), reported a past physical medical history (16.7% vs 7.1%, p = 0.003), and reported a past mental health problem history (6.1% vs 1.7%, p = 0.031).

Students at risk of suicidal behaviour also differed significantly from those not at risk in terms of age, GPA satisfaction, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history. Students at risk of suicidal behaviour were younger than those not at risk (21.5 years vs 22.8 years, p = 0.022). A higher proportion of students at risk of suicidal behaviour were not satisfied with their GPA (51.7% vs 31.5%, p < 0.001), perceived their family as not harmonious (33.3% vs 7.8%, p < 0.001), reported a past physical medical history (19.5% vs 9.0%, p = 0.010), and reported a past mental health problem history (10.3% vs 1.8%, p < 0.001).

After adjustment for age, sex, education level, GPA score, GPA satisfaction, parental socioeconomic status, place of residence, perceived family harmony, past physical medical history, and past mental health problem history, higher emotion regulation difficulties remained significantly associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Each one-point increase in DERS-16 score was associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation (AOR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10, p < 0.001) and suicidal behaviour (AOR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08, p = 0.019).

Life satisfaction also remained significantly associated with both suicide-related outcomes. Each one-point increase in life satisfaction score was associated with lower odds of suicidal ideation (AOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98, p = 0.005) and suicidal behaviour (AOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–1.00, p = 0.041).

Psychological well-being showed a different pattern across the two outcomes. Higher psychological well-being scores were not significantly associated with suicidal ideation after adjustment (AOR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.95–1.00, p = 0.067). However, higher psychological well-being scores were significantly associated with lower odds of suicidal behaviour (AOR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99, p = 0.006).

Overall, the primary continuous-variable models showed that emotion regulation difficulties and life satisfaction were associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour, whereas psychological well-being was associated only with suicidal behaviour after adjustment.

Full Adjusted Model for Suicidal Ideation

Table 4 presents the full adjusted logistic regression model for suicidal ideation using continuous psychological scores. After adjustment for sociodemographic, academic, family-related, and health-related covariates, higher emotion regulation difficulties and lower life satisfaction remained significantly associated with suicidal ideation. Discussion

This study examined the associations of emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students in Indonesia. In the primary continuous-variable models, higher emotion regulation difficulty scores were associated with higher odds of both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour, whereas higher life satisfaction scores were associated with lower odds of both outcomes. Psychological well-being showed a different pattern: higher psychological well-being scores were associated with lower odds of suicidal behaviour but were not significantly associated with suicidal ideation after adjustment. Perceived lack of family harmony was also consistently associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. These findings suggest that different psychological and family-related factors may show different patterns of association across suicide-related outcomes.

A key methodological revision in this study was the use of continuous psychological scores as the primary analytic exposures. This approach retained the full variability of emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction, and reduced the potential information loss associated with dichotomizing psychological measures. Median-based categorical analyses were retained only as sensitivity analyses to facilitate interpretation of higher and lower psychological risk groups. The consistency between the primary continuous-variable models and the median-based sensitivity analyses suggests that the observed associations were not solely dependent on sample-specific cut-off points.

The prevalence of suicide-related outcomes in this study indicates that suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour are important concerns among university students. Suicidal ideation was more frequently reported than suicidal behaviour, which is consistent with the understanding that suicidal thoughts may occur more commonly than behavioural suicide risk.43 This distinction is important because suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour may not share identical psychological correlates.44 By examining both outcomes separately, this study provides a more specific understanding of how emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, and life satisfaction are associated with different forms of suicide-related risk among students.45

Higher emotion regulation difficulty scores were associated with higher odds of both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that difficulties in understanding, accepting, and managing emotional responses are related to suicide-related outcomes among adolescents and young adults.46 Emotion regulation difficulties may be particularly relevant for university students because they often face academic pressure, interpersonal conflict, social adjustment, financial concerns, and uncertainty about their future.47 When students have difficulty regulating intense negative emotions, they may be more vulnerable to persistent distress, impulsive reactions, and difficulties seeking adaptive support.

The association between emotion regulation difficulties and suicide-related outcomes can also be understood through Gross’s process model of emotion regulation. This model suggests that individuals regulate emotions through several processes, including situation selection, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation.11,22 Students who experience emotion regulation difficulties may have problems reframing stressful experiences, controlling emotional responses, or choosing adaptive coping strategies.48 In a university context, this may be relevant when students experience academic failure, family pressure, relationship conflict, or loneliness.49–51 However, because the present study used a cross-sectional design, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence that emotion regulation difficulties cause suicidal ideation or suicidal behaviour.52,53

Higher life satisfaction scores were associated with lower odds of both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. Life satisfaction reflects students’ overall cognitive evaluation of their lives, including academic progress, relationships, future expectations, personal goals, and perceived quality of life.54 Students with lower life satisfaction may evaluate their life circumstances more negatively and may experience greater dissatisfaction with academic achievement, social relationships, family expectations, or future opportunities.55 This may be associated with greater vulnerability to suicide-related outcomes.56

In the Indonesian university context, life satisfaction may be closely connected to social and cultural expectations. Many students experience pressure to succeed academically, maintain family reputation, fulfil parental expectations, and prepare for future employment. When students perceive a gap between their expectations and their current life circumstances, their overall life satisfaction may decrease.27 This may help explain why lower life satisfaction remained associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour after adjustment for several covariates.57,58 Therefore, university-based mental health programs should not only focus on crisis response but also address students’ broader sense of life satisfaction, future orientation, academic adjustment, and social connectedness.59

A key finding of this study was that higher psychological well-being scores were associated with lower odds of suicidal behaviour but were not significantly associated with suicidal ideation after adjustment. This pattern suggests that psychological well-being may have a different relationship with suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour.60 In the unadjusted analysis, psychological well-being was associated with suicidal ideation; however, this association was attenuated after adjustment for other psychological factors and covariates. This may indicate that the association between psychological well-being and suicidal ideation overlaps with emotion regulation difficulties, life satisfaction, family harmony, academic satisfaction, or health-related factors.61

Psychological well-being is a broad multidimensional construct that includes autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance. Lower psychological well-being may reflect broader difficulties in functioning, meaning-making, social connection, and self-evaluation.62 These aspects may be more closely associated with suicidal behaviour than with suicidal ideation alone. Suicidal behaviour may represent a more severe level of suicide-related vulnerability, in which students experience not only suicidal thoughts but also broader impairment in psychological functioning, perceived control, interpersonal resources, and sense of purpose.63 This may explain why psychological well-being remained significantly associated with suicidal behaviour after adjustment.

This finding has important implications for suicide risk assessment in university settings. Screening for suicidal ideation alone may not be sufficient to identify students with broader behavioural suicide risk. Students with poorer psychological well-being, lower life satisfaction, greater emotion regulation difficulties, and perceived lack of family harmony may require more comprehensive assessment. University counselling services should therefore assess both immediate emotional distress and broader psychological functioning, including purpose in life, self-acceptance, environmental mastery, and social relationships.64

Perceived lack of family harmony was consistently associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour. This finding is important in the Indonesian cultural context, where family relationships often play a central role in emotional support, identity development, decision-making, and coping. Students who perceive their family as less harmonious may have fewer emotional resources when facing academic or personal difficulties.65 They may also feel less able to disclose distress or seek support from family members. Therefore, family-related factors should be considered in campus-based suicide prevention, particularly in cultural contexts where family remains a major source of psychological and social support.

The findings should also be interpreted in relation to mental health service availability in Indonesian universities and other low- and middle-income country contexts. Many universities may have limited counselling staff, limited routine screening, stigma toward mental health problems, and unclear referral pathways for students at risk. Therefore, recommendations based on this study should be realistic and resource-sensitive. Universities may consider stepped-care approaches, including mental health literacy programs, early screening, peer support, gatekeeper training for lecturers and academic advisors, brief emotion regulation interventions, and referral pathways for students who need professional care.66

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that campus-based suicide prevention should include both psychological skill-building and support system strengthening. Emotion regulation support may include psychoeducation on recognizing emotional triggers, cognitive reappraisal, problem-solving, distress tolerance, and adaptive communication. Programs aimed at improving life satisfaction and psychological well-being may include academic mentoring, peer support, purpose-oriented counselling, and activities that strengthen social connectedness.67 Because perceived family harmony was associated with both outcomes, universities may also consider family-sensitive approaches, such as involving families in mental health education where appropriate and culturally acceptable.

At the policy level, the findings support the integration of mental health promotion into higher education systems. Universities should consider routine student mental health assessment, confidential counselling access, suicide risk referral protocols, and collaboration with local mental health professionals. Mental health promotion should not be limited to counselling centres but should also involve student affairs units, academic departments, lecturers, peer leaders, and community health services. Such an integrated approach may help universities identify students at risk earlier and provide support that is feasible within available resources.

This study has several strengths. First, it examined three psychological factors within the same analytical framework, providing a more integrated understanding of suicide-related outcomes among university students. Second, it distinguished suicidal ideation from suicidal behaviour, allowing a clearer interpretation of factors associated with each outcome. Third, the study adjusted for several relevant covariates, including sociodemographic characteristics, academic factors, family harmony, and health history. Fourth, the study contributes evidence from Indonesia, where research on suicide-related outcomes among university students remains limited. Fifth, the analysis used continuous psychological scores as the primary analytic exposures, which reduced information loss and improved the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about temporal sequence or causality between emotion regulation difficulties, psychological well-being, life satisfaction, and suicide-related outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine whether these psychological factors precede changes in suicidal ideation or suicidal behaviour over time. Second, this study used self-report questionnaires, which may be affected by recall bias, social desirability bias, and underreporting, particularly because suicide-related experiences and mental health history are sensitive topics. Third, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, and the findings may not be generalizable to all Indonesian university students. Fourth, the sample was predominantly female, which may influence the estimated prevalence of suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour and limit generalizability to male students. Fifth, although the primary analyses used psychological measures as continuous variables to retain statistical information, the interpretation of odds ratios per one-point increase may be less intuitive for clinical or practical decision-making. Therefore, median-based categorical analyses were retained as sensitivity analyses to support interpretation of higher and lower psychological risk groups. However, these median-based categories were sample-specific and should not be interpreted as clinical cut-off points.

Future research should use longitudinal or prospective designs to clarify the temporal relationships between psychological factors and suicide-related outcomes. Further studies should include more balanced samples by sex, students from different regions and types of universities, and additional variables such as academic stress, family expectations, perceived stigma, social support, loneliness, and help-seeking behaviour. Intervention studies are also needed to examine whether emotion regulation training, life satisfaction enhancement, psychological well-being programs, and family-sensitive support strategies are feasible and acceptable in Indonesian university settings.

Conclusion

This study found that greater emotion regulation difficulties and lower life satisfaction were associated with both suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour among university students in Indonesia. Lower psychological well-being was associated with suicidal behaviour but was not significantly associated with suicidal ideation after adjustment for sociodemographic, academic, family-related, and health-related covariates. These findings were supported by primary continuous-variable models and were generally consistent with median-based sensitivity analyses, suggesting that the associations were not solely dependent on dichotomization of psychological measures.

The results highlight the importance of assessing multiple psychological factors in campus-based suicide prevention efforts. Universities should consider integrating mental health promotion, early screening, emotion regulation support, counselling services, and clear referral pathways into student health and higher education policies. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and to evaluate effective, culturally appropriate suicide prevention strategies for Indonesian university students.
