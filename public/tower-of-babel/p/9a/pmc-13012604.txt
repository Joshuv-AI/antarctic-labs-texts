Introduction

Identification of Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is common and characteristically defined by a transient and reversible cardiomyopathy, typically associated with stress. Electrocardiographic and echocardiographic findings may be challenging to differentiate from ischaemia but typically occur in the absence of obstructive epicardial coronary artery stenosis.1,2 Although most cases recover within 3 months,1,3 TTS is not a benign condition, as complications such as left ventricular (LV) outflow tract obstruction, LV thrombus, cardiogenic shock, and arrhythmias are common.1,4

Regional hypokinesia in TTS typically extends beyond a single coronary territory. The classic description of LV hypokinesia involves the apical and mid-ventricular segments with hypercontractility of the base. Whilst this variant accounts for 50%–80% of cases,1 other morphologies—including mid-wall, basal (reverse), and focal variants are increasingly recognized.5,6 Though there are several proposed mechanisms, acute and reversible coronary microvascular dysfunction is thought to be triggered by catecholamine responses to physical or emotional stressors, including medical procedures.2

Stress echocardiography is commonly utilized in the investigation of chest pain and dyspnoea. While uncommon, exogenously administered agents such as dobutamine utilized during stress echocardiography have been associated with the development of TTS, with case reports describing the scarce occurrence relative to the volume of tests performed.7–9 This association is even rarer in the setting of exercise stress echocardiography (ESE).7,10 Though the pathophysiology remains relatively unclear, it is thought that a catecholaminergic surge with associated microvascular dysfunction is the major driver of TTS secondary to dobutamine.5 Similarly, increased sympathetic stimulation is thought to be the dominant contributor to exercise-induced TTS.10 This case describes the occurrence of stress cardiomyopathy during ESE in a postmenopausal woman with exertional dyspnoea. A multimodality imaging approach—including echocardiography, cardiac MRI, and coronary angiography—was essential in establishing the diagnosis and excluding ischaemic causes. The case contributes to the growing recognition of exercise-induced TTS and highlights the importance of considering this diagnosis in patients with abnormal responses to stress testing.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old female was referred for investigation of dyspnoea and reduced exercise tolerance. Her medical history was significant for hypertension, on antihypertensive therapy (telmisartan 40 mg with hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg daily), though remained suboptimally controlled. She had a history of breast cancer surgical resection with previous radiotherapy and ongoing hormonal therapy (letrozole 2.5 mg daily), osteoporosis (on denosumab and Vitamin D), and Grover’s disease. She consumed alcohol five nights per week and previously smoked.

She reported progressive decline in exercise tolerance since completing first-line therapy for breast cancer in the year prior, with class II dyspnoea. She had occasional palpitations, but never chest pain. She had no known personal or family history of cardiac disease. Resting echocardiography showed normal LV size, ejection fraction (65%), and normal valvular function. Diastolic assessment was most consistent with normal left atrial pressure at rest. Her baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) was normal (Figure 1A).

Electrocardiography at (A) baseline (B) peak exercise, demonstrating ST-segment depression on stress, (C) 24-h and (D) 48-h, demonstrating T-wave inversion involving the anteroseptal leads and progressing to involve the lateral leads, with associated QTc prolongation.

An exercise stress echocardiogram was performed. Exercise induced severe dyspnoea and chest tightness at a relatively low workload but 109% of maximum age-predicted heart rate, prompting test termination after 4:30 min (Bruce protocol). She had a hypertensive response, with a peak captured blood pressure of 190/91 mmHg. The ECG was abnormal, with 2 mm of horizontal to down-sloping ST segment depression (Figure 1B) and frequent ventricular ectopy, including occasional ventricular couplets. Post-peak stress imaging revealed abnormal LV cavity dilatation and inducible regional wall motion abnormalities predominantly involving the septum and anterior wall (Figure 2 and Supplementary material online, Video S1). The apical cap appeared contractile, and although it did not exclude ischaemia in a left anterior descending artery distribution, it did support the consideration of non-coronary regional hypokinesia. Post-exercise left atrial pressure was likely elevated (based primarily on E/e’ 15)11 in the context of acute LV dysfunction. Despite resolution of the acute, severe symptoms, there was persistence of regional wall motion abnormalities beyond 10 min of recovery, and the patient was admitted to the hospital. Cardiac biomarkers were elevated, with B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) of 296 ng/l and the peak high-sensitivity troponin-I (TnI) of 2341 ng/l being recorded at 36 h following stress testing. Selective coronary angiography revealed minor irregularities in the left system, and a non-obstructive lesion (30% stenosis) of the mid right coronary artery (Figure 3).

Select echocardiography still images at baseline (A and B), and postpeak stress (c and D). Parasternal long (A) and short (B) axis imaging (diastole on left and systole on right) demonstrating normal left ventricular function (ejection fraction 65%) at baseline. Corresponding postpeak stress imaging (c and D) demonstrates left ventricular dilatation with associated severe hypokinesia of the anteroseptum, inferoseptum, anterior, and anterolateral segments.

Coronary angiography demonstrates normal coronary origins and only minor coronary disease.

Over the subsequent 3 days, initial anteroseptal T-wave inversion progressed to include the high-lateral limb and lateral precordial leads (Figures 1C and 1D). The QTc progressively lengthened to 560 ms. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram was performed on Day 2, showing persisting segmental LV dysfunction, with akinesis of the mid-anterior and septal segments but preserved contractility of the basal and apical segments. Inpatient cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a similar pattern of mid-wall regional hypokinesia (Figure 4A–C and Supplementary material online, Video S2) with corresponding myocardial oedema but a notable absence of late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 5A and B). Interestingly, the mid-anterior right ventricular wall was also hypokinetic. This pattern was most in keeping with bi-ventricular mid-wall variant stress cardiomyopathy with mildly reduced LV ejection fraction (48%) and evidence of mid-segment right ventricular involvement. The inpatient course was further complicated by symptomatic atrial fibrillation on Day 3 and bilateral pleural effusions.

Cardiac magnetic resonance. Cine frames of the 4-chamber (A), 3-chamber (B), and 2-chamber (C) at presentation, demonstrating regional hypokinesia of the mid segments (predominantly the inferoseptum, anteroseptum, anterior, and anterolateral) in systole (images on right). There was left ventricular recovery and no regional hypokinesia present on corresponding imaging at 3 months (D-F).

Myocardial oedema and late gadolinium imaging during inpatient admission (a and B) and at 3 months (C and D). 4-chamber (A) and short axis (B) T2-weighted black-blood imaging (left) and parametric mapping (middle), demonstrating myocardial oedema predominantly affecting the mid segments of the anterior, anteroseptal, inferoseptal, and anterolateral walls. No late gadolinium enhancement (right). At 3 months follow-up, there was no myocardial oedema or late gadolinium on corresponding views (images C and D).

The patient was commenced on furosemide and heart failure therapy was established (bisoprolol, perindopril, and empagliflozin). She was also commenced on rivaroxaban, given the occurrence of atrial fibrillation with a CHA2DS2-VA score of 2. She was discharged after a 5-day hospital admission with no significant ongoing symptoms. A repeat cardiac MRI was performed 3 months later, confirming complete resolution of the regional wall motion abnormalities and normalization of her LV ejection fraction (69%, Figure 4D–F and Supplementary material online, Videos S2). There was no myocardial oedema or late gadolinium enhancement. At follow-up reviews over the subsequent 2 years, the patient reported symptoms associated with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but a preference for conservative management. Anticoagulation was continued, alongside bisoprolol, perindopril, and empagliflozin as treatment for LV dysfunction and hypertension, but also as recommended therapy in patients with potential heart failure with preserved ejection fraction physiology as a contributor to dyspnoea.

Discussion

Stress cardiomyopathy may be triggered by a range of physical or emotional stressors. The association between TTS and pharmacologic stress testing, particularly with dobutamine, has been previously described.12 However, this case illustrates a less common scenario, with stress cardiomyopathy triggered by exercise stress testing. In this case, imaging findings were similar to those seen in major myocardial ischaemia with new, extensive regional wall motion abnormalities identified on post-peak stress echocardiography. A comprehensive, multimodality diagnostic approach, including coronary angiography, cardiac MRI, serial ECG, and echocardiography was essential to confirm the diagnosis and exclude obstructive coronary artery disease.13

Takotsubo syndrome predominantly affects postmenopausal women, with those aged over 55 years being five times more likely to develop the condition than younger women.6 The occurrence of TTS during stress echocardiography is rare, with most reported cases being associated with dobutamine infusion12 and relatively few cases being described in the context of exercise stress testing.7 Pertinent to this case, emotional stressors are more frequently implicated in women, but physical triggers are more commonly observed in men.1,4 No clear precipitating factor is identified in up to one-third of cases.6 There is no evidence to suggest that patients with exercise-induced TTS should avoid strenuous exertion. It may be that the medical procedure itself is sufficient to induce the stress response in such patients, and that regular physical activity is encouraged across the spectrum of health and disease, with no available evidence demonstrating harm post recovery from TTS.

The classically described apical ballooning pattern is recognized to be only one manifestation of TTS. Several alternate presentations have been described, including mid-ventricular, basal, and focal variants. In rare cases, regional wall motion abnormalities may correspond to a single coronary artery territory.6 In this case, the patient exhibited a mid-wall variant, which is described in approximately 10%–15% of cases of TTS.1,4,5 In addition, there was right ventricular involvement, which, though relatively uncommon, is thought to indicate a higher risk cohort and more severe clinical presentation.14,15 This patient was commenced on medical therapy for LV dysfunction, with beta blocker and angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor agents being continued for both blood pressure control and for reducing the risk of TTS recurrence.16 This therapy was alongside anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation and lipid-lowering therapy for minor coronary disease. As recommended in the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Takotsubo Syndrome Management Algorithm1 follow-up cardiac MRI at 3 months, confirmed resolution of regional wall motion abnormalities and normalization of LV ejection fraction.

Results from the International Takotsubo Registry have shown that 47% of patients with Takotsubo had LV recovery within 10 days, with early recovery associated with lower one-year mortality.17 Similar findings have been reported from the RETAKO registry.3 In recurrent cases, the pattern of regional hypokinesia may vary on subsequent presentations.1,6 Current recommendations suggest that repeat imaging should be performed at 3–6 months1 unless there are clinical indications to perform this sooner.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that exercise stress echocardiography may precipitate TTS. The initial presentation and echocardiographic appearance may be suggestive of myocardial ischaemia and, therefore, a multi-modality approach is needed to confirm the diagnosis. Clinicians should remain vigilant for atypical triggers and variant patterns of TTS.
