Biospecimens in the HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study: Rationale and protocol
Authors: Elinor L Sullivan, Ryan Bogdan, Ludmila Bakhireva, Pat Levitt, Joseph Jones, Michael Sheldon, Julie M Croff, Moriah Thomason, Jamie O Lo, Leigh MacIntyre, Susmita Shrivastava, Leigh-Anne Cioffredi, Andrea G Edlow, Brittany R Howell, Barbara H Chaiyachati, Nicole Lashley-Simms, Kelly Molloy, Cris Lam, Anna M Stoermann, Thanh Trinh, Namasivayam Ambalavanan, Jenae M Neiderhiser, HBCD Biospecimens Workgroup
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, a multi-site prospective longitudinal cohort study, will examine human brain, cognitive, behavioral, social, and emotional development beginning prenatally and planned through early childhood. The longitudinal collection of biological samples from over 7000 birthing parents and their children within the HBCD study enables research on pre- and postnatal exposures (e.g., substance use, toxicants, nutrition), and biological processes (e.g., genetics, epigenetic signatures, proteins, metabolites) on neurobehavioral developmental outcomes. The following biosamples are collected from the birthing parent: 1) blood (i.e., whole blood, serum, plasma, buffy coat, and dried blood spots) during pregnancy, 2) nail clippings during pregnancy and one month postpartum, 3) urine during pregnancy, and 4) saliva during pregnancy and at in-person postnatal assessments. The following samples are collected from the child at in-person study assessments: 1) saliva, 2) stool, and 3) urine. Additionally, placenta tissue, cord blood, and cord tissue are collected by a subset of HBCD sites. Here, we describe the rationale for the collection of these biospecimens, their current and potential future uses, the collection protocol, and collection success rates during piloting. This information will assist research teams in the planning of future studies utilizing this collection of biological samples.Keywords: HBCD, biospecimens, substance use; Environmental exposures; Prenatal, fetal programming

Introduction

Human brain development is a complex and dynamic process that begins prenatally and continues throughout the lifespan (Kelley and Pașca, 2022, Palmer and Jernigan, 2023). During the third week of human gestation, neural progenitor cells divide and differentiate into neurons and glia that ultimately form the cellular basis of the brain (Stiles and Jernigan, 2010). The late prenatal period and early infancy are characterized by extensive genetically organized but adaptive cellular events (e.g., neuro- and glial- and synapto-genesis, neural migration, myelination, and synaptic pruning) that drive the establishment and reorganization of brain structure and neural circuits ultimately influencing variability in complex biology, behavior, and health (Zhou et al., 2024). Given rapid prenatal and early childhood neurodevelopment, environmental experiences (e.g., deprivation, enrichment) and exposures (e.g. substances (i.e. opioids, tobacco, marijuana and alcohol), toxicants, and nutrients)) during these sensitive periods may affect developmental trajectories (e.g., psychopathology, physical health, cognition) across the lifespan (Barker, 2004). However, the mechanisms through which prenatal and early childhood experiences, exposures, and other factors (e.g., genetically programmed developmental changes) contribute to variability in these outcomes remain poorly understood.

The HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study is a 27-recruitment-site prospective longitudinal birthing parent-infant cohort study (nanticipated > 7000 birthing parent-child dyads; https://hbcdstudy.org/) designed to examine how prenatal and postnatal experiences and mechanisms influence neural, cognitive, behavioral, social, emotional, and health outcomes in children (Nelson et al.,; Volkow et al., 2021). The multimodal study was designed by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) (Volkow et al.,) and scientists across the United States (US) working collaboratively to curate a unified study protocol. This manuscript outlines the rationale for the selection of biospecimens; collection, processing, and storage protocols; and current, planned, and potential future uses of biospecimens in the HBCD Study. Phenotypic depth and scientific rigor were balanced with participant burden, site capacity, and study logistics. The objectives of this manuscript are two-fold: 1) to provide detailed information about the current HBCD biosampling and biobanking research protocol with rationale and a description of ongoing and planned analyses; and 2) to inform a larger scientific community about the open data source platform and extensive biobanking efforts which will allow the pursuit of novel hypotheses and investigations in the future.

HBCD study protocol

Briefly, the HBCD cohort is recruited to be representative of the U.S. population of pregnant individuals between 18 and 50 years of age who gave birth in the last 12 months (Jones Harden et al., submitted; Si et al., submitted). This approach ensures external validity and generalizability of results. Given the objectives of the HBCD study to characterize typical neurodevelopmental trajectories, assess effects of early life exposure to opioids, marijuana, alcohol, tobacco and/or other substances, and to assess the effect of prenatal and postnatal environment on neurodevelopment, participants are recruited into the three study groups. These groups include: 1) a racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse cohort that is representative of the US population (50 % of the overall sample); 2) pregnant persons with use of targeted substances (25 % of the overall sample); and 3) demographically and behaviorally similar women without substance use in pregnancy to enable valid causal inferences (25 % of the overall sample). Four primary substances of interest are opioids, marijuana, alcohol, tobacco; however, we expect a large proportion of individuals with polysubstance use. The similar non-Substance Using (snSU) group is recruited to ensure that the cohort has an adequate representation of socioeconomic and other confounders in both exposed and unexposed groups. Study design, eligibility criteria, and recruitment and retention strategies are described in detail in other manuscripts in this journal ((Gurka et al.,, Jones Harden et al.,, Nelson et al.,, Si et al.,).

The HBCD Study includes multimodal in-person and remote sessions (Fig. 1). In addition to biospecimen collection, the study also collects questionnaire, interview, biosensor, behavioral, electroencephalogram (EEG), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data to assess the following domains in the child: 1) physical health (Cioffredi et al.,), 2) behavior and caregiver-child interaction (Edwards et al.,), 3) neurocognition/language (Kable et al.,), 4) neurodevelopment (Dean et al submitted, Fox et al.,), 5) prenatal environmental exposures including substance use (Gurka et al.,), 6) social and environmental determinants of health (Cioffredi et al.,), and 7) infant activity and sleep (Pini et al.,). The HBCD Consortium Administrative Core (HCAC) provides overall coordination, management, and direction, and the HBCD Data Coordinating Center (HDCC) oversees participant, data, and recruitment tracking as well as generated data (Nelson et al.,).

HBCD Study Visit and Assessment Timeline. The HBCD Study is assessing prenatal and early childhood development using a variety of methods including caregiver-report, interviews, biomarkers and measures of child brain development, behavior, and physiology.

Biospecimens are a cornerstone of the HBCD Study because they can be used to generate reliable and objective estimates of environmental exposures (e.g., substance, toxicant exposure, nutrition) and putative biological mechanisms (e.g., genetics, epigenetics, proteins) that may influence neural, cognitive, behavioral, social, and emotional development. The following construct domains were considered and mapped to biological matrices: 1) substance exposure during pregnancy (ascertained from birthing parent urine, nails, dried blood spot (DBS) samples), 2) other environmental exposures (toxicants, nutrition) during the prenatal and postnatal periods (birthing parent urine, serum, plasma, whole blood; child urine, child stool), 3) genomics (birthing parent saliva, buffy coat; child saliva), and 4) biological function (birthing parent serum, plasma, whole blood, DBS, urine; child urine, child stool). The following factors were considered when selecting biospecimen type: utility of the biomarker to assess the impact on brain development, participant and site burden, cultural acceptability, logistics and cost of collection, storage and transportation, invasiveness, ability to capture a broad range of possible targets and highly sensitive and specific biomarkers, and expertise and infrastructure at sites. With the exception of birthing parent samples that are being analyzed for substance exposure (described below) to track recruitment goals (i.e., to ensure that 25 % of the sample is composed of individuals using substances (opioids, marijuana, alcohol, tobacco and/or other substances), all other samples are being stored in a biorepository for use in future research.

Biospecimens collection protocol

During the formative stages of the HBCD Study, participant preferences and site capabilities surrounding the research protocol were examined. The acquisition of biosamples emerged as a key aspect of the HBCD study that could impact recruitment and retention as well as site participation (Goldstein et al., 2021a, Goldstein et al., 2021b). The HBCD study biospecimens protocol was developed to balance scientific utility with participant acceptability and site capacity. In particular, sample matrices and collection protocols were chosen to minimize invasiveness and participant burden and address participant concerns (e.g., concern that urine substance testing would be conducted postpartum) while maintaining high scientific utility.

This manuscript differentiates samples collected from an enrolled adult participant who gave birth to a child - a birthing parent (also referring to a pregnant individual for prenatal collection) and the child followed throughout. Biospecimens (Table 1) from birthing parents (i.e., blood, nails, saliva, urine) and children (i.e., saliva, stool, urine) are collected alongside in-person assessments (Fig. 1) at Visit 1 (V1; prenatal; 2nd or third trimester), V2 (0–1 month), V3 (3–9 months), V4 (9–15 months), and V6 (15–48 months) with continued collection planned for future in-person assessments (the planned V4 assessments and beyond are current at the time of manuscript, but may change following session piloting). With the exception of child stool samples, which can be remotely acquired from participants at home by caregivers, all samples are collected during in-person visits by research staff and/or caregivers. To ensure consistency across sites, standard operating procedures were developed, and training was provided. Sites piloted collection, processing, storage, and shipping with oversight from HCAC site monitors and consultation from the HBCD Biospecimens Working Group (HBCD BIO WG). Currently, the only biosamples being analyzed are birthing parent nail, dried blood spot and urine samples for substance testing; all other samples are being stored for future planned and unplanned analyses.

Sample collection, shipping materials and storage are provided by SAMPLED (www.sampled.com), a biorepository and bioprocessing facility that receives and stores the majority of HBCD biospecimens. Samples for substance testing (birthing parent urine, nails, and DBSs) are sent to the US Drug Testing Laboratory (USDTL, Des Plaines, IL; https://www.usdtl.com/). Collection and storage materials are registered to each participant using the Longitudinal Online Research and Imaging System (LORIS; https://loris.ca/), a database with front-end web-interface; its implementation is overseen by the HDCC. LORIS is used to track materials, processing, samples, shipping, storage, the biospecimen generated data, collection and processing success rates, and recruitment goals related to biospecimen outcomes.

Birthing parent samples: rationale, collection, processing, storage, and shipment protocol

Blood, nails, saliva, and urine samples are collected from the birthing parent at the prenatal visit (V1) to permit alignment with clinical visits and to minimize participant burden. These samples are acquired to characterize substance exposures during pregnancy as well as in utero biological processes. Saliva samples are acquired at each in-person session to characterize genetics (i.e., genotypes; epigenetic signatures). Repeated sampling of saliva samples was done to permit the examination of epigenetic and mitochondrial DNA changes in the birthing parent alongside the child. Nail samples are also acquired at V2 to characterize substance exposures during later stages of pregnancy. Repeated sampling of birthing parent blood, and urine, was considered but ultimately not included due to concerns surrounding recruitment and retention, participant burden, and site capacity and study focus. The primary focus of the study is understanding early child development and health; however, examination of pregnancy outcomes, parenting behavior, and birthing parent health will also be possible.

Blood

Blood is collected from the birthing parent during the prenatal visit to characterize environmental exposures and biomarkers that may influence prenatal development. Collection. During V1, 20 ml of blood is collected in 10 ml red top serum, 6 ml royal blue top K2EDTA, and 4 ml purple top K2EDTA tubes to acquire serum, whole blood, and plasma, respectively, as well as a buffy coat sample. Royal blue top whole blood samples, which are designed to reduce contamination, were acquired to facilitate trace element and environmental toxicant research. To permit alignment with clinical visits and other HBCD assessments in V1, samples are collected at any time of day and participants are not instructed to fast (Nordestgaard, 2017, van Kerkhof et al., 2015). Processing. Standardized blood processing procedures are followed (Tuck et al., 2009) and individual aliquots are frozen within 2 hours. Specifically, serum and plasma tubes are centrifuged (1600 x g) at room temperature for 10 minutes. Serum (500 μl, n=10) and plasma (500 μl, n=1; 100 μl, n=10) aliquots and a buffy coat sample are distributed to cryovials. The whole blood tube is inverted 15 times and aliquoted onto 2 DBS cards (5 circles/card, ∼65 μl/circle; left to dry for 2–24 hours) and 500 μl aliquots (n=10) in cryovials. Cryovials are held in a micronic box on ice during aliquoting and stored in a −80°C freezer. After DBS cards have dried, they are placed in 2 separate biohazard bags with desiccant with one stored at room temperature for later substance testing and the other stored at −80°C.

Urine

Urine is collected at V1 study visits to assess prenatal substance exposures and for future studies of proteins and environmental exposures. We opted to obtain the urine sample at any time of day to reduce participant burden. Collection. A clean catch urine sample is collected from the birthing parent into a sterile collection cup at V1. Processing. The sample is stored at 4°C or on wet ice and processed within 2 hours of collection. A single 30 ml aliquot is made for substance testing at USDTL, and ten 2 ml aliquots are frozen at −80°C before being sent to SAMPLED for long-term storage.

Nails

Birthing parent nail clippings are collected at V1 and V2 to index substance exposure for up to 3–6 months, in the prenatal and early postpartum periods (Bandoli et al., 2024). Collection. Birthing parents clip their toenails (preferred) or fingernails (alternative) (ideally>100 mg) after washing their hands and removing nail polish (non-ethanol-based polish remover). Processing. Nail samples are weighed and stored at room temperature in a foil pouch inside a sealed specimen envelope. V2 samples, which would capture late pregnancy exposure, are currently sent at room temperature to USDTL for substance testing. The 14-analyte panel includes amphetamines, cannabinoids, cocaine, opiates, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, methadone, oxycodone, tramadol, fentanyl, buprenorphine, ketamine, alcohol (ethyl glucuronide), and tobacco (cotinine) biomarkers. The Consortium is planning to analyze V1 samples, which generally capture early pregnancy exposure, using the same substance panel in the near future. As the substance testing protocol typically uses all available nail samples (100 mg), no additional samples are stored for future analyses.

Saliva

Saliva samples are collected from the birthing parent at every in-person session for future genomics analyses. Repeated samples will allow for evaluation of changes in epigenetic markers over time, which can be contrasted from the epigenetic signatures in the child. Additionally, these samples will also permit mitochondrial DNA measures for the parent across time. Collection. Participants are provided with manufacturer instructions and the collection device (DNA Genotek OG-600). No food or drink (other than water) is permitted for 30 minutes prior to collection. Processing. A research staff member inverts the sample multiple times; the sample is stored on site at room temperature before shipment to SAMPLED. Upon receipt at SAMPLED, DNA is extracted for long-term storage (see Supplemental Information).

Child samples: rationale, collection, processing, storage, and shipment protocol

Saliva, fecal, and urine samples are collected from the child at all in-person assessments to assess postnatal exposure to environmental toxicants, the gut microbiome, gut metabolome, and DNA for genetic and epigenetic studies.

Saliva

Saliva samples are collected from the child at every in-person session to acquire genetic material. Collection. The collection protocol follows as described for adults with 2 exceptions: 1) an assisted sampling device is used by the caregiver or research staff (V1-V5: DNA Genotek OC-175; V6: DNA Genotek OG-675) and 2) the caregiver or research staff may also rub the cheeks of the child to stimulate saliva collection. Processing. Following collection, the RA transfers the saliva from the sponges according to manufacturer instructions before sealing the tube, shaking it vigorously and storing the sample at room temperature before shipping to SAMPLED. Upon receipt at SAMPLED, DNA is extracted for long-term storage (see Supplemental Information).

Urine

Urine is collected at every in-person session to facilitate examination of environmental exposures and proteins. Collection. Cotton balls (n=6–15) are placed in the infant’s diaper, which is checked regularly during breaks. If a bowel movement occurs simultaneously, then urine is only collected from cotton balls that are not contaminated with stool. For potty-trained children, samples are acquired using a toilet hat on a regular toilet (while disabling automatic flushing) or using a portable potty. If a bowel movement also occurs in the potty, the urine sample is not used. Multiple collection attempts are permitted. Processing. For samples collected from diapers, cotton balls are placed in a syringe to express urine (∼6 ml) into a collection cup. If a simultaneous bowel movement occurred, cotton balls away from contamination are used. For potty trained children, a syringe is used to aliquot into a sterile urine cup. Urine samples are placed on ice or in a refrigerator prior to processing. Three 1.5 ml aliquots are made and placed within a −80°C freezer within 2 hours of collection.

Fecal sample

Stool samples are collected from children at every in-person assessment for future gut microbiome and metabolome research. Collection. Child stool samples are collected using a microbiome (OMNImet GUT OMR-200) and metabolome (OMNImet GUT ME-200) collection kit sent home with the caregiver. Parents are provided with collection instructions (e.g., collect within 30 minutes; contamination concerns; placing sample; time and date logging) to collect samples from a fresh diaper (or use a provided paper toilet hat or potty if the child is potty trained). The sample is stored at room temperature for up to 4 days prior to their in-person visit. Collection is attempted at in-person visits if the family does not bring a sample to the visit. Samples are placed in a −80°C freezer within 7 days of collection.

Projected measures

In addition to continued collection of the biosamples described above, the HBCD BIO WG recommends the collection of shed deciduous teeth when the child reaches 5–7 years of age and child blood via venipuncture/finger prick when the child is 8–10 should HBCD Study data collection continue. Offspring deciduous teeth can be used to detect a variety of exposures (e.g., heavy metals, acetaminophen, phthalates, zinc, calcium) and an estimated timing of exposure (Barton, 2011; Bayo et al., 2001; Camann et al., 2013; Johnston et al., 2019; Nedoklan et al., 2021; Palmer and Jernigan, 2023; Shepherd et al., 2012). The collection of child blood would allow characterization of the child’s postnatal environment including nutrition, toxicants, substances, and biological factors (i.e. inflammation) that may influence complex behavior and biology.

Logistics: sample collection kits, shipping, and biorepository storage

Biospecimen collection and shipping (IATA and federal compliant) kits (barcoded collection supplies and cryovials; participant logging form/s) are designed and assembled at SAMPLED and shipped to sites (Supplemental Table 1). This process minimizes sample variability by ensuring that the same supplies are used consistently across sites. Kits are assigned IDs that are tracked allowing for reconciliation of collection events within and across subjects using LORIS. Each collection supply is registered to an individual barcode at SAMPLED that is scanned by each site to register the supply to the sample and link samples to de-identified participant IDs in LORIS, which sends this linked information to SAMPLED, for sample tracking. Samples are shipped overnight with sufficient dry ice (40–60 lbs) to maintain sample integrity with delays. Tracking information is logged in LORIS. Upon receipt, SAMPLED staff inspect packages and contents and accession (including a digital photo of each specimen), register, and store samples on site. Notification of receipt, delayed shipment, accessioning events and deviation codes (for samples that do not meet study criteria) are automatically generated at the time of sample accessioning and laboratory validation leading to instantaneous electronic communication of these events to the submitting site and HBCD leadership using LORIS. Sample accessioning at SAMPLED allows each sample to be electronically tracked through storage and laboratory processing workflows. This biorepository is Accredited by the College of American Pathologists (CAP) and equipped with safeguards that include temperature monitoring, alarms, as well as backup storage and power.

Pilot data collection and changes to protocol

Biospecimens pilot data collection for the first 3 study visits (birthing parent saliva, blood, nails, urine; child saliva, urine, stool) was completed at each site from July-December 2023. Sites were permitted to collect samples separate from other study session components. Parent consent was obtained from all participants for the pilot study. Analyses of pilot data revealed high rates of success for birthing parent samples as well as child saliva but challenges with child urine and stool collection (Fig. 2). Hence, stool collection was modified for remote collection at home and urine collection was transitioned from urine bags (U-bags), which when sticking out of the diaper distracted children, to cotton balls inside diapers as described above. Additionally, birthing parent nail samples were often of insufficient weight for the planned substance testing. Thus, sites were given instructions, additional training, and required to weigh samples and log this information into LORIS; additionally, the USDTL analytic protocol was modified to reduce the amount of sample required (Supplemental Information; Supplemental Table 4). As pilot participants reported comfort with our nail collection protocol, we added birthing parent V1 collection to provide greater temporal coverage of biological substance testing in earlier pregnancy and align with self-report. Assessment of DNA from child samples revealed sufficient DNA yield to generate genome-wide and epigenome-wide data (n=55; average: 5422 ng DNA, range 801–29,409 ng)

Alternative biosamples considered

Several other sample types were considered by the BIO WG (Croff et al., 2020) in consultation with NIH, but not selected for the main study protocol to balance participant burden, established utility of each sample, and consistency of sample availability. These included samples (Birthing parent: erythrocytes, stool, milk, hair, vaginal swabs, RNA saliva; Child: meconium, venipuncture or heel/finger pricks or capillary blood collection) that would permit additional assessments of nutrition, substance/toxicant exposure, biomarkers, microbiome, and transcriptomics. The BIO WG recommended collection of umbilical cord blood, umbilical cord tissue, and the placenta to measure substance exposure, biomarkers, and neurotoxicant exposure. Although these samples were not included in the final consortium-wide protocol due to site capabilities, 11 sites were awarded supplemental funds to collect these delivery samples.

Substance exposure analyses

Birthing parent’s nail clippings, urine, and DBS are tested for substance exposures at USDTL, typically within 1–2 months of collection. A broad substance testing panel, with add-on ethanol biomarker ethyl glucuronide (EtG), is tested in urine and nail clippings (see Supplemental Tables 2 and 3 for a list of substance panels). In DBS, the direct alcohol metabolite, phosphatidylethanol (PEth), is assayed using a published procedure (Jones et al., 2011) see Supplemental Information). PEth is detectable following a single alcohol consumption for 3–12 days and more regular use for 2–4 weeks; it correlates with past month use (Nanau and Neuman, 2015, Schröck et al., 2017). Substances are assayed from birthing parent urine and nails using validated immunoassay or laser diode thermal desorption tandem mass spectrometry (Supplemental Information). Presumptive positive specimens are confirmed using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) or 2-dimensional gas chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS) methods validated according to the Standard Practices for Method Validation in Forensic Toxicology (AAFS Standards Board, 2019). Ethyl glucuronide (EtG) in nails is determined using a validated LC-MS/MS method.

Recommended future use of samples

Aside from substance exposure testing, all other samples are banked and stored at SAMPLED for future use. The HBCD BIO WG recommends additional analyses in three primary domains: 1) environmental exposures; 2) genomics and epigenomics; and 3) biomarkers that may contribute to neurodevelopment (Croff et al., 2020). Sample aliquoting, describing in Section 3, was done at each collection site to minimize freeze-thaw cycles, to facilitate the following recommended analytes.

Environmental exposures

Nutrition

Prenatal and postnatal nutrition (e.g., undernutrition, overnutrition, micronutrients) plays a major role in brain and behavioral development (Arija and Canals, 2021, Cortés-Albornoz et al., 2021a, Gressens et al., 1997a, Matonti et al., 2020a, Urbonaite et al., 2022a). For example, micronutrients and vitamins (e.g., folate deficiency early in pregnancy results in neural tube defects) and trace metals (e.g., iron and zinc), play important roles in neurodevelopment (e.g., energy metabolism, dendritic and synapse development, initiation of myelination, neurotransmitter synthesis) as well as later psychiatric (e.g., ADHD) problems and intellectual disability (Lozoff and Georgieff, 2006, Lynch et al., 2018, Shaw et al., 2004, Wiegersma et al., 2019). Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids play a major role in neurodevelopment as they are a major structural component of the brain. The fetal brain rapidly accumulates omega-3 fatty acids, primarily DHA, in late stages of pregnancy (Dighriri et al., 2022). DHA is a key regulator of brain structure and function, including roles in cell signaling, neurogenesis, gene expression regulation, and neurotransmission, and is associated with vision and cognitive growth (Devarshi et al., 2019). Of particular relevance for HBCD, the use of substances can also impact nutrient metabolism and transport, making nutrition a plausible mechanism through which prenatal-substance exposure-related outcomes may emerge. For example, smoking during pregnancy decreases materno-fetal transport of iron and is associated with reduced serum folate and reduced folate consumption (Pateva et al., 2015). Poor nutrition and deficiency in micronutrients are often associated with lower socioeconomic status. Thus, nutrition may contribute to associations between low SES and alterations to child development. It is also critical that factors associated with lower resources that are known to influence child brain and behavioral development, such as neurotoxicant exposures, trauma, and stress are considered in these studies. Biological assessments of nutrition help avoid bias and inaccuracies associated with self-report as well individual differences (e.g., microbiome) influencing how nutrients are processed (Kuhnle, 2012). We recommend assaying the following micronutrients that have been identified to be related to offspring brain development: B-6 (pyridoxine), B-9 (folate), B-12 (cobalamin), iron, choline, zinc and omega-3 and omega 6 fatty acids (Cheatham, 2020; Cortés-Albornoz et al., 2021) and macronutrients including dietary protein levels (Gressens et al., 1997b, Matonti et al., 2020b), and dietary fat composition and levels (Cortés-Albornoz et al., 2021b, Urbonaite et al., 2022b) which have been shown to have a long-term influence on child physical and mental health.

Toxicants

Pre- and post-natal exposures to toxicants (e.g., heavy metals, chemicals) are associated with adverse birth outcomes, developmental delays, and long-term health. Lead, for example, is a well-known neurotoxicant, with risks elevated when exposures occur prenatally (Bellinger et al., 1987), and specifically during the first and second trimesters (Cantonwine et al., 2010). Prenatal lead exposures are associated with lower birth weight (Wang et al., 2020) and preterm labor (Vigeh et al., 2011). There is evidence of synergistic influence between multiple toxicants (i.e., dose-dependent developmental delays with exposure to lead and cadmium (Kim et al., 2013)) and between toxicants and other biological exposomes of interest (i.e. exposure to lead and maternal stress (Zhou et al., 2017); exposure to lead and polygenic risk score for developmental delay increasing toxicant effect). Prenatal exposures to phthalates, as measured in maternal urine, has been associated with developmental delay (Park et al., 2023) and poorer executive functioning as toddlers (Choi et al., 2021). As with lead, first and second trimester exposures to phthalates were more closely associated with adverse birth outcomes (Santos et al., 2021). Like other heavy metals, blood testing is the gold standard for measurement, with half-life of 36-days from a single exposure in adults (Todd et al., 1996), with infants and children experiencing a half-life of approximately 10 months from a single exposure and 20–38 month half-life from prolonged exposures (Manton et al., 2000). Urinary phthalates have a relatively short half-life (less than 5 hours), so measures are thought to indicate recent phthalate exposure rather than accumulation in the body (Frederiksen et al., 2007). However, there is evidence that the concentration of most urinary phthalates is stable across pregnancy (Rosen et al., 2023). To measure toxicant exposure in HBCD we recommend assaying birthing parent prenatal blood (e.g., whole blood) and urine samples as well as child postnatal urine for essential trace elements (i.e. Copper (Cu), Selenium (Se) and Zinc (Zn)), heavy metals (i.e Lead (Pb), Cadmium (Cd), and Mercury (Hg), and toxic metalloids such as Arsenic (As), fluoride, phthalates, organophosphate pesticides, pyrethroid pesticides, organophosphate esters (sometimes referred to as organophosphate flame retardants), Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs). As noted in Section 3, whole blood was acquired in royal blue top tubes designed to minimize contaminants for the analysis of trace materials.

Genomics and epigenomics

Genomics

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have begun to characterize the polygenic architecture undergirding the moderate-large heritability of complex behavioral and biological traits and health outcomes (ENIGMA Genetics Working Group, 2022, Polderman et al., 2015, Sullivan and Geschwind, 2019). For example, GWASs have linked common genetic variation within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) to schizophrenia with subsequent work identifying elevated Complement component 4-driven neural pruning as a plausible mechanism underlying this risk (Schizophrenia Working Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, 2014, Sekar et al., 2016). As hundreds of thousands of individuals are needed to identify individual loci within GWASs (Sullivan and Geschwind, 2019), the HBCD Study, in isolation, is likely underpowered for single variant discovery, though pathway approaches may be viable. The integration of HBCD genetic data with data from other studies could usefully drive single variant discovery as evidenced by data sharing consortia (e.g., Psychiatric Genomics Consortium; ENIGMA (ENIGMA Genetics Working Group, 2022, Sullivan and Geschwind, 2019).

Genetic data generated within the HBCD Study via repeated birthing parent and child saliva collection (as well as birthing parent buffy coat) will propel developmental genomics investigation and facilitate the investigation of within-person change during critical developmental periods. For example, investigating how variation in the aforementioned MHC locus may impact brain structure and function trajectories may help bridge our molecular understanding of genomic vulnerability to systems level brain-based biology. Genomic data will also enable the characterization of the developmental course of polygenic architectures by examining genetic correlations between early developmental trajectories in HBCD with outcomes in later life (e.g., substance use disorders) (Bulik-Sullivan et al., 2015, Grotzinger et al., 2019). Common genetic variation, when weighted by their association with a given phenotype as a polygenic score (PGS), can reliably predict variance in the same and related phenotypes in much smaller independent samples (Wray et al., 2021). Such PGSs can be generated in the HBCD Study to examine how genomic risk for outcomes in later life may be set forth during early development. This may be particularly informative as genes involved in neurodevelopment are enriched in psychiatric risk (Grotzinger et al., 2022, Paul et al., 2023). PGS also facilitate better control for genetic liability confounders in order to examine the plausibility that environmental correlates may contribute to the emergence of individual differences (Baranger et al., 2024, Bogdan et al., 2023, Hatoum et al., 2022, Paul et al., 2021). The acquisition of genetic data from not just the child, but also the birthing parent, will enable the investigation of genetic-nurture (i.e., genetically-conferred environments – e.g., depression risk alleles not transmitted to offspring could be harnessed to represent a rearing environment that may not be captured well by isolated self-report assessments (Kong et al., 2018). Finally, increasing evidence links mitochondrial DNA variability as well as copy number variation to complex behavior/biology (e.g., cognition and brain structure) and stress, which could be studied in HBCD (Zhang et al., 2023). While the HBCD Study plans to generate genomic data, a critical decision to make going forward is whether GWAS array data and/ or sequencing data should be generated. The cost of generating, storing, and processing sequencing data is substantially greater than GWAS arrays, but would enable the investigation of rare and potentially de novo mutations, especially with long-read sequencing (Noyes et al., 2022). As the static DNA sequence does not change over time, any collected saliva samples could be used to generate genotypic data. Repeated sampling, particularly in children, where DNA yields are typically lower, will allow samples with lower DNA yield to be prioritized for epigenetic analyses, described below, while samples with higher concentrations could be used for both genotyping and epigenetic analyses.

Epigenomics.

Epigenetic signatures, which includes DNA methylation (DNAm), microRNAs (miRNAs), and histone modifications, refers to processes affecting gene expression other than static DNA sequence, have been linked to variability in behavior and biology (O’Donnell and Meaney, 2020, Walton et al., 2023). Work primarily conducted in animals has identified experience dependent differences in methylation that contribute to the intergenerational transmission of health and development. For example, methylation in the glucocorticoid receptor gene has been shown to arise from variability in maternal care and to perpetuate a stress sensitive phenotype across generations that may be conserved across species (McGowan et al., 2009, Palma-Gudiel et al., 2015, Turecki and Meaney, 2016, Weaver et al., 2004). Additionally, methylation of µ-opioid receptor 1 (OPRM1) and miRNAs present in umbilical cord blood have been implicated in vulnerability and severity of Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) (Mahnke et al., 2022, Wachman et al., 2014). The generation of methylation data in the HBCD would allow for methylation-phenotype associations in early development to be revealed and the probing of a putative biological mechanism through which experience translates to biological function. Repeated sampling of saliva samples for genomic analyses was done so that epigenetic changes over time in the child and birthing parent can be investigated as putative mechanisms of developmental and environmental (e.g., parenting) variability. Further, examining the extent to which epigenetic signatures diverge or are consistent across generations (i.e., birthing parent, and child) may contribute to our understanding of divergence and concordance in health outcomes across generations.

Biological markers of neurodevelopment and putative biological mechanisms

Inflammation

Several lines of evidence support the role of the inflammatory environment experienced by the fetus in influencing neurobehavioral development and risk for mental health disorders. The fetal brain has a wide distribution of receptors for inflammatory factors, which play a role in neurodevelopment, including processes such as synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning, myelination, neuronal and glial cell proliferation and migration. Elevated prenatal inflammation (e.g., CRP, IL-6) is associated with psychopathology (e.g., depression, ADHD), behavior (e.g., memory), and related neural phenotypes (e.g., brain structure) (Oades, 2011, Oades et al., 2010, Rudolph et al., 2018, Terness et al., 2002, Zanoli et al., 2001). Increasing evidence highlights elevated inflammatory signaling during the prenatal period as a potential major modulator of child development and behavior (Buehler, 2011, Onore et al., 2012). For example, non-human primate offspring prenatally exposed to pro-inflammatory antibodies from mothers of human children with ASD displayed increased anxiety (Martin et al., 2008). Although inflammation is typically thought of as occurring in response to infection or injury, common early environmental factors, such as birthing parent obesity (Basu et al., 2011, Radaelli et al., 2003, Roberts et al., 2011), stress (Clayborne et al., 2023, Graham et al., 2022) and substance use (Collaborative Initiative on Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (CIFASD), 2018, Rompala et al., 2021) have been associated with low-grade chronic inflammation. A mounting body of work suggests that exposure to either acute inflammatory events (i.e., infection) or chronic inflammation can influence child neurobehavioral outcomes. Measuring inflammatory factors in the HBCD Study will allow examination of an important potential mechanism by which early environmental factors, including substance use, influence child neurobehavioral development. To examine inflammation in the prenatal environment, we recommend assaying a comprehensive array of cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory factors (i.e. IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-1RA, IL-12p70, IL-17A, TNF-α, eotaxin, G-CSF, MCP-1β) in the serum and/or plasma collected from the birthing parent using a combination of multiplex assays and high-sensitivity ELISAs along with proteomic arrays is recommended.

Gut microbiome

The gut microbiome (GM) has compositionally and functionally diverse microorganisms that dramatically influence host physiology, metabolism, immune function, brain structure and function, health, as well as child development (Hou et al., 2022, Ronan et al., 2021, Vos et al., 2022). Co-twin control designs as well as induced changes in brain behavior (e.g., neuroinflammation, depression, cognition) within fecal microbiota transplant experiments provide evidence that the GM causally contributes to these outcomes (Choi et al., 2023, Erny et al., 2015, Hartstra et al., 2020, Huang et al., 2023, Kelly et al., 2016, Turnbaugh et al., 2009, Vendrik et al., 2020). Given evidence that the most widely used approach for investigating the microbiome, 16S rDNA sequencing, incompletely characterizes GM species and their function, shotgun metagenomic sequencing could be used to increase species and strain resolution for taxonomic profiling within the HBCD Study (Blanco-Míguez et al., 2023, Caudill and Brayton, 2022, Chiu and Miller, 2019, Jeong et al., 2021, Johnson et al., 2019).

Metabolome

Emerging data suggests a role of the gut and fecal metabolome, including essential amino acids, phosphatidylcholines, volatile organic compounds, and lipid and inflammatory metabolites, on offspring growth and neurocognitive outcomes (De Angelis et al., 2013, Moreau et al., 2019). For example, children with developmental disorders including ASD have altered levels of free amino acids and volatile organic compounds, compared to healthy children (De Angelis et al., 2013). Changes in the infant fecal metabolome have also been associated with feeding patterns and neurodevelopmental scores (Chalifour et al., 2023). These findings suggest that distinct sets of metabolites associated with offspring growth and neurocognition may offer opportunities for targeted interventions to improve outcomes. As the gut and fecal metabolome can be affected by postnatal factors (Ouyang et al.), the HBCD Study provides a unique opportunity to examine the relationship between the gut metabolome and the composition of gut microbiota, early environmental factors including substance use, nutrition, and delivery mode. Furthermore, metabolomic findings from the HBCD Study can be linked to offspring growth, neurodevelopment and behavior to identify metabolic profiles potentially predictive of growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Future access to samples

Future access to de-identified banked biospecimens will be made available within a centralized process, similar to the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (https://nida.nih.gov/funding/nida-funding-opportunities/nih-brain-development-cohorts-biospecimen-access-program) via the NIH Brain Development Cohorts Biospecimen Access Program (https://nbdc.nida.nih.gov/request-biospecimens). It is expected that access will require approval from the HBCD Study, demonstrated financial resources for proposed analyses, rigorous methodological approaches, demonstrated relevant analysis expertise, and robust confidentiality protections to respect contribution of participants and limited nature of resource. Additionally, it is expected that requests from the broader scientific community will follow the general principles of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), ethical and cultural considerations described below. Relatedly, future analysis will also require adherence to data sharing requirements (https://sharing.nih.gov) as set forth by the National Institute of Health to support maximal public good.

Ancillary studies to collect delivery biospecimens

There are currently two ancillary HBCD biospecimen collection studies. The first includes the collection and banking of delivery samples (umbilical cord blood, umbilical cord tissue, and placental tissue [basal plate, chorionic villus, chorionic plate]) at 11 HBCD sites for planned substance testing (umbilical cord tissue) and future research (e.g., genetic and epigenetic analyses, environmental toxicants). The second ancillary study, called HEALthy Brain Child Development Enhanced (HBCD-E), takes place at 4 HBCD sites and collects additional blood samples from the birthing parent as well as placental samples for targeted downstream assays to examine how variability in birthing parent immune activation, fetoplacental development, and infant and child outcomes may emerge. Both ancillary studies leverage the extensive maternal and child phenotyping already being performed as part of the main/parent HBCD Study, and all participants in the ancillary studies are co-enrolled in HBCD.

Consideration of diversity, equity and inclusion

Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) principles, ethical and cultural considerations, as well as legal implications were paramount to the development of the HBCD biospecimen collection protocol (Murray et. al.,). In particular, the HBCD BIO WG carefully considered cultural practices and sensitives surrounding the collection of potential biospecimen matrices, participant burden, and the utility and quality of data these matrices may generate relative to alternative matrices. Below, we discuss considerations regarding the collection of material for genetic analyses, testing of substance exposure, collection of keratinized samples, as well as confidentiality.

Genetic material

A major consideration was the collection of genetic material given broad representation of racial and ethnic groups in HBCD, including groups with multiple intersecting vulnerabilities, and concern for genetic information being conflated with the social construct of race. The need for more diversity in genetic research needed to be carefully balanced with participant perspectives on genetic testing (Martin et al., 2022). A systematic review, which included studies focusing on how people who identify as Black perceive genetic testing, concluded that Black participants did not unambiguously support or oppose race-based genetic testing, but rather identified a series of potential benefits and concerns (Iltis et al., 2023). Conflation of race, genetic ancestry, and culture was identified as a concern. Given historical injustice and stigmatizing research practices which deeply affected many American Indians Alaskan Native (AIAN) communities in the past, ethical and cultural guiding principles of biospecimen collection among AIAN individuals were developed during the planning stages of HBCD (Bakhireva et al., 2020). Based on recommendations for community engagement efforts with tribal communities and inclusion of AIAN members in community advisory boards to address sensitivities around genetic data, an opt-in approach was developed for collection of genetic material from participants (Bakhireva et al., 2020, Croff et al., 2020). Provisions were also incorporated into data collection and data sharing plans to ensure that results cannot be linked to a specific AIAN population, including in secondary data analyses and de-identified banked specimens by a broader scientific community.

Assessing substance exposures

The need to confirm self-reported substance use data in pregnancy by biomarkers (substance panel, ethanol and nicotine metabolites) was carefully balanced against patient confidentiality and legal protection. Given that testing for substance exposures in biological samples obtained from the child present complex ethical and legal considerations, which broadly vary by state, the BIO WG, in consultation with the Ethics WG (Kingsley et al.,), recommended assessment of substance exposures in samples obtained from a birthing parent instead (e.g., urine panel and nail clippings). Additionally, the collection of delivery samples (i.e., placenta, umbilical cord blood and tissue) in the ancillary studies offer an opportunity to confirm prenatal substance exposures.

Collection of keratinized samples

Cultural practices and acceptability for collection of all biospecimens, beyond genetic material, were identified at the formative stages of the HBCD. For example, careful consideration was given to different types of keratinized biospecimens, i.e., hair versus nail clippings. Cultural and spiritual significance of hair in AIAN communities, potentially low acceptability of hair collection for research purposes among Black participants, and potential biases due to differential uptake and retention of substances based on the hair texture were identified as significant concerns (Kidwell et al., 2000, Wright et al., 2018). Previously identified barriers to collection of hair samples from Black, Hispanic, and multi-racial participants included hair length and hairstyles (particularly professionally styled braids and hair extensions) (Condon et al., 2020, Davidson and Gubrium, 2021). After conducting a systematic literature review on feasibility and validity of assessing analytes of interest in nail clipping samples (Bandoli et al., 2024), the BIO WG selected toenails (with fingernails as an alternative) over hair clippings as a keratinized matrix with a broad detection window.

The following substances are currently analyzed in nail clippings: amphetamines, cannabinoids, cocaine, opiates, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, methadone, oxycodone, tramadol, fentanyl, buprenorphine, ketamine, EtG (alcohol), cotinine (tobacco). Validity of four primary exposures of interest (opioids, alcohol, cannabinoids, nicotine) in nail clippings is presented in detail in the review conducted by HBCD investigators (Bandoli et al., 2024).

Confidentiality and other participant-focused considerations

In addition to standard research participant protections, such as collection and storage of only de-identified samples and legal protections offered by the NIH Certificate of Confidentiality, the results of substance testing are returned to sites in aggregate form only to facilitate recruitment target tracking. Detailed discussion of these legal and ethical issues is described elsewhere (Kingsley et al.,). Finally, minimal invasiveness, flexibility of collection approaches (e.g., during the study visit or at participant’s home, self-collected vs. collected by research personnel), and considerations to minimize the burden of the biosampling protocol were incorporated into the study design to ensure autonomy, acceptability of the research protocol by research participants, and high retention in this longitudinal study.

Summary and conclusions

The collection and banking of biospecimens from HBCD birthing parents and their children serves a critical role in the study, as it permits the integration of biological data with the patient-reported exposures and clinically-ascertained outcomes that are part of the detailed longitudinal study phenotyping. The ability for multi-modal integration of the biospecimen data with structural and functional neuroimaging, physiological, physical health, and behavioral developmental trajectory from prenatal to pre-adolescent period offer unprecedented opportunity to identify risk and resilience factors and inform novel interventions to improve outcomes in affected children. Broad biological domains captured by the HBCD biobanking protocol and longitudinal nature of the study, will allow not only for a comprehensive characterization of prenatal and postnatal exposures, but also will inform putative biological mechanisms underpinning variation in neurodevelopmental trajectories as well as identify crucial windows of vulnerability and intervention. Concerted efforts to aliquot collected samples and a well-characterized biorepository will allow a larger scientific community to pursue innovative research hypotheses and leverage rich resources of the largest longitudinal study of brain and development in the US.
