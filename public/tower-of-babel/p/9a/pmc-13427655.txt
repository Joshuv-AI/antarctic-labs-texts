Fast design of buoyancy-driven ventilation openings for multi-story buildings with shared shaft using analytical solutions
Authors: Tianhe Long, Dan Mei, Siwei Qin, Yongcai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBuoyancy-driven ventilation in multi-story buildings with shared shafts exhibits uneven effects across different stories. This study simplified Acred’s dimensionless design approach into user-friendly analytical solutions to balance the uneven effects. When openings are designed using the proposed analytical solutions, each story achieves close thermal comfort temperatures. The influence of key parameters in the analytical solutions and three application constraints was analyzed. The results show that when the total number of stories N increases from 1 to 25, the average shaft enhancement parameter increases from 1.00 to 2.27. As the top-story shaft enhancement parameter (EN, a design value) increases, the maximum total number of stories (Nmax) to which this method can be applied decreases sharply. Furthermore, near EN = 1.24, the governing constraint transitions from shaft roof area to critical area for bidirectional flow. The temperature gradient within the shaft leads to an approximately 11% deviation in practical performance.

Introduction

In line with China’s dual-carbon goals—peaking carbon emissions by 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality by 2060—there is an urgent need for effective strategies to reduce nationwide energy consumption. The building sector plays a significant role in this context, contributing approximately 20% and 50% of China’s total operational and life cycle carbon emissions, respectively.1 Therefore, implementing building energy efficiency measures contributes to achieving these two goals,2 among which natural ventilation is a promising solution.3 Ambient air at a suitable temperature serves as an ideal cooling source to offset building heat gains, reducing reliance on energy-intensive mechanical systems.4,5 Moreover, natural ventilation can enhance indoor air quality. Given that people spend the majority of their time indoors, indoor air quality significantly impacts both health and work efficiency.6,7 Well-designed natural ventilation can dilute pollutants and create a healthy indoor environment.8

Natural ventilation is driven by buoyancy and wind pressure. Compared to wind pressure, buoyancy-driven ventilation offers the following advantages: it correlates positively with indoor heat gain, promotes the formation of displacement flow, and is generally more predictable and controllable.9,10 Buoyancy-driven systems can be categorized based on their airflow loops. Single-loop systems, where airflow follows a single continuous loop without bifurcation,11 are relatively straightforward to analyze.12 For example, a single zone with two openings can only have one flow loop.13,14 In contrast, systems with multiple flow loops present greater complexity due to interactions between airflows from different loops.15,16 A typical example of multi-loop systems is a multi-story building with a shared shaft—such as a stairwell, light well, or atrium (hereinafter referred to as “shaft building”). The shaft zone can amplify the buoyancy effect induced by heat gains and enhance the overall ventilation performance of the building.17,18

Research progress has been made by some scholars regarding shaft buildings. Holford and Hunt19 proposed a “shaft enhancement parameter” to describe the impact of a shaft on story ventilation. A shaft enhancement parameter lower than 1.00 indicates that the shaft’s flow resistance exceeds its buoyancy-driven enhancing effect, while higher than 1.00 means the enhancing effect on ventilation is dominant. Hence, such buildings require reasonable design to ensure the positive effect of the shaft. To overcome the high-pressure difference caused by an excessively long shaft, Liu et al.20 proposed a vertical segmentation method for high-rise buildings. This method divides a high-rise building into several independent ventilation modules, each with a shaft and fewer stories. This method increases the number of stories suitable for shaft ventilation. Priyadarsini et al.21 studied the ventilation enhancement effect of a shaft on one story of a high-rise building, but their research did not involve the case that the shaft is shared by multiple stories. Lynch and Hunt22 conducted unsteady-state simulations on the buoyancy-driven ventilation of a shaft shared by two stories. Their study did not account for continuous zone heat gains, and the inclusion of jet models resulted in high computational complexity. Based on zone-averaged reduced gravitational acceleration,23 Acred and Hunt developed a dimensionless design approach for calculating the reasonable opening areas on shaft buildings.24 This approach uses ventilation areas to balance different shaft enhancement parameters and heat gains to achieve equal per capita airflow rates and excess temperatures across all stories.25 In summary, there is currently a lack of analytical design equations for buoyancy-driven ventilation in shaft buildings that can rapidly determine building opening areas. Furthermore, the influence of limiting factors for shaft ventilation on the design has not yet been sufficiently revealed.26

The novelties and key contributions of this study are summarized as follows. (1) The dimensionless design approach was simplified into two explicit analytical solutions. By substituting common parameters into the solutions, the design of the shaft building’s opening areas can be achieved in one step. (2) A parametric analysis was conducted on the proposed solutions, with their application limitations identified regarding building thermal performance, shaft roof area, and bidirectional flow occurrence. (3) A reduced-scale model designed based on the analytical solutions was experimentally tested. The results were used to validate the proposed method. This article is structured as follows: (1) the studied building type and derived analytical solutions; (2) comparison between theoretical values and experimental test results; (3) parametric analysis and application limits of the analytical solutions; (4) discussion and limitations of the study; (5) supplemental information and method details.

The studied building type and derived analytical solutions

Figure 1 shows the studied building type in this article (taking three stories as an example). As shown in Figure 1A, each story of the building has one lower opening and one upper opening for buoyancy-driven ventilation. The lower opening, which serves as the ventilation inlet, is connected to the surroundings, while the upper opening, functioning as the ventilation outlet, is connected to the shaft. Substituting the default values from Table 1 into Equation 14 yields an upper limit of 1.33 for the top-story ventilation enhancement parameter (EN). Therefore, the analysis considers an EN range from 1.00 to 1.33 (note that EN is a design value). Figures 5E and 5F present the variations in building opening areas with EN increases. The story opening area Ai gradually decreases as EN increases. For example, when EN increases to 1.33, A3 decreases from 0.46 to 0.30 m2. It indicates that an increase in EN enhances ventilation on each story. The shaft opening area As increases with EN, and its slope also gradually rises, ultimately exceeding As,cri (critical value for bidirectional flow). Therefore, the improvement in ventilation due to EN comes at the cost of an increased shaft opening area As and a higher risk of bidirectional flow. Consequently, EN should not be set too high in the design.

Discussion

It is noted that the distribution of reduced gravitational acceleration (g’) in the shaft has a minor influence on the proposed method. As the excess temperatures across stories are balanced to similar values, the gradient of shaft g’ is reduced by the comparable buoyant jet flows. However, if the heat gains differ significantly between stories, the interaction of buoyant jets with different characteristics within the shaft leads to the formation of complex distribution patterns. Figure S1 in the Supplemental Information section illustrates multiple such patterns obtained from the reduced-scale experiment by varying heat inputs in different zones. It is important to note the assumption of a uniformly distributed g’ becomes invalid for theoretical calculations when its gradient exceeds a threshold. On the other hand, the story excess temperature (ΔTi), as a design parameter in this study, reflects the potential of ventilation cooling. When the ambient temperature is low, the difference between the ambient temperature and target thermal comfort temperature is large, indicating high cooling potential. Conversely, when the ambient temperature is high, the available range for story excess temperature is narrowed. For example, assuming an ambient temperature of 24 °C and a target story temperature of 26 °C, the maximum excess temperature is only 2 °C. To remove the same amount of excess heat, it would require a larger ventilation opening area, significantly reducing feasibility. Under the given constraints, for the proposed method to be effective, the minimum achievable excess temperature in Figure 6 (ΔTi,min) must be less than the difference between the ambient temperature and the target control temperature.

Limitations of the study

(1) Since it is difficult to maintain airtightness while varying parameters such as the story height of the experimental model, it is worthwhile to conduct comprehensive validations for the analytical solutions using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) method in future work. (2) The effectiveness of the proposed method depends on the accurate estimation of heat gains. If heat gains are calculated by measuring the temperature and airflow rate at each opening, the method is limited by sensor accuracy. (3) When a story is divided into two zones by a partition wall and connected only through an opening on that wall, even if the average temperature is controlled to the comfort level, temperature disparity between the two partitioned zones may arise, potentially leading to control failure. Therefore, this method is not applicable to stories where significant local resistance exists internally. (4) Wind caps, installed to reduce the impact of unpredictable wind pressure, introduce additional resistance. Therefore, it is necessary to further consider the correction of this additional resistance on the effective ventilation area (see Equation 8). Moreover, even if the influence of wind is minimized, the design margin under adverse conditions should be considered. Furthermore, the proposed method is based on the premise of buoyancy-dominated flow. In practical applications, it is important to use dimensionless numbers (e.g., the ratio of the Grashof number to the square of the Reynolds number)28 to analyze whether the flow is buoyancy-dominated.

Through further investigation and development, the proposed analytical solutions are expected to serve as a reliable and rapid design tool for shaft buildings, with well-identified application limits, thereby contributing to reduced building energy consumption and healthier indoor environments.

Method details

Theoretical derivation

Dimensionless design approach

When hot air accumulates in the shaft, the ventilation enhancement parameter is highest for the first story, as its outlet is farthest from the shaft opening, allowing buoyancy to be fully utilized. Conversely, the parameter is lowest for the topmost story. When each story has equal heat gain, the ventilation enhancement parameter decreases progressively with story height; this reduction must be balanced by a corresponding increase in ventilation area. Acred24 derived Equations 1, 2, and 3 to balance the ventilation driving force differences across stories induced by variations in heat gain and shaft enhancement parameters. Under the building opening areas calculated from Equations 1, 2, and 3, all stories are regulated at a uniform temperature through buoyancy-driven ventilation. The theoretical analysis in this study also begins with Equations 1, 2, and 3.(Equation 1)Ai¯=1Ei3/2(Equation 2)Ei3=EN3+(N-i)(1+Bsˆ)(Equation 3)As¯=[1+(1+Bsˆ)ΔHˆ-EN3]-1/2Where, Ai¯ and As¯ are per capita dimensionless effective area for the ith story and shaft, respectively; Ei is shaft enhancement parameter of the ith story; N is the total number of stories; Bsˆ is dimensionless buoyancy flux for the shaft; ΔHˆ is dimensionless excess shaft height. The relationships between the dimensionless parameters in this study and the parameters with units are presented in Table S2 in Supplemental information section.

The definition and explanation of Ei are as follows.19 Consider a hypothetical case where the ith story of the shaft building is not connected to the shaft, making it an independent room with the same story height H (m), inlet area Ai,in (m2), outlet area Ai,out (m2), and heat gain Wi (W). Without the shaft’s impact, this independent room exhibits different airflow rate (Qi,iso, m3/s) and temperature (Ti,iso, K) compared to the shaft-connected scenario (Qi and Ti). The shaft enhancement parameter is defined as the ratio of Qi to Qi,iso.

Derivation of explicit analytical solutions

In this study, only the use of buoyancy force is considered for regulating the indoor environment of each story. The derivation process is based on the following assumptions: (1) Each opening is equipped with a wind-shielding cap to block wind pressure; (2) The heat gain and ventilation performance of the studied building are not influenced by adjacent buildings; (3) The building is ventilated solely through designed openings, with windows remaining closed for daylighting purpose; (4) The detailed temperature distribution within each zone is not considered, as the air is assumed to be fully mixed (see Figure S2). Design procedure

The design of a shaft building with vertical uniform buoyancy-driven ventilation using the derived results, follows these steps: (1) Select the target story temperature Ti, subtract the ambient temperature to obtain the story excess temperature ΔTi, and select the top-story shaft enhancement parameter EN. (2) Substitute the parameters into Equations 12 and 13 to calculate the opening areas. (3) Validate the results using Equations 15, 17, 18, 19, and 20. If the requirements are not met, return to step (1) to adjust the values of Ti and EN.

Reduced-scale experiment rig

Experimental design and measurement points arrangement

To investigate the actual operating performance of the building designed using the derived analytical solutions (Equations 12 and 13), a three-story reduced-scale building model was constructed and tested. The design parameter values are given in Table S3. The Froude number (Fr) was employed as the dimensionless parameter in the design of the reduced-scale experiment. After determining the geometric and temperature difference scales, we can derive the velocity, airflow rate, and heat gain scales based on the condition that the Froude number of the scaled-down model equals that of the full scale building; the detailed calculation process is provided in Equations 5 to 12 of Shi’s study.25 Table S4 summarizes all the scaling ratios used in the experiment. The dimensional comparison between the full scale building and the model is presented in Table S5. The dimensions of the experimental model are shown in Figure S3.

In Figure S3, the openings of each story were placed on horizontal plane, which is consistent with that of Linden’s experimental model.9 This is because buoyancy acts in the vertical direction, and measuring vertical airflow on a horizontal cross-section is more accurate. The cross-sectional area of the shaft is constant along its height, minimizing the flow resistance. Figure S4 shows a schematic of the experimental setup. The experimental model was placed in an enclosed area to eliminate the influence of wind on the experimental results. The bottom of the experimental model was placed on a base to reduce the influence of the ground on the airflow at the 1st story opening. The left, top, and right edges of the experimental model were kept at a distance from the wall surface of the enclosed area to reduce the influence of the wall surface on airflow. Electric heating films were placed in the stories and shaft of the experimental model to simulate heat gains. The heating film wires were connected to four voltage regulators outside the enclosed area, which adjust the heating power of 1st story, 2nd story, 3rd story, and shaft zone, respectively. K-type thermocouples were arranged in the stories, shaft, and openings of the experimental model to measure the air temperature data. The thermocouple wires were connected to a data acquisition instrument outside the enclosed area. The ambient temperature outside the building model in the enclosed area was also measured by thermocouples. The hot-wire anemometers were placed at the story and shaft openings, and the air velocity data were collected through wireless signals. The wall, floor, and roof materials of the experimental model are extruded polystyrene insulation board with a thickness of 4 cm, supported by steel angles. The gaps between the extruded polystyrene insulation boards were sealed with foam adhesive to ensure the airtightness. The heating powers of the four zones were initially set by using a power meter. To account for heat dissipation, adjustments were made based on thermal balance calculations. The experimental instruments, models and key parameters are summarized in Table S6.

The arrangements of the thermocouples, anemometers, and heat sources are shown in Figure S5. The volume or area occupied by each measuring point is equal. The arrangement of the thermocouples is denser along the vertical direction, as the buoyancy-driven flow exhibits stronger gradients in the vertical direction. Due to the entrainment of ambient air into the model room, the temperature gradient was expected to be steep near the air inlet. Therefore, to capture the temperature distribution in this entrainment zone, the thermocouples on the right side of the chamber were placed closer to the inlet. Within the temperature range measured, all the thermocouples were calibrated with mercury thermometers. The on-site experiment rig and measuring points are shown in Figure S6. The backs of the heating films were equipped with 1 cm thick heat insulation cotton to prevent high-temperature damage to the extruded polystyrene insulation boards and thereby ensure that nearly all of the heating power was transferred to the air. Aluminum foil was placed later in discussion the thermocouple probes to minimize radiative heat transfer from the heat source surfaces and thus improve the accuracy of the air temperature measurements.

The testing process for each experimental condition is as follows: (1) close the door of the enclosed experimental area to isolate the impact of external airflow; (2) measure the current and voltage of the main wire for the heat sources in each zone using the power meter, calculate the heating powers, and use the voltage regulators to preliminarily set the heating powers (for the design condition, Wi = 118 W, scaled from the full heat gain of 2000 W; Ws = 0 W); (3) turn on the data acquisition instrument, data acquisition computer, hot-wire anemometers, and wireless receiver, and set them to collect temperature and air velocity data every 20 s; (4) after the temperature and air velocity monitoring data gradually stabilized, calculate the actual heating power of each zone using thermal balance, and adjust the heating powers accordingly using the voltage regulators to approach the set values; (5) when the experimental parameters were stable, end the experiment. During the experiment, the ambient temperature of the enclosed area fluctuated from 18°C to 23 °C, close to the design value.
