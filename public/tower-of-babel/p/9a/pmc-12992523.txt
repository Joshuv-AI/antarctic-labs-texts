Sant’Andrea della valle dataset: Georeferenced 2D study models of a Theatine Church
Authors: Carlo Bianchini, Francesco De Stefano, Gabriele Giuliani, Marika Griffo, Francesca Porfiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a georeferenced dataset of 2D study models of the church of Sant’Andrea della Valle in Rome, a key monument of Theatine architecture. Integrating architectural, historical, and geospatial data, the dataset supports interdisciplinary research, heritage documentation, and digital preservation. It originates from a 3D laser scanning campaign conducted in 2023, combined with archival and bibliographic investigations mapping Theatine settlements in Italy and abroad. Sant’Andrea della Valle was selected for its historical relevance and unresolved questions about its architectural evolution. The dataset includes a CAD file with 2D restitution (bidimensional study model) derived from 3D point clouds, a GIS-compatible shapefile, and a KML file for Google Earth. The point cloud, captured with a Z + F 5016 scanner, was aligned in Z + F LaserControl and processed in AutoCAD to produce plans, elevations, and sections later georeferenced in AutoCAD Map 3D; Georeferencing ensured spatial consistency and integration with other datasets for multi-scale architectural and urban analysis. Archival sources were digitized and linked to the spatial models, enabling reconstruction of construction phases and stylistic evolution. The dataset supports architectural, historical, and urban studies by providing standardized, reusable CAD, GIS, and KML formats. Despite some gaps in archival data and variable point cloud density, it offers a robust, reproducible basis for analyzing Theatine architecture and preserving its heritage through digital documentation. The georeferencing of the plan derived from the surveys can contribute to creating a comprehensive, georeferenced mapping of all Theatine works in Italy and worldwide.Keywords: Architectural survey, 2D cad drawing, Geolocalization, Relational database, Information system

Background

The research presented here is part of the broader framework of Spoke 8 – PE5 CHANGES, which promotes the sustainable enhancement of cultural heritage through innovative digital documentation and representation strategies. The study focuses on the church of Sant’Andrea della Valle in Rome, one of the most significant Theatine architectural complexes, aiming to develop an integrated workflow that combines historical, architectural, and technological disciplines. This broader framework provided the foundation for the present study, which applies and refines the CHANGES methodologies through the integration of geo-environmental, historical, and topographical datasets into an interoperable digital infrastructure. This interdisciplinary approach allows for a detailed and dynamic understanding of Theatine sacred architecture, where each phase of documentation contributes to a comprehensive interpretation of the monument. The methodology is designed to integrate heterogeneous sources: bibliographic, iconographic, and philological data are associated with 3D metric information obtained from high-resolution laser scanning, producing a unified and accessible digital knowledge base. A central innovation of the project lies in the georeferencing of 1D, 2D, and 3D models derived from historical documents and 3D point cloud data. Through this process, particularly in this study, the 2D representations of the church are anchored to their real-world spatial coordinates, ensuring the consistency of spatial data across digital platforms. This enables advanced analyses, from spatial correlation with urban morphology to the integration of architectural datasets into larger geospatial frameworks for research and conservation. Furthermore, the georeferencing of the plan derived from survey can support the creation of a comprehensive, georeferenced mapping of all Theatine works in Italy and worldwide.

Data Description

The basilica of Sant’Andrea della Valle, located in the Sant’Eustachio district of Rome, stands as one of the most significant examples of late Renaissance and early Baroque architecture in the city. Its construction began in 1591, based on designs by Giacomo della Porta and Gian Francesco Grimaldi, and was largely financed by Cardinal Alessandro Peretti di Montalto, nephew of Pope Sixtus V. The project originated from the will of the Theatine Order —founded by Gaetano Thiene and the future Pope Paul IV, Gian Pietro Carafa— to establish a monumental church in central Rome. Carlo Maderno resumed the works in 1608, enlarging the transept and designing the impressive dome, once among the largest in Rome. The travertine façade, completed between 1655 and 1665 by Carlo Rainaldi, embodies the dynamic chiaroscuro and sculptural effects typical of the Baroque style [1]. The church has a Latin cross plan with a wide nave and richly decorated chapels, creating a grand and theatrical spatial effect [2]. Today, Sant’Andrea della Valle remains not only a place of worship but also the general seat of the Theatine Order, preserving an extraordinary historical and artistic heritage that reflects the religious patronage of Counter-Reformation Rome.

The core element is a georeferenced CAD file (.dwg) created using AutoCAD Map 3D 2025 in the EPSG:3004 coordinate reference system (Monte Mario / Italy Zone 2). This file contains the bidimensional study model of the church —the ground floor plan— derived from the 3D point cloud survey and restituted into precise vector geometry suitable for both architectural and geospatial applications. The same dataset is replicated in Shapefile (.shp) and KML (.kml) formats to ensure accessibility and visualization across multiple platforms. The Shapefile provides a GIS-compatible representation of the architectural footprint of Sant’Andrea della Valle, structured according to a consistent coordinate system to enable integration with urban, infrastructural, and cadastral datasets within GIS environments such as QGIS or ArcGIS. The Shapefile includes a concise attribute table, with fields ID (unique identifier), Feature_Type (e.g., wall, chapel, nave, dome) and Floor_Level (ground floor). The KML file, instead, allows visualization of the church within Google Earth, extending the dataset’s usability beyond specialized software and promoting dissemination for educational and outreach purposes. CAD and Shapefile maintain sub-centimetric accuracy suitable for architectural-scale analysis (1:50), while the KML file is optimized for visualization at city or neighbourhood scale, suitable for educational or outreach use. The dataset is fully compatible across platforms and can be transformed or reprojected into alternative coordinate reference systems, including WGS84 (EPSG:4326) and RDN2008 / UTM Zone 32 N, ensuring adherence to international geospatial standards. Transformations from EPSG:3004 (Monte Mario / Italy Zone 2) to RDN2008 and WGS84 were performed using the VertoOnline service of the Italian Military Geographic Institute (IGM; https://www.igmi.org/it/descrizione-prodotti/elementi-geodetici-1/verto-on-line), which implements the official national grid conversions between Monte Mario and RDN2008 or WGS84. This guarantees both the traceability and reproducibility of the results, achieving sub-metric accuracy suitable for WMS (Web Map Service). Future updates to the dataset are anticipated, including additional surveys of other Theatine churches or integration of more detailed archival information, maintaining georeferencing consistency and interoperability across platforms. Together, these files form an interoperable and georeferenced dataset that links architectural geometry, spatial positioning, and documentation, supporting both academic research and the digital management of cultural heritage. Each dataset component is directly linked to the reuse scenarios described in the “Value of the Data” section.

The dataset consists of processed and curated spatial data, specifically georeferenced 2D restitutions of the church of Sant’Andrea della Valle provided in CAD (.dwg), GIS Shapefile (.shp), and KML (.kml) formats. These files represent derived data, obtained through post-processing and vectorization of high-resolution 3D laser scanning point clouds. These processed datasets are publicly available through the Zenodo repository under the CHANGES Project. The original raw point cloud, acquired during the 2023 survey campaign, is not included in the public repository, as the focus of this article is on georeferenced 2D study models and their interoperability. These raw data are preserved by the authors and can be shared upon reasonable request for research, validation, or methodological comparison purposes. This structure ensures transparency and reproducibility, clearly distinguishing between publicly shared processed datasets and non-shared raw data, while providing conditions for access to the latter. The manuscript focuses solely on describing and enabling reuse of the georeferenced 2D datasets, without duplicating any repository metadata or external interpretations.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The methodological framework takes shape from procedures developed within the CHANGES Project (Spoke 8 – Sustainability and Resilience of Tangible Cultural Heritage) and from the data management principles proposed by Carafa and Bianchini (2025) [3]. It combines bibliographic and archival research, spatial analysis, and digital harmonization in a georeferenced CAD–GIS environment, facilitating the transition from diverse source collection to their integrated interpretation and spatial representation [4]. All processed datasets described in this article are publicly available through the Zenodo repository under the Project CHANGES. The repository includes the georeferenced CAD (.dwg), Shapefile (.shp), and KML (.kml) files corresponding to the ground floor plan of the church. The 3D laser scanning point clouds, while not included in the public repository, are used in this article exclusively for illustrative purposes to document the survey process and data quality (Figs. 1, 2). These visualizations do not constitute shared datasets or derived analytical results.

Preliminary researchand datacollection

Following the established methodological framework, an extensive bibliographic review and archival research campaign were undertaken. The relevant documentary materials are currently dispersed across multiple repositories, including the General House of the Theatine Order at Sant’Andrea della Valle, the State Archives of Rome, and the Vatican Apostolic Archives. The absence of a unified inventory complicated the acquisition process, necessitating a multi-source strategy to verify known documents and identify previously unpublished material. Both published and unpublished documents —ranging from construction records to historical drawings and iconographic representations— were catalogued in a dedicated digital database, developed within other research projects and case studies following the same methodological framework [5]. This ongoing process is progressively enabling the reconstruction of the church’s building chronology, highlighting successive architectural transformations from the 16th century to the present. The data organization phase is currently underway, focusing on harmonizing textual, graphical, and spatial information to enable cross-disciplinary analysis. From a methodological perspective, this systematic approach to data collection and organization represents a significant advancement toward an interdisciplinary and interconnected model for studying Theatine Cultural Heritage, combining historical research with digital technologies for documentation and analysis [6].

3D surveyand 2D restitution

The 3D laser scanning campaign conducted in 2023 served as the foundation for the geometric and spatial dataset. Using the phase-based Z + F IMAGER® 5016 3D scanner, the church was captured from 120 scan stations, producing point clouds with an average spacing of 6 mm to ensure complete spatial coverage of interior and exterior spaces. For reproducibility, the 3D scanning acquisition was conducted under controlled settings using the Z + F IMAGER® 5016, with default laser intensity and exposure parameters. Each scan station was positioned to ensure ∼30–40 % overlap with adjacent scans. The resulting point cloud —comprising millions of spatially referenced points— provides a metrically accurate record of the monument’s current condition (Figs 1, 2). While the 3D point cloud constitutes a raw, objective, and metrically verifiable dataset derived directly from instrumental acquisition, the 2D restitution represents a secondary interpretative product, dependent on methodological choices, projection criteria, and the expertise of the operator.

Post-processing operations included point alignment, registration, and noise reduction, producing a clean and unified 3D model [7]. Registration accuracy was assessed through RMS error, calculated as the root mean square of residual distances between overlapping scans in LaserControl, yielding a residual alignment error below 4 mm, ensuring both high relative and absolute metric reliability for subsequent 2D restitutions and georeferencing. The intrinsic precision of the Z + F IMAGER® 5016 further supports the reliability of the 3D dataset, while the resulting 2D restitutions retain a spatial resolution of approximately 5–10 mm, suitable for detailed architectural-scale analysis. Point clouds were processed in Z + F LaserControl (v2.8.1), using default noise filtering and registration tolerances adjusted to ±2 mm for optimal accuracy. This digital representation serves as both a spatial archive and the basis for generating bidimensional study models [8]. The high precision of the point cloud enables detailed analysis of architectural deformations, construction phases, and structural relationships among building components. The 2D restitution phase transformed the 3D survey data into bidimensional study models in CAD format (Fig. 3). The process involved orthogonal projection of the point cloud sections to produce accurate floor plans, elevations, and cross-sections. In the case of the ground floor plan, the section was taken at a height of approximately 190 cm above the floor, a deliberate choice to highlight the complexity of architectural elements, the detail of decorative features, and the spatial projection of the vaults. To preserve the correct alignment of building elements, local UCS (User Coordinate Systems) were carefully assigned in AutoCAD according to the main architectural axes, ensuring that all 2D projections remained consistent with the original geometry and facilitating accurate integration with GIS datasets. The drawing was carefully validated against metric references extracted directly from the 3D model to ensure dimensional consistency. The bidimensional models provide a clear and interpretable representation of the church’s architectural geometry, serving as a bridge between metric data and historical interpretation. They also constitute the primary source material for the georeferencing, and GIS integration phases described below.

Georeferencing in autoCAD Map3D

The project tackled the challenge of integrating heterogeneous spatial data by adopting georeferencing as a common framework, thereby ensuring that all datasets referring to the same physical structure were aligned within a consistent geographic reference system [9].

The first dataset to be georeferenced consisted of the bidimensional models derived from the 3D point cloud survey, specifically the ground floor plan, which served as the geometric foundation for subsequent spatial integration.

This 2D representation was positioned within the defined coordinate system to establish a metrically accurate and spatially coherent base layer [10]. This step was crucial for enabling the alignment of additional datasets, such as historical cartography, GIS layers, and archival documentation, within a unified geospatial environment, ultimately allowing for cross-referenced analysis between the architectural geometry and its historical and urban context.

The georeferencing procedure was implemented using AutoCAD Map 3D to spatially align the bidimensional CAD drawings with real-world coordinates. Georeferencing in this environment involves assigning geographic coordinates to each point within the CAD model, effectively situating the digital representation of the church in its true spatial context (Fig. 4).

Through this process, the bidimensional study models are embedded within a geospatial framework, enabling their integration with other digital datasets, such as urban morphology, cadastral boundaries, or historical cartography. The resulting georeferenced dataset thus supports multiscale analysis, from the architectural to the urban level, facilitating both scholarly research and heritage management.

Conclusion

The Sant’Andrea della Valle dataset represents a significant step toward the integration of architectural representation and geospatial data in the study of Theatine heritage. By combining 3D laser scanning, bidimensional restitution, and georeferencing, the project establishes a robust methodological framework for documenting, analyzing, and disseminating architectural knowledge.

The resulting georeferenced 2D restitutionmodels are not only a precise representation of the church’s current condition but also a foundation for further digital applications–, such as 3D reconstructions, GIS-based historical analyses, and interactive visualization. The inclusion of interoperable formats (.dwg, .shp, .kml) ensures accessibility and long-term preservation, fostering both scientific reuse and public engagement. Ultimately, this datasetcontributes to advancing the digital documentation of sacred architecture, offering a replicable model for the sustainable valorization of cultural heritage through data-driven methodologies.

Limitations

•Temporal constraints: the dataset reflects the church’s state as captured during the 2023 3D survey; any structural changes or restorations occurring after that date are not included.

•Point cloud density variability: certain areas of the building, particularly difficult-to-access interiors or high ceilings, may have lower point cloud density, potentially reducing local geometric precision in the derived 2D models.
