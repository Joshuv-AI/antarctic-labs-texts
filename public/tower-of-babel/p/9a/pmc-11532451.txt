Introduction

Acute internal carotid artery (ICA) thrombosis is a rare complication of mastoiditis. Mastoid inflammation can spread to intracranial vessels, leading to thrombosis and infarction. Here, we present a patient whose mastoiditis progressed to acute ischemic stroke due to ICA thrombosis. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [1].

Case presentation

An 83-year-old man with diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and sick sinus syndrome, presented to the neurologist with progressive left-sided peripheral facial palsy (PFP) lasting several weeks. The patient also reported left-sided hearing impairment, otalgia, ear swelling, and tenderness in the temporal region over 2–3 months. Neurological examination revealed only left-sided PFP and hearing impairment. Blood tests revealed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein levels. Temporal bone computed tomography (CT) revealed a cholesteatoma in the left middle ear with left mastoiditis and petrous bone erosion. Intravenous amoxicillin/sulbactam was prescribed empirically for chronic mastoiditis.

During treatment, the patient experienced sudden consciousness disturbance and right hemiplegia. Brain CT angiography (CTA) revealed a short occlusion in the posterior genu of the left ICA. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed acute infarction in the left hemisphere and left ICA occlusion (Fig. 1). The proximity of the petrous bone erosion to the ICA suggested mastoiditis-related ICA thrombosis. Antiplatelet therapy was started. Osmotherapy with mannitol was administered to lower intracranial pressure until follow-up brain CT scans showed resolution of the brain edema. Antibiotic was switched to vancomycin and cefepime for broad-spectrum coverage. Due to swelling in the external acoustic canal, myringotomy and pathogen culturing were not possible. An otolaryngologist was consulted for a mastoidectomy, but it was postponed because of large territory infarction. Despite treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics, serial clinical and laboratory data indicated worsening infection, including shock, hypothermia, elevated C-reactive protein, coagulopathy, and impaired renal function. The family was informed of the poor prognosis. After discussions with the hospice and palliative care team, the family chose hospice care. Palliative extubation was performed, and the patient passed away peacefully.

Discussion

The etiology of unilateral peripheral nerve palsy includes idiopathic causes (such as Bell's palsy), trauma, infection, and neoplasia. Traumatic causes can involve fractures of the petrous part of the temporal bone, facial wounds transecting branches of the facial nerve, and iatrogenic injury during surgery. Infectious causes include viral and bacterial infections (such as otitis media, necrotizing otitis externa, and Lyme disease). Neoplastic causes include parotid malignancies, facial and acoustic neuromas, meningiomas, and arachnoid cysts [2]. In this case, the patient's clinical presentation—hearing impairment, otalgia, and tenderness in the temporal region—suggested that the facial palsy was likely due to otitis media complicated by mastoiditis, which was confirmed through imaging studies.

Mastoiditis, a complication of otitis media, can present with symptoms such as fever, otalgia, otorrhea, swelling, and erythema of the external auditory canal and retroauricular region. Based on these symptoms, the diagnosis is often clinically apparent. However, complications of mastoiditis can be clinically occult but are often serious. Radiologic imaging plays a crucial role in the early detection of these complications. CT scans are valuable for evaluating bony structures and detecting abscess formation, while MRI is necessary for identifying intracranial abnormalities that may not be visible on CT [3].

Mastoiditis can lead to severe complications if untreated, categorized into intratemporal and intracranial types. Intratemporal complications include hearing loss, facial paralysis, and skull base osteomyelitis (SBO), while intracranial complications include meningitis, brain abscess, and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) [4]. Although the link between CVST and mastoiditis is well-documented [4], arterial thrombosis related to mastoiditis is less common but can occur with SBO. Possible mechanisms may include septic emboli, vasculitis, vasospasm, a hypercoagulable state, and inflammation spread to intracranial vessel walls [5].

Treatment methods for mastoiditis included antibiotics and surgical intervention. Conservative therapy with antibiotic is effective for uncomplicated acute mastoiditis, especially in children [6]. A systematic review and meta-analysis on the management of acute complicated mastoiditis in the pediatric population highlighted the role of early surgical intervention, such as abscess drainage or mastoidectomy, for patients with otitis media complications, rather than relying solely on conservative measures [7]. Mastoiditis with PFP requires aggressive intravenous antibiotics and urgent myringotomy. If PFP persists after 7 days, mastoidectomy is necessary [4].

Evidence or guidelines for the treatment of mastoiditis complicated by ICA thrombosis are notably lacking due to the rarity of this condition. Only a few case reports have been published. Manso MC et al. [8] reported a case in which a patient with extensive infection in the right skull base and ischemic stroke in the right hemisphere was discharged after six weeks of intravenous antibiotic treatment without surgical intervention. However, one month after discharge, the patient experienced a sudden change in consciousness and died before receiving medical attention. Doyle EJ 3rd et al. [9] described a similar case where acute ischemic stroke and ICA subocclusion arose from mastoiditis. Early mastoidectomy with an extended facial recess effectively controlled the infection, resulting in improvements in neurological deficits. This case highlights the importance of early surgical intervention to prevent severe complications and improve outcomes.

In our case, we diagnosed the complicated mastoiditis early through brain imaging, before severe complications occurred. However, delayed surgical intervention resulted in inadequate infection control, leading to ischemic stroke, and the patient ultimately passed away. Given the anatomical proximity, the ischemic stroke was likely a result of mastoiditis, which caused SBO of the petrous bone and involvement of the ICA. This is further supported by the absence of flow-related signals in the magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), indicating thrombosis or occlusion (Fig. 1B-E).

Conclusion

This case highlights mastoiditis as a potential precursor to ICA territory infarction. Recognizing complications of mastoiditis is crucial for timely surgical intervention, as delayed treatment may result in severe complications and poor outcomes.
