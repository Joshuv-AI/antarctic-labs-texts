Resolving the Delusion Paradox
Authors: Predrag Petrovic, Philipp Sterzer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisThe neurocomputational framework of predictive processing (PP) provides a promising approach to explaining delusions, a key symptom of psychotic disorders. According to PP, the brain makes inferences about the world by weighing prior beliefs against the available sensory data. Mismatches between prior beliefs and sensory data result in prediction errors that may update the brain’s model of the world. Psychosis has been associated with reduced weighting of priors relative to the sensory data. However, delusional beliefs are highly resistant to change, suggesting increased rather than decreased weighting of priors. We propose that this “delusion paradox” can be resolved within a hierarchical PP model: Reduced weighting of prior beliefs at low hierarchical levels may be compensated by an increased influence of higher-order beliefs represented at high hierarchical levels, including delusional beliefs. This may sculpt perceptual processing into conformity with delusions and foster their resistance to contradictory evidence.Study DesignWe review several lines of experimental evidence on low- and high-level processes, and their neurocognitive underpinnings in delusion-related phenotypes and link them to predicted processing.Study ResultsThe reviewed evidence supports the notion of decreased weighting of low-level priors and increased weighting of high-level priors, in both delusional and delusion-prone individuals. Moreover, we highlight the role of prefrontal cortex as a neural basis for the increased weighting of high-level prior beliefs and discuss possible clinical implications of the proposed hierarchical predictive-processing model.ConclusionsOur review suggests the delusion paradox can be resolved within a hierarchical PP model.Keywords: delusions, predictive processing, prefrontal cortex, psychosis, schizophrenia, delusion proneness

Introduction

Delusions are defined as “fixed beliefs that are not amenable to change in light of conflicting evidence” in DSM-5.1 They can occur as a symptom of a number of psychiatric disorders including depression, bipolar disorder, and dementia, but are most characteristic of psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia. Delusional beliefs, especially those in schizophrenia, are often paranoid in nature, revolving around ideas of being followed, threatened, or harmed by others. In addition to such persecutory delusions, there is a variety of other delusional themes, including referential, grandiose, somatic, romantic, or religious ideas. The high degree of conviction characteristic of delusional beliefs usually results in the absence of insight, which evidently represents a major challenge for therapeutic interventions. Delusions often lead to social withdrawal and isolation, with detrimental consequences for long-term outcomes. There is thus a need to better understand how delusions emerge and why they are held onto so strongly. Here, we aim to provide a model of delusions that is based on neuroscientific evidence and helps to address the above challenges. We elaborate on the emerging notion that delusions may be best explained by drawing on a hierarchical model within the framework of predictive processing (PP)2,3 and provide a comprehensive and up-to-date review of empirical findings supporting such a model. Our focus is on delusions as a symptom dimension. Thus, we will discuss both delusion-like beliefs related to delusion-proneness in the general population4,5 and delusions as symptoms of psychiatric disorders, with a focus on schizophrenia. For simplicity, we refer to both delusion-proneness and pathological forms of delusions as delusion-related phenotypes.

Understanding Psychosis Through the Lens of Predictive Processing

Key neurobiological models of schizophrenia—the disorder most commonly associated with delusions—have drawn on morphological findings such as reduced synaptic concentration, especially in prefrontal cortex6,7; and on neurotransmitter-related abnormalities, most prominently concerning the dopamine system8 and the balance of excitation and inhibition in cortical neural circuits (E/I balance), which is largely determined by the ratio of excitatory glutamatergic and inhibitory GABAergic synaptic inputs to pyramidal neurons.9,10 However, such models do not explain how neural changes are linked to the phenomenology of psychosis. Neurobiological explanations are, as Fletcher and Frith succinctly put it, “unsatisfactory because they leave an explanatory gap between the mental and the physical. How can dopamine cause a voice or a belief?”11 The computational framework of PP has been proposed to bridge the explanatory gap between neurobiology and phenomenology in psychosis.3

In PP the brain is conceptualized as an inference machine that uses prior knowledge to infer the causes of the sensory data,12 which can be formalized as probabilistic Bayesian inference.13 Prior knowledge is used to generate probabilistic predictions (“priors”) that are combined with sensory data (“likelihood”) to compute a posterior probability (“posterior”) that corresponds to a “best guess” regarding the current state of the world. This process is implemented in the brain using an internal model of the world, whereby prior beliefs resulting from this model are compared to the incoming sensory data. Mismatches between priors and sensory data result in prediction errors (PE) that may update the internal model, with the ultimate goal of minimizing PE. The respective precisions of prior and likelihood play an important role in this process. In Bayesian terms, precision corresponds to the inverse variance of the probability distributions representing prior and likelihood, respectively. High precision of the likelihood and low precision of the prior will result in larger precision-weighted PEs and vice versa.14

Importantly, the internal model subserving such inference is thought to be organized hierarchically,13 whereby priors regarding basic sensory features are implemented at lower hierarchical levels, while higher levels represent increasingly more abstract beliefs about the world.11,15,16 According to the canonical PP account of psychosis,3,14 psychotic symptoms, including delusions, can be explained by a reduced prior-to-likelihood ratio, ie, reduced precision of priors relative to the sensory data, leading to stronger weighting of sensory data in Bayesian inference and thus larger PEs (figure 1).

PP is not “yet another theory” that competes with extant neurobiological models. In contrast to such accounts, PP conceptualizes psychosis at an algorithmic level.3,14 It, therefore, may provide a mechanistic link between pathophysiology and phenomenology by describing how these computations relate to mechanisms at the neural level on the one hand, and how they translate to psychopathology on the other. For example, increased cortical excitability resulting from E/I imbalance may result in greater weighting of sensory information in Bayesian inference. Dopamine has been proposed to play a key role in the precision-weighting of PEs, thus reporting the salience of sensory events17 (for experimental evidence see18,19). Both mechanisms thus result in enhanced precision-weighting of PEs. To minimize PEs, belief models at high hierarchical levels may be modified in a way that allows them to encompass aberrant PE signaling originating from lower hierarchical levels. Psychologically, enhanced precision-weighted PEs result in an exaggerated assignment of salience to otherwise inconspicuous stimuli. Such aberrant salience calls for unusual explanations, thereby triggering the development of delusional moods and thoughts. For example, if normally inconspicuous events (eg, cars passing by in the street or a traffic light turning red) are perceived as overly salient, this may trigger the conclusion that such events have a special meaning (eg, that they are meant to convey a threatening signal from a malevolent person), which may, in turn, give rise to a persecutory delusion. The notion of aberrant salience,20,21 which in its original form was limited to motivational salience, can thus be embedded in the more general framework of PP.2,3,22 It should be noted that other algorithmic models of information processing, such as Circular Inference23 provide similarly useful computational accounts of delusion formation.

The Delusion Paradox

While PP may thus provide a link between neurotransmitter alterations and the emergence of delusions, what remains unexplained is the well-known rigidity of delusional beliefs. The persistence of delusions in the face of counterevidence has been a key criterion in most attempts to define delusions, from Jaspers’ characterization of delusions as “incorrigible”24 to the above-cited DSM-5 definition 100 years later.1 In the light of the canonical PP account of psychosis outlined above, the rigidity of delusions seems paradoxical. According to this account, it is a reduced precision of priors, relative to the sensory evidence, that gives rise to aberrant salience and consequently the formation of false beliefs. However, that delusions are so resistant to conflicting evidence seems at odds with the notion of weak, or imprecise, priors. To the contrary, the rigidity of delusions suggests that they actually correspond to overly strong priors, ie, priors with abnormally high precision.3

Two obvious explanations for this apparent paradox come to mind. First, not only delusions, but also “normal” high-level beliefs, in particular those concerning our worldviews, are usually held with strong conviction. Within hierarchical PP, such abstract, cognitive beliefs about the world are thought to be represented at the highest hierarchical levels and can thus be conceptualized as high-level priors.11,15,16 They provide overarching explanations for how events in the world relate to each other and are therefore naturally highly resistant to counterevidence. The rigidity of delusions could therefore just reflect the general tendency of humans to cling to strongly held beliefs despite counterevidence, ie, conceptual conservativism.25 In that case, the notion of altered prior precision might be restricted to low levels of the PP hierarchy. Delusions may then just be the best possible explanation of the aberrant salience21 resulting from weak low-level priors (and overly precise PEs),3 but would be just as rigid as any “normal” high-level belief.

Alternatively, there may be a dissociation between low- and high- level priors. While low-level priors may indeed be weak, as suggested by the canonical PP account of psychosis, high-level priors might be overly precise in people with delusions, as a compensatory mechanism.3,14 The goal of such a compensatory mechanism would be to minimize PEs by strengthening higher-level priors when low-level PEs are overly precise. This could make high-level delusional beliefs particularly resistant to updating by PEs.

In the following, we will discuss work supporting a dissociation between the precisions of low- vs. high-level priors in delusional individuals. Of note, the distinction between low- hand high-levels is an oversimplification, (1) because the neural implementation of PP comprises more than 2 hierarchical levels, and (2) because there is not just one PP hierarchy in the human brain, but multiple hierarchies that likely interact with each other in complex ways. Our gross distinction between low and high levels is intended as a first step towards building a more nuanced PP model that allows for different functional roles of different hierarchical levels. With “low-level priors,” we refer to predictions that operate implicitly, concern perceptual processes, and are implemented predominantly within sensorimotor cortices (but may involve multiple anatomical levels therein) or subcortical circuits. With “high-level priors,” in contrast, we refer to more abstract beliefs that are often (but not always) explicit and consciously accessible, and involve predominantly (but not exclusively) prefrontal brain regions. Of note, the implicit–explicit distinction used here is just a rule of thumb. There are types of priors for which the classification as either high- or low-level is not obvious. For instance, conditioned hallucinations, which have been reported to be stronger in people experiencing auditory verbal hallucinations26 (but see also Valton, et al 201927) are not easily categorized as either high- or low-level priors. We will begin by providing a summary of empirical research that supports the concept of weak low-level priors linked to psychosis, in line with the canonical predictive processing (PP) theory of psychosis. .3 We will then turn to studies in which high-level beliefs were induced experimentally and that indeed point to an association of delusions with overly strong high-level priors.

Evidence for Imprecise Low-Level Priors

Extensive experimental research supports the notion that delusions are associated with weak low-level priors, ie, decreased precision of low-level priors relative to the sensory data. Here, we focus on a few instructive examples.

In the perceptual domain, increased resistance to some, but not all visual illusions has often been reported in delusion-related phenotypes.28 A well-known example is the hollow-mask illusion. When viewing the backside of a face mask, healthy observers usually perceive the mask falsely as convex rather than concave. Patients with schizophrenia are more likely to experience the (veridical) percept of a concave face,29–31 and show fMRI-connectivity suggesting reduced feedback processing29 compatible with reduced precision of the perceptual prior for convex faces.

Similarly, some context illusions, in which a contextual surround of a visual stimulus provides a statistical prediction of the central signal, are less pronounced in schizophrenia. Healthy individuals perceive, eg, the contrast of a central stimulus as reduced in the presence of a similar high-contrast pattern in the surround.32 Such context suppression is diminished in individuals with schizophrenia, both behaviorally33–37 and neurally38 (but see35,36,39), again supporting the notion of weak low-level priors.

While visual illusions likely reflect the effects of priors acquired over long periods of time, recent evidence also points to a reduced weighting of newly acquired perceptual priors. In perceptual decision-making, perceptual choices are often biased toward previous choices.40 Such choice history biases have been conceptualized as ad hoc priors that represent an adaptation to temporal auto-correlation in natural sensory environments. In other words, as upcoming sensory events are usually not independent of past events, it is adaptive to use past perceptual decisions as priors for whatever comes next.41 Serial presentations of ambiguous stimuli result in strong choice history biases reflected by a high stability of perception between stimulus presentations.42–44 These history biases in the perception of ambiguous stimuli (eg, random dot kinetograms; RDK—figure 2 “Baseline”) are reduced in delusion-related phenotypes.45,46 Moreover, when probing the weighting of sensory information in perceptual inference by manipulating the ambiguity of such stimuli, individuals with schizophrenia display increased sensitivity to disambiguating information, again pointing to reduced weighting of priors relative to sensory information.47 In perceptual decision-making under uncertainty, ie, when sensory information is noisy, choice history biases are reduced in delusion-related phenotypes.48

Another well-established finding pointing to weak low-level priors is that of reduced mismatch negativity (MMN), an electrophysiological signal elicited by unexpected deviation from a regular stimulus sequence, independent of whether observers attend to the stimuli or not.49 It likely reflects pre-attentive, automatic detection of events that violate learned priors and is thought to be equivalent to sensory PE.50 MMN is based on a subtraction of the cortical responses to regular (frequently occurring) stimuli from responses to deviant stimuli. A reduced MMN is expected if the prior-to-likelihood ratio is decreased, eg, if the prior learned from regular stimuli is imprecise and thus reduced in weight, relative to the sensory evidence. In individuals with schizophrenia, high-risk individuals who later develop schizophrenia, and first-degree relatives of schizophrenia patients, reduced MMN amplitude has been consistently reported.51–53 Experimental work has related reduced MMN amplitude to NMDA receptor hypofunction,54–56 which may result in increased cortical excitability and thus reduced weighting of priors relative to sensory information.14,57,58

While the studies reviewed so far focused on perceptual processes that primarily involve cortical structures, a large body of work on reinforcement learning points to aberrant PE signaling in subcortical circuits both in psychotic patients59–62 and related phenotypes.63 At the neural level, the ventral striatum (VS)—a key structure in dopamine-dependent reward learning64—shows reduced activation during reward anticipation in these groups.59–62 This effect is partially driven by greater responses to non-reward predicting stimuli,59–62 in line with the notion of aberrant salience21 and analogous to the effects of a reduced prior-to-likelihood ratio in the context of the MMN findings discussed above. Importantly, reward PE expressed in VS activation is smaller in psychotic patients than in controls65 and a correlation between VS reward PE and VS dopamine synthesis capacity is only observed in healthy individuals.66

While reward learning is associated with phasic dopamine responses67,68 which are altered in delusion-related phenotypes,7,69 it is debated whether the dopamine system is similarly involved in fear learning.68 Still, psychotic patients show a lower degree of fear conditioning70 and a lower activation of amygdala associated with such learning.71,72 As in reward learning, these differences are largely due to aberrant responses to stimuli not associated with fear learning, an effect that also was related to delusion severity.70 The similarities between suboptimal dopamine-dependent reward learning and dopamine-independent fear conditioning suggest a more general dysfunction not confined to the dopamine system.

Evidence for Overly Precise High-Level Priors

The hypothesis that delusions reflect high-level prior beliefs with abnormally high precision is difficult, if not impossible, to test directly. However, the propensity of an individual to form overly strong high-level beliefs can be tested experimentally. A number of experimental approaches have been devised to first induce explicit, consciously accessible beliefs through some kind of learning; and to then test the strength of the newly acquired belief, eg, by probing its influence on the processing of new sensory data or by testing its resistance against contradictory information.

A prime example of experimental belief manipulation can be found in research investigating the placebo effect. Participants are typically informed that they will be treated with a drug or procedure that will lower a negative experience such as pain or unpleasant emotions, which will then impact the experience itself and the underlying information processing.73 This may be achieved in a non-explicit way involving conditioning or by explicit verbal suggestions. On a computational level, PP mechanisms involving both low-level expectations (based on conditioning) and higher-order expectations (based on verbal suggestions) may drive the placebo effect.74,75 Higher-order placebo expectations correspond to explicit prior beliefs implemented at high levels of the PP hierarchy.

On a neural level, a network including rostral anterior cingulate cortex (rACC) and lateral orbitofrontal cortex (lOFC) has been associated with the placebo effect.73,76,77 While both regions have been noted in placebo responses, they seem to represent different cognitive components.75,78,79 rACC is involved in regulation of emotion and pain.80 Accordingly, rACC displays an abundance of opioid receptors that are activated in placebo analgesia81 and seem to regulate pain processing through a descending opioid network involving the brainstem.79,82–84 In contrast, lOFC is not activated by opioids to the same extent and seems to represent a cognitive, non-opioid component of the placebo analgesic response75,79 and may thus harness higher-order expectations driving the placebo effect.75 Similar effects of verbal suggestions were observed in the subjective experience of wine85 and smell.86 Such experiments also show orbitofrontal activation in a way comparable to placebo studies.

Effects of placebo-like manipulations can also be observed for emotionally neutral stimulus features. For example, the perception of motion direction in an ambiguous visual stimulus (RDK; figure 2) can be changed by an experimentally induced explicit belief.87 This suggests that the placebo effect is only a special case of how integrated higher-order information may change our experience of external input. When probing the effect of such experimentally induced explicit beliefs in relation to delusions, the belief-induced perceptual bias was found to correlate positively with delusion-proneness in healthy individuals45 (figure 2). Interestingly, the effect of high-level prior beliefs correlated negatively with the effect of low-level priors as reflected by a reduced choice history bias during serial presentation of the same ambiguous stimuli. To the best of our knowledge, this is so far the only study observing such an inverse relationship between the effects of low- and high-level priors within one stimulus paradigm, thus supporting the notion that strong high-level priors may indeed represent a compensatory mechanism for weak low-level priors.45 Perceptual events that were congruent with the induced high-level belief were associated with activity in left lOFC and functional connectivity between lOFC and visual areas encoding motion perception (ie, area hMT/V5), both correlating positively with delusion-proneness.45

A subsequent study using the same experimental paradigm found a reduced behavioral effect of explicit beliefs on reported perception in medicated and clinically stable patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls, but a positive correlation of the experimentally induced belief effect with positive psychotic symptom severity in the patient group46 (figure 2). Of note, functional connectivity between lOFC and visual cortex was enhanced in patients with schizophrenia, in line with the previously observed correlation with delusion-proneness.45 Previous research has also suggested that lOFC is involved in top-down regulation of visual processing.88 Together, these findings indicate that lOFC mediates the impact of high-level beliefs on perception. With regard to delusions, these findings support the notion of an increased precision of high-level priors in delusion-related phenotypes, possibly mediated by enhanced connectivity between lOFC and sensory cortices.

Another way to experimentally induce explicit beliefs is instructed fear learning. Here too, verbal suggestions are used to induce explicit beliefs about aversive experiences, but without the surreptitious manipulation and deception that is often used in placebo-like experiments. A fear response including both autonomic reactions and an unpleasant subjective experience occurs when a previously neutral stimulus has been associated with an aversive (eg, painful) stimulus using verbal suggestions89—similar to classical fear conditioning. However, in contrast to classical conditioning, instructed fear learning is dependent on higher-order abstract information about the relations between stimuli. This type of learning is more stable and shows less extinction than classical conditioning.89 As for placebo experiments, several studies have suggested that lOFC is involved in instructed fear learning.90–92

A recent study investigated the effect of instructed fear learning on perception in psychosis-related phenotypes.91 Faces that would be used as CS+ or CS− stimuli were shown with written suggestions about possible aversive outcomes right before a classical fear conditioning task (also 2 non-instructed face stimuli were included). As both instructed fear learning and classical fear conditioning were used, instructed and non-instructed components could be teased apart (figure 3). The fear learning effect was stronger for the instructed than for the non-instructed stimuli, suggesting an additive effect of explicit beliefs. Moreover, instructed stimuli were associated with bilateral lOFC activation when compared to the non-instructed stimuli. Notably, the behavioral instructed fear learning effect was larger in the group with high delusion-proneness. In addition, delusion-prone participants showed stronger functional connectivity between right lOFC and regions processing fear and pain. Thus, both the behavioral effect of experimentally induced explicit beliefs and the functional connectivity of lOFC in delusion-prone individuals conceptually replicated previous work using placebo-like manipulation in visual perception.45,46 Together, placebo-like manipulations and instructed fear learning indicate that the influence of high-level beliefs on information processing is greater in delusion-related phenotypes. Future studies should focus on whether these processes relate to why delusion-related phenotypes more readily acquire delusion-like ideas including conspiracy narratives outside the experimental setting.93,94

In addition to studies suggesting stronger effects of high-level beliefs on information processing, there is also experimental evidence for a greater resistance of such beliefs to new contradicting evidence in delusion-related phenotypes. A well-established experimental model for studying the resistance to counterevidence is bias against disconfirmatory evidence (BADE),96,97 where participants are first presented with an ambiguous scenario and asked to rate the plausibility of various interpretations. There are “lure” interpretations that seem highly plausible initially but are rendered increasingly unlikely as more information is revealed. Patients with schizophrenia,97–101 schizotypical individuals,102,103 and healthy individuals with high delusion-proneness93,94,104,105 typically stick longer to their first choices, that is, they more often fail to adequately down-rate the plausibility of lure interpretations. This failure to integrate new information that contradicts an initially formed belief is again in line with the Bayesian notion of overly precise high-level beliefs. Several brain networks including attention-, default mode-, and cognitive evaluation networks (including lOFC) have been reported to be involved in BADE.106-108 While increased activation was observed in the attention network , the default-mode- and cognitive evaluation network showed reduced activations in schizophrenia, most pronounced in patients with delusions107 (figure 4). A similar activation pattern was associated with delusional ideation in healthy individuals.108

Another promising way to study the mechanisms underlying the integration of new information is fear extinction, especially as much is known about the underlying neural processes, most notably the role of medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) in inducing re-learning within the amygdala.109 As in the BADE experiments discussed above, fear extinction also involves the integration of novel information that will change the meaning or value of stimuli. Accordingly, a recent study showed that high delusion-proneness is associated with reduced fear extinction, as assessed by likability scores of conditioned faces and underlying brain activation in a fear conditioning experiment.95 The study also showed that mPFC was less activated in delusion-prone participants than in controls during extinction (figure 4). Moreover, stronger negative functional connectivity between mPFC and amygdala was observed in low vs. high delusion-prone individuals. Preoccupation with odd beliefs was associated with more frequent switches between brain network states as well as a lower overall probability of the state involving lOFC in delusion-prone individuals. These findings point to less stable information processing in brain networks and lower re-learning within lOFC during extinction. At the same time, lOFC showed stronger functional connectivity with the fear-related network, specifically in the instructed fear contrast, in delusion-prone subjects during extinction in a similar way as during conditioning (figure 3).

Altogether, these studies suggest a complex role for lOFC: Both the study on fear extinction95 and work on BADE-like processes107,108 suggest weaker lOFC involvement during updating of previously acquired beliefs in delusion-related phenotypes. At the same time, lOFC shows stronger connectivity with low-level processing after higher-order learning (ie, when a belief has been acquired).45,46,91,95 This pattern suggests a role for altered lOFC function in the tendency of delusion-related phenotypes to stick with their beliefs in the face of counterevidence.

Concluding Remarks

In conclusion, we suggest that the delusion paradox—that the tenacious persistence of delusional beliefs seems at odds with a large body of evidence for weak priors—may be resolved within the framework of hierarchical PP. Specifically, weak priors at low hierarchical levels may co-exist with strong high-level priors that render delusional beliefs resistant to change, possibly reflecting a compensatory mechanism. Indeed, converging evidence indicates that delusion-related phenotypes are associated with overly strong high-level prior beliefs. Moreover, brain imaging suggests that such strong high-level beliefs involve prefrontal cortex, especially lOFC and ventromedial PFC (vmPFC). This evidence adds to a longstanding debate on how prefrontal cortex (dys−)function relates to psychotic symptoms.110

What remains to be elucidated, is whether the tenacious persistence of delusional beliefs indeed reflects a compensatory mechanism. Even if consistent across a number of experimental paradigms (but see35,36,39), the reduction in strength of low-level priors is rather subtle. For example, even though delusional patients show a reduced susceptibility to the hollow-mask illusion, this alteration in perceptual inference does not seem to strongly affect their everyday visual experience. One may therefore argue that the need to compensate for such a subtle perceptual abnormality is limited. Another possibility is that high-level priors may be just one of many influences (including emotions, habits, etc.) that might dominate more strongly when low-level inference is altered, without reflecting an actual compensatory mechanism.111 Finally, an additional prefrontal deficit may cause impaired high-level belief updating, thus contributing to belief rigidity in delusional individuals, akin to a 2-factor theory of delusions.110 The latter question has been difficult to answer by studying brain morphology in patients with schizophrenia, as altered brain structure is a global rather than regionally specific phenomenon and may reflect secondary effects of a chronic brain disorder or neuroleptic medication.112 However, research on individuals at-risk for psychosis or with early drug-naïve psychosis have shown morphological alterations in vmPFC and OFC113–115 (figure 5). Similarly, genetic risk for schizophrenia is associated with lower cortical thickness in vmPFC and lOFC in bipolar patients and healthy controls116 (figure 5). Interestingly, this effect was independently shown for healthy controls and patients in vmPFC. Thus, a specifically altered prefrontal brain morphology that relates to psychosis may exist apart from the more general brain alterations discussed previously. Possibly, some of the neurobiological changes affecting the whole brain, such as increased synaptic pruning or impaired E/I balance take place while the prefrontal cortex goes through a critical developmental period, rendering this structure particularly vulnerable. Any such premorbid prefrontal abnormality might contribute to altered inference mechanisms underlying psychosis. For example, while a compensatory response of prefrontal cortex to increased low-level PE signals is what would be expected in order to minimize them, altered prefrontal function may additionally contribute to reduced belief flexibility. Such a mechanism, which must remain speculative at this point, would complement the PP account with a component of the 2-factor model of psychosis.110

We suggest that the improved understanding of the formation and maintenance of delusions within the PP framework has important clinical implications. First, explaining bizarre delusions as a response to the aberrant salience caused by weak low-level priors helps to understand why such beliefs emerge in the first place. Second, the notion that strong higher-order beliefs reflect the brain’s attempt to compensate for weak low-level priors can explain why patients tenaciously stick to even highly disconcerting beliefs, even though they are evidently false and cause suffering: Strongly held delusional beliefs may safeguard against the agonizing uncertainty that may be caused by aberrant salience. This implies an important role of delusions for the day-to-day functioning of the individual in that they may permit ”continued engagement with an overwhelming world, and ongoing function in the face of paralyzing difficulty.”117 Such an understanding can be used in the therapeutic setting and may enhance empathy with psychotic patients’ experiences, which has important implications for the therapeutic alliance.118
