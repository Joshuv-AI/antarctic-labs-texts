ARDS during haematopoietic reconstruction after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) in non-child patients: based on the new global definition
Authors: Chang Gao, Ying Wang, Di Yin, Daxiong Zeng, Ye Gao, Yu He, Yuanyuan Yang, Haiyan Wang, Depei Wu, Qiang Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaematopoietic reconstitution is marked by immunosuppression and pancytopenia, representing the initial high-risk period following allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). However, little is known about the occurrence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) during haematopoietic reconstitution.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1024 patients who underwent allo-HSCT in Suzhou from 2016 to 2019. Clinical data and follow-up information were collected from medical records. ARDS was defined according to the new global definition established in 2023. The primary outcomes were the incidence of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution after allo-HSCT and 1 year post-transplantation mortality.ResultsAmong the 1024 patients, 58 (5.6%) died within 1 year after HSCT. ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution occurred in 45 patients (4.4%), of whom 29 were treated with high-flow nasal oxygen only. The median onset of ARDS was 9.0 days post-transplantation. Patients who developed ARDS had a significantly higher risk of 1-year mortality after HSCT (HR 7.99, 95% CI 4.13 to 15.44). Independent risk factors for ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution included longer intervals between disease onset and transplantation (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.02), a greater number of previous HSCTs (OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.04 to 3.19), and higher red cell distribution width at admission (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.22).ConclusionsAccording to the new 2023 global definition, ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution is independently associated with increased 1-year mortality after allo-HSCT. Early identification of ARDS during this period is particularly important, and recognising its risk factors may aid in timely diagnosis and intervention.Keywords: ARDS

Introduction

Allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is widely employed in the treatment of leukaemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, myelofibrosis, lymphoma and other malignant haematological diseases, with the number of transplant recipients increasing annually.1 Despite advances in transplantation, respiratory failure in haematological malignancies confers high mortality,2 and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is recognised as an important underlying cause.

Conditioning regimens for HSCT can induce irreversible or nearly irreversible pancytopenia.3 A period of haematopoietic reconstitution is necessary to achieve neutrophil and platelet engraftment.4 This phase is marked by immunosuppression and deficiencies in granulocytes and platelets, making it the initial high-risk period following HSCT. The most attention during this period has been directed towards infectious and haemorrhagic complications, with infection being a major cause of ARDS.5 6 In recent years, research on ARDS following HSCT has mainly focused on the period 1–5 years post-transplantation.7,9 However, little is known about the occurrence of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution.

Previous studies on ARDS in HSCT recipients have primarily been based on the Berlin definition,7,10 which emphasises the assessment of mechanical ventilation and the Oxygenation Index for diagnosing ARDS, often necessitating more invasive procedures. In immunosuppressed patients, including those with haematological malignancies, invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) has been linked to an increased risk of mortality,2 while high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) is being increasingly adopted for the management of respiratory failure.11 Consequently, there is a growing need to minimise invasive interventions in this population. These factors may partly account for the limitations of the Berlin definition in studying ARDS among such patients.

In 2023, the new global definition of ARDS12 incorporated the HFNO and the ratio of oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry to the fraction of inspired oxygen (SpO2/FiO2) as non-invasive diagnostic criteria. Consequently, there is an urgent need to investigate ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution and its impact on outcomes after allo-HSCT using this revised definition. This retrospective cohort study aimed to characterise ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution, assess its association with 1-year mortality post-allo-HSCT, and identify risk factors for ARDS development in this period.

Methods

Study participants

Participants in this study were identified from a comprehensive list of patients who underwent allo-HSCT at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (National Clinical Research Center for Hematologic Diseases) between 10 February 2016 and 31 March 2019. All patients aged 15 years or older who underwent allo-HSCT for any indication were included. Patients who did not proceed to transplantation due to severe pretransplant complications and those diagnosed with ARDS prior to transplantation were excluded. For individuals who underwent multiple HSCT procedures, data from the allo-HSCT performed within the study period were analysed, while only the number of prior HSCTs (including both autologous and allogeneic) before the study period was recorded.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcomes were incidence of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstruction after allo-HSCT and the 1-year mortality after transplantation. Secondary outcome measures included the duration of haematopoietic reconstitution, in-hospital mortality, incidence of acute graft versus host disease (GVHD), chronic GVHD and relapse after allo-HSCT.

Medical records of all patients were reviewed for up to 5 years following transplantation, and 5-year survival was recorded. Follow-up was conducted through review of medical records, telephone calls or text messages. Given that the number of patients lost to follow-up (n=325) increased beginning in the second year post-transplantation, 1-year mortality following allo-HSCT was selected as the primary outcome. The ascertainment of vital status at 1 year was achieved for all 1024 patients. The missing data of this study are presented in online supplemental table S1. All patients were followed from the time of admission until death or the date of the last available medical record. Loss to follow-up was defined as the inability to determine the survival status of the patient.

Data collection

Clinical data were reviewed by trained physicians specialising in critical care medicine and haematology to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Demographic information (age and gender), clinical characteristics (comorbidities, details of allo-HSCT, laboratory findings at admission and after allo-HSCT, types of oxygen therapy, complications and outcomes), as well as the occurrence of acute GVHD and relapse during and after hospitalisation, were collected and independently entered into the database by two analysts. In cases of discrepancies between the two data sets, the original medical records were consulted to ensure data accuracy.

All clinical data were collected until death or loss to follow-up. SpO2, blood gas analyses and oxygen therapy were continuously documented in nursing or medical records during the haematopoietic reconstitution period, and abnormal results were recorded to assess the onset and severity of ARDS. Personnel responsible for data collection and diagnostic review received training in ARDS diagnosis. The presence of ARDS was initially determined by trained physicians based on clinical data. For blood gas analyses and SpO2, the worst values within a 24-hour period were selected. Diagnoses of ARDS were independently evaluated by two physicians (GC and ZD). Disagreements were resolved by a senior intensivist (GQ). Cases with excessive fluid overload or heart failure were excluded during initial review. Patients with heart failure were excluded from ARDS diagnosis if heart failure was the primary explanation for respiratory failure, but remained in the cohort for overall complication and outcome analysis.

Hematopoietic reconstruction post-HSCT was defined4 as an absolute neutrophil count >0.5 × 109/L for three consecutive days without granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, platelet count >20 × 109/L for seven consecutive days without platelet transfusion, and haemoglobin >70 g/L without red blood cell transfusion. ARDS was diagnosed according to a new global definition of ARDS:12 (1) HFNO with a minimum flow rate of ≥30 L/min; (2) Use the ratio of partial pressure of oxygen in arterial bloodto the fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2)≤300 mm Hg or oxygen saturation as measured by SpO2/FiO2≤315 (if SpO2 is ≤97%) to identify hypoxaemia; (3) Retain bilateral opacities for imaging criteria but add ultrasound as an imaging modality, especially in resource-limited areas; and (4) In resource-limited settings, do not require positive end-expiratory pressure, oxygen flow rate or specific respiratory support devices. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes clinical practice guidelines: an increase in serum creatinine values ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 μ mol/l) within 48 hours, serum creatinine values ≥1.5 times the baseline within the previous 7 days, or urine volume ≤0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6 hours.13

Clinical manifestations, haematological indicators, cardiac evaluation, evaluation of liver function, haemodynamic parameters, documents of physician diagnoses, and related guidelines or standards were used to identify GVHD, chronic GVHD, acute heart failure, acute liver injury and shock.14,17

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as median (IQR) as they showed a skewed distribution. Proportions were used to express frequency data. Comparisons of continuous variables were made with the Mann-Whitney U test, while the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess differences in categorical variables, as appropriate.

Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between patients with or without ARDS using the log-rank test. To determine the independent risk factors for 1-year mortality, multivariate Cox regression models were used. Variables with p<0.1 in univariate Cox proportional hazards regression (online supplemental table S2) were included in the multivariate model. Probabilities of entering and removing variables in a stepwise manner in the multivariate model were 0.05 and 0.10, respectively. To determine the association between ARDS and mortality, HRs and 95% CIs were estimated with adjustment of age, interval months between illness onset and this transplantation, albumin level on admission, acute bleeding, shock and chronic GVHD (online supplemental table S3). Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression was also performed in discharged patients to determine the variables used for adjustment (online supplemental tables S4 and S5). Sensitivity analyses excluding patients without blood gas analyses (n=21) were performed to assess diagnostic robustness.

To determine the independent risk factors for ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution, multivariate logistic regression models were used. Variables with p<0.1 in univariate logistic regression (online supplemental table S6) were included in the multivariate model. Probabilities of entering and removing variables in a stepwise manner in the multivariate model were 0.05 and 0.10, respectively.

Data were analysed using SPSS V.25.0 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA). We used StataMP V.16 (StataCorp, 147 College Station, Texas, USA) and R x64 V.4.3.3 to create statistical charts. A two-tailed value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this retrospective study. This research analysed existing de-identified clinical data (2016–2019) obtained prior to the conceptualisation of the research question. Direct patient involvement was not feasible given the critical condition of patients with ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution and the post hoc nature of data analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

After excluding patients who did not undergo transplantation due to severe complications and those diagnosed with ARDS prior to HSCT, a total of 1024 patients with allo-HSCT were included in this study. The median age was 33.5 years (IQR, 24.0–45.0), 59.3% (n=607) were male, and 69.7% (n=714) of all patients underwent haploidentical HSCT.

Complications during hospitalisation included AKI in 46 patients (4.5%), acute liver injury in 250 patients (24.4%), acute bleeding in 32 patients (3.1%), acute heart failure in 60 patients (5.9%) and shock in 22 patients (2.1%). The median time to haematopoietic reconstitution was 16.0 days (IQR, 15.0–19.0). After allo-HSCT, 45 patients (4.4%) developed acute GVHD, 60 patients (5.9%) developed chronic GVHD and 37 patients (3.6%) experienced relapse.

A total of 45 patients (4.4%) developed ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution after allo-HSCT. The median time to onset of ARDS was 9.0 days (IQR, 5.0–13.0) following HSCT during haematopoietic recovery. The most common aetiology was infection (such as pneumonia and bloodstream infection), identified in 41 patients. ARDS was attributed to engraftment syndrome in two patients and to blood transfusion in another two patients. Arterial blood gas analysis was performed in 24 cases, while all patients underwent either chest radiography or chest CT scan. Among the 45 patients with clinically diagnosed ARDS, respiratory support modalities included: HFNO only (n=29), non-invasive ventilation (NIV) only (n=9), IMV only (n=5), sequential HFNO to NIV (n=1), and NIV escalated to IMV (n=1). SpO2/FiO2 was used to assist in the diagnosis. 41 patients developed ARDS with SpO2/FiO2≤235. Detailed information regarding ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution is provided in online supplemental tables S7 and S8. Of the 45 patients with ARDS, only 9 (20.0%) fully met Berlin definition criteria (online supplemental table S8). Given the potential for GVHD treatments to confound ARDS risk, we formally evaluated the sequence of clinical events. No incident acute GVHD cases preceded ARDS onset (online supplemental table S8), and chronic GVHD does not occur within the haematopoietic reconstitution phase.

58 patients (5.7%) died within 1 year after transplantation, of whom 22 (2.1%) died during the post-transplant hospitalisation. Among the 58 patients who died within the first year after allo-HSCT, severe infectious diseases were the most common predominant clinical diagnoses immediately preceding death. A comprehensive listing of individual diagnoses is provided in online supplemental table S9.

Patients with ARDS had a lower proportion of chemotherapy (75.6% vs 88.6%, p=0.009) and complete remission (80.0% vs 92.0%, p=0.005) compared with those without ARDS. The ARDS group also exhibited lower median values of white cell counts (2.4×109/L vs 3.6×109/L, p=0.007), lymphocyte counts (0.7×109/L vs 1.0×109/L, p=0.002), monocyte counts (0.2×109/L vs 0.3×109/L, p=0.024), neutrophil counts (1.1×109/L vs 2.0×109/L, p=0.009), platelet counts (31.0×109/L vs 147.0×109/L, p<0.001), haemoglobin (77.0 g/L vs 103.0 g/L, p<0.001), haematocrit (24.2% vs 32.3%, p<0.001), albumin (39.7 g/L vs 43.7 g/L, p<0.001) and prealbumin (230.1 mg/L vs 275.6 mg/L, p=0.013) at admission. However, they had a higher median blood urea nitrogen level (4.9 mmol/L vs 4.3 mmol/L, p<0.001) and a longer duration from illness onset to transplantation (6.8 months vs 5.8 months, p=0.015) (table 1).

Haematopoietic reconstitution ARDS and mortality

Patients who developed ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution had a significantly higher 1-year mortality after HSCT compared with those without ARDS (24/45 vs 34/979, log-rank p<0.0001) (figure 1a). A similar result was observed among the 1002 patients who were discharged from the hospital (10/31 vs 26/971, log-rank p<0.0001) (figure 1b).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for 1-year mortality after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). (a) All patients (n=1024). (b) Patients discharged alive (n=1002). The numbers under the graph represent the number of patients with or without haematopoietic reconstitution period acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) at risk of death on the indicated day.

In the multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression model, patients who developed ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution had a significantly higher risk of 1-year mortality after HSCT compared with those without ARDS (HR 7.99, 95% CI 4.13 to 15.44). Other independent risk factors included acute bleeding during hospitalisation (HR 4.50, 95% CI 2.16 to 9.40), shock during hospitalisation (HR 9.22, 95% CI 4.32 to 15.44) and chronic GVHD (HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.69 to 7.13) (figure 2a).

Among the discharged patients, analysis further demonstrated that ARDS remained an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality after HSCT (adjusted HR 7.80, 95% CI 3.44 to 17.67) (figure 2b). In addition, previous chemotherapy before allo-HSCT (adjusted HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.14), as well as chronic GVHD during hospitalisation or after discharge (adjusted HR 3.49, 95% CI 1.55 to 7.85), were identified as additional independent risk factors for 1-year mortality.

To quantify potential bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses: after excluding patients lacking blood gas analyses (n=21), the association between ARDS and 1-year mortality remained significant in the full cohort (n=1003; adjusted HR: 7.72, 95% CI 3.34 to 17.82, p<0.001) (online supplemental table S10). Consistency was confirmed in patients surviving initial hospitalisation (n=981; adjusted HR: 11.97, 95% CI 4.38 to 32.72, p<0.001) (online supplemental table S11).

Discussion

This study investigated over 1000 patients following allo-HSCT. Employing the new global ARDS definition for the first time, we characterised ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution and evaluated its association with post-transplant outcomes. Notably, applying the new definition suggested that ARDS during this period may be a non-negligible complication. It was associated with increased early (in-hospital) mortality and was identified as an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality post allo-HSCT, regardless of whether in-hospital deaths were excluded from the analysis. These findings highlight the critical need for increased vigilance and early recognition of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution after allo-HSCT.

With ongoing advances in allo-HSCT research and improvements in technique, the number of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplants performed each year continues to rise. Haploidentical transplantation has become the preferred option for high-risk patients due to its stronger antileukaemia effect.18 However, previous studies have reported a higher incidence of ARDS following allo-HSCT compared with auto-HSCT.9 Given the large patient population and high incidence of ARDS, we included only allo-HSCT recipients in this study. The LUNG SAFE Study indicated that two-thirds of ARDS cases are either diagnosed late or missed altogether.19 In settings with limited resources, SpO2/FiO2 can be used as a substitute for PaO2/FiO2.20 Irreversible (or nearly irreversible)3 pancytopenia caused by conditioning regimens and haematopoietic stem-cell infusion necessitates haematopoietic reconstitution, during which patients are particularly vulnerable. For immunocompromised patients, it is important to minimise the risks associated with invasive procedures. Therefore, it is reasonable to investigate the occurrence and impact of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution using the new global definition of ARDS. In our study, we found that 29 patients with ARDS were managed with HFNO alone, and SpO2/FiO2 was used to assist diagnosis in 21 patients who did not undergo blood gas analysis. The adoption of the new global definition may lead to increased recognition of ARDS in this population. Critically, our sensitivity analysis excluding patients lacking blood gas analysis demonstrated persistent significance of reconstitution-phase ARDS on 1-year mortality, validating the 2023 definition’s applicability in retrospective cohorts with incomplete physiological data. This methodological robustness mitigates concerns regarding temporal discordance between our data collection period (2016–2019) and the 2023 definition formalisation.

Previous studies have indicated that ARDS occurring within the first year after HSCT most frequently develops during the engraftment phase or shortly thereafter,9 yet the precise incidence and timing of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution had not been established; our findings help to address this gap. ARDS following HSCT can be divided into infectious and non-infectious aetiologies, and favourable outcomes rely on a comprehensive initial evaluation to identify the underlying cause of respiratory deterioration.21 22 In this study, we found that infection was the predominant cause of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution, consistent with previous reports,6 10 12 which may be attributable to the higher level of immunosuppression associated with allo-HSCT.8 Patients experiencing neutropenia or undergoing allo-HSCT are at elevated risk for respiratory complications and acute respiratory failure due to various specific factors,23 such as the worsening of pre-existing lung injury during recovery in patients with neutropenia, or the development of non-infectious pulmonary diseases after HSCT.24 25

ARDS, as here defined by the new global definition during the haematopoietic reconstitution period, was identified as an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality after allo-HSCT. The elevated mortality risk may be attributable to organ dysfunction resulting from respiratory failure, the need for IMV and worsening of patients’ underlying conditions.2 In addition, this study identified other risk factors for 1-year mortality after HSCT, including pretransplant laboratory abnormalities, in-hospital organ dysfunction and GVHD. While the distribution of these risk factors varied between the overall patient cohort and those who survived to discharge, ARDS consistently emerged as a significant risk factor for mortality.

Transcriptome profiling of whole blood has indicated that the immune response to lung injury in ARDS following HSCT differs markedly from that seen in non-HSCT-related ARDS, with immune dysregulation after HSCT potentially predisposing patients to ARDS.26 Additionally, there is indirect evidence suggesting that the lung may participate in platelet biogenesis.27 Since platelet transfusion is often required during haematopoietic reconstitution, lung injury may have a detrimental impact on this process. These factors may partially account for the increased mortality observed in patients who develop ARDS during the haematopoietic reconstitution period.

Given the association of ARDS with higher mortality after allo-HSCT, we further explored the risk factors for ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution. A prolonged interval from disease onset to HSCT, as well as multiple prior HSCTs, indicated a longer disease course and extended immunosuppression, both of which increase susceptibility to ARDS, particularly due to infection.5 In this study, RDW also emerged as a risk factor for ARDS. This easily obtainable biomarker has been previously identified as an independent predictor of mortality in patients with ARDS.28 Acute inflammation, both local and systemic, is a hallmark of ARDS,29 which may help explain the observed association between elevated RDW and ARDS. The inflammatory response impairs bone marrow function, and cytokine-mediated inhibition of erythrocyte maturation leads to increased production of reticulocytes, resulting in elevated RDW.30 The pathogenesis of ARDS involves inflammatory cell aggregation, synthesis of inflammatory mediators, and activation of the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems.29 Albumin, due to its physiological properties, may help ameliorate these injuries.31,33 Therefore, correcting hypoproteinaemia could potentially contribute to the prevention of ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution.

Limitations

This single-centre retrospective study may restrict the generalisability of our findings. However, we enrolled over 1000 patients, and the consistent trends in the results suggest reliability. While the data collection period (2016–2019) preceded the formal publication of the new 2023 global definition, we emphasise that all diagnostic criteria (acute hypoxaemia, bilateral opacities, non-cardiogenic origin) were rigorously applied during chart review. A significant proportion of patients (n=325) were lost to follow-up after the first year. While this attrition limits the interpretability of long-term survival beyond 1 year in this cohort, it does not impact the analysis of our primary endpoint, first-year mortality, for which vital status was ascertained in all patients. Berlin-defined ARDS incidence could not be fully ascertained due to: missing physiological data (eg, arterial blood gases not routinely performed in unstable, thrombocytopenic patients), and therapeutic constraints (some families decline mechanical ventilation for critical illness due to financial burdens and prognostic concerns). Consequently, ARDS diagnosis may overestimate Berlin-defined cases in patients lacking PaO2/FiO2 assessment (n=21). Conversely, exclusion of pulmonary oedema attributable to heart failure/volume overload could underestimate true ARDS incidence when overlapping pathologies existed. Lung ultrasound data were unavailable for further refinement. While missing blood gas analyses may limit phenotyping granularity, its omission was clinically necessary for this bleeding-prone cohort. Sensitivity analyses confirmed our primary outcome’s stability, aligning with the 2023 global definition’s flexibility in high-risk settings.

Conclusion

Applying the 2023 new global definition, our findings suggest that ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution may represent a non-negligible complication after allo-HSCT. ARDS in this phase was associated with increased in-hospital mortality and served as an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality following allo-HSCT. Given its significant impact, ARDS during haematopoietic reconstitution should not be overlooked. Prospective multicentre studies are urgently needed to establish improved strategies for monitoring, early detection and management.
