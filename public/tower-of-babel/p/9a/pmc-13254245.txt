Qualified but Unprepared: The Readiness Paradox in Early Career Mental Health Nurses—A Descriptive Qualitative Study
Authors: Louise Alexander, Gabrielle Burdeu, Anita Cappello, Keith Griffin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMental health issues such as anxiety and depression are reported at higher rates in undergraduate nursing students than in the general population, highlighting the potential value of enhanced wellbeing supports for recent graduates. Evidence suggests that supportive strategies, including clinical supervision and structured wellbeing programmes, can improve graduate wellbeing and retention within health services. This study aimed to explore mental health nursing educators' perceptions of early career nurses' work readiness, wellbeing and preparedness for practice. Using a qualitative descriptive design, semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 10 registered nurse educators employed at a large tertiary metropolitan health service in Melbourne, Australia. Data were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke's method. Four key themes emerged: (1) Registered but not ready, (2) Perceptions, practice and role identity, (3) The work of wellbeing and (4) At the boundaries of education and care. Findings indicate that graduates frequently lack readiness for independent practice and demonstrate a disconnect between expectations and the realities of mental health nursing. Emerging mental health concerns among graduates further highlight the emotional demands of the role and gaps in coping strategies. Educators face the added challenge of navigating a dual role: supporting graduates' wellbeing while maintaining educational responsibilities, without assuming a counselling function. These insights underscore the need for structured interventions to support both graduates and educators, including enhanced preceptorship, resilience training and clear delineation of educational versus therapeutic roles, to strengthen workforce readiness and sustainability in mental health nursing.Keywords: early career mental health nurses, mental health nursing, nurse educator role, nursing education, role clarity, wellbeing, work readiness

Introduction

While there is minimal seminal evidence on the motivations of Australian pre‐registration Bachelor of Nursing students in choosing mental health as a career (Happell and Gaskin 2013), there are few studies exploring the experiences and perceptions of early career nurses (ECN) working in mental health. This evidence gap is particularly salient given the outcomes of the Royal Commission into Victoria's Mental Health System, which identified critical workforce shortages, high levels of occupational stress, and the need to strengthen recruitment, retention and capability of the mental health workforce, including nursing (State of Victoria 2021). Subsequent mental health and wellbeing reform in the post Covid‐19 era has further emphasised workforce sustainability, wellbeing and career pathways for mental health professionals (Victorian Department of Health 2023).

Understanding why nurses choose to work in mental health, and how they can be supported to stay in the profession may assist in both recruiting and retaining ECNs (Alexander et al. 2024). Developing strategies to support the wellbeing of ECNs may also reduce psychological distress (Foster, Steele, et al. 2024), and support career longevity in mental health (Alexander et al. 2015). Additionally, this information may be valuable in providing the evidence necessary to inform Bachelor of Nursing curricula, which continues to experience a dilution of therapeutic communication skills due to consistent inclusion of technical skills deemed more important for a beginner nurse working in the acute general nursing setting (Warrender et al. 2024).

Background

Australia, like many other developed countries, is experiencing a significant shortage of nurses (State of Victoria 2021), with difficulty in attracting and retaining newly graduated mental health nurses (Adams et al. 2021; World Health Organization 2021). Evidence suggests that pre‐registration nursing students experience higher rates of mental health issues such as anxiety and depression compared to the general population (Alexander et al. 2023). The transition from student to professional practice is well documented as a period of heightened stress, driven by increased role expectations, heavier workloads and challenges unique to clinical practice areas (Joseph et al. 2022). In Victoria, early career mental health nurses report high levels of stress and poor mental health outcomes (Foster, Roche, et al. 2021). These difficulties are compounded by workplace stressors specific to mental health nursing, including exposure to violence, aggression, staff conflict and workforce shortages (Foster, Roche, et al. 2019; Foster, Roche, et al. 2021). Collectively, these factors represent significant threats to early career mental health nurses' job satisfaction and wellbeing and are strongly associated with intentions to leave the specialty during the early stages of their careers (Foster, Shochet, et al. 2024).

Nursing leaders have identified significant gaps in the work readiness of graduate nurses entering mental health settings (Joseph et al. 2022; Lakeman et al. 2024). Work readiness requires pre‐registration programmes to equip students with clinical competencies, transferable workplace skills and individualised capabilities to support transition into professional practice (Hazelton et al. 2024; Hurley et al. 2023). In Australia, however, regulatory frameworks emphasise preparing generalist nurses with technical skills for acute medical and surgical contexts (Alexander et al. 2023; Hurley et al. 2023) and recommend dispersing mental health concepts across the curriculum rather than teaching them as discrete units (Happell et al. 2018). This generalist approach to nursing education prioritises appropriately supported acute care clinical placements (Ward and Barry 2018), with less emphasis on securing student access to acute mental health placements with qualified mental health nurse educators (Moxham et al. 2024). In some tertiary settings, students are often allocated clinical placements in settings such as aged care or community practice, which inadequately provide foundational experiences, leaving some students poorly informed about the realities of mental health nursing (Happell and Gaskin 2013; Foster, Roche, et al. 2019; Happell et al. 2021). Furthermore, the timing and duration of placements in some tertiary settings are tightly constrained within the curriculum, potentially leaving little flexibility for students who express interest in the specialty to pursue supplementary placements in mental health (Alexander et al. 2025). Consequently, early‐career mental health nurses frequently enter the workforce without the requisite skills and attributes for a positive transition, contributing to workplace stress, poor retention and a persistent challenge to build a sustainable workforce (Foster, Withers, et al. 2019; National Mental Health Commission 2024).

Despite the critical need for a sustainable workforce, evidence consistently shows that mental health nursing remains an unpopular career choice, largely due to pervasive stigma surrounding mental illness (Edward et al. 2015; Happell et al. 2018). Limited exposure to mental health content within nursing curricula and insufficient clinical placements restrict students' opportunities to develop positive attitudes toward people experiencing mental illness (Foster, Roche, et al. 2019; Patterson et al. 2017). These educational gaps (Connell et al. 2022), combined with negative societal perceptions (Happell et al. 2018), reduce students' willingness to pursue mental health nursing, while those who do enter the field often face challenges to retention as stigma and prejudice persist within practice environments (Hazelton et al. 2024; National Mental Health Commission 2024). While such external factors discourage many from entering the specialty, when students do choose to work in this specialty area, they are often individuals with their own lived experience of mental distress (Alexander et al. 2024). This alternative pathway brings unique strengths but also introduces complex professional risks.

ECN with their own lived experiences of mental health challenges can foster empathy, authenticity and a strong commitment to supporting recovery‐oriented practice. This intrinsic motivation aligns with broader movements to value lived experience within the mental health workforce (Roennfeldt and Byrne 2021), yet the literature cautions that such personal connections can also create professional vulnerabilities (National Mental Health Commission 2024). Nurses with lived experience may struggle to maintain clear professional boundaries, particularly around self‐disclosure, and can experience role confusion when navigating the dual identity of ‘survivor’ and clinician (Roennfeldt and Byrne 2021). Exposure to client distress may also heighten risks of re‐traumatisation or secondary trauma (Henderson et al. 2025), leaving these nurses more vulnerable to burnout (National Mental Health Commission 2024). Furthermore, stigma within the profession can compound these challenges, as colleagues may question competence or view disclosure negatively (Hungerford 2025). These vulnerabilities are exacerbated by the lack of exposure to essential pre‐registration mental health curricula and appropriate experiential learning opportunities (Barry et al. 2025). As a consequence, some nurses may enter the specialty without the fundamental skills and personal attributes required to fully understand the professional roles and expectations of the mental health nurse. Evidence demonstrates that while lived experience can enrich mental health nursing practice, structured support, supervision and boundary training are essential to ensure that the benefits of empathy and commitment are balanced against the risks of blurred roles and psychological stress (Hungerford 2025; Hurley et al. 2023).

Early career mental health nurses' job satisfaction and intention to remain working in mental health settings are positively influenced by early workplace experiences, including the availability of professional and organisational support, opportunities for skill development, and the quality of team and organisational culture (Foster, Withers, et al. 2019; Hudays et al. 2024; Lakeman et al. 2024). ECNs frequently report supportive leadership, access to skilled clinical supervision, structured graduate programmes and positive interdisciplinary relationships enhance confidence, professional identity, and job satisfaction (Cleary and Armour 2022; Foster, Roche, et al. 2019). Conversely, deficits in skills and knowledge, particularly in therapeutic communication, risk assessment, boundary setting and managing challenging behaviours, are commonly identified by ECNs as sources of anxiety and reduced self‐efficacy (Hurley et al. 2023; Joseph et al. 2022). Where these gaps are proactively addressed through targeted education, mentorship, reflective practice and protected learning time, ECNs report improved wellbeing, stronger professional engagement, and increased commitment to remaining in mental health nursing (Foster, Roche, et al. 2019; Ibrahim et al. 2024).

The limitations of pre‐registration education, combined with the demands of mental health nursing, place ECNs at heightened risk during transition to practice. Clinical nurse educators have considerable expertise in mental health clinical education and play a central role in mitigating this risk by designing and delivering education and support programmes that bridge gaps between undergraduate preparation and the realities of mental health nursing. While clinical nurse educator roles often extend beyond formal teaching to include clinical leadership, workforce development and service responsibilities, they are uniquely positioned to observe patterns of distress, skill deficit, challenges and skill development among ECNs over time. Despite their integral role in supporting transition, wellbeing and work readiness, clinical nurse educators' insights are therefore essential to inform effective transition programmes, reduce early career psychological distress, and strengthen retention within the mental health nursing workforce. To that end, the aim of this study was to explore mental health nursing educators' perceptions of ECNs' work readiness, wellbeing and preparedness for practice.

Methods and Design

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive methodology (Neergaard et al. 2009). A qualitative descriptive design was chosen as it allows for a rich account of participants' experiences, aligning with the study's aim to explore mental health nursing educators' perceptions of early career nurses' work readiness, wellbeing and preparedness for practice (Sandelowski 2000). Exploration of clinical nurse educators' perspectives is necessary to inform strategies to improve early‐in‐career nurses' experiences. This design is particularly suited to studies seeking to remain close to participants' accounts while generating practice‐relevant insights. This method was selected as semi‐structured interviews enable participants to articulate the relational and nuanced nature of their experiences in working with recent graduates, capturing depth and complexity that would not be accessible through survey data alone. While focus groups were considered as an alternative approach, they were deemed less suitable for this study, as they may limit the exploration of individual perspectives and reduce the depth and contextual richness of the data generated. As such, semi‐structured interviews were conducted employing Braun and Clarke's (2006) method of thematic analysis. This study is reported using the EQUATOR Network recommendations for qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines (Tong et al. 2007; Data S1).

Participants and Setting

Registered and enrolled mental health nurses working in nursing education employed in a large tertiary metropolitan health service were invited to participate in this study. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Participants were accessed through their workplace setting, where an invitation outlining the study purpose and participation requirements was distributed, with participants completing an online eligibility survey to register interest. Eligibility criteria included being an Enrolled Nurse or Registered Nurse educator employed at the targeted health service. Written and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The decision to include both Registered and Enrolled Nurses in the study was intentional. The educational sessions provided by the health service as part of all graduate programs (EN and RN) are merged meaning early‐in‐career Registered and Enrolled Nurses attend the same sessions, and these sessions are delivered by educators from both cohorts. Educators, regardless of division, also provide support to ECNs on units and, as such, are familiar with the issues experienced across all ECNs. Interviews were recorded using Microsoft Teams by two senior research academics (L.A. and G.B.) and deidentified and transcribed verbatim. Interview transcripts were returned to participants for verification. No transcripts were returned with annotations. Data were collected between April–May 2025 continuing until data sufficiency was reached. Recruitment continued until data sufficiency was achieved in accordance with Guest et al. (2006) and Braun and Clarke (2021), in which data collection continues until additional information no longer contributes meaningful new insights to the research questions. This method emphasises the adequacy of data for robust analysis rather than an absolute numeric threshold and in this instance was reached with a sample size of N = 10.

Data Analysis

We used thematic analysis to analyse data. Thematic analysis involves a six step process where: (1) the researcher becomes familiar with data through repeated readings; (2) codes are identified and generated to label pertinent elements of the data; (3) researchers look for themes through grouping codes; (4) themes are reviewed and refined for accuracy; (5) themes are defined and named; and (6) analysis is written up in the form of structured results. The first two steps of thematic analysis were undertaken by members of the research team independently, with convergence of the team occurring in step three. Several meetings were conducted where codes were discussed and shared among the team to establish patterns. We employed a consensus method when developing preliminary themes (Vonna et al. 2022) and in the event there were any conflicts within the research team, we agreed to resolve differences in accordance with Hemmler et al. (2020). Once the team had agreed on themes, the first author led subsequent stages of theme definitions and consistency across the data set.

The research team engaged in ongoing reflexive practice throughout the study. The authors bring backgrounds including senior research and academic positions, and clinical expertise and leadership, which informed both the research design and the interpretation of findings. Despite this, we acknowledge that our professional experiences and perspectives may have influenced data collection and analysis. To address this, reflexive discussions were held regularly within the research team to critically examine assumptions and interpretations (Berger 2015). These processes supported a more transparent and thoughtful engagement with the data and contributed to the credibility of the findings.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the health service (LR24‐066‐112 907) and reciprocal ethics obtained from the University (2025/HE000295). There were no requests to stop an interview or withdraw consent from this project.

Results

Ten mental health nurse educators (RN, n = 8; EN, n = 2) participated in interviews, consisting of two males and eight females (see Table 1). The average length of each interview was 50.24 min, with a range of 36.40 to 70.65 and SD 10.93. There were no terminated interviews. Participant experience in nursing education ranged from one to over 10 years, with most participants having between one and 3 years of experience.

After analysis, four themes emerged: (1) Registered but not ready! (2) Perceptions, practice and role identity; (3) The work of wellbeing; and (4) At the boundaries of education and care.

Theme 1: Registered but Not Ready!

This theme captures educators' concerns about the appropriateness, duration, and timing of mental health clinical placements in undergraduate nursing programmes. It also demonstrates how these factors, combined with underdeveloped interpersonal skills, were perceived to impact ECNs' readiness for the workplace, with participants consistently describing a misalignment between training and clinical practice expectations. Across participants, there was a shared perception that current placement models did not adequately reflect the complexity or demands of contemporary mental health nursing.

Educators described significant concerns about the quality and appropriateness of mental health placement, with several noting that some ECN had decided on a career in mental health nursing after only having a placement in a dementia ward, or in private psychiatry: ‘…usually they end up on a dementia ward, and they call it mental health’ (CNE8). Another educator also raised concerns about students being located in the community for a mental health placement, with educators highlighting that placement settings were often poorly aligned with the demands of acute mental health practice: ‘…they've done their mental health placements in nursing homes…they don't know what they're in for’ (CNE 5). This was perceived to limit ECNs' exposure to more complex clinical environments and contribute to a lack of preparedness for inpatient care.

Additionally, the length of the placement was also scrutinised by educators with participants highlighting that short placement durations limited opportunities for meaningful learning and skill consolidation: ‘…two weeks or three weeks maximum…it's actually not long enough for people to learn (CNE7). Educators consistently identified that limited placement exposure contributed to underdeveloped interpersonal skills with many describing the need to go back…into the basics’ (CNE10) when supporting ECNs. Participants further described a tendency for ECNs to prioritise task completion over relational engagement, reflecting a greater comfort with structured, procedural aspects of care and ‘ticking a lot of questions off the list to get through your work’ (CNE 9). This was interpreted as a lack of confidence in navigating the more complex and nuanced aspects of therapeutic interaction.

Some educators suggested that limited confidence in therapeutic skills may have contributed to a reliance on task‐based or pharmacological responses: ‘Sometimes all we need is that person just to sit with us, and that's often missed. I think people (ECN) feel like they need to give a medication or do something’ (CNE4). The difficulty ECNs experienced in embedding therapeutic engagement within routine clinical tasks further highlighted a gap in both clinical skill development and role clarity, particularly in understanding how therapeutic interactions are integrated into everyday practice. Many educators described a mismatch between expectations of the role and its realities, as one educator explained:

…they're thinking they're going to be sitting down, perhaps, and chatting with someone about, ‘how's your life?’ But that's not it. It's like, you're expected to get all this information while you're taking their physical observations, or while you're administering medication. It's about a consolidation of tasks. I think they're coming in with the idea that they get to sit down and listen to people and provide them with understanding. And that's how their nursing role will be fulfilled. But it's not so. I think it's a basic misunderstanding of what mental health nursing is. (CNE1)

This reflects a broader misunderstanding of the mental health nursing role and the integration of therapeutic engagement within routine clinical tasks.

Theme 2: Perceptions, Practice and Role Identity

This theme described educator perceptions about learner's role clarity, particularly the impact of lived experience on their motivations for entering a career in mental health. This resulted in a propensity to overshare personal lived experience, creating boundary issues and further confusing the role between peer support, and mental health nursing. Additionally, educators noted a perception that ECNs believed that general nursing skills (e.g., taking bloods, ECGs and attending to ADLs) were not essential skills for a mental health nurse.

ECNs personal lived experience was a topic that was frequently raised by educators, particularly in relation to nursing role clarity. Many educators described ECNs thinking they would be able to use their lived experience to positively influence interactions with consumers: with participants highlighting concerns regarding the appropriateness of this approach and its impact on maintaining professional boundaries: ‘I think there's problems, especially when they share a diagnosis…this isn't about you! This is about them!’ (CNE5). These accounts suggest a blurring of roles, with educators distinguishing between the expectations of mental health nursing and those of peer support. Educators further described concerns that over‐disclosure negatively impacted therapeutic relationships, particularly where consumers were highly vulnerable.

Educators consistently described the tension between recognising ECNs' lived experience and maintaining a professional, consumer‐focused orientation. While lived experience was often framed as a potential strength, it also raised concerns about blurred boundaries and role confusion. Some educators observed that ECNs could be motivated by personal recovery or self‐understanding, rather than a clear focus on consumer care, with one noting that certain ECNs approached the role to ‘work myself and my family out’ (CNE1). At the same time, educators highlighted the delicate balance between openness and over‐disclosure; as one explained, while transparency can support destigmatisation, there are limits, particularly given that ‘as a nurse we don't want you to be telling everybody…their colleagues don't really want to know’ (CNE6). Together this account pointed to the ongoing challenge of integrating lived experience in ways that enhanced, rather than disrupted, therapeutic professional boundaries.

These tensions were closely linked to broader issues of role clarity. Educators frequently described ECNs as entering mental health nursing with incomplete or inaccurate understandings of the role's scope; a byproduct of poor placement preparation. A common perception was that the work primarily involved relational or conversational care, illustrated by the view that mental health nursing consisted of ‘sitting down with people and holding their hands and having a good chat’ (CNE5). This understanding often underestimated the practical and physical components of care, with some educators claiming ECNs reportedly selected the speciality to ‘avoid tasks such as activities of daily living (ADLs)’ (CNE7). Similarly, educators noted a tendency to assume that general nursing competencies were less relevant in mental health settings, despite the ongoing need for acute clinical skills. As one educator emphasised, inpatient mental health environments still require responses to ‘code blues…MET calls…(and) knowing what's in the resus trolley’ (CNE2).

This misalignment extended to knowledge gaps in core clinical skills, including physical health assessment and procedural competence, reinforcing educators' concerns that ECNs were underprepared for the realities of practice. Such gaps were not simply technical but reflected a broader misunderstanding of the integrated nature of mental and physical healthcare within contemporary services.

A lack of role clarity was further compounded by limited understanding of the complexity of the mental health system. Educators described ECNs as often entering the field with strong ideals about advocacy, reform, and making meaningful change, but with little appreciation of systemic constraints that inhibit this. As one educator reflected, many arrived wanting to ‘be the sort of nurse that I wish I had’ (CNE1), yet these aspirations could quickly be challenged by the realities of clinical work. Early workplace experiences were therefore frequently described as disorienting or disillusioning, particularly when ECNs encountered the routine clinical and physical care demands embedded within mental health settings.

Theme 3: The Work of Wellbeing

This theme explored the challenges experienced by ECNs as they transitioned into their ECN roles, with educators identifying concerns related to vicarious trauma, emotional labour and difficulties maintaining work‐life balance. Collectively, these issues highlighted the significant emotional and psychological demands associated with early professional practice in mental health settings.

Educators described ECNs as often underestimating the cumulative impact of workplace stress, particularly in the absence of consistent self‐care practices. Rather than recognising stress as ongoing and accumulative, ECNs were reported to interpret difficult shifts as isolated events, which limited proactive coping. As one educator explained, stress was often normalised until it reached a tipping point, with ECNs ‘breaking down and needing…support…or having to come off the ward’ (CNE4). This pattern suggested a limited capacity early in transition to anticipate and manage the ongoing emotional demands of the role.

These challenges were frequently framed in terms of vicarious trauma, with educators emphasising the emotional weight of exposure to consumers' lived experiences. ECNs were described as becoming overwhelmed by ‘hearing people's stories’ and ‘just having to sit with all that’ (CNE5), particularly when they felt unprepared to process these experiences. At the same time, educators highlighted the inherent tension within mental health nursing between emotional engagement and self‐protection. Emotional responsiveness was viewed as essential to therapeutic practice yet also carried risk; as articulated by one educator, being impacted by consumers was seen as part of being human, but the challenge lay in not ‘taking that on and being traumatised’ (CNE4). This reflected the complex emotional labour required to remain empathically present while maintaining psychological boundaries.

The emotional demands of the role were described as particularly pronounced for ECNs with lived experience, who in some instances appeared more vulnerable to distress. Educators recounted situations where ECNs experienced significant emotional overwhelm in the workplace, including expressions of self‐harm ideation and visible distress, such as ‘crying under the (nursing station) desk…when they felt really overwhelmed’ (CNE4). These accounts pointed to the intersection of personal vulnerability and professional demands, raising further challenges for maintaining wellbeing during transition.

Difficulties with work‐life balance further compounded these pressures. Although educators reported that the organisation promoted strategies to support wellbeing, these efforts were often undermined by systemic workforce constraints and interpersonal dynamics within teams. ECNs were described as highly motivated to belong and contribute, which sometimes resulted in over‐engagement with work demands including working additional hours or double shifts in understaffed environments. This tendency to overextend was not simply an individual choice but reflected a broader workplace culture and staffing context that made boundary‐setting difficult. Consequently, educators observed that burnout could emerge early in transition, with some ECNs becoming ‘burnt out already…a few months down the track’ (ECN5) despite prior attempts to guide them.

Taken together, these findings illustrate the ‘work of wellbeing’ was not solely an individual responsibility, but was shaped by the interaction between emotional labour, personal vulnerability and systemic pressures. ECNs' initial enthusiasm and commitment to care, when combined with limited coping strategies and high workplace demands, contributed to heightened risk of distress and burnout during early professional practice.

Theme 4: At the Boundaries of Education and Care

This theme examined the intersection experienced by educators who balance the care of ECNs in distress with the demands of their primary role in nurse education. Educators described the challenges of navigating this dual responsibility and the ambiguity surrounding the point at which their role shifted from educator to counsellor.

Educators consistently described the emotional toll of supporting ECNs in distress, positioning this as an increasingly prominent yet implicit component of their role. Concern for ECN wellbeing extended beyond academic or clinical performance, with educators describing heightened vigilance regarding emotional stability, as reflected in one educator's comment that they were ‘very concerned about their (ECNs) wellbeing’ (CNE3). This concern often translated into practical and environmental adaptations, such as creating psychologically safe spaces where ECNs could disclose distress, with one educator noting the introduction of ‘tissues and sensory items’ (CNE3) in their office in response to frequent emotional episodes.

Many educators reported that they had been initially unprepared for the extent and intensity of this pastoral dimension of the role. The need to support ECBNs in distress was described as evolving over time, often in response to repeated incidents that exposed gaps in organisational processes. As one educator explained, ‘no one knew what to do…we needed a clear path for escalation…(now) we've put in policies…flow charts’ (CNE5) highlighting how individual uncertainty prompted the development of more structured responses. However, while such changes improved clarity at a systems level, they did not remove the day‐to‐day emotional labour experienced by educators.

Educators also reflected on the cumulative impact of this responsibility, describing a sense of ongoing exposure to ECN distress that could be difficult to manage. One educator characterised this pattern as a repeated ‘train wreck…each time the ECNs come in…(it's) hard to watch sometimes’ (CNE1), illustrating both the emotional intensity and cyclical nature of the experience. Even when organisational support was perceived as present, the persistence of these situations suggested educators remained closely positioned to the emotional consequences of ECN transition.

A key tension identified across accounts was the challenge of maintaining professional boundaries while responding to ECNs' emotional needs. Educators described blurring of roles, with some experiencing their work as shifting from patient‐focused care to supporting ECNs in a comparable way, noting that ‘it's not that different (to nursing patients) …other educators have really struggled with it’ (CNE4). This blurring introduced ethical uncertainty, particularly when educators extended support beyond formal expectations. For example, one educator described contacting an ECN outside of work hours following a difficult shift, while simultaneously questioning whether this ‘crossed a boundary’ (CNE2). Such examples illustrate the complexity of negotiating appropriate limits in a context where concerns about safety and wellbeing were immediate and personal.

These findings demonstrate that the educator role had expanded beyond its formal education remit to include substantial emotional and relational labour. This expansion was shaped by the convergence of ECN vulnerability, limited preparedness for distress and evolving organisational responses. While structures such as escalation pathways provided some guidance, they did not fully resolve the ambiguity surrounding role boundaries or mitigate the emotional impact on educators. As such, the work of supporting ECNs in distress required ongoing negotiation between care, responsibility, and professional limits, underscoring the need for clearer role clarity and more comprehensive supports for educator wellbeing.

Discussion

The aims of this study were to explore the perceptions and experiences of educators in relation to early career mental health nurses' workplace readiness, wellbeing and preparedness to work in mental health. This is the first study to describe the impacts to educators of ECNs' insufficient preparedness, as well as the tensions arising from educators' negotiation of a dual role encompassing both counselling and educational responsibilities. While the transition experiences of ECNs have been more recently the subject of empirical research, comparatively little attention has been given to the impact of these challenges on educators responsible for supporting them. Furthermore, this paper contributes to the limited body of literature addressing the implications of employing ECNs with lived experience who enter the workforce without adequate preparation or clear role delineation.

Role clarity refers to the extent to which nurses have a clear understanding of their professional responsibilities, expectations and practice boundaries (O'Rourke 2021). Establishing role clarity is essential for ensuring accountability and supporting effective collaboration within multidisciplinary healthcare teams. This is consistent with evidence demonstrating that unclear role expectations are associated with increased stress, reduced job satisfaction and poorer transition outcomes among ECNs (Aguas and Mallari 2025). Despite its importance, the concept of professional role clarity has been insufficiently examined within the nursing literature, prompting recent calls for its stronger integration into undergraduate curricula (Prosen and Ličen 2023). The motivations influencing ECNs' decisions to pursue careers in mental health nursing in Australia have been attributed to several interrelated factors. Hazelton et al. (2024) identified that many ECNs report limited exposure to mental health content during their undergraduate programmes, a challenge compounded by inconsistent placement quality, including placements that lack contextual relevance (e.g., nursing homes). This aligns with broader evidence emphasising the value of high‐quality mental health placements for shaping career intentions (Alexander et al. 2025; Foster, Giandinoto, et al. 2021). This relationship between placement quality and career choice is well established, with positive supported clinical experiences consistently linked to increased interest in speciality areas (Anyango et al. 2024). Such educational and clinical experiences collectively shape ECNs' expectations as they transition into the mental health workforce, where role ambiguity and uncertainty can undermine confidence and successful adjustment to the registered nurse role. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that early career nurses often experience a disconnect between expectations formed during undergraduate education and the realities of clinical practice, particularly in speciality areas such as mental health (Foster, Giandinoto, et al. 2021; Rogers et al. 2023).

While Hazelton et al. (2024) argue that structured ECN programmes and mentorship are needed to address persistent theory‐to‐practice gaps, we contend that many of these foundational issues fall within the remit of the university sector. In particular, our findings lend support to ongoing concerns regarding generalist undergraduate nursing models, which may not adequately prepare graduates for the complexity of specialised fields such as mental health. This concern reflects broader international debate regarding the capacity of generalist nursing programmes to adequately prepare graduates for increasingly complex and specialised practice environments (International Council of Nurses 2022; Wynne et al. 2024). Ensuring that students receive well‐designed, sufficiently lengthy mental health placements—paired with appropriate preparatory learning—is a core educational responsibility. When mental health nursing curricula are diluted (International Council of Nurses 2022), students enter clinical environments inadequately prepared and are subsequently less likely to choose mental health nursing as a career pathway.

Recent findings by Alexander et al. (2025) further demonstrate that students often describe negative and polarising experiences during community mental health placements. Reports of incivility and reduced learning opportunities were influential in shaping students' decisions not to pursue mental health nursing upon graduation. Consistent with this, educators in our study expressed concern about the suitability of community placements for students, noting that such settings may not offer an accurate representation of mental health nursing practice, as community roles in most services are reserved for experienced clinicians. Similar concerns have been raised in the literature regarding variability in placement quality and the impact of negative clinical learning environments on student engagement and career decision‐making (Russ et al. 2023).

Our findings also indicate that some ECNs are motivated to enter mental health nursing because of their own lived experience of mental illness. The inclusion of lived experience within the mental health workforce is increasingly recognised as a valuable and enriching contribution, consistent with recovery‐oriented practice; however, its integration into professional roles remains complex and under‐theorised (Cleary and Armour 2022). Importantly, lived experience should not be positioned as a risk factor in itself, but rather as a dimension of practice that requires appropriate preparation, boundaries, and organisational support to be safely and effectively integrated. While the presence of lived experience among ECNs may not be unexpected, emerging evidence suggests that student nurses often experience poor mental health at the commencement of their studies (Alexander et al. 2023), with symptoms frequently worsening over their degree (Foster et al. 2025). These patterns highlight the need for strengthened psychosocial supports, reflective supervision and clear role expectations within graduate programmes, rather than suggesting inherent limitations associated with lived experience. The broader literature similarly emphasises the importance of structured and accessible support for ECNs during transition (Foster, Shochet, et al. 2024), particularly as many transition‐to‐speciality programmes aim to rapidly ‘upskill’ ECNs for practice in complex clinical environments. This need is especially pronounced in mental health settings, where the relational and emotional demands of care are high (Happell et al. 2018).

The decision to pursue mental health nursing on the basis of lived experience should be understood within a broader context of role ambiguity and evolving workforce expectations, rather than a limitation of lived experience itself. Educators in our study reported that ECNs frequently misunderstood the scope and function of mental health nursing, a challenge that reflects gaps in preparation, role clarity and systems literacy, particularly within a mental health system undergoing reform as is the case in Victoria, Australia. Several educators noted that ECNs entered the workforce believing they would have significant influence on consumer recovery trajectories, an expectation that may reflect recovery‐oriented ideals but can become challenging to enact within current service structures in Australia. Educators also observed that ECNs with lived or living experience often conflated peer work with mental health nursing, with some expressing uncertainty about whether, and how, their lived experience could be used therapeutically. This uncertainty highlights the need for clearer guidance, structured supervision and education regarding the purposeful and ethical integration of lived experience into nursing roles. This aligns with King et al. (2020), who caution that disclosure of lived experience requires careful consideration, including attention to boundaries, therapeutic intent and appropriate clinical supervision. In the absence of explicit professional guidelines and organisational supports, there is a risk that uncertainty and inconsistent practice will persist, underscoring the importance of developing frameworks that both value and appropriately support lived experience within mental health nursing.

Workplace readiness is a multifaceted construct encompassing clinical competence, confidence, professional identity formation, and communication proficiency; yet the quality and effectiveness of transition programmes remain variable (Rogers et al. 2023). Notably, our study identified that many ECNs lacked basic foundational nursing skills—competencies that are core components of pre‐registration curricula—again raising concerns regarding the adequacy of undergraduate preparation. Across the findings, several strategies were either described or implied to address these challenges. These included strengthening the quality and relevance of undergraduate mental health placements, embedding clearer expectations regarding the scope of mental health nursing within curricula, and enhancing structured transition‐to‐practice programmes that explicitly integrate both clinical skills development and psychological support. Educators also highlighted the importance of clearer role delineation, targeted supervision regarding the use of lived experience in practice, and organisational frameworks that support both ECN and educator wellbeing. Collectively, these approaches point to the need for coordinated responses across both education and health service sectors.

Educators in our study also described challenges in determining when and how to intervene to support ECNs experiencing distress, noting the blurring of boundaries between their roles as mental health experts, clinical educators and informal counsellors. This is consistent with Deschenes and van Kessel (2024), who reported that moral distress is common among ECN and that educators often lack formalised strategies or organisational support to assist learners in processing emotionally challenging clinical encounters. While educators in our study felt supported by some organisational structures designed to manage these issues, they nonetheless identified persisting ambiguities—particularly concerning the extent of their responsibility to provide follow‐up support and the risk that such support might be perceived as intrusive by ECNs.

From December 2025, the Victorian Government will implement new WorkSafe Psychological Safety Laws, requiring health services to manage psychological hazards with the same level of rigour applied to physical hazards (WorkSafe Victoria 2025). Under this legislative framework, organisations will be obligated to systematically identify, assess, and mitigate psychosocial risks—including occupational stress, bullying, role ambiguity and exposure to traumatic events—supported by strengthened requirements for leadership accountability, reporting and regulatory compliance. These reforms carry substantial implications for healthcare environments characterised by high emotional labour, vicarious trauma and elevated burnout risk. These risks are documented within mental health nursing populations, where sustained exposure to distressing clinical experiences is a recognised occupational hazard (Cranage and Foster 2022). In this context, ECN programmes must prioritise the development of transition structures that explicitly embed psychological safety principles to ensure that ECNs are adequately supported and protected during their entry to practice. Equally, the psychological demands placed on clinical educators responsible for supporting distressed ECNs warrant focused attention, with health services needing to consider strategies that safeguard educator wellbeing alongside that of their learners. To operationalise these expectations, health services should adopt multi‐level strategies that include structured psychological safety training for ECN and educators, routine reflective supervision and formalised debriefing pathways following exposure to distressing clinical events. Additionally, embedding psychologically informed leadership practices (Johnson et al. 2025), alongside clear escalation pathways and access to independent wellbeing supports, may strengthen organisational capacity to proactively mitigate psychosocial risk. System‐level approaches, including protected time for supervision and ongoing workforce development in trauma‐informed care, will be critical to ensuring sustainable and psychologically safe learning environments.

Strengths and Limitations

The absence of newly registered nurses within the research team represents a limitation, as their involvement may have enhanced the study through the inclusion of lived‐experience perspectives in both the development of interview questions and the interpretation of findings. It is also important to acknowledge that these findings reflect educators' perceptions of ECNs' preparedness, motivations and experiences and should therefore be taken with caution. While these perspectives provide valuable insights into workplace challenges, they do not constitute direct evidence of ECNs' intentions or experiences, but rather how these are understood and managed within the clinical education context. Furthermore, the findings are derived from a specific sample of educators and may not be generalisable to all settings or contexts.

Despite these limitations, this study addresses a notable gap in the literature specifically examining educators' experiences of supporting ECN in mental health in relation to preparedness, wellbeing and role development. As such, this study provides important initial insights into an underexplored area of practice and highlights key considerations for both education and workforce development.

Conclusion

This study underscores the complex interplay between educational preparation, role clarity and workplace demands in shaping the experiences of ECN entering mental health practice. Despite sustained calls for strengthening mental health content within undergraduate curricula, many ECNs continue to report limited exposure to high‐quality placement opportunities, inadequate foundational skills, and persistent ambiguity about the scope and function of mental health nursing. These educational gaps contribute not only to uncertainty during transition but also to misinterpretations of the professional role—particularly among ECNs with lived or living experience—who may conflate nursing with peer work or hold unrealistic expectations regarding their capacity to influence consumer recovery. Educators, positioned at the frontline of supporting these ECNs, face substantial emotional and professional demands, often navigating blurred boundaries in the absence of structured institutional supports.

As the sector prepares for the implementation of the WorkSafe Psychological Safety Laws (WorkSafe Victoria 2025), the imperative for robust, psychologically safe transition programmes becomes even more pronounced. Health services will be required to address psychosocial hazards with heightened diligence, and this obligation must extend to both ECNs and the educators who support them. Strengthening undergraduate mental health education, ensuring the quality and appropriateness of clinical placements and developing transition programmes that integrate psychological safety as a core component are essential steps toward building a sustainable and well‐prepared mental health nursing workforce. Without coordinated action across universities and health services, the challenges identified in this study are likely to persist, perpetuating preventable distress among ECNs and undermining workforce retention in a specialty already under significant pressure.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

This study has important implications for clinical practice, particularly in the preparation and support of ECNs working in mental health settings. Educators consistently report that ECNs often lack readiness for independent practice, with a notable disconnect between their perceptions of the mental health nursing role and the realities of day‐to‐day clinical work. Persistent gaps in role clarity—exacerbated by variable undergraduate preparation and inconsistent placement experiences—leave ECNs entering the workforce with an incomplete understanding of the scope, boundaries and responsibilities of mental health nursing. These findings highlight the shared responsibility between universities and health services for ensuring work readiness. Universities play a critical role in providing robust mental health curricula, high‐quality and appropriately structured clinical placements and sufficient opportunities for students to develop foundational competencies prior to graduation. Without this educational grounding, transition programmes are forced to compensate for deficits that should have been addressed during pre‐registration training.

ECNs are also presenting with emerging mental health challenges of their own, underscoring the emotional demands of the role and the inadequacy of existing coping strategies. With the introduction of the WorkSafe Psychological Safety Laws (WorkSafe Victoria 2025), health services will be required to address such psychosocial risks proactively, reinforcing the importance of creating psychologically safe learning and practice environments for early career nurses.

Finally, a key finding of this paper is the substantial burden placed on educators, who must navigate the complex (unofficial) dual role of supporting ECNs' emotional wellbeing while maintaining their educational responsibilities. This tension between providing guidance and pastoral support without assuming a therapeutic or counselling role places considerable strain on educators and may compromise their capacity to deliver effective teaching. These insights underscore the urgent need for structured supports for both ECNs and educators, including enhanced preceptorship, targeted resilience and psychological safety training and clear organisational frameworks that delineate educational versus therapeutic responsibilities. Collectively, these measures are essential to fostering a sustainable, well‐prepared and well‐supported mental health nursing workforce.
