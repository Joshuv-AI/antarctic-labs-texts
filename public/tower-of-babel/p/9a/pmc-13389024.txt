Water engineering via surfactant coacervates enables efficient and robust solar hydrogen evolution
Authors: Xiaojuan Bai, Jiahong Liu, Huaiyu Song, Caiwei Zhang, Jiachen Guo, Letian Yuan, Jintao Tong, Cheng Ma, Yiou Wang, Jianbin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater is essential in photosynthesis, acting both as a reactant and a medium regulating charge transport and reaction kinetics. While catalyst design has received significant attention, the impact of water structure on reactivity remains almost unexplored. Here, we report that water engineering via surfactant coacervates can significantly improve the performance of artificial photosynthesis for hydrogen production. By creating dynamic aqueous coacervate phases within a disrupted hydrogen-bond network, we create environments that yield more active, disordered water molecules. This tuned water state yields a stable hydrogen evolution rate of 918 mmol·g−1·h−1 and an apparent quantum efficiency of 69% at 420 nm in an aqueous methanol solution, demonstrating competitive catalytic performance. Spectroscopic and calorimetric characterizations, as well as computational simulations, indicate that surfactant coacervates increase the amount of intermediate water while weakening hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing the apparent activation energy, promoting charge transfer, and facilitating efficient catalysis. This study presents an approach to regulate water structure to enhance catalytic reactivity for solar hydrogen production, with broad potential for applications in water-involved catalytic systems.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis, Solar fuels

Introduction

Water molecules play a pivotal role in natural photosynthesis, not only as substrates for oxygen evolution but also as dynamic mediators of proton transfer and energy conversion1–3. Due to the existence of attractive hydrogen bonds, water molecules tend to form three structures on a catalyst surface, namely, the free water, the intermediate water, and the tightly bound water, among which the intermediate water is believed to be the dominant factor in reactivity due to its appropriate activation energy4,5. Engaging water molecules in further chemical reactions requires, first, dissociation from the hydrogen-bonding network to become intermediately bound to the catalytic center6. Nature precisely regulates water, including its positioning, hydrogen bonding, and mobility, through specialized channels and protein gating7–9. This fine control enables rapid, directional proton and electron flows within Photosystem II, ensuring the marked efficiency of the water-splitting reaction and facilitating seamless conversion of solar energy. For example, the hydrogen-bonding network of water around the protein can be disrupted by potassium ions (K+), promoting water transport to the O1 channel near the oxygen-evolving center and thereby enhancing oxygen evolution10. Mimicking natural photosynthesis, photocatalytic water splitting into hydrogen (H2) and oxygen (O2) is considered a sustainable strategy for addressing global energy and environmental issues11,12. Over the past half-century, the field has witnessed progress in catalyst development to enhance light absorption, charge separation, and surface reactions13–17. In contrast to nature’s ingenious coordination, the understanding of the impact of water structure on photocatalytic hydrogen evolution remains limited and underexplored.

Accelerating photocatalysis by tuning the water structure requires the construction of a large number of intermediate water molecules via disrupting the intrinsic hydrogen-bond network18–20. For example, doping a semiconductor can alter the hydrogen-bond topologies of the first water layer on its surface by tuning the local electric field, thereby promoting water splitting21. Furthermore, the thickness of the interfacial water layer has been reported to influence the activity of photocatalytic H2 production4. Specifically, the single hydrogen bond in the water dimer facilitates water splitting, whereas the one-dimensional hydrogen bond in water chains hinders the reaction. Therefore, a water structure with weakened hydrogen bonding is crucial for promoting photocatalytic water splitting22,23. However, strategies that rely exclusively on precise photocatalyst design to modulate water structure at the solid-liquid interface have limitations: their influence is typically restricted to a few interfacial water molecules around the catalysts, yielding only marginal enhancements in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Furthermore, such approaches have predominantly remained within theoretical and modeling studies, failing to meet the high performance required for practical application. Therefore, a method for tuning the bulk water structure is highly desirable to unlock significantly higher photocatalytic efficiency. This reactant-centric strategy could be applied broadly to diverse photocatalysts, thereby overcoming current limitations.

In contrast, aqueous surfactants offer a feasible approach to tailor the overall water structure by creating numerous microscopic liquid-liquid interfaces in bulk water through the formation of colloids, such as micelles24 or vesicles25. The hydrophobic tails of surfactants aggregate, disrupt the hydrogen-bond network of water, and form various assemblies26–30. Due to the strong electric fields at the interface and the reduced tetrahedral order, water molecules at the interface exhibit weaker hydrogen bonding and a lower activation energy31,32. As such, the strength of hydrogen bonds, the thickness, and the status of water molecules on the catalyst surface can be carefully engineered to increase the percentage of active, loosely bound intermediate water, thereby facilitating efficient solar hydrogen production (Fig. 1a). Therefore, reactions occurring at the colloid-water interface have been reported to exhibit improved reaction kinetics, higher yields, and selectivity33–37. However, the application of this effect in photocatalytic water splitting, by leveraging the modulated hydrogen-bond network and the reactive water environment at the liquid-liquid interface, has rarely been studied.

Herein, we demonstrate that the water structure can be effectively tailored by surfactant coacervates composed of dodecyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (DTAC) and sodium octyl sulfonate (SOS), resulting in a boosted and robust HER of 918 mmol∙g−1∙h−1 on a standard Pt/CdS photocatalyst at 25 °C with an apparent quantum efficiency exceeding 69% at 420 nm, which shows competitive performance among reported photocatalytic and thermal catalytic systems. In contrast to the 71% drop in activity during four cycles on Pt/CdS in pure water, the surfactant coacervate system stably produces hydrogen over eight cycles (32 h) with only a small loss in activity (~10%). Moreover, the concept is universally applicable to the use of common surfactant-coacervate mixtures to enhance hydrogen production. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), optical spectroscopies, and computational simulations reveal that the presence of surfactant coacervates increases the concentration of intermediate water, facilitates rapid electron transfer, and lowers the activation energy. As such, a more disordered water structure with a disrupted hydrogen-bonding network in the reaction system promotes photocatalytic H2 production. This work provides valuable insights into efficient photocatalysis involving water, as well as other catalytic applications.

Results

Compositions of the surfactant coacervates and characterizations of the catalyst

The present study aims to investigate the influence of aqueous surfactants on water structures and, consequently, their impact on hydrogen production performance in photocatalytic water splitting (Fig. 1a). Hence, instead of developing catalysts, we focused on three surfactant systems: an Aqueous Surfactant Two-Phase system (ASTP), a Super Homogeneous Phase system (SHP), and a Homogeneous Phase system (HP). Mixing stock solutions of two surfactants, DTAC and SOS, at different total concentrations (cT) and molar ratios (XDTAC) yields three phases, as illustrated in the phase diagram at 25 °C (Figs. 1b and S1). Most DTAC-to-SOS concentration ratios result in the formation of HP (blue regions in Fig. 1b, middle). In our previous studies38,39, we found that a mixture of cationic and anionic surfactants forms a coacervate phase at a specific concentration and molar ratio, which separates from the dilute phase and appears as ASTP. ASTP covers two narrow ranges with a DTAC/SOS molar ratio deviated from electroneutral mixing (30% < XDTAC < 35% and 60% < XDTAC < 65%) at different cT (purple regions in Fig. 1b, middle). The coacervate phase of ASTP (ASTP-C), rich in numerous and condensed nanoscale vesicles, is commonly located in the upper phase, while the corresponding dilute phase (ASTP-D) is located in the lower phase.

As the cT increases, the ratio between ASTP-C and ASTP-D rises (Fig. S3), until the entire water system consists of a homogeneous coacervate phase (red regions in Fig. 1b, middle). Here, the reappearance of a homogeneous phase at higher surfactant concentration is defined as SHP and remains stable for more than 1 year without phase separation or precipitation. Unlike other general homogeneous phases (HP), SHP has a similar microscopic structure to the coacervate phase of ASTP, and can be turned into ASTP by dilution with water. We collectively refer to SHP and ASTP-C as surfactant coacervates, in which numerous nanoscale colloids form a large surface area of microscopic interfaces. In the photographs of the samples (XDTAC = 33%, with cT ranging from 50 to 400 mM; Fig. 1b, Fig. S3), one can observe that HP is a homogeneous, opalescent solution, attributed to the presence of large aggregates that scatter light. All the samples in the surfactant coacervate systems are transparent and are therefore suitable for light-driven hydrogen production.

We then selected the well-documented photocatalyst cadmium sulfide (CdS) with platinum (Pt) as a cocatalyst to evaluate H2 production performance. The crystal structure of the synthesized catalyst was first analyzed by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2a). These peak positions closely match those of hexagonal wurtzite CdS (JCPDS card no. 41-1049). The sharp diffraction peaks indicate good catalyst crystallinity. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) shows that the peaks at 161.54 and 162.89 eV correspond to the S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 components of S2-, and the peaks at 405.15 and 411.95 eV correspond to the Cd 3d5/2 and Cd 3d3/2 components of Cd2+, respectively (Fig. 2b, c).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal well-defined nanorod morphology for Pt/CdS (Fig. 2d, e). Two distinct lattice fringes corresponding to Pt/CdS (0.338 nm, (002) plane and 0.222 nm, (111) plane, respectively) were observed in a high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) image (Fig. 2f), further revealing the formation of Pt/CdS composite. Statistical analysis indicated an average length of ~22.7 nm with high uniformity (Fig. 2g). Notably, distinct contrast differences between the body and tips of individual nanorods suggested an asymmetric structure. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping confirms selective Pt localization at the nanorod tips, while Cd and S are uniformly distributed. This structure suppresses electron-hole recombination and enhances charge-carrier transfer efficiency (Fig. 2h–k).

Photocatalytic H2 production performance in the presence of surfactant coacervates

We then evaluated the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution rates (HER) in different surfactant systems in an aqueous solution containing 1% methanol (CH3OH) as a hole acceptor under visible-light irradiation from a 300 W xenon lamp using Pt/CdS. The custom-built photocatalytic reactor setups and the standard curve for the quantitative determination of H2 by gas chromatography are shown in Figs. S1 and S2, respectively. Adding 1% CH3OH to the surfactant solution does not alter the phase behavior, turbidity, or pH value (Fig. S3, Table S1-S2). We first systematically compared the photocatalytic HERs in water, a NaCl solution, single surfactant solutions, dual surfactant systems of HP, the dilute phase of ASTP (ASTP-D), the coacervate phase of ASTP (ASTP-C), and SHP with an identical concentration of surfactants (Fig. 3a). Only the coacervate phases (e.g., SHP system: cT = 300 mM, XDTAC = 33%) exhibit a pronounced activity increase—up to threefold higher than pure water, while all non-coacervate systems perform similarly to the aqueous control. Furthermore, varying the concentration of individual surfactants does not yield comparable improvements (Fig. S4). At a DTAC-SOS concentration of 300 mM, only XDTAC at 33% and 60% show significantly high HERs, corresponding to the SHP and coacervate phase of ASTP, respectively (Fig. S5). In water without methanol as a sacrificial agent, SHP again exhibited the highest HER (Fig. S6). As the coacervate concentration increases from 100 mM to 400 mM, HER initially increases, then slightly decreases at 400 mM (Fig. 3b), possibly due to increased viscosity (Table S3). Together, the above results directly confirm that the coacervate phase in the SHP or the ASTP can promote the photocatalytic HER, and the enhanced photocatalytic performance arises from the distinct physicochemical microenvironment of the coacervate phase rather than from the surfactant molecules alone.

To clarify the role of Pt species, we compared CdS nanorods mixed with independently synthesized Pt nanoparticles to Pt-decorated CdS. The mixed system shows minimal hydrogen production (Fig. S7), ruling out the possibility that randomly dispersed Pt nanoparticles act as effective cocatalysts under these conditions. In contrast, Pt attached to CdS enables efficient charge transfer and reduces recombination, thereby increasing catalytic performance.

To verify the general applicability of surfactant coacervates for enhancing HER, we measured the HERs using various SHPs comprising typical surfactant mixtures (DTAC-SOS, DTAC-SD, BDDAC-SOS, BDDAC-SD), various hole sacrificial agents (CH3OH, Na2S/Na2SO3), as well as representative photocatalysts (Pt/CdS, Pt/TiO2, Pt/C3N4). All of them exhibit improved performance in solar hydrogen production compared to that in pristine H2O (Figs. 3c and S8–S10), indicating that the strategy is universal. Moreover, as the percentage of CH3OH increases from 1% to 20% in the solution (Fig. 3c and S11), the system exhibits a marked increase in HER up to 918 mmol∙g−1∙h−1 at 25 °C and under ambient conditions, which is competitive in both photocatalysis and thermocatalysis (Tables S5 and S6).

Apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) is a crucial criterion for demonstrating how efficiently photons are utilized for redox reactions. The trend of AQEs is consistent with the UV-Vis absorption spectrum, indicating that the reaction is driven by light (Fig. 3d). The AQEs of H2 production in SHP were determined to be 69% at 420 nm, 54% at 450 nm, and 23% at 500 nm, which are 3.7, 3.8, and 6.6 times that in pure water, respectively. Such an enhancement suggests that surfactant coacervates can promote the utilization of visible solar energy for hydrogen production.

Metal sulfides are typically unstable due to photocorrosion40; therefore, the stability of SHP was also evaluated over 8 cycles (32 h). Extended cycling tests under light show that the 1% methanol aqueous system without SHP exhibits a 71% decay over the first four cycles (16 h) (Fig. 3e). In contrast, the surfactant coacervate (SHP) system produces hydrogen stably over eight cycles (32 h) with only a small loss in activity (~10%). This clear difference demonstrates that the coacervate phase significantly improves catalytic durability under operating conditions, supporting its protective effect on the photocatalyst.

In addition, we evaluated the structural stability of Pt/CdS before and after photocatalysis to understand the role of the SHP environment. XRD patterns indicate that the crystal structures remain unchanged in both catalysts (Fig. S12). XPS analysis shows that Pt/CdS experiences partial surface photocorrosion in methanol and water without SHP, as indicated by a positive shift in the S 2p binding energy after the reaction, with unchanged Cd signals (Fig. S13), suggesting that sulfur oxidation is the main degradation pathway. In contrast, no noticeable shifts in either the S or the Cd spectra are observed in the SHP environment, indicating that surface chemical degradation is effectively suppressed (Fig. S14). Similarly, TEM reveals a reduction in nanorod size only in the system without SHP, whereas the morphology remains intact in the presence of SHP (Fig. S15). Overall, these results confirm that SHP stabilizes both the surface chemistry and the structural integrity of Pt/CdS, which is consistent with the improved catalytic durability observed during long-term operation.

Moreover, the marked AQE of H2 production at 420 nm and the light-driven HER at 25 °C in SHP are both competitive among the reported hydrogen production systems from aqueous methanol solutions, although the present study was carried out at 25 °C, which is much lower than the previous benchmarks (Fig. 3f, Table S5 and S6).

Microstructures and water distribution of surfactant coacervates

To gain a fundamental understanding of the mechanism by which water structure enhances H2 production activity in surfactant coacervates, the microstructures, water distribution, and viscosity were carefully characterized using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). We then selected the SHP containing surfactant coacervates (XDTAC = 33%) as the model system in the presence of 1% CH3OH to minimize CH3OH’s effect on water structure.

From the TEM images (Figs. 4a and S16), a distinct shift in the size and density of the formed vesicles is observed as the surfactant concentration increases from 50 mM to 400 mM, corresponding to the HP, ASTP-C, and SHP phases. In HP, the system consists of large, discrete vesicles with a diameter of approximately 259 nm, resulting in strong light scattering and an opalescent appearance. As cT increases, aggregates initially grow through adhesion in ASTP-C, then shrink and rearrange more densely after forming SHP, resulting in a transparent and uniform appearance (Figs. S17–18). More importantly, when cT = 400 mM, the aggregates in the system become so closely packed that some of them even connect, which corresponds to two peaks in DLS and a sudden increase and shear-thinning behavior in shear viscosity (Table S3).

The Pt/CdS nanorods are distributed in the space between vesicles or on the boundary of surfactant aggregates (Fig. S19). Such a spatial arrangement facilitates direct interaction between Pt/CdS and water molecules in a specific region of the water-coacervate interface.

Changes in the microstructure and the arrangement of aggregates inevitably alter water’s properties. Therefore, to understand differences in water structure in these surfactant systems, we used DSC to determine the concentrations of free, intermediate, and tightly bound water. By monitoring the melting process of previously frozen systems, the temperatures at which crystallites of free water and intermediate water melt are recorded as T1 and T2, respectively. The melting enthalpy of specific types of water was calculated from the area of the endothermic peak during the melting process, and the instrument was calibrated by pure H2O, yielding a melting enthalpy of bulk water (△HW = 335 J/g). The contents of W1, W2, and W3 represent the content of free water, intermediate water, and tightly bound water, respectively. According to the DSC melting curves at different cT in the presence of 1% CH3OH (Fig. 4b), compared with the pure system (Fig. S20), T2 moves towards higher temperature and splits into two peaks around −6 °C to −3 °C, while T1 does not change position. Additional DSC results for the SHP system over a more finely resolved concentration range are presented in Fig. S21. Interestingly, the W2 peak is observed only in the ASTP-C and SHP systems, where coacervate is present, suggesting interactions among dense aggregates.

Then, the relationship between the surfactant concentration (cT) and the contents of different types of water (W1, W2, W3), as well as the melting temperature of free water (T1), was analyzed. As the concentration of surfactant (cT) increases, the melting temperature (T1) and the concentration (W1) of free water decrease (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, the intermediate water (W2) emerges at cT = 100–400 mM and increases with cT, corresponding to the ASTP-C and SHP systems where coacervate is present. The tightly bound water (W3) is always present due to the hydration effect of the ionic headgroups of the surfactants. In other words, W2 and W3 are formed within a coacervate by breaking the original ordered hydrogen-bond network of water molecules. To further explore the type of water structure that influences HER, we compared the W2 and W3 contents with HER in ASTP-C and SHP at 200 mM. Although the sum of W2 and W3 in the two systems is quite similar, the HER in SHP (with a higher W2) at cT = 200 mM is far higher than that in ASTP-C, indicating that W2 is more likely the key factor determining HER. W1, W2, and W3 in the pure surfactant system show a similar trend with increasing cT (Fig. S22). For the control experiments, the DSC results for the single-surfactant solution, NaCl, and ASTP-D (Figs. S23 and S24) show little difference relative to pure water. Therefore, this intermediate water is observed only in coacervate-containing systems.

On the other hand, attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-IR) spectra (Fig. S25) show that as the total concentration (cT) increases, the O-H stretching vibration peak shifts to higher wavenumbers, indicating weakening of the original hydrogen-bond network of water. In contrast, minimal changes are observed in the single-surfactant solutions NaCl and ASTP-D compared to pure water. Furthermore, in low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) (Fig. S26), the coacervate system shows an additional, faster relaxation component with a shorter T2, unlike other systems, which exhibit only one peak in the 2400–2700 ms range. As cT increases, this component intensifies and shifts to shorter T2 values, suggesting a progressive perturbation of the hydrogen-bond network. The pronounced high-wavenumber shift of the O–H stretching band (ATR-IR) and the presence of a shorter-T2 component (low-field NMR) jointly substantiate that the surfactant coacervate plays a crucial role in increasing the fraction of intermediate water by disrupting the hydrogen-bond network.

The relationship between solar hydrogen production and the fraction of intermediate water in different surfactant systems is summarized (Fig. 4c, d). As the surfactant concentration increases from 0 to 300 mM, the amount of intermediate water grows, and the HER shows a positive correlation. The observed positive relationship between the HER and the fraction of intermediate water suggests that the enrichment of intermediate water is closely linked to improved photocatalytic performance. The drop in the HER at cT = 400 mM is due to the increased viscosity (Fig. S27–S28, Table S3), which hinders mass transfer. Figure 4e shows a schematic of the water distribution around the surfactant aggregates and Pt/CdS. The surfactant coacervates, composed of abundant nanoscale vesicles, exhibit a distinct concentration-dependent accumulation of intermediate water, which facilitates enhanced interactions with Pt/CdS located around the surfactant aggregates, thereby promoting hydrogen evolution performance.

To distinguish the effect of the coacervate microenvironment from possible surfactant adsorption, a pre-adsorption control experiment was performed. The catalyst, pretreated with a surfactant and then washed, showed no significant increase in activity, indicating that the catalytic improvement primarily results from the coacervate-regulated microenvironment rather than surface adsorption alone (Fig. S29).

Notably, while the enhanced hydrogen evolution is primarily attributed to the specific microenvironment of the coacervate phase, the abundant liquid–liquid interfaces may also generate strong interfacial electric fields that could further promote charge separation and catalytic kinetics, as suggested by recent studies31,41. The formation of coacervates is therefore expected to modify the local electric field distribution, providing an additional, plausible contribution to the observed performance. To evaluate the role of the interfacial electric field, we performed ζ-potential measurements under two conditions: varying the total surfactant concentration at a fixed cation-anion ratio and adjusting the cation-anion ratio at a fixed total concentration. Although the HER varies significantly with surfactant concentration, the ζ-potential remains nearly unchanged. Conversely, changes in ζ-potential mainly indicate the presence of excess ionic surfactant, while enhanced catalytic activity occurs only when the SHP or coacervate phase forms (Fig. S30). These findings suggest that electrostatic effects may be involved but are not the main cause of the activity enhancement.

Furthermore, to decouple the effect of ionic strength from water-structure regulation, non-surface-active salts (NaCl, KCl, and Na2SO4) were added to produce ionic strengths comparable to or higher than those of the coacervate system without forming a coacervate phase. Under these conditions, the HERs remain similar to those in pure water, with only minor variation (Fig. S31), showing that ionic-strength or electric-field modulation alone does not explain the observed catalytic enhancement.

Additional control experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of pH (Fig. S32), methanol concentration (Fig. S11), viscosity (Fig. S28c), and light scattering (Table S7). None of these factors alone reproduces the observed enhancement in activity, indicating that they are not the primary contributors to the improved hydrogen-evolution performance. In contrast, the water microenvironment shows the clearest connection to hydrogen evolution performance, as the fraction of intermediate water consistently correlates with the observed changes in activity. Notably, although it remains experimentally challenging to precisely distinguish catalyst-interfacial water from vesicle-associated interfacial water in this complex system, the catalytic reaction takes place only at the catalyst interface. Therefore, any water-state change that consistently accompanies activity enhancement must be relevant to the catalytic interface rather than merely to bulk properties.

Mechanism of high H2 production performance in surfactant coacervates

To understand the origin of the significant catalytic performance induced by SHP, we first employed femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy to investigate the charge carrier dynamics of Pt/CdS in various solutions: pure water (H2O), methanol aqueous solution (H2O + CH3OH), surfactant coacervates (SHP), and SHP + CH3OH. The TA spectra show induced absorption changes as a function of wavelength and variable delay time (from fs to ns) following a 355 nm pump pulse, probed with a white-light pulse. Samples in both pure H2O and SHP solution exhibit a dominant exciton bleach signal at 460-470 nm (Figs. 5a and S33), which originates from the filling of conduction band states42,43. Figure 5b illustrates the detailed kinetics of exciton bleaching in the above samples within 0 ps to 1 ns. In the absence of a hole acceptor, the recovery of bleaching in pure H2O (pale green) is primarily due to the recombination of charge carriers (τH2O = 2.3 ps). In contrast, the recovery in the presence of a hole acceptor, CH3OH (dark green), shows distinctly slower decay, indicating a suppressed charge recombination (τH2O+CH3OH = 9.6 ps). Notably, the surfactant coacervates (SHP, pink) exhibit an even more significant prolongation of the charge recombination, reaching a 59-fold longer average time of 135.6 ps compared to water. Such a phenomenon suggests that surfactant coacervates play a distinct role in facilitating charge separation and migration.

To clarify whether surfactant coacervates facilitate electron transfer or hole transfer, we further compared the detailed kinetics of electron transfer in a methanol aqueous solution in the presence and absence of SHP (Fig. 5b, inset). The recovery of bleaching in a solution containing hole-accepting CH3OH primarily represents the transfer of electrons. Compared to traces in a methanol solution, the signals show a sharp drop within the first 100 ps in the presence of surfactant coacervates (dark pink), indicating rapid electron transfer. As such, the average charge-transfer time in SHP (τSHP+CH3OH=0.8ps) is 3.6 times shorter than that in methanol solution (τH2O+CH3OH=2.9ps). Such accelerated electron transport by surfactant coacervates is likely due to the specific water structure in the SHP system, in which protons from intermediate water molecules can be efficiently delivered across a weak hydrogen-bonding network for reduction43, thereby enhancing photocatalytic HER, a phenomenon not previously reported.

To understand the influence of surfactant coacervates on the surface redox reactions, in situ EPR was conducted to capture the reaction intermediates (Fig. 5c). In the dark, the population of unpaired electrons or holes was virtually undetectable. However, under illumination, in samples containing an identical concentration of Pt/CdS, the characteristic quartet EPR signal of hydroxyl radicals (∙OH) confirms the effective utilization of holes for water oxidation. The stronger signal in the SHP system indicates a more efficient hole-consumption pathway, which not only suppresses electron-hole recombination and thereby boosts HER but also protects the CdS lattice from oxidation, leading to the observed enhancement in both activity and stability. In addition, the absence of signals of alkyl radicals (∙R) indicates that surfactants do not decompose during the photocatalytic process. ATR-IR spectra obtained from the systems before and after the 32-h stability test also showed that the structures of the surfactant molecules remained unchanged (Fig. S34). Additionally, the photocurrent was recorded in the presence and absence of surfactant coacervates. A 1.7-fold enhancement in the transient photocurrent was observed in SHP compared to a 300 mM Na2SO4 solution, attributed to the rapid charge-carrier separation in Pt/CdS within the SHP system (Fig. S35), which again benefits efficient photocatalysis.

Moreover, to verify whether the energy barrier for photocatalytic water splitting differs in SHP and pure H2O, the apparent activation energies in SHP and H2O were calculated according to HERs measured at 293–343 K (Figs. 5d and S36) following the Arrhenius equation. The corresponding apparent activation energy (Ea) in H2O is calculated to be 18.7 kJ/mol. In contrast, the activation energy in SHP was lowered to 6.8 kJ/mol, which is only <36% of that in pure water. The lower apparent activation energy indicates that the protons in SHP solutions require less energy to be activated, likely because the more disordered water structure induced by the surfactant coacervates disrupts the hydrogen-bonding network. Since an identical amount of solar energy was provided to the Pt/CdS catalysts during the reactions, a lower energy barrier to overcome results in higher HER in SHP than in H2O, leading to highly efficient solar hydrogen production.

Therefore, we illustrate the overall mechanism of the competitive performance of surfactant coacervates in photocatalysis in Fig. 5e. Compared with pristine H2O (the green line on the left), the presence of surfactant coacervates (the pink line on the right) increases the content of intermediate water (W2), facilitates more efficient electron transfer, and lowers the activation energy. This indicates that a more disordered water structure within a disrupted hydrogen bonding network in the reaction system is favorable for promoting photocatalytic H2 production. This advantage is only found in surfactant coacervate systems, where numerous and densely arranged vesicles are formed. These vesicle structures create additional interfaces, thereby generating more intermediate water than other systems. From this perspective, hydrogen-evolution performance can be significantly increased upon the formation of stable, abundant vesicles, which are likely responsible for the high HER activity and improved stability observed across different surfactant coacervate systems and photocatalysts. More importantly, by optimizing the CH3OH proportion, we achieved a considerable HER rate of 918 mmol·g−1·h−1 and an AQE of 69% at 420 nm. As such, the present method for water modification offers a rational, universal approach to photocatalytic water splitting.

Furthermore, we performed simulations to provide molecular-level support for the proposed mechanism. The computational model comprises two parts: a catalyst–water system and a catalyst–water–surfactant coacervate system (Fig. 6a, b, see Supplementary Data 1 for details of the modeling). Given the complexity of the real catalytic interface under operating conditions, this simplified model is intended to capture the possible influence of the coacervate environment on interfacial water structure rather than to provide a fully quantitative description of the exact reaction system. To examine how coacervates may affect water activation at the molecular level, we first analyzed the O–H bond-length distribution of water in pure H₂O and in surfactant coacervates using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. As shown in Fig. 6c, the average O–H bond in the coacervate phase is slightly longer and exhibits a broader distribution than that in pure water, suggesting weakened O–H bonding and enhanced structural flexibility. This broader distribution is consistent with the presence of diverse local aqueous microenvironments within coacervates, which may favor the initial activation of water. These computational trends are consistent with experimental characterizations of water structure obtained from DSC, IR, and low-field NMR measurements. Subsequent density functional theory (DFT) calculations based on this model give a lower HER transition-state barrier in the coacervate system than in pure H₂O (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 37), supporting the experimentally inferred role of the coacervate environment in facilitating O–H bond activation.

The surfactant coacervate also has potential for practical applications in photocatalysis, including solar hydrogen production, when water serves as the solvent or reactant. Surfactants can be recovered by evaporation and reconcentration for reuse in forming fresh coacervates via established methods44. Moreover, engineering a continuous-flow reactor to ensure uniform light distribution and efficient hydrogen mass transfer within the coacervate is critical, as is evaluating long-term catalyst stability for sustained operation45. In addition, Pt/CdS catalysts can be effectively isolated by centrifugation or microfiltration after dissociation of the coacervate phase, thereby enabling catalyst recycling46.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate how surfactant coacervates, comprising numerous nanoscale vesicles and microscopic interfaces, tailor the water structure, resulting in a competitive photocatalytic HER of 918 mmol·g-1·h-1 on a standard Pt/CdS at 25 °C, with AQE exceeding 69% at 420 nm, which is competitive in both photocatalysis and thermocatalysis. Whereas the HER of Pt/CdS in pure water dropped to only <30% of the initial HER in 16 h of four consecutive cycles, the surfactant coacervate system retained robust performance during eight cycles (32 h) with only a small activity loss (~10%). Furthermore, this strategy demonstrates broad applicability for enhancing photocatalytic HER using various surfactant mixtures, photocatalysts, and hole-sacrificial agents. Characterizations, including TEM, DSC, ATR-IR, LF-NMR, photocurrent, EPR, and TA spectroscopies, along with computational simulations, reveal that the surfactant coacervates in SHP can effectively increase the concentration of intermediate water (W2) and disrupt the hydrogen bond network, resulting in rapid electron transfer and a lower activation energy for favorable photocatalysis. This finding not only reveals the functions of surfactant coacervates in solar hydrogen production performance but also provides fundamental insights into engineering the structure of water molecules in catalytic applications involving water as a reactant or medium.

Methods

Materials and synthesis

All chemicals were commercially available and used without further purification unless otherwise stated. Sodium octyl sulfonate (SOS, 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Dodecyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (DTAC, 99%), sodium decanoate (SD, 99%), benzyl dimethyl dodecyl ammonium chloride (BDDAC, 99%), carbon nitride (C3N4, 99%), titanium dioxide (TiO2, 99%), sodium sulfide (Na2S, 99%) and sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, 99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99%) was purchased from Beijing Tongguang Fine Chemicals Company. Hydrochloric acid (37% aq. HCl) was purchased from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. Methanol (MeOH, ≥99.5% AR) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. A water purification system (Synergy® UV, Millipore, Merck, China) was used to produce ultrapure water with resistivity above 1.82 × 105 Ω·m at 25 °C.

Synthesis of catalysts: The Pt/CdS NRs were synthesized by the hot-injection method using tetradecylphosphonic acid (TDPA) and trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO). CdO (0.23 g), TOPO (7.0 g), and TDPA (1.2 g) were degassed at 80 °C for 1 h, while sulfur (0.18 g) in TOP (18 mL) was degassed separately and then heated to 120 °C under argon47. The Cd precursor was heated to 320 °C, equilibrated at 300 °C, and rapidly injected with the sulfur precursor. After 55 min of growth, the reaction was quenched with toluene at temperatures below 80 °C. The NRs were purified by repeated methanol precipitation and centrifugation.

Subsequently, Pt nanoparticles were grown at the CdS tips using a reported method. Oleic acid, oleylamine, 1,2-hexadecanediol, and diphenyl ether were degassed, heated to 200 °C under argon, and injected as a dichlorobenzene dispersion of CdS NRs containing Pt(acac)2. After ~4 min of reaction, the mixture was cooled, washed with methanol, and centrifuged to yield Pt/CdS NRs.

Bare and Pt-decorated CdS NRs were transferred to water by exchanging the native ligands with MPA or L-cysteine in methanol at pH >10.5, adjusted with KOH. After vigorous stirring, the particles were isolated by acetone/methanol precipitation, washed with toluene, dried under N2, and redispersed in deionized water. The final dispersions were filtered, concentrated, and stored in the dark, remaining stable for months. The actual Pt loading of the catalyst, measured via ICP-OES, is 16.5 wt%.

To clarify the functions of random Pt NPs and Pt NPs attached to CdS, we also synthesized random Pt NPs from platinum acetylacetonate using identical hot-injection methods, then mixed them with pristine CdS NRs (referred to as Pt NPs + CdS NRs).

Sample preparation

Accurate masses of the selected surfactants were weighed and dissolved at 25 °C to prepare the stock solution. Test samples were then prepared by combining the anionic and cationic surfactant stock solutions at the specified mixing ratios and target concentrations. All prepared sample vials were tightly sealed and vortexed vigorously. Thereafter, the samples were placed in a thermostatic incubator and maintained at 25 °C for 24 h to achieve full thermodynamic equilibrium before subsequent characterization and experimental measurements48.

Characterizations of the catalysts

TEM images were obtained by using Tecnai T20. The accelerating voltage is 200 kV. XRD studies were performed using a model XKS-2000 X-ray diffractometer (Scintag Inc.). The X-ray wavelength was obtained from a Cu Kα1 beam (λ = 1.5406 Å) generated with a Cu anode. XPS tests were performed on an AXIS Supra X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Kratos Analytical Ltd.) using a monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectra were obtained with a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrometer at 25 °C. About 50 mg of catalysts were loaded, and UV-Vis spectra were recorded within the wavelength range of 250–800 nm.

Characterizations of aqueous surfactant systems

TEM images were obtained by using Tecnai T20. The samples were operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV and kept in air overnight before observation. Turbidity measurements were performed using a UV-Vis Shimadzu spectrophotometer. Since all systems exhibit no characteristic absorption within the 250-800 nm wavelength range, we chose absorbance at 400 nm as a measure of turbidity. 1 cm quartz cuvettes were used. All spectral measurements were recorded at 25 °C. DLS data were obtained by using the BI-200 SM instrument. The samples were filtered through 800-nm filters before observation. All measurements were performed at 25 °C. The rheological properties of all the samples were evaluated with a Thermo Haake RS300 rheometer at 25 °C. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were measured at a heating rate of 1.5 °C/min from −80 °C to 10 °C using a Q2000 instrument. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained by using a Nicolet Magna-IR 750 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Co.). The method used for SMART iTR was attenuated total reflectance (ATR) with a scanning speed of 1 cm−1. About 0.2 mL of the liquid samples was dropped onto a diamond substrate. LF-NMR spectra were obtained using a VTMR20-010V-I instrument (Niumag, China). The magnetic field strength is 21 MHz, and the average transverse (spin-spin) relaxation time T2 of water protons in the sample was measured using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence. The light scattering intensity was measured before the DLS test.

Photocatalytic reactions

The photocatalytic hydrogen evolution tests were conducted in a 170 mL optical reaction container with constant stirring at 25 °C. A 0.50 mg aqueous dispersion of the photocatalyst was introduced into a mixed solution containing 9.9 mL of surfactant solution and 0.1 mL of methanol, which served as the sacrificial reagent. The pH of the system was adjusted to 12.8 through the controlled addition of NaOH. Then, the reactor was purged with argon (Ar) for 10 min to remove air. The reaction mixture was irradiated under an Xe lamp to simulate sunlight; each irradiation lasted 4 h, and hydrogen production in the system was monitored hourly. For each hydrogen generation evaluation, 500 μL of headspace was injected into the gas chromatograph and analyzed using a calibration plot. Cycling tests of the photocatalytic system were performed using the same procedures, except that the samples were collected and degassed with Ar again. The relevant experimental details, including the quantitative method for hydrogen content, are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS)

Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) was conducted using a femtosecond amplifier laser system (Coherent Evolution, 800 nm, 140 fs, 1 kHz). The output was split into two beams. The 355 nm pump pulse was generated via optical parametric amplification and focused onto the sample. The probe pulse was generated as a white-light continuum using a CaF₂ crystal, directed through a mechanical delay stage, and spatially overlapped with the pump pulse at the sample position. After the sample was excited by the pump pulse, the probe light passing through the sample was collected by a 500 mm focal length monochromator/spectrograph (Acton SP 2500, Princeton Instruments, USA) and recorded by a charge-coupled device camera.

For measurement, the Pt/CdS aqueous dispersion was first dispersed in pure H2O and SHP (cT = 300 mM, XDTAC = 33%, 25 °C) at specific concentrations, respectively, by sonication, followed by standing for 12 h. The upper suspension of finely dispersed particles was then transferred to a cuvette, purged with argon, and measured under a pump power of 3 μW.

Photocurrent measurements

The photocurrent measurements were performed in a standard three-electrode system on an electrochemical workstation. The working electrode was prepared by coating 100 μL of a calculated catalyst dispersion onto the surface of indium tin oxide (ITO) glass (1 cm²) at 25 °C. The Pt plate and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. Specific surfactant solutions and a 300 mM Na2SO4 solution were used as the electrolyte. During the measurement, a 300 W lamp with λ = 365 nm was used as the light source.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)

EPR measurements were performed on a JEOL JES-FA200 spectrometer. Samples were prepared by adding a radical trapping agent (5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide, DMPO) to the surfactant aqueous solution containing the same concentration of the photocatalyst, then loaded into a quartz capillary (sealed at the top with laboratory film) with a filling length of about 1 cm. For photocatalytic tests, the sample was irradiated in situ before measurement using a 355 nm light source from a 300 W Xe lamp to detect the type and concentration of radicals generated during the reaction.

Computational procedure

The computational model comprises two parts: a catalyst-water system and a catalyst-water-surfactant coacervate system. For the latter, one DTAC molecule and two SOS molecules are placed at the water surface to construct the model, consistent with the actual system’s mixing ratio. The initial model features a catalyst surface with dimensions of 7.221×8.338 Å, on which 24 water molecules are placed. A vacuum layer of approximately 30 Å is added above, resulting in total model dimensions of 7.221×8.338×50 Å. For the pure H2O system, the only difference is the absence of surfactants; all other conditions remain the same. Details for the modeling are presented in Supplementary Data 1.

For the calculation, classical molecular dynamics simulations with the COMPASS force field were used to pre-equilibrate the coacervate and pure H2O systems. First-principles calculations were conducted using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional with the DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH basis set50, incorporating DFT-D3(BJ) dispersion correction51. The aqueous structure was subsequently sampled via a 4 ps ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation, and the O–H bond properties were analyzed from the last 2 ps of the production run. Subsequently, a transition-state (TS) search was conducted to determine the reaction barrier. The energy barrier is defined as the difference between the TS energy (the highest point along the reaction path) and the energy of the initial reactant structure, with “Split” representing the final product.

Given the simplified representation of the catalyst–water–coacervate interface, these calculations are intended to provide qualitative molecular-level support for the experimentally observed trends, rather than a fully quantitative description of the exact catalytic system under operating conditions.
