Open reduction and internal fixation versus distal femoral replacement for periprosthetic distal femur fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tyler Brady, Sam Shapiro, Kurt Barger, Niloofar Dehghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

1. Introduction

Periprosthetic distal femur fractures (PDFF) after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are a growing clinical challenge due to the increasing prevalence of TKA procedures.1 These fractures occur at an estimated rate of 0.3% to 2.5% and present notable treatment considerations due to anatomical and biomechanical challenges.2–5

Historically, periprosthetic distal femur fractures have been treated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), as this approach preserves native bone. ORIF techniques include intramedullary nailing (IMN), plating, or a combination of the two.6 However, ORIF has a risk of failure in the setting of severely comminuted fractures, poor bone stock, osteopenia, and component loosening.7 Total joint arthroplasty with distal femoral replacement (DFR) is another treatment option for these injuries, which has increased in use over time.8–10 Both techniques have demonstrated utility in specific patient populations, with ORIF offering the benefits of bone preservation and potentially lower invasiveness, whereas DFR most notably allows for immediate weight bearing postoperatively.11–14

Although several reviews and meta-analyses have examined the comparative outcomes of ORIF vs DFR, including surgical complications, reoperations, and functional measures, there remains a critical gap in the literature.6,15–17 Specifically, no review has focused primarily on patient-reported outcomes (PROs), despite the growing recognition that a patient's subjective experience is intimately related to general recovery outcomes.18–22 Given the influence of PROs on treatment satisfaction and long-term quality of life, understanding this facet alongside complication and functional data is crucial for guiding treatment decisions.

Prior reviews on this topic have weaknesses, such as the inclusion of case series that lacked a control group, or the inclusion of revision long-stemmed TKA procedures as DFR, instead of only including true DFR megaprosthesis, which creates the potential for inaccurate results and misleading conclusions.6,15–17 Furthermore, as the use of DFR for treating PDFF has increased in recent years, a significant body of new literature has emerged since the most recently published reviews on this topic.11,23–27 This influx of data presents an opportunity for an updated review.

The primary objective of this study was to systematically summarize the available PRO data from studies comparing ORIF vs DFR for the treatment of periprosthetic distal femur fractures. Given the surge of novel data, secondary objectives include comparative analysis of perioperative measures, functional outcomes, and complications between the two treatment modalities.

2. Methods

PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and ScienceDirect were queried for peer-reviewed journal articles using keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH). The search strategy took the following general form: (distal femur fracture) AND (periprosthetic) AND (open reduction internal fixation) AND (distal femur replacement). See Appendix 1, http://links.lww.com/OTAI/A130 for the search strategies used for each database.

Once identified, all manuscripts were uploaded to Rayyan.ai, a platform for screening and managing references, and duplicates were removed. Study titles and abstracts were screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by full-text review. Manuscript screening was conducted independently and blinded by two of the authors. Any conflicts were resolved by an additional designated author acting as a third party. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement was used and is presented in Figure 1. Institutional Review Board approval was not required, as this study involved only the analysis of previously published data.

PRISMA diagram—a flowchart illustrating the systematic review process, including study identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final inclusion.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of periprosthetic distal femur fractures, (2) studies that reported on the treatment of ORIF and DFR, (3) studies published in the English language, (4) studies with at least 10 patients in total, (5) studies published since 2010, and (6) published abstracts and presentations with available data (authors were contacted directly if additional information was required). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) systematic reviews and meta-analyses, (2) single-arm case series without a comparative group, and (3) studies reporting on outcomes of DFR for primary joint replacement or nonperiprosthetic fractures.

Two reviewers conducted the initial data extraction, and any conflicts were resolved by a third author. The data extraction was completed by combing through the full-text studies using a standardized data form. Study design, Newcastle–Ottawa score, patient demographics, interventions, and treatment outcomes were recorded for each study. PROs were defined as Knee Society Score (KSS), Knee Society Functional Score (KSFS), Oxford Knee Score (OKS), and patient reports of “pain.” Perioperative measures included length of hospital stay (LOS), estimated intraoperative blood loss (EBL), and operative time. Functional outcomes included range of motion (ROM), days till weight bearing, ambulatory status, and return to preoperative mobility. Plate and nail cohorts were treated collectively as the ORIF group in the analysis.

Outcome incidence rates (IR) were calculated for each study as the number of events divided by the total sample size for the respective group. The pooled incidence rates were estimated using a meta-analytical approach. A random-effects model was applied to account for potential between-study variability, and inverse variance weighting was used to combine the study-specific incidence rates. The pooled incidence rates were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2-statistic and Cochran Q-test. The Z-test for two proportions was used to compare pooled incidence rates.

To statistically compare the two groups, a risk ratio analysis was performed using a meta-analytical approach. Study-specific RRs and their standard errors were combined using the Mantel–Haenszel method, which calculates a weighted average of risk ratios across studies. Both the common-effect model and the random-effects model were applied for each outcome, and heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2-statistic and Cochran Q-test. Between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method. The statistical significance of the pooled risk ratios was assessed using a Z-test, testing the null hypothesis that risk ratios (RR) = 1. The corresponding 95% CIs were computed using the log-transformed risk ratios and their standard errors. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using I2 statistics and Cochran Q-test.

Patient-reported outcome scores were converted to OKS using the validated regression by Maempel et al.28 A random-effects model with the DerSimonian–Laird method for variance estimation and Hartung–Knapp adjustment was applied to compare OKS outcomes between ORIF and DFR. Chi-square analysis was performed on categorical measures such as gender, and an independent two-sample t test was conducted to compare weighted mean age, ROM, and time to weight bearing. Fisher exact test was used to analyze categorical weight-bearing restriction data.

All analyses were conducted using the meta package in R software.

3. Results

Upon final query, 2416 abstracts were identified, and 1212 duplicates were removed. Of the remaining 1204 manuscripts, 1191 were excluded, yielding 13 manuscripts that met inclusion and exclusion criteria. Quality analysis is visualized in Figure 2.

Newcastle–Ottawa Scale scores for included studies—quality assessment of included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, evaluating selection, comparability, and outcome criteria.

All studies included were retrospective cohort designs. Across all included studies, seven different types of plating hardware and five types of megaprostheses were used. DFR implants included the Stryker Global Modular Replacement System, Lidakang Prosthesis, Wright Medical Guardian Limb Salvage System, Zimmer Segmental System, and Zimmer Modular Options for Severe Bone Loss and Trauma System. The most commonly cited approach for ORIF was direct lateral, whereas for DFR, the medial parapatellar approach was used most frequently. None of the included studies combined plate and nail fixation; therefore, all ORIF patients received either a plate or IMN, but not both. All IMN were placed retrograde.12,26,29 The most common cause of fracture was low-energy trauma.

3.1. Patient characteristics

In total, 881 patients were identified across the 13 included studies. Mean age was 78.3 years and 74% were female. Five hundred fifty-four (63%) patients underwent ORIF, and 327 (37%) patients had a DFR procedure. Gender distribution within each group was significantly different, with a higher proportion of females compared with males in both the ORIF group (79% female, P < 0.001) and the DFR group (71% female, P < 0.001). In addition, a comparison between the two groups revealed a statistically significant difference in gender distribution, with ORIF having a slightly higher female-to-male ratio than DFR (χ2 = 5.06, df = 1, P = 0.025). There was no significant difference between groups in patient age (ORIF: 77.8 ± 9.8, DFR: 79.2 ± 8.2, P = 0.15). See Table 1 for additional details.

3.3. Perioperative measures

A meta-analysis of five studies (388 patients: 160 DFR, 228 ORIF) was conducted to compare LOS between patients treated with DFR vs ORIF.13,14,23,31,32 The pooled mean difference using a random-effects model was 0.53 days (95% CI: [−1.53 to 2.59], P = 0.51), indicating no statistically significant difference. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 54.4%, τ2 = 1.61), suggesting variability in effect sizes across studies.

Due to incomplete reporting and the absence of variance data for EBL and operative time, a meta-analysis and valid statistical comparison of these continuous variables could not be performed. Four studies reported EBL, with weighted means of 601.3 mL in the ORIF group and 411.7 mL in the DFR group.11,12,14,29 Five studies reported operative time, with weighted means of 122.4 minutes for ORIF and 130.1 minutes for DFR.12,13,24,29,30

3.4. Functional outcomes

A meta-analysis of four studies (263 patients: 103 DFR, 160 ORIF) was conducted to compare the likelihood of returning to preoperative mobility between patients treated with DFR vs ORIF.13,14,31,32 The pooled RR using a random-effects model was 0.88 (95% CI: [0.66–1.16], P = 0.35), indicating no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 48.5%, τ2 = 0.041), suggesting some variability in effect sizes across studies.

A meta-analysis of five studies (337 patients: 148 DFR, 229 ORIF) was conducted to compare the likelihood of achieving nonassisted ambulation postoperatively.13,23,26,30,31 The pooled RR in the DFR group compared with the ORIF group was 0.62 (95% CI: [0.26–1.45], P = 0.27) using a random-effects model. Although the common-effect model suggested a statistically significant difference (RR = 0.60, 95% CI: [0.38–0.92], P = 0.02), substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 66.0%, P = 0.02).

Range of motion data was sparsely and inconsistently reported. From the two studies that provided numeric values, the combined average ROM in the DFR group was 87°, whereas the ORIF average was 76°.11,12 These were not significantly different (P = 0.21). Studies that reported ROM outcomes narratively noted reduced knee mobility in two DFR patients; there was no mention of reduced mobility in any ORIF patient.25,31

Time to weight bearing was reported inconsistently across studies, with some providing exact timeframes and others describing weight-bearing restrictions. Among studies reporting numerical values, the weighted mean time to weight bearing was significantly shorter in the DFR group (2 days) compared with the ORIF group (78 days, P = 0.04).11–13 Among the two studies describing weight-bearing restrictions, ORIF patients were significantly more likely to have postoperative restrictions, including non–weight bearing and partial weight bearing (23 patients, 51%) compared with the DFR group (0 patients, 0%, P < 0.001).14,24

3.5. Complications

The pooled risk ratio for infection in the DFR group compared with the ORIF group was 1.10 (95% CI: [0.60–2.01], P = 0.77). There was no significant difference between the two groups, and heterogeneity among studies was low (I2 = 0%, P = 0.79). Results are visualized in Figure 3.

Infection risk ratio forest plot.

The pooled risk ratio for reoperations in the DFR group compared with the ORIF group was 0.92 (95% CI: [0.63–1.35], P = 0.67). There was no significant difference in reoperation risk between the two groups, and heterogeneity among studies was low (I2 = 0%, P = 0.50). Results are visualized in Figure 4.

Reoperation risk ratio forest plot.

The pooled risk ratio for 1-year mortality in the DFR group compared with the ORIF group was 0.93 (95% CI: [0.55–1.59], P = 0.80). There was no statistically significant difference in mortality risk between the two groups. Heterogeneity among studies was low (I2 = 9%, P = 0.36). Results are visualized in Figure 5.

4. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive comparison of ORIF and DFR for the management of periprosthetic distal femur fracture after total knee arthroplasty, specifically reporting on patient-reported outcomes that have been omitted in prior literature. Overall, the analysis demonstrated similar functional outcomes and complication rates between the two treatment modalities.

The uniqueness of this review is its analysis of PROs. OKS is a purely patient-reported metric, whereas KSS includes a physician-assessed component (Knee Score) in addition to a patient-reported portion (Function Score/KSFS).33,34 The Knee Score covers aspects including ROM, alignment, and stability, whereas the Function Score addresses topics such as walking distance, stair-climbing ability, and the requirement of a walking aid.33 Therefore, KSFS is a subscore of KSS that highlights the patients' self-reported functional ability, providing possible insight into functional outcomes that may be missed with physician-assessed data alone.

It is interesting to note that both studies reporting a KSS and the KSFS subscore found significantly higher KSFS scores in patients treated with ORIF compared with DFR, although KSS scores were comparable.24,29 Although the available data is notably sparse, a plausible explanation for this difference could be that ORIF is used for less comminuted/reconstructable fractures, allows for better preservation of native bone, and potentially preserves more of the soft tissues. In contrast, DFR requires more invasive surgery, likely due to the severity of injury, more comminution, and subsequent soft tissue insult, potentially affecting recovery and functional capacity in the long term. More globally, however, this seems to suggest the possibility that the lived patient experience may differ from clinician-assessed metrics. Bedside measures of the mechanical restoration of the joint alone may not completely capture the functional consequences of treatment. At the very least, it highlights the ambiguity of interpreting results due to limited data, emphasizes the need for collecting PRO metrics moving forward, and underscores the utility these outcomes have in optimizing patient care.

A well-cited advantage of DFR is a patient's ability to immediately weight bear postoperatively.35–38 Studies included in this review, however, seem to suggest that this may not strongly imply longer-term functional advantages.14,31,32 Although the DFR group bore weight significantly earlier than the ORIF group, patients treated with DFR were significantly less likely to achieve unassisted ambulation postoperatively compared with those treated with ORIF, although this finding was only significant with analysis using the common-effect model, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 66.0%, P = 0.02). One study in particular made note that although full weight bearing was permitted immediately after surgery, almost none of the DFR patients were able to do so through discharge.31 This, however, does not discount the benefits of early mobilization in reducing other comorbidities associated with the postoperative course. When comparing the likelihood of returning to preoperative mobility between treatment modalities, a metric with possibly fewer confounders, no significant difference was found.

The reoperation rate was significantly larger, nearly double, in the ORIF group compared with the DFR group. This could be attributed to the salvage nature of DFR. Few additional surgical options are available following this procedure, especially given the old age and frailty of the patient population being studied. It is also plausible that surgeons may trial fixation to conserve native anatomy and fall back on salvage procedures if the postoperative course is unsuccessful, naturally leading to a significantly higher rate of reoperation in the ORIF group. Given the frail elderly population that most often presents with PDFF and their diminished ability to tolerate multiple operations, it is of critical importance to elucidate risk factors associated with conversion to DFR. Further research should be conducted exploring primary DFR vs secondary DFR associations with patient outcomes.

Patient selection should also be considered when interpreting these results. Patients treated with DFR had significantly worse Charlson Comorbidity Index and ASA scores in 2 studies.24,29 Comminuted fractures were significantly more likely to be treated with ORIF in a single study, and fractures with prosthetic loosening were treated with DFR significantly more.13,14,25,30 In addition, some studies reported DFR patients being significantly older than ORIF patients, although our pooled analysis found no statistical significance between groups.29,31 A lack of randomization and selection biases due to fracture location and hardware integrity should be realized when interpreting differences in treatment outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Given the nature of a systematic review and meta-analysis, the quality of results is intimately dependent on the quality of available published data. We chose to include only two-armed studies to increase evidence quality but found substantial heterogeneity in the way results were reported, and incomplete reporting across the dataset limited grouped and subanalysis. In addition, all included studies were retrospective. It was also not possible to control for variables that were inconsistently reported, such as implant type or preoperative comorbidities. As for functional outcomes, assessing preoperative to postoperative differences in function can be a challenge given how the surgeon responsible for treating the periprosthetic distal femur fractures is rarely the original arthroplasty surgeon who performed the TKA. Therefore, accurately elucidating the degree of functional change postoperatively is difficult and once again points to the importance of PROs in this domain.

Due to the lack of stratified data for patients treated with nails, this analysis combined intramedullary nailing and locking plate fixation into a single ORIF group, despite prior studies showing differences in outcomes between these methods.38–41 In addition, none of the included studies reported on patients treated with nail–plate combination fixation, which has been associated with improved outcomes compared with single-implant strategies.42 More recent literature suggests that nail–plate constructs and dual plating techniques may reduce fixation failure and facilitate earlier weight bearing.43–45 However, these techniques have only recently emerged in clinical practice, and studies comparing these outcomes to DFR have yet to be produced.

Moreover, it is not unreasonable to consider that both operations may be appropriate in specific clinical scenarios, with each offering advantages for particular fracture morphologies; however, such subgroup analysis was not feasible given the limitations of the available data. Last, nonunion is a complication unique to ORIF and thus could not be compared with DFR, despite its important implication in decision making.

In conclusion, both ORIF and DFR appear to be viable options for managing periprosthetic distal femur fractures after total knee arthroplasty. Although ORIF may be associated with improved patient-reported functional scores in some studies, these findings must be interpreted cautiously due to potential selection bias and the heterogeneity of included cohorts. Although DFR enables immediate postoperative weight bearing, current results do not support a clear long-term mobility benefit over fixation. The observed higher reoperation rate in ORIF-treated patients may reflect both case selection and implant-related factors. Given the retrospective nature and variability in the reporting of current evidence, which preclude detailed subanalysis, further prospective research is warranted to clarify which patient–fracture characteristics are most appropriately managed with ORIF versus DFR. Patient-reported outcomes should continue to be collected and reported as their utility was demonstrated in this study.
