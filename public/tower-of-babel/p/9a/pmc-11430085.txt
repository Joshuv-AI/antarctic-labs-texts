The association between dietary quality, sleep duration, and depression symptoms in the general population: findings from cross-sectional NHANES study
Authors: Yue Du, Min Wang, Yu Wang, Yikai Dou, Yushun Yan, Huanhuan Fan, Ningdan Fan, Xiao Yang, Xiaohong Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnhealthy lifestyles, which include unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration, have been widely recognized as modifiable risk factors for depressive symptoms. This study aims to explore the associations between dietary quality, sleep duration, and depression symptoms, as well as their combined effects.MethodsThe NHANES 2007 to 2014 cycles provided the data for this study, in which 19,134 adults aged 20 years and older were included. Unhealthy diet, measured using an average Healthy Eating Index (HEI)-2015 score below the 60th percentile, and unhealthy sleep duration, measured as sleep duration at night less than 7 h or greater than/equal to 9 h, were the primary exposures. Then participants were divided into four different lifestyles. A weighted-multivariable logistic regression was employed, controlling for relevant variables. Furthermore, stratified analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings and identify potential high-risk groups.ResultsThe overall prevalence of depressive symptoms among all participants was 8.44%. Among the participants, 56.58% met the criteria for healthy sleep duration, and 24.83% scored at or above the 60th percentile on the HEI-2015. Unhealthy diet (OR: 1.40, 95% CI: 1.18–1.67, p < 0.001) and unhealthy sleep duration (OR: 1.94, 95% CI: 1.63–2.31, p < 0.001) exhibited positive associations with depression symptoms. Individuals who maintained an unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration (OR: 1.60, 95% CI: 1.20–2.13, p = 0.002), healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.64–3.80, p < 0.001), or an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration (OR: 2.91, 95% CI: 2.16–3.92, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with depressive symptoms compared to those with a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration, respectively. In stratified analyses, females, middle-aged individuals, those with a college degree or higher education, and individuals who do not meet the recommended level of physical activity exhibit heightened susceptibility to depressive symptoms when exposed to unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration.ConclusionIn summary, our study suggests that individuals affected by the individual and synergistic effect of an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration are more susceptible to experiencing depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20093-9.

Introduction

Depression is characterized by prolonged feelings of sadness, reduced interest, guilt, difficulty concentrating, and impaired social functioning [1]. Previous survey results show that the prevalence of depression in the United States increased from 6.6% in 2005 to 7.3% in 2015. Early reports from 2020 and later indicate that the COVID-19 pandemic worsened the mental health crisis in the U.S., with the prevalence of depression reaching 9.2% in 2020 [2]. Depression has a high recurrence rate and causes significant disability, imposing a heavy burden on society, families, and individuals [3]. Therefore, identifying and understanding the risk factors associated with depression is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies and interventions. By targeting these risk factors, we can better prevent the onset of depression and alleviate depressive symptoms in those affected. Numerous studies have identified various risk factors, including demographics [4], physical condition [5] and lifestyles [6]. Recently, researchers have been exploring the links between different lifestyles and depression, including dietary patterns [7], physical activity [8], sleep duration [9] and more, which can influence the risk of depression in the general population. Importantly, several studies suggest that diet quality [10, 11] and sleep duration [9, 12] may be independent predictors of depression risk.

Previous studies have consistently shown that an unhealthy diet is associated with an increased risk of depression [7, 10, 13]. Individuals with a low-quality diet often tend to consume excessive amounts of added sugar, fat, and refined grains, leading to elevated inflammation levels in the population [14, 15]. A high-fat diet can disrupt gut pH, stimulate excessive bacterial growth, activate immune cells, generate excessive reactive oxygen species, and trigger oxidative stress in the body, ultimately affecting depressive symptoms through the brain-gut axis [16]. Similarly, a diet high in added sugar and refined grains can increase the abundance of mucolytic bacteria in the intestines, promoting the production of endotoxins, inducing TH17 cell activation, and contributing to intestinal inflammation. Consequently, this compromises the integrity and function of the intestinal barrier, allowing pro-inflammatory factors to cross the blood-brain barrier and damage the central nervous system, thus triggering depressive emotions [17, 18]. While much of the existing research has focused on the link between specific nutrients or dietary components and depressive symptoms, there is substantial evidence supporting the association between overall diet quality and the risk of depression. A meta-analysis revealed a consistent link between inflammatory diets and depressive outcomes [19]. This finding supports the hypothesis that avoiding pro-inflammatory foods and opting for anti-inflammatory diets may help prevent depression and its symptoms. The Healthy Eating Index (HEI) measures dietary quality and adherence to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA), representing an individual’s overall dietary patterns [20]. Several cohort studies, including US [21], United Kingdom [22], France [23] and so on, have shown that individuals with higher HEI scores have a lower risk of developing depressive symptoms. Actually, understanding the role of diet in the etiology of chronic diseases, including depression, remains challenging due to the complexity of dietary exposures. Large-scale studies are necessary to validate the relationship and explore potential variations among different demographic groups within the US population. Previous studies have also investigated the influence of other lifestyle factors on the relationship between diet and depression. For instance, Liang et al. suggested that engaging in adequate physical activity (PA) could help mitigate the negative effects of an unhealthy diet on depression [24]. In addition, there is a connection between socioeconomic status (SES), dietary quality, and depressive symptoms, which can be further influenced by factors such as sex, race, and central obesity [25]. However, further investigation is needed to understand the combined effects of other lifestyle factors such as sleep duration and dietary quality on depressive symptoms.

In 2005, the National Sleep Foundation recommended the appropriate sleep duration for different age groups. Young adults (18–25 years old) and adults (26–64 years) were advised to aim for 7 to 9 h of sleep, while older adults (> 65 years old) should aim for 7 to 8 h. Surprisingly, only 48% of adults in the US reported consistently sleeping for the recommended 7–9 h per night, while 26% averaged 6–7 h of sleep per night, and 20% slept less than 6 h per night [26]. Intentionally restricting sleep over a prolonged period can have negative impacts on an individual’s health and mood [27]. Previous studies have also indicated a U-shaped relationship between depression and sleep duration, with 7–9 h of sleep at night being considered optimal for reducing the risk of depression [9, 28]. Short sleep duration (< 7 h at night) is associated with increased daytime tiredness and psychological fatigue, which are predictive of higher rates and worse outcomes in depression [29]. Prolonged sleep deficiency is associated with chronic, systemic low-grade inflammation [30], activating inflammatory pathways such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), activator protein 1 (AP-1), and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) family proteins. When short sleep duration combines with low-grade inflammation, the risk of depression may increase [31]. Meanwhile, long sleep duration (≥ 9 h at night) often leads to increased sleep fragmentation [32], which, in turn, is associated with various negative health outcomes, including reduced energy and vitality, core symptoms of depression [33]. It is important to note that other factors may influence the relationship between sleep duration and depression. Previous studies have suggested that engaging in adequate PA can mitigate the negative effects of unhealthy sleep duration on depression [34–36]. Data from the NHANES database also indicates an interactive effect of SES and sleep problems on depression [37]. The impact of diet quality and sleep duration in relation to depressive symptoms remain understudied. Large-scale studies examining their interaction and conducting in-depth discussions regarding different demographic groups are currently lacking.

This study aims to evaluate how diet quality and sleep duration, both separately and combined, influence the occurrence of depression among the US population using NHANES data. Additionally, we will examine how the combination of diet quality and sleep duration impacts the risk of depressive symptoms across different demographic groups.

Methods

Survey description

This cross-sectional study utilizes data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between July and August 2023, based on the 2007–2014 NHANES samples, to investigate general health-related behaviors and nutritional status (see: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about_nhanes.htm; https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx; https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/2021-2023/documents/2022-Overview-Brochure.pdf; https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_01/sr01-066.pdf). NHANES employed a multistage stratified probability sampling method to ensure comprehensive representation of the noninstitutionalized US population. Weighted analysis results were used to represent the US adult aged 20 years and older, following NHANES Analytic Guidelines (see: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/Weighting.aspx). The study received approval from the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and participants provided written informed consent. This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines (Table S1).

Participants

In this study, cross-sectional data of participants from four consecutive cycles of the NHANES (2007–2014) were initially included. The exclusion criteria were set as follows: (1) participants aged < 20; (2) participants without relevant information on dietary intake, sleep duration and other covariates.

Measures

Depression symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). This questionnaire assesses the severity of depression on a scale ranging from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater levels of depression [38]. A cutoff score of 10 or above on the PHQ-9 is commonly used to identify clinically significant depressive symptoms, demonstrating a sensitivity and specificity of 88% in screening for severe depression [39].

Assessment for healthy diet

To estimate dietary intake, data was collected regarding the types and quantities of foods and beverages consumed within a 24-hour period before each dietary recall interview (from midnight to midnight). This information was used to calculate energy, nutrient, and other food component values. Participants completed two 24-hour dietary recall interviews: the first interview was conducted in person in the Mobile Examination Center, and the second interview was conducted 3 to 10 days after the initial one via telephone. The Healthy Eating Index (HEI)-2015 was used to provide a comprehensive evaluation of individual dietary patterns. The HEI-2015 comprises 13 components: nine adequacy components (total fruits, whole fruits, total vegetables, greens and beans, whole grains, dairy, total protein foods, seafood and plant proteins, and fatty acids) and four moderation components (refined grains, sodium, added sugars, and saturated fats) [40]. We used SAS 9.4 to calculated the HEI scores (see: https://epi.grants.cancer.gov/hei/sas-code.html). The HEI-2015 total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better overall dietary quality. Individuals with an average HEI score at or above the 60th percentile over a two-day period were considered as adhering to dietary guidelines or maintaining a healthy diet [41]. The HEI-2015 has demonstrated good construct validity, internal consistency, and reliability in various population groups, the HEI-2015’s ability to reflect dietary quality and predict health outcomes has been supported by numerous studies, indicating its robustness as a measure of dietary adherence [42–44].

Assessment for healthy sleep duration

Participants reported their sleep duration at night on workdays or weekdays in hours (participants were required to answer this question: How much sleep do you usually get at night on weekdays or workdays?). Healthy sleep duration for adults aged 20 years and older was defined as a duration greater than or equal to 7 h and less than 9 h, inclusive [9, 27, 45].

Definitions for lifestyle behavior groups

Based on diet quality and sleep duration, participants were categorized into four lifestyle behavior groups: (1) Healthy diet and healthy sleep duration, (2) Unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration, (3) Healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration, and (4) Unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration. The criteria for a healthy diet were met if individuals fell within the 60th percentile range of HEI [40] and the criteria for a healthy sleep duration were met if individuals have greater than or equal to 7 h and less than 9 h [27]. Relevant studies utilizing similar classifications have been cited to support this categorization [9, 24].

Covariates

The selection of covariates is based on previous literature and substantive reasoning [46]. Covariates associated with depressive symptoms included sex, age groups (20–39 years old, 40–50 years old, 60–79 years old, 80 + years old), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other/Multiracial), education level (less than high school, high school, some college, college degree or higher), body mass index (BMI) categories (Underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), Normal (18.5 kg/m2 to < 25 kg/m2), Overweight (25 kg/m2 to < 30 kg/m2), Obese (30 kg/m2 or greater)), alcohol consumption habits (Non-drinker, 1–5 drinks/month, 5–10 drinks/month, 10 + drinks/month), smoking status (Never smoker, Former smoker, Current smoker), and PA levels. According to the recommendations in the American Physical Activity Guidelines, meeting PA criteria meant engaging in moderate-intensity PA for at least 150 min per week or vigorous-intensity PA for at least 75 min per week; otherwise, it is categorized as not meeting PA [47]. The World Health Organization’s analysis guidelines involve converting PA into metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week, specifically for moderate to vigorous PA [48]. NHANES provides recommended MET values for various activities, including intense work-related activity (MET = 8.0), vigorous leisure-time physical activity (MET = 8.0), moderate work-related activity (MET = 4.0), walking or cycling for transportation (MET = 4.0), and moderate leisure-time physical activity (MET = 4.0). To calculate PA in MET-min/week, it is necessary to multiply the MET value by the weekly frequency and duration of each PA session using the following formula: PA (MET-min/week) = MET × Weekly Frequency × Duration of Each PA Session [49].

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression models with sample weighting were employed to assess the associations between depressive symptoms and different lifestyle groups. Estimates were calculated using odds ratio (OR) and accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three models were utilized: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, and race or ethnicity; and Model 3: further adjusted for age, sex, race or ethnicity, education level, BMI, alcohol consumption habits, smoking status, and physical activity (PA).

In addition, the interaction effects between diet quality and sleep duration were evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and synergy index (SI). An RERI and AP value greater than 0 within the 95% CI indicates an additive interaction between the two exposures. An SI value greater than 1 suggests additive interaction if its 95% CI is also greater than 1, indicating enhanced synergistic effects when both factors are simultaneously present. Conversely, a value less than 1 implies weakened synergistic effects [50].

Furthermore, stratified analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings and identify potential high-risk groups. These stratified analyses were performed based on age, sex, race or ethnicity, education level, BMI, alcohol consumption habits, smoking status, and PA. The characteristics of participants in each group were analyzed using ANOVA and chi-square tests for mean (SD) and proportional results (%), respectively. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value below 0.05. Given the large sample size and potential for false positives, results were considered to be statistically significant when the overall Wald χ2 was less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the “survey” package in R version 4.2.0.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The study analyzed a total of 40,617 participants. Following the participant selection process outlined in Fig. 1, a total of 19,134 individuals were included. We excluded participants below 20 (n = 17135) years of age. After excluding participants with missing data on dietary quality (n = 2452), sleep duration (n = 2422), and covariates (n = 1748), a total of 19,134 individuals were included. Participant characteristics are provided in Fig. 2 and Table S2. Among the respondents, females accounted for 50.62% of the sample, with an average age of 47.15 years (mean [SD: 17.66]). Notably, 24.83% participants met the criteria for a healthy diet, and 56.58% reported healthy sleep duration. Additionally, 8.44% participants exhibited least clinically significant depressive symptoms, as shown in Fig. 3 and Table S3.

Diet quality and depression symptoms

In the unadjusted model, participants with an unhealthy diet had over twice the odds of experiencing clinically significant depressive symptoms compared to those with a healthy diet (OR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.72–2.37, P < 0.001). This association remains robust after adjusting for sex, age, and race (OR: 2.09, 95% CI: 1.77–2.47, P < 0.001). Furthermore, even after adjusting for additional factors such as education, BMI, alcohol consumption habits, and smoking status, the association remains statistically significant, with participants having 40% higher odds of experiencing depressive symptoms (OR: 1.40, 95% CI: 1.18–1.67, P < 0.001).

Sleep duration and depression symptoms

In the unadjusted model, participants with unhealthy sleep duration had more than twice the odds of developing clinically significant depressive symptoms compared to those with healthy sleep duration (OR: 2.23, 95% CI: 1.96–2.71, P < 0.001). This association remains robust after adjusting for sex, age, and race (OR: 2.26, 95% CI: 1.92–2.67, P < 0.001). Furthermore, even when additional factors such as education, BMI, alcohol consumption habits, and smoking status are considered, participants with unhealthy sleep duration still had nearly twice the odds of developing depressive symptoms (OR: 1.94, 95% CI: 1.63–2.31, P < 0.001).

Combined impact of diet quality and sleep duration on depression symptoms

Compared to individuals with a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration, participants with an unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration have more than twice the risk of clinically significant depressive symptoms (OR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.63–2.82, P < 0.001). Similarly, those with a healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration also faces more than twice the risk of such symptoms (OR: 2.70, 95% CI: 1.81–4.01, P < 0.001). Individuals with both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration exhibit more than four times the risk of depressive symptoms (OR: 4.60, 95% CI: 3.46–6.13, P < 0.001). The overall χ2 test was significant, with P < 0.0001.

After adjusting for sex, age, and race, the association between an unhealthy diet combined with healthy sleep duration and clinically significant depressive symptoms remains significant (OR: 2.24, 95%CI: 1.69–2.96, P < 0.001). Likewise, participants with a healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration have more than twice the risk of experiencing depressive symptoms (OR: 2.65, 95%CI: 1.77–3.97, P < 0.001). Individuals who display both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration continue to face more than four times the risk of depressive symptoms (OR: 4.71, 95%CI: 3.53–6.30, P < 0.001). And the overall χ2 P < 0.0001.

Even after adjusting for factors such as sex, age, race, education, BMI, alcohol consumption habits, and smoking status, the association between an unhealthy diet combined with healthy sleep duration and clinically significant depressive symptoms remains significant (OR: 1.60, 95%CI: 1.20–2.13, P = 0.002). Similarly, participants with a healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration have more than twice the risk of experiencing depressive symptoms (OR: 2.50, 95%CI: 1.64–3.80, P < 0.001). Individuals with both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration still exhibit more than twice the risk of depressive symptoms (OR: 2.91, 95%CI: 2.16–3.92, P < 0.001), with an overall χ2 of p < 0.001.

Importantly, the results also indicate an additive interaction between the two exposures: diet quality and sleep duration. This interaction is supported by the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI: 0.66, 95%CI: 0.10–1.23), attributable proportion (AP: 0.15, 95%CI: 0.02–0.27), and synergy index (SI: 1.23, 95%CI: 1.01–1.51).

Stratified analyses

Sex

When compared to females who maintain a healthy diet and sleep duration, those with an unhealthy diet or unhealthy sleep duration face an increased risk of experiencing depressive symptoms. Specifically, the risk is higher for females with an unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration (OR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.11–2.56, p = 0.015), a healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration (OR: 3.02, 95% CI: 1.67–5.49, p < 0.001), or both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration (OR: 3.26, 95% CI: 2.05–5.17, p < 0.001), as indicated by the overall χ2 (p < 0.001). However, among males, only those with both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration exhibit more than twice the risk of depressive symptoms (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.33–3.82, p = 0.003). Detailed characteristics of different sexes are provided in Table S6.

Age group

Similarly, individuals in the middle-aged category (aged 40–59 years) experience a higher risk of depression symptoms when they have either an unhealthy diet or unhealthy sleep duration. In the elder age group (aged 60–79 years), individuals with unhealthy sleep duration concurrently demonstrate a higher risk of depression symptoms compared to those with a healthy diet and sleep duration pattern (healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.27, 95% CI: 1.19–4.33, p = 0.014; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.32, 95% CI: 1.82–6.04, p < 0.001). However, similar results were not observed in young adults (aged 20–39 years) and adults aged 80 years and above. The detailed characteristics of different age groups are provided in Table S7.

Education

In the subgroup with a college degree or higher, both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration lead to a higher risk of depression symptoms compared to maintaining a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration: OR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.23–6.46, p = 0.015; healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.96, 95% CI: 1.26–6.93, p = 0.014; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 5.20, 95% CI: 2.32–11.60, p < 0.001). In the high school and some college subgroups, unhealthy sleep duration is associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms (High school subgroup: healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.28, 95% CI: 1.23–8.72, p = 0.019; unhealthy diet and healthy sleep duration: OR: 2.72, 95% CI: 1.34–5.51, p = 0.007. Some college subgroup: healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.23, 95% CI: 1.21–4.10, p = 0.011; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.37–4.34, p = 0.003).

BMI category

In the subgroup with normal weight, both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration lead to a higher risk of depression symptoms compared to maintaining a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration: OR: 2.57, 95% CI: 1.16–5.69, p = 0.021; healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 6.36, 95% CI: 2.70–15.00, p < 0.001; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 4.81, 95% CI: 2.23–10.40, p < 0.001). In overweight subgroup, those with unhealthy sleep duration exhibit more than twice the risk of depressive symptoms (healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.38, 95% CI: 1.16–4.90, p = 0.019; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.67, 95% CI: 1.68–4.24, p < 0.001), comparing to those with both a healthy diet and sleep duration. In obese subgroup, only those with both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration exhibit more than twice the risk of depressive symptoms (OR: 2.67, 95% CI: 1.68–4.24, p < 0.001).

Alcohol use

In the non-drinker subgroup, both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration lead to a higher risk of depression symptoms compared to maintaining a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration: OR: 2.32, 95% CI: 1.26–4.28 p = 0.008; healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.74, 95% CI: 1.80–7.80, p < 0.001; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 4.15, 95% CI: 2.41–7.13, p < 0.001). In 5–10 drinks/month subgroup, those with unhealthy sleep duration exhibit higher risk of depressive symptoms (healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 13.90, 95% CI: 3.10–62.10, p < 0.001; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.51, 95% CI: 1.07–11.50, p = 0.039), comparing to those with both a healthy diet and sleep duration. In 1–5 drinks/month subgroup and 10 + drinks/month subgroup, only those with both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration exhibit higher risk of depressive symptoms (1–5 drinks/month subgroup: OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.48–3.41 p < 0.001; 10 + drinks/month subgroup: OR: 3.73, 95% CI: 1.37–10.20, p = 0.011).

Smoking status

In the never-smoker subgroup, both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration lead to a higher risk of depression symptoms compared to maintaining a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration: OR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.23–2.96 p = 0.005; healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.37–4.36, p = 0.003; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.61, 95% CI: 2.20–5.90, p < 0.001). In former-smoker subgroup and current-smoker subgroup, those with unhealthy sleep duration exhibit higher risk of depressive symptoms ((former-smoker subgroup: healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.05–5.05, p = 0.039; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.37, 95% CI: 1.27–4.42, p = 0.008); (current-smoker subgroup: healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.07, 95% CI: 1.07–8.83, p = 0.038; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.47, 95% CI: 1.22–4.98, p = 0.013)), comparing to those with both a healthy diet and sleep duration.

Physical activity

In the subgroup with insufficient physical activity (PA), individuals with an unhealthy diet or unhealthy sleep duration have an increased risk of experiencing depression symptoms compared to those who maintain a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration: OR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.10–2.90, p = 0.019; healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.63, 95% CI: 1.56–4.45, p < 0.001; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 3.57, 95% CI: 2.16–5.89, p < 0.001). The detailed characteristics of different education groups could be seen in Table S8. In the subgroup with sufficient physical activity, individuals with unhealthy sleep duration face an increased risk of depression symptoms compared to those who maintain a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration pattern (healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.29, 95% CI: 1.25–4.19, p = 0.009; unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration: OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.37–3.71, p = 0.002). The detailed characteristics of different PA groups are provided in Table S9.

Discussion

Based on a representative sample of the US population, this study included 19,134 participants across four waves spanning from 2007 to 2014. The findings indicate a correlation between unhealthy sleep duration, poorer dietary quality, and the risk of depressive symptoms. Moreover, our analysis revealed significant additive interaction effects between dietary quality and sleep duration in relation to depressive symptoms. Specifically, the combined effects of poor dietary quality and insufficient sleep duration were associated with a greater increase in depressive symptoms than either factor alone, highlighting the importance of considering both dietary quality and sleep patterns when addressing mental health outcomes. Further analysis revealed that both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration were positively associated with depressive symptoms among females, middle-aged adults and population without sufficient physical activity.

Previous studies using NHANES database data have consistently indicated a link between poor dietary quality and an increased risk of depression [14, 24], aligning with our findings. Rather than focusing solely on single nutrients or foods, comprehensive food-based diet patterns, which consider the interplay between various nutrients and food groupings, have gained widespread popularity in understanding health trajectories [51, 52]. These patterns, such as healthy/prudent, Mediterranean, and Tuscan diet patterns, emphasize higher intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and fish, which have anti-inflammatory properties [53]. Chronic inflammation is considered an underlying factor in depression, whereas consumption of refined grains, sodium, added sugars, saturated fats, and ultra-processed foods is discouraged [54]. Our study comprehensively examines these nutrients, highlighting their potential positive impact on depression symptoms in large populations. Therefore, promoting a healthy diet to reduce the risk of depression. In our study, we also identified a link between unhealthy sleep duration patterns and a higher risk of depressive symptoms. This result aligns with the findings of Firth et al., who highlighted that sleep duration, as a modifiable lifestyle factor, is an independent risk factor for depressive symptoms [54]. Notably, various trials have demonstrated the positive impact of sleep interventions on both subclinical depressive symptoms and individuals with mental illness [55–57]. Consequently, it is crucial to prioritize and address unhealthy sleep duration as a modifiable risk factor for depression within the population.

In our study, participants were categorized into four lifestyle groups based on varying dietary quality and sleep duration to investigate the interactive impact on depressive symptoms. We found that the risk of depression symptoms was significantly correlated with three specific lifestyles: unhealthy diet but healthy sleep duration, healthy diet but unhealthy sleep duration, and unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration, compared to the pattern of a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration. Therefore, it can be hypothesized that both a healthy diet and healthy sleep duration play crucial roles in preventing depression symptoms and should not be overlooked. Moreover, our study further explored the interactive effect of dietary quality and sleep duration on depressive symptoms. We discovered an additive interaction between dietary quality and sleep duration, which amplifies the synergistic effect on the risk of depression symptoms when both an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration are simultaneously present. This finding aligns with the emerging field of ‘lifestyle psychiatry’, which suggests that considering multiple lifestyle factors concurrently is more appropriate for comprehending and managing risk factors across all mental health conditions [58]. It is important to note that adopting a healthy lifestyle, which includes maintaining a high-quality diet and adhering to recommended sleep duration, is appealing due to its absence of adverse effects while promoting overall well-being [59]. Therefore, based on these findings, integrating a high-quality diet and healthy sleep duration into national and community strategies targeting depression symptoms is crucial to achieve optimal prevention outcomes.

In our study, we found that both dietary quality and sleep duration influence the risk of depressive symptoms in females. Previous studies have highlighted that females are more susceptible to depressive symptoms than males due to physiological differences and behaviors [60]. Females typically have twice the risk of developing depressive symptoms compared to males, and they also face a higher risk of experiencing more severe depression [61]. The nuanced interplay between the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sex-specific hormones likely plays a significant role in the onset of depression in females [62]. Data from NHANES has even shown that an inflammatory diet can increase the risk of depression in females [63]. Interestingly, we found that dietary quality in females was significantly higher than in males, highlighting the importance of females paying attention to their diet. Additionally, we observed that a higher percentage of females had longer sleep duration (8.09% of females had equal to or more than 9 h of sleep at night, and 5.74% of females had equal to or more than 9 h of sleep at night) combined with lower levels of PA measured in metabolic equivalent (MET) compared to males (females averaging 182.49 min per week compared to 381.62 min per week for males). Previous studies have suggested that extended sleep duration is often associated with low levels of physical activity [64], which can affect neurotransmitter release and brain aminergic synaptic transmission, potentially increasing the incidence of depression [65]. It is worth noting that when an unhealthy diet and poor sleep duration coincide, males can also be at high risk of depression. This could be because males tend to engage in more health-risk behaviors than females [66], making them more susceptible to developing depressive symptoms when their combined lifestyle risks reach a certain threshold [67].

Additionally, our study found that middle-aged adults were more likely to suffer from depression due to an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration. We also observed that middle-aged adults were more likely to consume 10 or more drinks per month. Previous studies have suggested that binge drinkers tend to have a poorer quality diet compared to non-drinkers, with higher consumption of total fat and sugary foods and lower intake of whole grains and fiber [68, 69]. Such a diet can led to low-grade inflammation and an increased risk of depression [14, 24]. Furthermore, our findings showed that the mean sleep duration for adults aged 20–59 years in this study was less than the recommendation of 7–9 h per day. A prospective study spanning 20 years suggested that short sleep duration during young adulthood (18–30 years old) was associated with changes in white matter integrity in various regions of the brain, in middle-aged adults, compared to those with moderate sleep duration [70]. Reductions in white matter integrity within the left superior longitudinal fasciculus, a crucial association pathway that links the frontal regions to all other cerebral lobes, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, a key area in the emotion-regulation circuit [71]. Ischemic changes in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex increase the susceptibility of individuals to major depressive disorder, and imaging studies suggest that more subtle microstructural and functional changes in the connectivity of this region may also contribute to depression in younger adults [72, 73].

Our study also found that adults with a college degree or higher education were more susceptible to the impact of both dietary quality and sleep duration on depressive symptoms. Previous research suggests that higher education levels can increase individuals’ susceptibility to mood disturbances [74, 75]. Higher education is often associated with greater stress and pressure, as students face demanding academic requirements, competitive environments, and high expectations. This ongoing stress can, in turn, contribute to the development of depressive symptoms [76]. Meanwhile, these individuals showed a higher tendency to consume 10 or more drinks per month while engaging in low levels of physical activity. Both excessive alcohol use [68, 69] and low level of physical activity [65] have been linked to an increased risk of depression. Moreover, the risk of depressive symptoms was influenced by both dietary quality and sleep duration among individuals with a normal BMI, who did not drink alcohol and had never smoked, consistent with previous studies [77, 78]. For individuals with otherwise healthy lifestyles, the presence of one unhealthy lifestyle factor was associated with a 10% increase in the risk of depressive symptoms [79]. However, it’s important to highlight the impact of dietary quality and sleep duration on depressive symptoms could vary among individuals who are overweight, obese, consume alcohol, or smoke.

Despite the valuable findings, our study also had some limitations. Firstly, we were unable to differentiate the different outcomes between short sleep duration (< 7 h at night) and long sleep duration (≥ 9 h at night) due to the limited number of individuals meeting the criteria for healthy diet and long sleep duration (1.59%) or unhealthy diet and long sleep duration (5.35%) categories. Furthermore, the self-reporting nature of sleep duration in our study introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability biases. Moreover, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, establishing causal links between dietary quality, sleep duration, and depression is challenging. Meanwhile, the population under 20 years of age was not included in the analysis, and those over 80 years old could not be classified for in-depth study. A significant limitation is the age of the data, as our study utilized NHANES data from the 2007 to 2014 cycle, which is over 10 years old and not representative of a contemporary sample. Finally, it is worth mentioning that certain potential risk factors, such as physical condition and socioeconomic status, could influence depressive symptoms. However, these factors were not considered in our study due to a significant amount of missing data in this regard.

Conclusion

Our study, based on NHANES data from the 2007 to 2014 cycle, revealed a positive correlation between an unhealthy diet, unhealthy sleep duration, and depression symptoms in a representative sample of the US population. This suggests that individuals influenced by the synergistic effect of low-quality diet combine with unhealthy sleep duration are more prone to experiencing depressive symptoms. Specifically, females, middle-aged individuals, those with a college degree or higher education, and individuals who do not meet the recommended level of physical activity exhibit heightened susceptibility to depressive symptoms when confronted with an unhealthy diet and unhealthy sleep duration. These findings underscore the importance of integrated public health interventions that promote both healthy eating and healthy sleep duration, particularly among vulnerable populations, to mitigate the risk of depression.
