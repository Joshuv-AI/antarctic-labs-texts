Hospital length of stay and the role of ICU transfer of care in postoperative oral cancer patients
Authors: Junpeng Tang, Yan Huang, Jinzhao Zhang, Fanrong Lin, Siqi Liu, Weipeng Liu, Jingying Hou, Zhengfei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe decision to transfer postoperative oral cancer patients to the intensive care unit (ICU) lacks standardized criteria and remains contentious. This study aimed to determine whether ICU transfer independently prolongs hospital length of stay (LOS) and identify the primary determinants of LOS in these patients.MethodsA retrospective single-center cohort study was conducted in 674 patients who underwent oral cancer surgery. Patients were categorized into the ICU and general ward groups. Data on demographics (age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, and betel chewing), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes and other chronic diseases), intraoperative factors (tracheotomy, duration of operation, lymph node dissection, flap transplantation, and blood transfusion), and clinical scoring systems (SOFA, APACHE-II, CAPRINI and NRS 2002), were all analysed. Linear regression analyses were performed to identify independent determinants of LOS.ResultsPatients in the ICU group were older and had higher rates of hypertension and diabetes (p < 0.05). Between-group comparisons exhibited a higher incidence of intraoperative blood transfusion in the ICU group (p = 0.001), whereas flap transplantation rate was lower (p < 0.001). Comparisons of postoperative clinical scores showed that the ICU group had significantly higher scores of SOFA (p < 0.001), APACHE-II (p < 0.05), and CAPRINI (p < 0.05). Additionally, patients with a SOFA score > 2 or an APACHE-II score ≥ 15 accounted for a higher proportion in this group. Subsequent analysis revealed that there was a longer unadjusted LOS in the ICU group (p < 0.001). However, ICU transfer was not an independent predictor of LOS after adjustment for confounders (p > 0.05). Independent determinants of prolonged LOS included tracheotomy (p < 0.001), intraoperative blood transfusion (p < 0.001), and bilateral lymph node dissection (p < 0.001). Age-stratified analyses indicated that ICU admission independently prolonged LOS in older adults (≥ 65 years, p < 0.05), but not in younger adults (< 65 years, p > 0.05).ConclusionICU transfer is not an independent determinant of the overall LOS of postoperative oral cancer patients, instead, surgical complexity and lymph node dissection play a more critical role. In older adults, ICU transfer represents a key modifiable factor associated with prolonged LOS. These findings support a risk-stratified and age-based triage protocol to optimize ICU utilization and improve healthcare efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07997-1.Keywords: Hospital Length of Stay, ICU Transfer, Oral Cancer

Background

Oral cancer is a tumour with high morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in developing countries. According to the recent released report, an estimated 370,000 new cases and 190,000 deaths from lip and oral cancers (LOC) occurred globally in 2019 [1]. Among all cancer types, lip and oral cancers ranked 16th in terms of incidence worldwide [2]. The incidence of oral cancer is significantly higher in Asia, and this phenomenon is closely related to the specific diet and lifestyle, including areca nut chewing, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and other high-risk factors, which constitute the core aetiologies of oral cancer [1, 2].

Currently, surgery remains the primary treatment for locally advanced or aggressive oral cancer. Extensive resection and complex reconstructive procedures during the surgery usually increase its risk [3, 4]. Consequently, postoperative monitoring and patient management after surgery is critical, particularly in the first few days following surgery [5, 6]. Some patients may be directly transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring during this vulnerable period in order to prevent and manage the potential postoperative complications without delay [5]. However, there are currently no standardized clinical criteria to determine whether all postoperative patients require ICU admission. At present, decision-making of ICU admission is largely based on individual clinical experience of surgeons and anesthesiologists [7].

In recent years, debates have been continued on the pros and cons of ICU admission. Abundant evidences have indicated that routine ICU monitoring is not mandatory for all postoperative head and neck cancer patients. Clinical outcomes of the patients who are carefully selected and managed in general wards are even comparable to those receiving the ICU care [8, 9]. Additionally, patients admitted to the ICU tend to have a longer length of stay (LOS), which might adversely affect their prognosis. Therefore, it remains unclear whether ICU transfer prolongs LOS or simply serves as a perioperative risk factor. in this study, we aimed to evaluate whether postoperative ICU admission independently influences LOS in patients undergoing oral cancer surgery. Relevant clinical data and postoperative indicators were compared and analysed between the ICU and general ward care. Stratified analyses were performed afterwards according to different clinical scoring systems and indexes.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: patients with a definite diagnosis of oral cancer, and with surgical treatment in the Oral and Maxillofacial Department of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSMH), Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China. Patients were admitted to the ICU immediately after the surgery if they had severe organ function impairment, risk factors, or comorbidity. In some cases, admission to the ICU was due to the decision of the operating surgeon. All participants were 18 or more years of age and had complete medical records. Exclusion criteria: those with repeated admission records or with non-malignant tumours were excluded from the research (Fig. 1).

General data and group divisions

674 patients undergoing surgical treatment for oral cancer were eventually recruited in the stomatology department of SYSMH from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2022. This study was approved by the ethics committee of SYSMH (Ethics Code: SYSKY-2024-783-01). The detailed study design and data processing are shown in the flow chart in Fig. 2.

Detailed data processing of the study, including the selection and group divisions for the patients enrolled in the analysis. ICU: intensive care unit

The enrolled patients (n = 674) were divided into the ICU group (n = 134, 19.88%) and the general ward group (n = 540, 80.12%). Further division of the subgroups was performed according to Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE)-II scores [10, 11]. The detailed information was as follows: (1) ICU group: SOFA ≤ 2 (n = 33, 24.63%) and SOFA > 2 (n = 101, 75.37%); APACHE-II < 15 (n = 72, 53.73%) and APACHE-II ≥ 15 (n = 62, 46.27%); (2) General ward group: SOFA ≤ 2 (n = 437, 80.93%) and SOFA > 2 (n = 103, 19.07%); APACHE-II < 15 (n = 534, 98.89%) and APACHE-II ≥ 15 (n = 6, 1.11%).

For the age stratification, the whole set of 674 patients was separately divided into two groups: (1) Older adults: age ≥ 65 years (n = 199, 29.53%); (2) Younger adults: age < 65 years (n = 475, 70.47%).

Clinical data collection and evaluation

The clinical data of the patients were collected through the electronic medical records system of the hospital. The baseline information included age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) [12]. Personal histories were also recorded, which contained smoking, drinking, and betel nut chewing habits. In addition, information on medical histories was collected, including hypertension, diabetes, prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and other diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cerebral infarction (CI).

In terms of clinical indexes, we mainly focused on preoperative treatments, operation duration, tracheotomy, lymph node dissection, flap transplantation, fibula/femur flap transplantation, intraoperative blood transfusion, as well as other relevant factors that might influence prognosis. Relevant clinical scoring systems, including SOFA, APACHE-II, nutrition risk screening (NRS) 2002 and Caprini Venous Thromboembolism Risk Assessment Model (CAPRINI) scores were applied in the evaluation of organ dysfunction and postoperative complications [10, 11, 13, 14]. The SOFA was comprised by respiratory function, coagulation function, liver function, cardiovascular system, central nervous system and kidney function. SOFA ≤ 2 usually indicates mild organ function damage [10], while SOFA > 2 indicated a moderate to severe damage. APACHE-II score was specifically designed to assess the severity of illness in ICU patients and predict their prognosis [11]. Patients with an APACHE-II score ≥ 15 could be defined as critically ill patients. NRS 2002 score was used to assess functional status impairment and the overall condition of the patient [13]. The cumulative score ≥ 3 often indicated a risk of malnutrition. The CAPRINI score was an important assessment scale for the risk of venous thrombosis in clinical practice [14]. Higher total score represented higher risk of venous thrombosis. Comparisons of the LOS were conducted between groups and subgroups based on relevant stratifications.

Statistical analysis

The R (R Software, Tsinghua University, China) language and relevant packages were used for statistical analysis. Detailed packages included readxl (data import), dplyr (data processing), stats (t-test, correlation, rank-sum, Mann-Whitney U), broom (regression output), writexl (export), and tibble (variable management). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Continuous variables were initially assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots prior to further data analysis. The variable distribution was presented as the normal (or approximately normal) or skewed type according to the normality test results. Data with normal (or approximately normal) distribution included LOS, SOFA score, BMI, APACHE-II score, NRS 2002 score, and CAPRINI score. Other variables, including age and duration of operation, showed a skewed distribution. Continuous variables with normal distributions were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with skewed distribution were described as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. In addition, they were dummy-coded for inclusion in regression models. For univariate analysis, between-group differences were assessed using two-tailed independent-samples t-tests in continuous variables with normal distribution, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to variables with skewed distribution (i.e., age and duration of operation). Associations between categorical variables and relevant outcome were evaluated using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, and results were reported as corresponding test statistics (t, U, or χ²) and p-values (Supplementary Table 1). Variables showing a significant association (p < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis (Supplementary Table 2) [15, 16].

Multiple linear regression was employed to identify key factors correlated with LOS (Supplementary Table 2) [15, 16]. The exported results were mainly presented as regression coefficients, standard errors (SE), t-values, and p-values. To explore potential effect modifications, patients in the ICU group were further stratified by age for subgroup analysis. Additionally, the clinical implications of the relevant scoring systems were further investigated.

In the univariate analysis, we used a p-value threshold of p < 0.1 to select candidate variables for inclusion in the multivariate model. This approach is widely adopted in clinical and epidemiological research because the goal of univariate screening is not to test hypotheses but to identify potential predictors that could be relevant in a multivariable context. Using a more liberal threshold (e.g., p < 0.10 instead of p < 0.05) reduces the risk of excluding variables that may not reach conventional significance in univariate tests but could be important predictors when adjusted for confounders in the multivariate model. This approach has been recommended in regression modeling literature, including the purposeful selection strategy described by Hosmer et al. [17] and further supported by subsequent methodological studies [18].

The overall age of the 674 patients ranged from 18 to 99 years old, and the median age was 56. There was a significant difference in the median age between the ICU and the general ward (65 vs. 54, p < 0.001, Table 1). The number of patients with a personal history of alcohol consumption was lower in the ICU group in contrast with the general ward group (14.93% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.006, Table 1). ICU group had a significantly longer LOS (15.44 ± 6.91 vs. 13.19 ± 5.24 days, p < 0.001, Table 1). Statistical analysis also showed obvious differences in previous disease history in the two groups. There were more patients with hypertension and diabetes in the ICU group than the general ward group (hypertension: 33.58% vs. 20.93%, p = 0.002; diabetes: 18.66% vs. 10.93%, p = 0.015, Table 1). However, the percentage of patients with other diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cerebral infarction (CI) (CVD and CI) were much lower in the ICU group compared with the general ward group (45.52% vs. 63.52%, p < 0.001, Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in other indexes such as sex, BMI, smoking, betel chewing and preoperative treatments, including surgery and chemoradiotherapy (p > 0.05, Table 1).

Subgroup analysis for flap transplantation revealed that differences existed in fibula flap transplantation (26.12% vs. 36.67%, p = 0.022, Table 2). No significant differences were found in other indexes, including femur flap transplantation, other flap transplantation types, operation duration, tracheotomy, and lymph node dissection (p > 0.05, Table 2).

Factors influencing LOS

The factors that might impact LOS were examined step by step via linear regression analysis afterwards (Table 4). There was a weak correlation between ICU transfer and LOS in a univariate regression model, but with relatively lower effect size (point biserial correlation coefficient, rPB = 0.158, p < 0.001, Table 4). Other variables with statistical significances in univariate analysis included intraoperative blood transfusion (rPB = 0.306, p < 0.001, Table 4), tracheotomy (rPB = 0.286, p < 0.001, Table 4), bilateral lymph node dissection (rPB = 0.204, p < 0.001, Table 4), history of other diseases (rPB = 0.095, p = 0.009, Table 4), age (correlation coefficient, r = 0.088, p = 0.022, Table 4), BMI ( r = − 0.136, p < 0.001, Table 4), and surgical duration (r = 0.192, p < 0.001, Table 4). However, factors such as diabetes, skin flap transplantation, and hypertension did not demonstrate statistical significances (p > 0.05, Table 4).

Variables with p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were further employed in the multifactor linear regression model to evaluate their independent effects on LOS. No statistical significance was revealed in the regression coefficient of ICU transfer (estimate, β = 0.9118, p = 0.1626, Table 4), suggesting that the effect of ICU transfer on LOS was not statistically significant after adjustment of the other variables. The independent factors that demonstrated statistically significant influence on LOS included intraoperative blood transfusion (β = 2.905, p < 0.001, Table 4), tracheotomy (β = 3.381, p < 0.001, Table 4), bilateral lymph node dissection (β = 1.832, p < 0.001, Table 4), history of other diseases (β = 1.283, p = 0.001, Table 4), SOFA score (β = 0.695, p = 0.001, Table 4), and CAPRINI scores (β = 0.238, p = 0.009, Table 4). The abovementioned results showed that ICU transfer was not an independent influencing factor that dominated LOS, and the main determinants were intraoperative blood transfusion, tracheotomy, bilateral lymph node dissection, history of other diseases, CAPRINI and SOFA scores.

Factors influencing LOS based on age group analysis

The enrolled patients were separately stratified into the older adults and younger adults based on age (Tables 5 and 6). Linear regression analysis indicated that ICU transfer could extend LOS for the patients in the older adults (p = 0.021, Table 5). There were several other positive factors that strongly impacted LOS, including bilateral lymph node dissection (p = 0.010, Table 5), tracheotomy (p = 0.002, Table 5), and intraoperative blood transfusion (p = 0.005, Table 5). However, preoperative treatment was shown to reduce LOS (p = 0.017, Table 5). Other variables (SOFA score, APACHE-II score, BMI, and operation duration) did not demonstrate statistically significant effects in multivariate analysis.

Discussion

In this study, retrospective analysis was used to comprehensively investigate the clinical indicators and LOS in 674 postoperative oral cancer patients. To address potential confounding, multivariable models were constructed adjusting for key demographic, clinical, and surgical factors, including age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cerebral infarction), preoperative treatment, surgical type and duration, tracheotomy, intraoperative blood transfusion, lymph node dissection, and flap transplantation. After risk adjustment, ICU transfer was not identified as an independent determinant of LOS. The main determinants of LOS across different age groups included tracheotomy, intraoperative blood transfusion, and bilateral lymph node dissection. For the older adults, ICU transfer and preoperative treatment were the main factors that affected LOS, while for the younger ones, SOFA and CAPRINI scores, other chronic diseases, and other skin flap transplantation were the main factors.

In the present work, we initially compared the baseline clinical information of the enrolled patients between the ICU and the general ward group. The median age was higher in the ICU group, and hypertension and diabetes were more prevalent. The ICU plays a critical role in the postoperative care of surgical patients, and there are numerous oral cancer patients receiving ICU monitoring post surgery [7, 19]. The ICU provides advanced monitoring and comprehensive life-support for high-risk conditions in postoperative management, such as acute cardiovascular events and perioperative hyperglycaemia [20–22]. In contradistinction to hypertension and diabetes, the proportion of patients with other diseases, such as CVD and CI, were lower in the ICU group, well-controlled chronic conditions alone may not independently necessitate postoperative ICU admission. This finding might be attributable to adequate preoperative managements and comprehensive perioperative assessment, which mitigate postoperative risk and reduce the need for intensive care [23, 24].

For patients with oral cancer in the early stage, surgical resection remains the preferred primary treatment modality [25]. The complexity of surgical procedures is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, including infection, bleeding, flap necrosis, and aggravation of swallowing disorders [26]. Accordingly, we further investigated the intraoperative determinants of the patients. Distinct between-group differences were observed in intraoperative flap transplantation and blood transfusion. The ICU group showed a lower rate of flap transplantation, which was contrary to some previous reports and should be further validated in larger samples [27, 28]. On the other side, intraoperative blood transfusion was more frequent among patients admitted to the ICU, suggesting that transfusion may reflect greater surgical complexity or perioperative risk, which should be carefully balanced in clinical decision-making [29].

After the investigation of relevant intraoperative indexes, we further appraised postoperative organ dysfunction and complications through several clinical scoring systems. The ICU group exhibited significantly higher SOFA and APACHE-II scores, with a greater proportion of patients exceeding clinically relevant thresholds (SOFA > 2 or APACHE-II ≥ 15). For patients with elevated scores, postoperative ICU admission may facilitate early risk stratification and comprehensive supportive care, thereby reducing complication rates and improving postoperative recovery [30, 31]. Patients with lower scores (SOFA ≤ 2 or APACHE-II < 15) did not experience prolonged LOS when managed in the general ward, indicating that standard ward monitoring is sufficient for clinically stable patients, and this may also help to avoid unnecessary ICU utilization while preserving medical resources [7, 32].

In the evaluation of the key factors affecting LOS, the SOFA and APACHE-II scores demonstrated statistically significant predictive value, which was consistent with some other literatures [33, 34]. Notably, patients with higher SOFA scores did not exhibit prolonged LOS in the ICU group, suggesting that high-quality ICU care may mitigate the adverse impact of severe postoperative organ dysfunction. In addition to SOFA and APACHE-II scores, several intraoperative indexes, including blood transfusion, tracheotomy, and bilateral lymph node dissection, were also found to be associated with LOS, supporting previous reports that surgical complexity could be closely linked with prolonged recovery time [35–38]. Age-stratified analyses further revealed that ICU admission and preoperative treatment were key determinants of LOS in older adults, highlighting the importance of preoperative optimization in this population [39]. In contrast, LOS in younger adults less than 65 years was primarily influenced by baseline severity scores and flap transplantation, indicating that underlying health status plays a dominant role in postoperative recovery among younger adults [33, 40, 41]. In summary, these findings stressed the necessity of selecting appropriate postoperative management based on age groups.

Limitation

This study was based on a single-centre design, which might be affected by the factors at the local medical management level and regional level, thus restricting the wide applicability of the study results. The lack of long-term follow-up data after discharge directly caused incomplete assessment of the influence of the ICU and the general ward on prognostic factors, such as postoperative survival status, quality of life, and functional recovery of the body. Additionally, there was insufficient exploration of various complications, including their incidence rates, optimal intervention times, and treatment effects. Moreover, the scoring systems used in the evaluation process were unable to comprehensively capture all potential factors influencing postoperative recovery, leaving certain aspects of patient outcomes unaddressed.

Conclusion

This study validated the prominent role of the ICU in the treatment of high-risk oral cancer patients after surgery. It was revealed that ICU admission was not an independent determinant of LOS in postoperative oral cancer patients. Major influencing factors included intraoperative blood transfusion, tracheotomy, bilateral lymph node dissection, and the SOFA score. For the older adults, ICU transfer and preoperative treatment were the main factors of LOS, while for the younger ones, SOFA and CAPRINI scores, chronic diseases, and other skin flap transplantation were the main factors.

Key Messages for Clinical Practice: ICU admission should not be a routine in postoperative oral cancer patients, while instead it could be guided by objective risk assessment strategies. Several clinical score systems would be applicable in the evaluating procedure, such as SOFA and APACHE-II. As ICU transfer is a key determinant of prolonged hospitalization in the older adults whose age have already reached 65 years old or more, particular caution should be warranted when considering ICU admission for this population group. For the patients with low illness severity (e.g., SOFA ≤ 2), postoperative care in a general ward is a safe and resource-efficient alternative. Clinicians should anticipate a longer LOS in patients requiring tracheotomy, intraoperative blood transfusion, or bilateral neck dissection, regardless of their age or ICU admission status.
