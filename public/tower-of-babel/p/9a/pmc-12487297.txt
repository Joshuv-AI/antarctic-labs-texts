Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) gene family identification reveals GhMPK10-mediated regulation of abiotic stress tolerance through protein kinase signaling cascades in cotton
Authors: Yan Li, Ruqi Su, Kunfeng Wang, Jie Wang, Dajian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) family plays a crucial role in environmental signal transduction and stress adaptation in plants. However, the MAPK gene family in Gossypium hirsutum remains incompletely characterized, limiting its utilization for improving stress resilience. In this study, we identified 56 GhMPKs in cotton and systematically analyzed their genomic organization, phylogenetic relationships, and expression patterns under drought, salinity, and heat stress. Phylogenetic classification grouped GhMPKs into four distinct categories (A-D). Tissue-specific expression profiling revealed that GhMPK10 was ubiquitously expressed across developmental stages, suggesting its broad regulatory roles. Under abiotic stresses, GhMPK10 displayed sustained induction, implicating its central role in stress signaling. Functional validation through Arabidopsis overexpression and virus-induced gene silencing in cotton confirmed GhMPK10 as a critical regulator of drought and salt tolerance. Transcriptomic analysis of silenced plants highlighted disrupted MAPK signaling pathways, including downregulation of ABA-responsive stomatal closure genes (PYR/PYL, ABF) and ethylene biosynthesis regulators (GhACS1). Protein interaction networks further implicated GhMPK10 in key stress-responsive kinase pathways (GhMAPKK8/9/10) and transcription factors (GhSPCH), which regulate stomatal patterning. This study is the first to identify GhMPK10 as a key regulator of abiotic stress tolerance through phosphorylation signaling, providing valuable molecular targets for breeding stress-resilient cotton varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07195-1.Keywords: Gossypium hirsutum, Mitogen-Activated protein kinase, Abiotic stress

Introduction

Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling is a well-conserved molecular mechanism in eukaryotic organisms, including animals, yeasts, and plants [1]. This pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression, metabolism, motility, cell division, apoptosis, and survival, conferring resistance to various environmental stimuli (heat, cold, drought, salinity, etc.) and pathogen attacks [2–6]. A typical MAPK pathway consists of three interconnected kinase cascades: (i) upstream signals that activate MAPK kinase kinases (MAP3K), (ii) MAPK kinase (MAP2Ks or MAPKKs), and (iii) the downstream of MAPK [7, 8]. In this signaling cascade, upstream components phosphorylate MAPKs to activate them. MAPKKKs (serine/threonine kinases) are activated via phosphorylation of two amino acids in the Thr/Ser motif within the T-loop of MAPKKs, in response to various stimuli. MAPKKs are subsequently activated by phosphorylated MAPKKKs, and MAPKs—the final components in the cascade—are activated through dual-specific phosphorylation of Thr and Tyr residues in the conserved TEY or TDY motifs [5, 9].

MAPK genes contain 11 domains (I-XI) that are critical for the catalytic function of serine/threonine protein kinases [10]. Domains VII and VIII are well-conserved in plant species. Plant MAPK family genes are categorized into four major groups (A, B, C, and D) depending on protein sequences and phosphorylation motifs. Members of groups A, B, and C possess a TEY (Thr-Glu-Tyr) phosphorylation motif at their active site [11]. In contrast, group D members contain a TDY (Thr-Asp-Tyr) phosphorylation motif and have a long C terminal sequence [11]. The MAPK gene family has been systematically studied in various plants, including Arabidopsis [12], rice [13], maize [14], Populus trichocarpa [15], Brachypodium distachyon [16], wheat [17], barley [18], soybean [19], and foxtail millet [20]. To date, 55 MAPK genes have been identified in G. hirsutum [21]. but the regulatory roles of GhMAPKs in response to abiotic stresses remain largely unexplored. Characterizing the complete set of GhMAPKs and understanding their roles in abiotic stress tolerance will provide critical insights into cotton’s stress signaling responses.

MAPKs play crucial roles in plant responses to both biotic (e.g., pathogen infections) and abiotic (e.g., drought, high salinity, extreme temperatures, heavy metal pollution, UV radiation) stresses. In Arabidopsis, several MAPKs [22], such as AtMPK3 and AtMPK6, are involved in pathogen defense and oxidative stress responses [12, 23]. AtMPK4 regulates innate immunity and cell death [24]. In rice, 15 OsMAPKs have been validated as key regulators of growth, development, and stress responses [13]. For example, the OsMPK4-IPA1 signaling cascade regulates the rice response to salt stress, while OsMAPK5 is involved in responses to salt, drought, and cold stresses, and OsMAPK6 plays a role in pathogen defence and hormone signaling [25–28]. In cotton, GhMPK2 is up-regulated under salt and drought stress, contributing to antioxidant defense and stomatal closure mechanisms [29, 30]. Similarly, GhMPK3 enhances tolerance to both drought and salt stress [31]. GhMPK7 appears to function at the intersection of defence responses and plant hormone signal transduction, suggesting a cross-regulatory role in coordinating these pathways [32]. GhMPK16 modulates disease resistance and drought sensitivity [33], and GhMPK31 is essential in regulating defense mechanisms against cotton bollworms [21]. These findings highlight the diverse functions of MAPKs in cotton. Comprehensive characterization of the entire MAPK gene family in G. hirsutum may uncover additional key candidates for improving multi-stress tolerance in cotton plants.

In this study, we conducted a genome-wide identification and structural characterization of the MAPK gene family to (i) identify all GhMPKs, (ii) examine their phylogenetic relationships, genomic structures, chromosomal locations, conserved motifs, and expression patterns across different organs and under abiotic stresses, and (iii) validate the functions of key GhMPKs in response to these stresses. Our findings provide valuable insights for future functional studies of GhMPKs, which will aid in the development of cotton varieties with improved multi-stress tolerance.

Results

Genome-wide identification of the MAPK family genes in G. hirsutum, phylogenetic relationships, and chromosomal distribution

To identify the MAPK family genes in G. hirsutum, well-characterized AtMPKs (from Arabidopsis) and OsMPKs (from rice) were used as queries in a BLAST search against the G. hirsutum genome sequence database. A total of 56 GhMPKs were identified and designated as GhMPK1 to GhMPK56 based on their homology to Arabidopsis MAPK genes (Table S1). These 56 GhMPKs encode proteins ranging from 117 amino acids (GhMPK55) to 716 amino acids (GhMPK51), with molecular weights ranging from 13.80 kDa (GhMPK55) to 82.45 kDa (GhMPK51) and isoelectric points (pIs) ranging from 5.04 (GhMPK31) to 9.32 (GhMPK19/GhMPK44) (Table S1). The open reading frame (ORF) lengths of these GhMPKs ranged from 351 bp (GhMPK55) to 2148 bp (GhMPK51). Regarding subcellular localization, 33 GhMPKs (58.93%) are predicted to be localized in the nucleus, 18 (32.14%) in the cytoplasm, and 5 (8.93%) in the mitochondria (Table S1).

A phylogenetic tree was constructed to clarify the evolutionary relationship of MAPK genes among Gossypium hirsutum, Arabidopsis, rice, and Populus trichocarpa. As shown in Fig. 1A, the 56 GhMPKs were categorized into four groups (A, B, C, and D), along with their orthologs. Group D was the largest, containing 21 GhMPKs, while groups A, B, and C contained 10, 13, and 12 GhMPKs, respectively (Fig. 1A). The phylogenetic analysis revealed that many GhMPKs, except for GhMPK11 and GhMPK55, are paralogs, such as GhMPK22 and GhMPK26, GhMPK23 and GhMPK24, and GhMPK12 and GhMPK25 (Fig. 1A). All GhMPKs were unevenly distributed across the 24 chromosomes of G. hirsutum, with the exception of GhMPK55, which was not mapped to any chromosome (Fig. 1B). The number of GhMPKs per chromosome ranged from one (A04, A08, A09, D04, D07, D08 and D09) to five (A03 and D02) (Fig. 1B). Several GhMPKs from different groups, such as GhMPK31/GhMPK3, GhMPK15/GhMPK29, and GhMPK30/GhMPK8 exhibited cluster pairs of chromosomes A05, A12, and D05, respectively. Other cluster pairs of genes from the same group were observed on chromosomes A07 (GhMPK11/GhMPK22) and D11 (GhMPK44/GhMPK54) were both located on chromosomes from the same group D, respectively. Notably, two paralogous gene pairs, GhMPK11/GhMPK22 and GhMPK44/GhMPK54, were found on chromosomes A07 and A11, respectively (Fig. 1B).

To gain further insights into the gene structure and phylogenetic relationships among MAPK genes, we examined their exon/intron organization (Fig. S1). The number of exons in GhMPKs ranged from 2 to 13. Notably, members of groups A and B share a similar exon count of five to six, except for GhMPK13, which contains eight exons. GhMPKs in group C have a unique structure, with only one intron and two exons, consistent with group C members in Arabidopsis [12], Populus [15], tomato and apple [34, 35]. In contrast, group D GhMPKs exhibit more complex and divergent exon/intron patterns, with intron numbers ranging from eight to twelve (excluding GhMPK55), which is higher than observed in Arabidopsis, Populus, tomato, and apple. Comparisons between GhMPK15 and GhMPK9, GhMPK52 and GhMPK56, GhMPK45 and GhMPK46, and GhMPK50 and GhMPK51 in group D revealed deletions or gains of exons at the 5’ end and the 3’ terminal. It is evident that the exon/intron structures within each gene cluster are likely the result of tandem or segmental duplication events. For example, the gene pairs GhMPK31/GhMPK3 and GhMPK30/GhMPK8, located on chromosomes A05 and D05, respectively, share identical exon/intron structures, suggesting segmental duplications. Tandem duplications may have led to the formation of the clusters GhMPK31/GhMPK3 and GhMPK30/GhMPK8.

Multiple alignments and domain analyses of GhMPKs

ClustalX2.0 analysis revealed that all GhMPKs contain 11 domains (Fig. S2A). As shown in Fig. S2A, GhMPKs in groups A, B, and C possess a TEY domain, except for GhMPK26 and GhMPK22 in group B, which contain a TEC domain. GhMPK members of group D harbor a TDY activation domain (except GhMPK46, which includes the TDC domain) (Fig. S2A), consistent with findings in Arabidopsis and rice [1]. In addition to these domains, GhMPKs in groups A and B possess a conserved docking (CD) domain at the C-terminus, defined by the sequence (LH) DxxDE(p)xC (Fig. S2B), similar to that observed in Arabidopsis [36]. The CD domain acts as a docking site for MAPK phosphatases, MAPKKs, and substrates [37]. In contrast, GhMPKs in groups C and D lack the CD domain, consistent with previous reports in Brachypodium distachyon [16].

Additionally, ten conserved motifs, labeled motif 1 to motif 10, were identified (Fig. S2C). Most GhMPK proteins contained seven motifs (motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 10), with the exceptions of GhMPK40, GhMPK46, and GhMPK55 (Fig. S2C). Notably, all 56 GhMPKs contain motifs 1 and 6, indicating these motifs are highly conserved. Members within the same GhMPK group exhibited identical motifs with nearly identical arrangements (Fig. S2C). Motif 8 was specific to group D GhMPKs (Fig. S2C), suggesting that it may play a distinct functional role that warrants further investigation.

Expression patterns of GhMPKs in different tissues and under abiotic stress conditions

To explore the biological functions of GhMPKs, we examined their expression in ten cotton tissues: leaves, stems, roots, torus, petals, stamens, cylicle, pistils, fibers, and ovules. As shown in Fig. 2A, the majority of GhMPKs were expressed across all cotton tissues. However, GhMPK20, GhMPK21, GhMPK46, and GhMPK55 exhibited very low expression levels in all tested tissues (Fig. 2A). GhMPK23 and GhMPK26 showed relatively low expression in petals, while GhMPK30 and GhMPK41 had lower expression levels in stamens (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, GhMPK44 and GhMPK19 from group D, which share high sequences similarity, were expressed in nearly all tissues, predominantly in stamen and ovule (Fig. 2A). GhMPK16 and GhMPK49 (group D) were also expressed moderately in all tissues, with a preference for petals and stamens (Fig. 2A). GhMPK1 and GhMPK5 (group A) were highly expressed in the torus and showed low expression in the pistil (Fig. 2A).

To investigate the potential roles of GhMPKs under various abiotic stresses, their expression profiles were examined under cold, heat, salt, and drought conditions. As shown in Figs. 2B and 30 GhMPKs were highly expressed under these stress conditions. Most GhMPKs were induced by all stress conditions, indicating their essential role in cotton’s stress responses (Fig. 2B). GhMPK47 and GhMPK48 were highly induced at the early stages of stress, but their expression levels decreased after 3 h (Fig. 2B). In contrast, GhMPK1, GhMPK2, GhMPK3, GhMPK5, GhMPK8, GhMPK23, GhMPK24, and GhMPK39 showed increased expression levels under abiotic stresses (Fig. 2B). GhMPK36, GhMPK40, GhMPK46, and GhMPK55 exhibited very low expression levels under abiotic stresses (Fig. 2B). Notably, GhMPK10, GhMPK17, and GhMPK27 displayed high and stable expression across all stress conditions (Fig. 2B), suggesting that they may play central regulatory roles in cotton’s response to abiotic stresses. To further investigate the role of GhMPK10, we profiled its expression across tissues and under drought and salt stresses using RT-qPCR. GhMPK10 expression was significantly elevated in roots, stems, flowers, and pistils; moderate in bracts and stamens; and relatively low in leaves (Fig. 2C). Under abiotic stress (drought/salt), GhMPK10 was rapidly induced within 1 h, peaked at 6 h, and declined by 12 h (Fig. 2D-E).

Functional verification of GhMPK10’s role in abiotic stress tolerance

To clarify the biological functions of GhMPKs in cotton stress responses, we selected GhMPK10 for functional verification under drought and salt stress. We constructed a GhMPK10 overexpression vector and introduced it into Arabidopsis plants. The WT and GhMPK10-overexpressing lines showed similar cotyledon greening rates and growth under normal conditions (Fig. 3). However, under drought and salt stress, the OE plants showed significantly higher cotyledon greening rates and root length compared to WT plants. These results indicate that GhMPK10 plays a crucial role in abiotic stress responses, and its overexpression enhances the ability of transgenic plants to tolerate drought and salt stress.

Cotton plants carrying the PYL156:PDS vector exhibited an albino phenotype, confirming the success of the VIGS experiment (Fig. 4A). The VIGS plants (PYL156:GhMPK10−1, PYL156:GhMPK10−2, PYL156:GhMPK10−3) and control plants (PYL156) were subjected to RT-qPCR analysis, which revealed that the expression levels in the VIGS plants were reduced to less than half of those in the controls. The RT-qPCR results confirmed the effective silencing of GhMPK10(Fig. 4B). After treatment with 18% PEG for 4 h, the wilting in PYL156:GhMPK10 plants was more pronounced than in the control plants (Fig. 4C). Under simulated drought stress, the oxidative stress indicators in PYL156:GhMPK10 transgenic plants exhibited significant alterations. Compared with the control group (PYL156), the transgenic plants showed markedly increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) were significantly reduced (Fig. 4F and G). These findings suggest that GhMAP10 may play a positive regulatory role in modulating cotton’s antioxidant defense response under drought stress.

Transcriptomic analysis of GhMPK10-silenced cotton under drought stress

To reveal the molecular mechanisms affected by GhMPK10 silencing under drought stress, we performed a comparative transcriptome analysis. A total of 510.29 M of high-quality clean reads were obtained, with an average proportion of Q20 and Q30 bases of 97.53% and 92.14%, respectively (Table S2). Correlation analysis among samples confirmed the repeatability of the RNA sequencing (Fig. S3). DEG analysis across various treatment groups revealed significant regulatory roles for GhMPK10 in cotton’s response to drought stress. Between WT vs. VIGS, 809 genes were up-regulated, and 487 genes were down-regulated (Fig. 5A). When comparing WT to WT_D, 2,480 genes were up-regulated and 3,977 genes were down-regulated (Fig. 5A). The VIGS vs. VIGS_D comparison showed 1,945 up-regulated and 1,421 down-regulated genes. Notably, the WT_D vs. VIGS_D comparison exhibited a striking difference, with 3,801 up-regulated and only 924 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A). Cross-comparisons further highlighted these differences: WT vs. VIGS_D had 1,851 up-regulated and 891 down-regulated genes, while the WT_D vs. VIGS comparison revealed the largest disparity, with 5,735 genes up-regulated and 3,234 genes down-regulated (Fig. 5A). Only 62 DEGs overlapped between the pairwise comparisons (Fig. 5B). These findings underscore the broad regulatory roles of GhMPK10 in modulating transcriptome shifts in response to drought stress, indicating its significance in the complex regulatory networks governing cotton’s stress response. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed distinct functional enrichment patterns across the experimental comparisons. DEGs identified between WT and WT_D were primarily enriched in GO terms related to stimulus response, response to oxygen-containing compound, cell periphery, plastid, and chloroplast (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, DEGs from the VIGS vs. VIGS_D comparison showed main enrichment in response to stimulus, response to chemical, response to stress, and cell periphery (Fig. 5E). In contrast, DEGs identified in the WT_D vs. VIGS_D comparison were mainly enriched in terms related to cell periphery, plastid envelope, plastid thylakoid, chloroplast thylakoid, response to radiation, response to light stimulus, and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 5G). KEGG pathway enrichment revealed distinct functional patterns across experimental comparisons. In the WT vs. WT_D comparison, DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways related to metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and protein families: metabolism (Fig. 5D). Conversely, DEGs from the VIGS vs. VIGS_D comparison showed significant enrichment in signal transduction pathways (including MAPK signaling pathway-plant and plant hormone signal transduction), environmental information processing, transcription factors, transporters, metabolism, and protein families: metabolism (Fig. 5F). This pattern indicates that silencing GhMPK10 regulates drought-responsive signaling mechanisms in cotton. Meanwhile, DEGs in the WT_D vs. VIGS_D comparison were mainly enriched in metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, protein families: metabolism, and protein families: signaling and cellular processes (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results indicate that GhMPK10 silencing remodels signaling architectures to enhance regulatory adaptation under sustained drought in cotton.

Silencing the GhMPK10 altered MAPK signaling and hormonal pathways under drought stress

KEGG pathway enrichment showed that the DEGs between VIGS vs. VIGS_D were mostly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway and plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 5F). We examined the expression patterns of key genes involved in ABA-induced stress adaptation, stomatal closure and ethylene-induced defense response (Table S4). Compared to WT, a significant down-regulation of PYR/PYL genes, including Gh_D10G2388, Gh_A05G2630, Gh_A10G0340, Gh_D06G1764, and Gh_D05G2920 was observed under drought (Fig. 6A). The MAP3K17/18 genes Gh_A05G1471, the MKK9, including Gh_A03G1976 and Gh_D12G2575 were significantly up-regulated under drought compared to WT (Fig. 6A). In contrast, two ABF family genes Gh_D09G0925 and Gh_A09G0900 were down-regulated considerably under drought compared to WT (Fig. 6B). The PP2C and SnRK2 family genes exhibited similar expression patterns in GhMPK10-silenced and WT plants. These coordinated changes in gene expression enhanced the sensitivity of GhMPK10-silenced plants to drought stress.

GhMPK10-interacting genes involved in cotton drought response

The regulation of stress responses in cotton involves a very complex network in which many genes, proteins, and metabolites interact. Therefore, we searched for GhMPK10 interacting genes using the online tool STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/). The results revealed that GhMPK10 has strong interactions with eight genes, including two MKK4 (GhMAPKK9 and GhMAPKK10), two MKK3 (GhMAPKK8 and GhMAPKK2), two GhPTP1, one GhACS1, and one GhSPCH (Fig. S4A). The critical roles of these genes in modulating abiotic stress tolerance in plants have been demonstrated [38–41]. Among these genes, GhSPCH (Gh_D12G2689) exhibited over 4-fold significant downregulation in both WT vs. VIGS and WT_D vs. VIGS_D comparisons (Fig. S4B), and GhACS1 (Gh_D12G2746) was significantly upregulated in the WT vs. VIGS comparison, but showed no significant differential expression between WT_D vs. VIGS_D (Fig. S4C). This demonstrates that GhACS1 requires MPK10-mediated signalling to activate drought-responsive pathways.

Discussion

Genome-wide identification, structural characterization, and expression analysis of gene families represent powerful genomic tools for uncovering key regulators that enhance crop development, yield, and stress tolerance. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the MAPK gene family in Gossypium hirsutum, laying a solid foundation for cotton genetic improvement.

GhMPKs are typical MAPKs with similar structures, phylogenetic relationships, and evolution

We identified 56 GhMPK genes in G. hirsutum, with predicted subcellular localization in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and mitochondria, indicating their involvement in a wide range of cellular processes. Compared to Arabidopsis (20), rice (17), and poplar (21), the larger number of GhMPKs suggests gene family expansion in cotton. Phylogenetic analysis grouped these GhMPKs into four clades (A–D), consistent with previous classifications in other plant species. Notably, group D was the largest, comprising 21 GhMPKs, and members of this group also possessed longer C-terminal regions and TDY/TDC phosphorylation motifs, indicating functional divergence [35].

In addition, structural analyses showed that most of the GhMPKs in groups A and B shared the same six exons and similar intron phases. All GhMPKs in group C had only one intron, and those in group D had a complex divergence of introns and exons. GhMPKs in the same group have identical exon/intron structures, which is in accordance with reports from other plants [16]. Collectively, these findings support the high conservation of the MAPK gene family in plants. The observed divergence between GhMPK members from different groups suggests diversified functions for GhMPKs in different groups.

Meanwhile, GhMPKs in the same clade often have similar biological roles [42]. For instance, AtMPK3 and AtMPK6 (group A members) are both critical for Arabidopsis innate immunity [43]. The tomato SlMAPK2, SlMAPK1, and SlMAPK3 in group A respond to both the oligosaccharide elicitors, wound signaling peptide systemin, the fungal toxin fusicoccin, and ultraviolet-B radiation [35]. Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that MAPK genes in G. hirsutum evolved by both the segmental and the tandem duplication events, which provides support for the high number of GhMPKs.

GhMPKs are essential for various developmental and abiotic stress-responsive processes

The MAPK pathway is an essential signaling cascade indispensable for each aspect of plant development, growth, yield, and stress (biotic and abiotic) tolerance [44]. Transcript expression profiles of GhMPKs revealed that 45 out of 52 (87%) genes were highly expressed in many cotton plant tissues, including vegetative organs (leaves, stems, and roots) and reproductive organs (petals, ovules, stamens, torus, pistils, cylycle, and fibers). 44% (23) of GhMPKs showed significantly high expression both in the petals and stamens. These results suggest that GhMPKs might play critical metabolic or regulatory roles in vegetative tissues and particularly in reproductive organ development in G. hirsutum. As support, GmMPK4 plays crucial role in regulating plant growth and development in soybean [45]. SlMPK3 is prone to exhibit markedly high expression levels in stamens [42]. Gibberellins and cytokinins activate PsMPK3, which is critical for fruit set [46]. Interestingly, GhMPK1/GhMPK5 and GhMPK44/GhMPK19 in groups A and D, respectively, showed relatively high expression in nearly all organs. These four genes might play central roles in regulating plant development, growth, reproduction, and yield in G. hirsutum.

Previous studies have shown that numerous MAPK genes are implicated in the biotic and abiotic stress-responsive mechanisms of plants [44]. For instance, AtMPK6, AtMPK4, and AtMPK3 in Arabidopsis were found to modulate responses to pathogens, abiotic stresses, and oxidative stress [47]. OsMAPK3 is reported as a negative regulator of biotic signaling in rice [48]. GbMPK3 in cotton is activated by drought stimuli to promote oxidative stress tolerance [49]. AtMPK9 and AtMPK12 function in SA-induced stomatal closure [50]. Here, the expression pattern of GhMPKs under heat, salt, and drought stress conditions unveiled that most of them are crucial for cotton response to abiotic stresses. GhMPK24, GhMPK1, GhMPK5, GhMPK23, GhMPK2, GhMPK39, GhMPK3, and GhMPK8 showed increasing expression levels under both abiotic stresses. Meanwhile, GhMPK10, GhMPK17, and GhMPK27 had high and almost stable expression under both abiotic stresses. These results suggest that these genes may play central regulatory tasks in response to abiotic stresses in cotton. As support, previous research demonstrated that GhMPK2 is involved in processes regulating drought and oxidative stress tolerance [30]. These genes are potential candidates for the molecular-assisted breeding of multi-abiotic stress-tolerant G. hirsutum varieties. Functional investigations on these genes are required to dissect and understand the MAPK signaling network in response to environmental stimuli.

GhMPK10 can confer multi-abiotic stress tolerance by modulating the MAPK signaling cascade

Among the GhMPKs, GhMPK10 was selected for functional validation. Overexpression of GhMPK10 in transgenic Arabidopsis enhanced salinity and osmotic stress tolerance. Silencing of GhMPK10 amplified the sensitivity of G. hirsutum plants to drought stress. These results confirm that GhMPK10 plays critical roles in salinity, osmotic, and drought stress tolerance of G. hirsutum. Moreover, they show that GhMPK10 can be targeted to improve multi-stress tolerance. Silencing of GhMPK10 significantly altered the drought stress-responsive mechanisms in cotton plants. 4,725 DEGs between WT_D vs. VIGS_D were identified, and mainly the Environmental Information Processing, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, Plant hormone signal transduction, Signal transduction, and Cytochrome P450 pathways were perturbated. Many key genes involved in ABA and ethylene signaling pathways exhibited significant expression changes. These findings indicate that GhMPK10 may play a central role in MAPK signaling that regulates abiotic stress responses. Gene network analysis revealed that GhMPK10 interacts with eight key abiotic stress-responsive genes, including two MKK4 (GhMAPKK9 and GhMAPKK10), two MKK3 (GhMAPKK8 and GhMAPKK2), two GhPTP1, one GhACS1, and one GhSPCH. SPEECHLESS (SPCH) is a bHLH (basic helix-loop-helix) transcription factor that integrates endogenous signals and acts as an acceptor of environmental and hormonal signals to control stomatal patterning and density during development [51]. GhSPCH expression was significantly down-regulated in GhMPK10-silenced plants, suggesting that GhMPK10 might be an activator or positive regulator of GhSPCH’s expression under abiotic stress conditions. Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) regulate the redox status of enzymes, thus creating a molecular switch to control stress-induced signaling pathways, including MAPK signaling and other signalings [41, 52]. ACS (1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase) is the rate-limiting enzyme for ethylene (master regulator of abiotic stress tolerance) biosynthesis [38, 53]. The expression of GhACS1 (Gh_D12G2746) was significantly induced in VIGS plants under normal and drought conditions, confirming that silencing of GhMPK10 enhanced the sensitivity to drought, leading to stimulated ethylene synthesis. MKKs are activators of MAPKs and are involved in the complex cross-talk between abiotic stresses and hormones [40]. MKK3 plays a crucial role in plant responses to abiotic stress, acting as a key integrator in stress signaling pathways. For instance, MKK3 serves as a central hub in stress-responsive MAPK cascades, integrating multiple signals to coordinate plant adaptation to abiotic stress conditions [39]. MKK4 activates downstream MAPK3/6 via phosphorylation [40], which promotes the expression of ROS-scavenging enzymes (such as SOD and APX) and thereby alleviates oxidative damage. In cotton, the overexpression of MKK4 enhances ABA-induced stomatal closure, reducing water loss [41]. The MKK4-MPK3/6 module regulates the expression of proline biosynthesis genes (such as P5CS), leading to the accumulation of osmoprotectants and the maintenance of cell turgor [38]. The findings support the pivotal roles of GhMPK10 in regulating abiotic stress responses. Further functional studies on GhMPK10 and its interacting genes are needed for an in-depth understanding of its regulatory functions.

Conclusion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive genome-wide identification and characterization of the MaPK gene family in Gossypium hirsutum. A total of 56 GhMPK genes were identified and systematically analyzed in terms of their chromosomal distribution, subcellular localization, gene structure, duplication events, exon-intron organization, phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs, and expression profiles across various tissues and under abiotic stress conditions (drought, heat, and salinity).

Expression analysis showed that GhMPKs are essential for diverse developmental and abiotic stress-responsive processes in G. hirsutum. Many GhMPKs, such as GhMPK47, GhMPK48, GhMPK10, GhMPK24, GhMPK1, GhMPK5, GhMPK23, GhMPK2, GhMPK39, GhMPK3, GhMPK8, etc., were highly induced under both drought, heat, and salinity stress conditions.

Functional validation of GhMPK10 confirmed that it is a key candidate gene for improving multi-stress tolerance. GhMPK10 may interact with eight key abiotic stress-responsive genes, including two MKK4 (GhMAPKK9 and GhMAPKK10), two MKK3 (GhMAPKK8 and GhMAPKK2), two GhPTP1, one GhACS1, and one GhSPCH to promote abiotic stress tolerance in G. hirsutum. Silencing of GhMPK10 significantly altered the MAPK signaling pathway-plant, Environmental Information Processing, Plant hormone signal transduction, and Cytochrome P450 pathways. Our findings will trigger the functional analysis of GhMPKs for their use in developing stress-tolerant cotton varieties. Moreover, they establish a foundation for functional dissection of the MAPK signaling network in cotton or other plant

Materials and Methods

Identification of MAPK gene family in G. hirsutum

The completed protein and genome sequences of G. hirsutum were downloaded from CottonGen (https://www.cottongen.org/) and used to create a local database. Additionally, the MAPK proteins from Arabidopsis and rice were downloaded from TAIR (http://www.arabidopsis.org/browse/genefamily/MAPK.jsp) and RGAP (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/), respectively, and used as queries in a BLASTP search. Candidate MAPKs in G. hirsutum whose proteins had a 50% identity were selected. The candidate GhMPKs were confirmed using the PROSITE program (http://prosite.expasy.org/) to check the presence of PS01351 (MAPK domain), PS00107 (ATP-binding domain), and PS50011 (protein kinase (PK) domain). All redundant proteins were discarded. The MEME tool (http://meme.nbcr.net/meme/) was used for precision domain analysis. The ExPASy server (http://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/) was exploited to reveal the molecular weight and theoretical isoelectric point of GhMPK proteins. The subcellular localization was assessed using the CELLO v2.5 tool (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/).

Phylogenetic analysis and identification of gene duplication

Multiple sequence alignment of GhMPK proteins, together with MAPKs from Arabidopsis, rice, and poplar, was performed using ClustalX2.0. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA v7.0 with the neighbor-joining method and 1,000 bootstrap replications. Conserved motifs were revealed using the MEME tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme). Three criteria were applied to investigate gene duplication events: (i) the aligned region had an identity > 80%; (ii) the alignment length covered > 80% of the longer gene; and (iii) only one duplication event was counted for closely linked genes [54, 55].

Chromosomal locations, gene structure, and expression analysis of GhMPKs

Chromosomal positions of GhMPKs were determined via local BLASTN and visualized using MapInspect. Gene structures were analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS; http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/). The transcriptome data of various tissues and under abiotic stresses were downloaded from the NCBI (accession number PRJNA248163) [56]. Expression levels (FPKM) were presented as heat maps of hierarchical clustering using HmeI1.0 [57].

Functional validation of GhMPK10 in response to abiotic stresses

The GhMPK10 overexpression vector (pBI121-GhMPK10) and virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector (PYL156-GhMPK10) were synthesized and sequence-verified by GenScript Biotech (Nanjing, China). Full-length GhMPK10 (Gh_A03G0759) cDNA was amplified using primers listed in Table S3. PCR products were visualized (Fig. S5). GhMPK10 was overexpressed in Arabidopsis to verify its functions in drought and salt stress response. WT (wild-type) and transgenic Arabidopsis seeds were disinfected and seeded in 1/2 MS medium, supplemented with 150 mM NaCl (salt stress) or 250 mM Mannitol (drought stress). After vernalization in a dark environment at 4 °C for 2 days, plants were transferred to an incubator for culture. Cotyledon greening rate was assessed after 7 days. Root length was measured by the ImageJ software.

For VIGS, the GV3101 strains harboring PYL156 (WT), PYL156:GhMPK10 (VIGS), pYL192 (helper vector), and PYL156:PDS (positive control), were cultured to an OD600 of 1.2 to 1.5. After incubation, 7-day-old seedlings were infiltrated with the mixed bacterial suspension. Subsequently, the seedlings were kept in the dark overnight and then transferred to a growth chamber (16-hour light/8-hour dark at 25 °C). The VIGS experiment was deemed successful (effective gene silencing) when plants infiltrated with PYL156:PDS construct exhibited an albino phenotype. The silencing efficiency of the GhMPK10 was further confirmed through RT-qPCR analysis. The leaves of WT and VIGS plants were taken, and the expression of GhMPK10 in plants was detected by fluorescence quantitative experiment, and the silencing efficiency of plants was calculated. The phenotypes of WT and VIGS seedlings were observed and recorded after 18% PEG treatment for 4 h. The physiological parameters, including peroxidase (POD, KTB1150), superoxide dismutase (SOD, KTB1030), malondialdehyde (MDA, KTB1050), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, KTB1041), were measured using kits provided by Abbkine, Inc.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA extraction from leaves was achieved using the FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quality and quantity assessment of the RNA samples was carried out using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and Nanodrop-2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Sequencing was performed at BGI (Shenzhen, China) following standard protocols [58, 59]. DEGs detection was performed with the DESeq2 software [60], with criteria of FDR (false discovery rate) ˂ 0.05 and |Log2fold change| ≥ 2. Gene annotation information is obtained on eggnog-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) [61]. KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and GO (Gene Ontology analysis) enrichment were performed by TBtools-Ⅱ(v2.152) [62]. The top 20 enriched KEGG pathways and GO categories were visualized using the ChiPlot web tool (https://www.chiplot.online/). Transcriptome data of GhMPK10-silenced and wild-type plants under drought stress have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under BioProject PRJNA1267982.

RT-Qpcr analysis

First-strand cDNAs were synthesized using the TransScript® First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). RT-qPCR was conducted on LightCycler96 instrument (Roche, Switzerland) with SYBR Green Mix (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The internal reference was the cotton GhActin (AY305733) gene. The specific primers for each analyzed gene are presented in Table S3. Relative expression levels were computed by the 2−ΔΔCt method.
