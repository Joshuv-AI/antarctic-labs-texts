Impact of multicultural doula support on pregnant migrant women’s healthcare service utilisation in Norway: a multi-centre case–control study
Authors: Hanna Oommen, Linda Reme Sagedal, Jennifer Jean Infanti, Ulrika Byrskog, Marit Stene Severinsen, Mirjam Lukasse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNewly arrived migrant women face barriers to accessing childbirth-related care, delaying necessary treatment and negatively affecting birth outcomes. Multicultural doula (MCD) support may help women overcome these barriers. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of MCD support on the use of hospital healthcare services during pregnancy and postpartum.MethodsThis case-control study included 339 MCD-supported women and 339 controls (women who did not receive MCD support) from all nine Norwegian hospitals implementing the MCD programme (2018–2023). Cases met MCD programme’s inclusion criteria, whereas controls met similar criteria but did not receive MCD support. Data from hospital records were analysed using binary logistic regression with crude and adjusted associations reported. Adjustments included parity, age, education, employment, maternal region of birth, reason for migration, delivery site in Norway, acute consultations, labour induction, preterm birth, mode of delivery, estimated blood loss ≥1000 mL and admission for newborn to intensive care.ResultsWomen in the MCD case and control groups were similar in age, marital status, parity and interpreter needs (95% vs 96%). Compared with controls, MCD-supported women more often had limited education (63% vs 42%, p<0.01) or were asylum seekers (82% vs 66%, p<0.01). They were more likely to consult for reduced foetal movement (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.86, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.84), other pregnancy-related concerns (aOR 1.46, CI 1.05 to 2.03), telephone before admission (aOR 3.21, 95% CI 2.26 to 4.58), had fewer unplanned out-of-hospital births (OR 0.06, CI 0.01 to 0.43) and reduced ambulance use (aOR 0.32, CI 0.18 to 0.58) and had fewer postpartum stays ≥4 days (aOR 0.58, CI 0.39 to 0.87), with a greater proportion discharged within 2 days after delivery (aOR 1.87, CI 1.26–2.78).ConclusionMCD support was associated with increased antenatal hospital consultations and timely labour admission among migrant women in vulnerable circumstances. This assistance may improve the childbirth experience and positively influence migrant maternal health, birth outcomes and children’s well-being.

Introduction

Europe has experienced a significant surge in migration since 2015, including an increased number of women of childbearing age, many of whom are refugees or asylum seekers.13 By early 2023, 16% of resident women in Norway (428,100) were migrants, mainly from Europe, Asia and Africa.4 Over a quarter had been in Norway for ≤5 years, and 8.5% were resettlement refugees, often with lower education levels than other migrants and the general population.4 This has introduced new challenges for healthcare systems, particularly in addressing the needs of newly arrived migrant women. Many women face significant barriers to healthcare access—such as unfamiliar systems, communication challenges, systemic biases and socio-economic or migration-related stressors—heightening their risk of adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes.59

The organisation of routine antenatal care also varies significantly across different countries, further shaping the challenges migrant women face. In Norway, the present study context, antenatal care is free of charge to all, including asylum seekers, and primarily delivered by community midwives and general practitioners in community-based healthcare settings, except for two routine ultrasounds at hospitals at 12 and 18 weeks. However, women with complications or needing specialised care are referred to hospitals for urgent consultations or scheduled appointments. Most births, including uncomplicated births by low-risk women, occur in hospitals with the assistance of midwives or obstetricians. Whenever possible, women are advised to phone the hospital before arriving in labour or if experiencing worrisome signs or symptoms. This allows midwives to guide them on appropriate timing and need for admission.

For recent migrants, multiple barriers hinder access to childbirth-related care, including navigating unfamiliar healthcare systems, communication difficulties often exacerbated by isolation, immigration stress, disrupted social networks, low socioeconomic status and mental health challenges.5 10 11 Additional obstacles, such as negative professional attitudes and discrimination within the healthcare system, further contribute to reduced service utilisation.5 8 10 Practical issues, such as limited health literacy, remote asylum centre locations, inadequate transportation, complex bureaucratic processes and insufficient interpreting services, exacerbate these challenges, especially in emergencies where timely, clear and effective communication is critical.1214 These challenges can be understood through the migration three-delay framework, which identifies three critical stages: (1) deciding to seek care, (2) identifying and reaching medical facilities, and (3) receiving adequate and appropriate treatment.15

Given these obstacles, many migrant women also initiate prenatal care late and attend fewer prenatal visits, further increasing their risk of complications.1618 The subgroups who are in the most vulnerable circumstances include asylum seekers, undocumented migrants and women from conflict zones, who face compounded pre-migration and post-migration stressors, such as violence and trauma.101921

These interconnected challenges underscore the pressing need for targeted interventions to improve maternal and infant health outcomes, particularly among migrants in the most vulnerable circumstances. Cultural factors significantly influence the use of maternity care services.22 Effective transcultural care, healthcare that respects and integrates patients’ cultural beliefs, values and practices, is essential, with communication playing a central role in ensuring culturally appropriate, patient-centred care.23

Despite Norway’s high-quality maternity care, many migrant women report barriers to access and experiences of suboptimal care.2426 To address these issues, nine hospitals implemented the multicultural doula (MCD) support programme alongside standard care between 2018 and 2021. The MCD programme in Norway included 120 doulas speaking 23 languages by the close of 2023. MCDs are laywomen from migrant communities trained to provide cultural and language assistance during labour and the perinatal period.2729 They offer physical, emotional and informational support, helping women navigate the maternity care system, advocating for their active involvement and promoting trust in healthcare providers.27 30 31 MCDs also raise awareness about danger signs and encourage early help-seeking, thereby enhancing cultural sensitivity and continuity of care.29 31 32

Although doula support has increasingly shown positive effects on perinatal outcomes and childbirth experiences, likely through mechanisms such as fostering continuity, reducing turnover-related stress and providing culturally and linguistically familiar support,313337 its impact on the use of hospital-based childbirth services remains largely unexamined. Thus, this study aimed to explore the effect of MCD support on healthcare utilisation, specifically acute consultations, pre-admission phone calls, childbirth locations, transportation methods, length of hospital stay, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions and post-discharge hospital care.

Methods

Design

In this multi-centre case–control study, case women received MCD support in addition to standard care, whereas controls received standard care only.

MCD support and standard care

The MCD programme, launched in Norway in 2018, provides culturally adapted physical, emotional and informational support during pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum. In addition to bridging language and cultural gaps, MCDs help newcomer women navigate the healthcare system, strengthen their trust towards healthcare providers and act as ‘bridge-builders’ by facilitating communication and understanding during childbirth. The programme reflects a system-level theory of change for refugee newcomers, whereby community-based support operates as a mechanism for strengthening trust, health literacy and social connections. These proximal outcomes, in turn, contribute to longer-term goals of integration and improved health outcomes.38 MCDs must have given birth in Norway and completed a 56-hour training course (online supplemental file 1). The criteria for receiving MCD support are having lived in Norway for ≤5 years, a limited social network and lacking proficiency in Norwegian. In areas where the MCD programme is established, midwives identify women as eligible through antenatal care. Eligible women are informed and offered participation in the programme. The programme particularly targets women originating from low-resource countries and conflict-affected regions. However, exceptions are made occasionally for women with a stay longer than 5 years when the other two criteria weigh heavily, similarly for women from high-income countries. Furthermore, the offer of participation is dependent on the availability of MCDs with the same language and cultural background. Expectant mothers are paired with a primary and a reserve MCD, who share their language and cultural background, around the 30th week of gestation. Face-to-face meetings (once or two times) are held at locations chosen by the mother, with additional contact by phone. Mothers can also request that MCDs accompany them to healthcare appointments. While this is permitted, it is not generally recommended because MCDs are not professional interpreters. In all cases, MCDs were present throughout labour and delivery. MCDs are allotted 24 paid contact hours per mother-to-be, increased to 30 hours in 2023. Ideally, half of these hours are dedicated to labour support. On average, MCDs provide 10 hours of support during pregnancy and postpartum and 10 hours during childbirth.37

Standard care includes support from healthcare workers (midwives and physicians), which aims to be individualised. The services of an interpreter are to be provided by telephone when needed, before, during and after childbirth, with urgent requests fulfilled within 20 min.

Setting, population and data collection

Cases and controls were drawn from the nine hospitals implementing the MCD programme: Oslo University Hospital (comprising Ullevål and Rikshospitalet), Akershus University Hospital, St. Olavs Hospital, Haukeland University Hospital, Stavanger University Hospital, Sørlandet Hospital and Vestre Viken Hospital (Drammen and Bærum Hospitals). These institutions serve urban and rural areas across four of Norway’s five regions.

Data were collected from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2023. Women were classified as having received MCD support if they were provided MCD support during pregnancy, childbirth and the postpartum period. For each woman who received MCD support, a control was selected from the same hospital and time period. Controls were matched based on recent migration to Norway (lived in Norway≤5 years), limited language proficiency skills and origin from low-resource or conflict-affected countries. These were identified through temporary ID numbers indicating recent arrival and/or documented interpreter use during childbirth. A flowchart of excluded participants (n=8) is provided in online supplemental file 2).

The outcome variables were selected based on previous literature or their clinical relevance.27 Data extracted from hospital records included demographic information (such as age, marital status, education level, country of origin, partner’s country of origin, duration of residency since immigration, employment status, need for an interpreter and reason for migration), clinical data (including parity, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), presence of female genital cutting, childbirth location and presence of a support person other than the MCD) and healthcare utilisation (including ambulance use, pre-admission consultations and number of phone calls, length of hospital stay and specific data on post-discharge hospital care, such as the number of out-patient consultations and re-admissions within 6 weeks).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using frequencies (number or n) and proportions (percent or %). Differences between groups were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test. Binary logistic regression was used to examine associations between group membership and healthcare service use outcomes, calculating crude and adjusted ORs with 95% CIs.

Participants’ countries of origin were categorised into three groups based on United Nations standard country codes: 1) Sub-Saharan Africa, 2) the Middle East, Central Asia and South Asia and 3) Eastern Europe.39

Co-variates with significant associations in univariate analyses were further evaluated in multivariable models. Socio-demographic characteristics and clinically relevant variables were included. Depending on the outcome and the number of events for this outcome, we developed 2−3 models. For outcomes with <10 events for either cases or controls, multivariable analyses were not performed, as these models may yield unstable and unreliable estimates, even when p values are statistically significant. In line with the commonly cited rule of thumb for logistic regression, a minimum of 10 events was required for each adjustment.40

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 3.6 (R Core Team, 2020). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Migrant women from diverse backgrounds (including Syria, Afghanistan, Ethiopia, Eritrea, Turkey, Somalia, Ukraine, Kurdistan, Russia, Kazakhstan, Sudan and Pakistan) contributed to the study through group discussions held in Oslo (21 May 2022) and Kristiansand (7 September 2022), before the start. The purpose of these meetings was to ensure cultural sensitivity, shape terminology and strengthen the relevance of the planned research. Study results will be shared with participants through meetings and community networks.

Results

The study included 678 pregnant women, with equal numbers in the MCD-supported (339) and standard-care (339) groups. Participants represented 52 countries and 36 different languages (detailed language data are available in online supplemental file 3). The socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of both the MCD-supported and control groups were largely similar (table 1). However, women in the MCD-supported group were more likely to have lower levels of education (p=0.005) and be unemployed (p=0.012). Additionally, 82% of women in the MCD-supported group were asylum seekers or refugees, compared with 66% in the control group (p=0.001).

Discussion

In this multi-centre case–control study among newly arrived migrant women in Norway, women who received MCD support had significantly higher rates of hospital consultations for reduced foetal movement and other pregnancy-related concerns. Additionally, MCD support was associated with substantially reduced prevalence of unplanned deliveries outside hospitals, use of ambulance transport to reach the hospital during labour and shortened postpartum stays. These results highlight MCD support as an intervention that may improve timely access to essential maternity care while potentially reducing strain on emergency resources.

Emergency hospital consultations

Migrant women face well-documented increased risks for pregnancy and childbirth complications. Limited knowledge about the healthcare system and communication difficulties can exacerbate these challenges, often resulting in inadequate access to and use of healthcare services.9 11 In Norway, pregnant women are encouraged by midwives and doctors to monitor foetal movements and contact the hospital if they notice reduced movement, a policy associated with better neonatal outcomes.41 Our findings, which show increased acute hospital consultations, particularly for concerns such as reduced foetal movement among women who received MCD support, indicate that this intervention may contribute to improved neonatal outcomes for this group.

Increased acute consultations by women receiving MCD support may partly reflect the unmet need for interpretation services among migrants, as some women may not have understood the importance of contacting the hospital for decreased foetal movement or other concerning symptoms or signs. The WHO has emphasised the importance of reducing communication barriers for European migrants through strategies such as interpretation, translation, cultural mediation and healthcare worker education.42 Despite these recommendations and Norwegian guidelines promoting greater use of interpreter services, a recent study highlighted persistent gaps in the availability of professional interpreters for pregnant migrant women.11 We postulate that the presence of a culturally aligned doula, who builds trust with the pregnant migrant woman, likely increases her confidence and comfort in seeking hospital care when needed, addressing some of these communication challenges.43

Health literacy is often discussed as a barrier for migrants; the key issue may not be a general lack of health knowledge but a mismatch between existing health literacy skills and the demands of the host country’s healthcare system. Studies show that immigrants may struggle to navigate unfamiliar healthcare settings because of differences in terminology, processes and expectations.44 45 For example, a study from Denmark, whose healthcare system is similar to Norway’s, found that migrant women with diverse origins were significantly less aware of how to manage pregnancy complications compared with the host population.46

In Norway, community midwives typically discuss common danger signs during prenatal visits; however, additional, crucial information is provided through pregnancy courses at maternal and child health centres or hospitals, where partners are also encouraged to participate. However, fewer than one in five migrant women in Norway attend these courses, which are primarily conducted in Norwegian.11 A systematic review of migrant women’s experiences across Europe revealed that many available resources are inaccessible because of language or format barriers.7 A study on healthcare access for refugee women with limited literacy further showed how these barriers hinder women’s ability to obtain essential health information.47 Our findings suggest that MCD support may help bridge this gap by providing culturally and linguistically appropriate guidance, empowering women to seek care when necessary.

Childbirth location

Unplanned out-of-hospital births are associated with a significantly higher risk of adverse perinatal outcomes and perinatal mortality.48 49 In our data, migrants without MCD support appeared to more frequently have out-of-hospital births than not only women receiving MCD care but also the general population of childbearing women in Norway in 2023 (5.1% vs 0.6%).50 Unplanned delivery outside the hospital is a rare event in Norway, with a well-documented national rate of 0.6%–0.7% annually according to the Medical Birth Registry.50 The rate among women receiving MCD support (0.3%) is consistent with this national level, whereas the markedly higher rate observed among migrants without MCD support stands out as substantially above both the national average and their counterparts receiving MCD support. Although small numbers prevented multivariable analysis, this finding merits discussion and further research. In line with a French study linking unplanned out-of-hospital deliveries to limited prenatal care, a situation often affecting migrants, our findings indicate that barriers such as language, cultural differences and limited knowledge of the healthcare system may contribute to this outcome.

The lower proportion of unplanned out-of-hospital births among women with MCD support further indicates that MCDs may help address these challenges by fostering trust, improving communication and supporting timely care-seeking, in line with the WHO’s call for respectful maternity care.32 51

Transportation methods

Transportation to the hospital during childbirth can be a significant challenge for migrant women and is a topic thoroughly discussed during the MCD training course. We found that migrants without MCD support were more likely to use ambulances as transport to the hospital during childbirth, whereas those who received MCD support were more likely to use public transport or taxis. A systematic review reported that migrants are generally more likely to rely on emergency services than on regular public services.52 Additionally, variations in the quality and availability of professional interpreters may contribute to the inappropriate use of ambulance services.13

Migrants may also be unfamiliar with the specific role of ambulance services, particularly if they come from countries where the term ‘ambulance’ refers more broadly to any vehicle capable of transporting individuals to the hospital. These factors may explain the higher reliance on ambulances among the group in this study that did not receive MCD support. Furthermore, the shift to digital tools has introduced new challenges for migrants.53 For example, in Norway, digitalisation can affect migrants’ ability to purchase local transportation tickets, potentially complicating their use of alternative transportation options.

Postpartum stay

Unlike our findings, which showed that women receiving MCD support were more often discharged within 2 days, whereas women in the control group more often had hospital stays >4 days, a Swedish register-based cohort study reported that nulliparous migrant women supported by community-based bilingual doulas had higher odds of a postpartum hospital stay >48 hours than those without such support.27 The contrasting results may reflect differences in healthcare organisation, such as discharge practices and availability of follow-up care, as well as differences in study populations and in the models of doula support. In our cohort, the shorter hospital stays may partly be explained by the better breastfeeding outcomes observed among women supported by MCDs before discharge and by the sense of safety fostered by knowing that support would continue beyond hospital discharge.37 Together, these findings highlight the need for further research to clarify the factors influencing postpartum hospital stays among migrants with and without MCD.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its nationwide coverage, which encompasses both urban and rural areas and includes all hospitals implementing the MCD programme across most regions. This broad scope minimises selection bias and improves the generalisability of the findings. With >74% of participants being refugees or asylum seekers, the study provides valuable insights into a group often underrepresented in research because of language barriers and other accessibility issues.

Matching the MCD and non-MCD groups was a challenge; given the well-established nature of the MCD programme, it was difficult to find sufficient candidates for the control group at each site. In addition to the factors the groups were matched on, our results indicate that the groups were broadly comparable in key factors, including age, marital status, parity, time since immigration, interpreter needs, partner’s country of origin and delivery site. The control group tended towards higher socio-economic status, suggesting that the MCD group had no inherent advantage beyond the intervention itself. The MCD-supported group likely had a greater need for this assistance, as women with higher socio-economic status generally find it easier to navigate a new environment.54 The co-variates in which the groups differed significantly were controlled for in the multivariable analyses. The intervention group included all women who received MCD support during the study period, increasing the generalisability of our findings and reducing the risk of bias. The standardisation of MCD training further strengthens the reliability of our results, as it ensures consistency in the support provided across hospitals.

A limitation of this study is the retrospective observational design. The use of register data restricts the range of available variables and relies on accurate documentation, which may omit relevant factors or nuances (eg, variations in how MCD support was provided across cases). Additionally, although register data allowed for control of key sociodemographic factors, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as unmeasured variables such as social support networks, individual health literacy and pre-existing conditions may have influenced the outcomes. In addition, this study lacked detailed data on the amount of antenatal support provided, whereas hours during childbirth were systematically documented. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution.

Although a randomised controlled trial would have provided stronger causal evidence, it was deemed impractical and ethically inappropriate in this context because of the challenges of engaging women unfamiliar with clinical trials and the risks of withholding support from migrant women in vulnerable circumstances. A larger intervention group would have increased the statistical power of the study, potentially allowing more detailed analysis of rare outcomes, but would also have required extending the observation period, which in turn could have introduced additional sources of variability and potential confounding factors.

Conclusions

This study shows that MCD support is significantly linked with improved use of health services among migrant women, potentially leading to better health outcomes for both mothers and infants. It also underscores the critical role of MCD support in addressing systemic barriers to healthcare access for this population in vulnerable circumstances. The significantly reduced risk of unplanned out-of-hospital deliveries associated with MCD support observed in this study suggests that MCD support improves communication between patients and healthcare providers and enables timely and appropriate responses to emerging health issues.

Future studies should explore the long-term effect of MCD use on maternal and neonatal outcomes, as well as the cost-effectiveness of implementing MCDs at increased scale. Additionally, research into patient and provider experiences with MCDs could provide valuable insights into optimising their design and integration into prenatal care.
