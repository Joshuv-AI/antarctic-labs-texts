Social Determinants of Health and Long‑Term Joblessness Among Severe COVID‑19 Survivors: Secondary Analysis of a Nationwide 2‑Year Prospective Cohort (COVID-19 Recovery Study II)
Authors: Akira Kawauchi, Mitsunobu Nakamura, Shuhei Maruyama, Shinichiro Morioka, Katsuji Teruya, Takeshi Nishida, Toshiyuki Harada, Hideki Yoshida, Satoshi Miike, Hideaki Kato, Junji Hatakeyama, Shigeki Fujitani, Tomohiro Asahi, Kensuke Nakamura, Yuichi Sato, Taku Oshima, Futoshi Nagashima, Kohei Ota, Tatsuya Fuchigami, Nobuyuki Nosaka, Hiroshi Kamijo, Takeshi Hattori, Hayato Taniguchi, Katrina Hauschildt, Snigdha Jain, Arisa Iba, Miyuki Hori, Yoko Muto, Mariko Hosozawa, Hiroyasu Iso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:Return-to-work varies among previously employed patients after critical illness. Social determinants of health (SDH) may play a role in this variation. We compared patient characteristics and explored the association between SDH and long-term joblessness among patients who experienced severe COVID-19.DESIGN:A secondary analysis of a prospective nationwide database.SETTINGS:The COVID-19 Recovery Study II (CORES II) study, a nationwide prospective observational study in Japan that examined long-term symptoms and social status over two years following hospitalization.PATIENTS:We included adults hospitalized with severe COVID-19 between April 1 and September 30, 2021, who were employed before hospitalization. SDH included education history and pre-hospitalization employment status.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:The primary outcome was joblessness at the first- and second-year follow-ups. We used generalized estimating equations for the main analysis to examine longitudinal associations between SDH and joblessness, with the SDH variables entered into the model as separate categorical variables.Of 3297 hospitalized patients, 337 met inclusion criteria. At the first-year follow-up, 275 had returned to work, whereas 62 (18.4%) remained jobless. At the second-year follow-up, 242 (82.3 %) had returned to work, and 52 (17.7%) remained jobless. Patients who were jobless at the first-year follow-up showed more persistent symptoms and greater physical and mental impairment compared with those who returned to work. Among SDH factors, non-permanent employment was associated with joblessness over the 2-year follow-up period (odds ratio 2.35; 95% CI [1.26–4.40]) compared with permanent employment. Education history was not associated with joblessness at the first- or second-year follow-ups.CONCLUSIONS:Non-permanent employment was associated with joblessness during the pandemic after hospitalization for severe COVID-19. Pre-hospitalization employment status may be relevant to long-term social recovery after critical illness.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the association between social determinants of health (SDH) and long-term joblessness among severe COVID-19 survivors?

Findings: Among 337 survivors, approximately 18% remained jobless after 2 years. Pre-hospitalization non-permanent employment was significantly associated with persistent joblessness. Jobless individuals initially reported more physical/mental symptoms and lower quality of life.

Meaning: Non-permanent employment was associated with joblessness during the pandemic after hospitalization for severe COVID-19. Patients with more vulnerable pre-hospitalization employment status may warrant closer follow-up during recovery.

Survivors of critical illness often experience long-term morbidity resulting from persistent physical, mental, and cognitive impairments following discharge from the ICU, termed post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) (1, 2). These impairments reduce quality of life (QOL) for ICU survivors and make it challenging for them to return to their pre-illness life. Beyond PICS-related impairments, social reintegration, including returning to work, is important for previously employed patients following critical illness (3–6). Joblessness after discharge has been reported in approximately one-third to two-thirds of patients across various types of critical illness (5). A recent systematic review found that current evidence is limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up periods, making it challenging to describe the specific characteristics that distinguish those who returned to work from those who remained jobless (7, 8). A 6-month follow-up study of 212 survivors of severe COVID-19 showed approximately 10% remained jobless at follow-up (7). The unprecedented scale and impact of the COVID-19 pandemic underscored the need for further investigation into the long-term effects of acute respiratory illness, such as severe COVID-19, on employment.

Social determinants of health (SDH), such as socioeconomic status, living environment, and social support, are key factors influencing physical, mental, and cognitive recovery after hospital discharge from critical care (9–13). Although the role of SDH has been explored in the context of COVID-19 recovery (14), its impact on return to work among survivors of severe COVID-19 remains inadequately understood. Understanding the impact of SDH will be valuable for developing more effective interventions in future pandemics and for identifying high-risk populations who may benefit from long-term follow-up and social support after hospitalization for critical illness.

This study had two aims: first, to describe the clinical and social characteristics of patients who returned to work and those who remained jobless after severe COVID-19; and second, to examine the association between SDH factors and long-term joblessness.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Collection

This study was a secondary analysis of the COVID-19 Recovery Study II (CORES II) (15), a nationwide, multicenter, prospective observational study in Japan. CORES II collected in-hospital data on patient backgrounds, treatments, and outcomes, along with follow-up questionnaire responses gathered after hospital discharge from hospitalized adult patients (20 yr or older) with COVID-19. The follow-up questionnaire was developed by the CORES II study team and assessed detailed persistent symptoms, social status, and physical and mental health disorders. Before the first-year follow-up, patients were contacted by telephone on multiple occasions to confirm their willingness to participate in the follow-up study. Questionnaires were then mailed to patients who agreed, and responses were accepted by mail. Return of the first-year questionnaire was regarded as enrollment in the CORES II follow-up study. Completion of the questionnaire generally required approximately 10–20 minutes. Responses were obtained primarily from patients themselves, although proxy or family-assisted responses were permitted with patient consent when needed. Participants received a 1000-yen QUO card for each completed questionnaire. Mortality after discharge was not systematically ascertained beyond the questionnaire process, and reasons for nonresponse at subsequent follow-up were not available in the database. All procedures involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards of the Japan Institute for Health Security (approval number: NCGM-S-004471; approval date: April 26, 2022) and approved by the ethics committees of all participating institutions. The study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture, a secure, web-based data capture application hosted at the JCRAC data center of the Japan Institute for Health Security (16).

As a secondary analysis of CORES II, this study was approved by the ethics committee of Maebashi Red Cross Hospital (approval number: 2023-51; approval date: December 28, 2023). COVID-19 infection was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction or equivalent tests. Patients hospitalized with COVID-19 at participating hospitals between April 1 and September 30, 2021, received follow-up questionnaires administered by CORES II. Patients or proxies completed and returned questionnaires via mail or web form. First-year follow-up was distributed between August and September 2022; second-year follow-up between August and November 2023. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained upon return of the first-year questionnaire.

Patient Selection

Patients in the CORES II registry were assessed for eligibility to be included in this study. This study included previously employed patients who had been admitted to the ICU due to severe COVID-19 and who responded to the first-year follow-up questionnaire. Severe COVID-19 was defined as requiring a high-flow nasal cannula, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). We excluded patients who were not admitted to the ICU, those who received only oxygen supplementation, and those who were jobless before hospitalization, because this study focused on return-to-work outcomes among previously employed patients. The present analysis was restricted to patients who responded to the first-year follow-up questionnaire. Including patients who were unemployed before hospitalization would have reflected labor-market entry or reentry rather than return to work after severe COVID-19.

Patient and Illness Characteristics

From the CORES II registry, we collected patient information, including background characteristics and treatments; age, sex, body mass index, history of receiving COVID-19 vaccination, frailty assessed by the Clinical Frailty Scale (17), multimorbidity (defined as having ≥ 3 self-reported chronic conditions, of a possible 13) (18, 19), EuroQol-5D-5 (EQ-5D-5L) utility score before hospitalization (20, 21), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score at ICU admission, oxygenation therapy (high-flow nasal cannula/noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, IMV, ECMO), prone positioning therapy, ICU mobility scale at day 7 (22), steroid therapy, mechanical ventilation duration, ECMO duration, ICU duration, and total hospital stay duration. Baseline EQ-5D-5L utility score and visual analog scale (VAS) before hospitalization were retrospectively reported on the first-year follow-up questionnaire.

We also collected self-reported long-term symptoms (Supplemental material, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B654): awareness of recovery (23), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale (24), performance status (25), the number of self-reported long COVID-19 symptoms (23), EQ-5D-5L (utility score and VAS), impaired QOL, and anxiety and depression scores from the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A and HADS-D, respectively) (26). Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was assessed as greater than or equal to 2 psychiatric symptoms on the brief PTSD Scale (27). Loneliness was assessed using the three-item University of California, Los Angele Loneliness Scale (Japanese version) (28). Impaired QOL was defined as an EQ-5D-5L utility score below 0.80 (29), and psychiatric symptoms of anxiety and depression as HADS-A/HADS-D scores greater than or equal to 8 (30).

SDH Factors

SDH factors were selected based on previous literature and data availability (10, 19, 31). We focused on employment status, education level, and living arrangement because these domains have been associated with post-ICU recovery and social reintegration in prior studies and were available in this dataset. Broader structural or composite measures of social disadvantage were not available in the registry.

We collected information on employment status (permanent employment, non-permanent employment, self-employment), education level (junior high school, high school, college), and occupation before hospitalization and after discharge. Permanent employment was defined as full-time employment under an open-ended contract with an employer. Non-permanent employment refers to employment arrangements with less contractual security than permanent employment, including, for example, fixed-term, part-time, dispatch, and other nonstandard employment arrangements. Self-employment was treated as a separate category because employment continuity and return-to-work decisions differ conceptually from those in employee-employer contractual relationships.

Occupation was categorized as: 1) Manager, 2) Licensed professional, 3) Office worker, sales, or service, 4) Blue-collar worker, and 5) Other. At each follow-up, we gathered household size, living alone (yes or no), marital status, annual household income, and reasons for work style changes. These five variables were assessed only at follow-up; corresponding baseline data were neither collected nor available. We collected data on return to the same work status, defined as returning to the same job held before COVID-19 infection.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was joblessness, defined as not returning to any form of work or employment at the follow-up assessment.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To assess differences between individuals who returned to work and those who were jobless, we used chi-square tests for binary and categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (Supplemental Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B654) (32). We did not estimate a sample size before analysis because this study was a secondary analysis of an available database (32). For missing data on covariates and SDH factors in analyses, we performed a random forest-based imputation method with the missForest package (33). Variable-specific missingness for baseline characteristics and adjustment variables is summarized in Supplemental Table 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B654). For all models, results were presented as odds ratios with corresponding 95% CIs and p values. All models were adjusted for baseline covariates: age, sex, presence of multimorbidity, type of oxygen therapy received, and duration of hospital stay.

For the primary analysis, we used generalized estimating equations (GEEs) to evaluate longitudinal associations between baseline SDH and jobless status over two years, as GEE handles correlated repeated measurements and provides population-average estimates. The model used a binomial distribution with a logit link and exchangeable working correlation structure. Baseline SDH factors included employment type and education level, with time (first and second year) included as a variable. Employment type and education level were entered simultaneously to estimate their independent associations with joblessness while accounting for each other.

As sensitivity analyses, we performed separate logistic regression models to assess associations between baseline SDH factors and joblessness at each follow-up (with joblessness at the first-year follow-up as the outcome in one model and joblessness at the second-year follow-up as the outcome in the other). These associations were compared with GEE population-average estimates to assess consistency.

To further explore factors related to long-term joblessness, we conducted an exploratory logistic regression analysis focusing on jobless status at the second follow-up. This analysis included both baseline SDH and SDH factors measured at the first follow-up (living alone) to examine their combined association with joblessness at the second follow-up.

RESULTS

Among 3297 hospitalized adult COVID-19 patients in the CORES II registry, 2512 patients were sent first-year follow-up questionnaires, and 1109 responded (Fig. 1). We included 337 previously employed patients with severe COVID-19 (Table 1, Supplemental Tables 2 and 3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B654). At the first-year follow-up, 275 patients had returned to work, whereas 62 remained jobless (18.4%). Of these, 294 patients subsequently responded to the second-year follow-up. At the second-year follow-up, 242 patients had returned to work, whereas 52 were jobless (17.7%).

Differences at First-Year Follow-Up

At first-year follow-up, baseline demographic and in-hospital characteristics were generally similar between patients who returned to work and those who remained jobless, although the jobless group had a longer hospital stay (19.0 d [9.3–52.8] vs. 16.0 d [9.0–28.0], p = 0.03). At first-year follow-up, patients who remained jobless were less likely to have been discharged home and more likely to have been transferred to another hospital or facility than those who returned to work (17.7% vs. 33.1% and 82.3% vs. 64.7%, p = 0.02).

Descriptively, patients who remained jobless showed worse recovery status, dyspnea, and performance status, along with a greater symptom burden and a higher prevalence of impaired QOL than those who returned to work (Table 2). They also had higher loneliness scores. Regarding pre-hospitalization social determinants (Table 3), the jobless group had a lower proportion of permanent employment (46.8% vs. 62.5%) and a higher proportion of non-permanent employment (37.1% vs. 16.9%) than the return-to-work group, whereas education level and occupation category were similar between the two groups.

Differences at Second-Year Follow-Up

In the second-year follow-up cohort, patients who remained jobless were older than those who returned to work (62.0 yr [53.5–67.5] vs. 56.0 yr [49.5–61.0], p < 0.01), whereas other baseline characteristics were generally similar between the two groups. Descriptively, at second-year follow-up, patients who remained jobless had worse dyspnea and performance status, whereas other follow-up measures, including awareness of recovery, symptom burden, and impaired QOL, were broadly similar between the groups. As in the first-year follow-up, the jobless group had a lower proportion of permanent employment (48.0% vs. 61.8%) and a higher proportion of non-permanent employment (40.0% vs. 17.4%) before hospitalization. In addition, prior joblessness was more common in the second-year jobless group than in the return-to-work group.

Association of Social Determinants of Health with Joblessness

In the primary analysis, non-permanent employment was significantly associated with higher odds of joblessness over the 2-year follow-up period compared with permanent employment (OR 2.35; 95% CI [1.26–4.40]) (Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B654). This result was broadly consistent with the first-year sensitivity analysis, in which non-permanent employment was also associated with joblessness (OR 2.73; 95% CI [1.28–5.82]).

Permanent employment before hospitalization served as the reference category for employment status, and junior high school education served as the reference category for educational attainment. Other SDH factors, including self-employment, education history, and living alone status, showed no consistent association with joblessness.

DISCUSSION

Key Points and Strengths

This study investigated differences inpatient characteristics and the impact of SDH on joblessness following severe COVID-19. Patients who were jobless at the first-year follow-up had worse physical and mental health, lower QOL, and more persistent symptoms than those who returned to work, although these differences diminished by the second year. Non-permanent employment before hospitalization was associated with joblessness over 2 years.

Our study has several strengths. First, limited evidence is available regarding long-term follow-up for return to work in patients with severe COVID-19. Among the 20 studies included in the recent meta-analysis, 19 had follow-up periods of 12 months or less, and the remaining study had a 2-year follow-up with a sample size of 24 (8, 34). In contrast, our cohort comprised 294 survivors followed for two years, enabling detailed characterization of long-term joblessness using longitudinal data. Second, we specifically focused on survivors of severe COVID-19 pneumonia who were hospitalized during the pandemic. This design reduced heterogeneity in both the underlying illness and the broader social context, allowing us to evaluate the relationship between SDH and return to work within a relatively uniform clinical and societal setting. Previous studies on return to work after critical illness often included patients with heterogeneous diseases across different time periods, which introduced limitations due to variations in clinical care and social environments (6, 35, 36). Our study offers a clearer and more internally consistent view of how SDH relate to long-term joblessness in the recovery from severe COVID-19.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Previous studies indicated that critically ill survivors often encountered difficulties returning to work after discharge. Among 10,015 ICU survivors, approximately two-thirds returned to work by 3 months post-discharge, whereas about two-fifths and one-third remained jobless at 12 and 60 months, respectively (5). A recent systematic review of 20 studies indicated that approximately one-third of severe COVID-19 survivors remained jobless (about 23–50%, corresponding to roughly 65–141 of 281 pooled patients) one year after discharge (8), which was higher than the rate observed in our study. Variation in the pandemic phase or the stringency of lockdown measures may have influenced return-to-work outcomes. The present cohort was hospitalized between April and September 2021, corresponding largely to Japan’s fourth and fifth COVID-19 waves. During the follow-up period, the national unemployment rate in Japan remained low at 2.6% in both 2022 and 2023 (37), indicating that the joblessness observed in our cohort was substantially higher than the contemporaneous background unemployment level. Taken together, these differences suggest that between-study variation in joblessness rates should be interpreted cautiously and does not necessarily indicate lower support needs among survivors of severe COVID-19.

SDH Factors and Joblessness

The observed disadvantage of non-permanent employment for return to work aligns with previous research across different patient populations. Studies of cancer survivors have shown that part-time or non-permanent employees were less likely to return to work compared with full-time employees (38, 39). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the vulnerability of non-permanent employees became more apparent, as they were more likely to lose their jobs during the economic downturn (40, 41). Taken together, these findings suggest that patients with more vulnerable pre-hospitalization employment status may be at particular risk of prolonged joblessness after severe COVID-19. One potential approach to support labor-force reentry is the graded return-to-work system in Germany, a social-insurance-based scheme that coordinates rehabilitation clinicians with employers and has been associated with a lower risk of permanent work disability (42). Comparable social supports may also merit consideration, particularly in combination with PICS clinics, where multidisciplinary teams monitor and address persistent symptoms (43). However, return-to-work outcomes are likely shaped not only by individual health status but also by workplace conditions and broader labor-market factors. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify what types of support may be most effective in improving return-to-work outcomes.

Pre-pandemic studies found that workers with higher educational attainment were more likely to return to work than those with less education (44–46). This association has been interpreted as reflecting education-linked, non-economic resources—such as cognitive skills, social support, health literacy, and capacity to navigate post-ICU care. During the COVID-19 pandemic, however, the mechanisms linking education to employment likely shifted. In the United States, workers with higher education were more likely to transition to remote work and maintain employment (47, 48), whereas in Japan, remote work adoption depended more on industry and firm size than on education (49). These contrasts suggest that the education–employment continuity relationship may have varied across countries, shaped by labor-market structures, workplace norms, and social policy environments. At the sectoral level, many essential and frontline services—staffed disproportionately by workers with lower formal education—continued on-site, potentially preserving employment opportunities (50). For individuals recovering from critical illness, however, employment continuity and return to work are not the same; many essential jobs are physically demanding and may not suit workers in recovery. These pandemic-specific features complicate the interpretation of how educational attainment relates to return to work, warranting further investigation to clarify the role of workplace structures.

Previous studies have reported no association between family support and return to work (51), which is consistent with our finding that living alone was not associated with joblessness. Spousal support and income-replacement benefits, such as disability payments, can reduce immediate income pressure, which may defer the timing of return to work (52). As such, measuring joblessness during recovery from critical illness should be contextualized by the role of supplemental support and patient preferences.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, selection bias may exist due to response patterns to follow-up questionnaires. Our data did not allow us to determine reasons for nonresponse at subsequent follow-up, and deaths could not be distinguished from other causes of loss to follow-up within the analytic cohort. In addition, detailed in-hospital characteristics and severity data were not available for nonrespondents, precluding direct comparison between respondents and nonrespondents. We also acknowledge potential measurement bias, as baseline EQ-5D-5L before hospitalization was retrospectively reported on the first-year follow-up questionnaire and may have been subject to recall bias. Second, we examined individual SDH factors at baseline rather than measuring the cumulative impact of SDH and or cumulative SDH-related disadvantage. In addition, our registry lacked information on broader structural and contextual determinants, such as housing stability, residential context, transportation access, access to social security systems, and regional economic conditions, which are also considered important components of SDH. Furthermore, the category of non-permanent employment was heterogeneous, and finer subtypes such as temporary, dispatch, or part-time work were not available in the registry. Therefore, the observed association may partly reflect underlying employment vulnerability unrelated to severe COVID-19 itself. Third, as this was a secondary analysis of an existing registry, the study was not specifically designed to detect associations for every exposure domain examined. Therefore, limited statistical power for some associations cannot be excluded, and null findings should be interpreted cautiously. This is particularly relevant for respiratory support modality, which was included as an adjustment factor rather than a primary exposure and may also reflect both illness severity and clinical treatment selection. Fourth, race and ethnicity were not collected in this registry, which limits assessment of generalizability to more ethnically diverse populations. In addition, because the database comprised patients hospitalized in Japan, the association between SDH factors and joblessness identified in our study may not be generalizable to populations in other countries with different social, cultural, and demographic contexts. Furthermore, differences in health insurance systems may further limit generalizability; Japan operates a universal public health insurance system, which may not be directly comparable to settings in which coverage is largely provided through private insurance. Fifth, we cannot rule out the possibility that unemployment included cases of age-related retirement. Some individuals may have retired earlier than planned due to the pandemic, and our data did not allow us to identify such cases with precision. This issue should be interpreted in the context of the Japanese employment system, in which the mandatory retirement age cannot be set below 60 years, and employers are generally required to secure employment opportunities up to 65 years under specified conditions. However, since we expect these cases to be few because many unemployment cases were attributed to health reasons, their inclusion is unlikely to have affected the results. Finally, our data did not allow us to disentangle health-related limitations, workplace-related factors, and broader pandemic-related labor-market effects as determinants of joblessness. In addition, the higher prevalence of anxiety, depression, and PTSD-related symptoms among jobless patients should be interpreted as a descriptive, potentially bidirectional association rather than a causal relationship. Therefore, the present findings do not identify whether individual-level follow-up alone would be sufficient to improve return-to-work outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

In this two-year follow-up study of severe COVID-19 survivors, patients who were jobless at the first-year follow-up had worse physical and mental health, lower QOL, and more persistent symptoms than those who returned to work. Non-permanent employment before hospitalization was a significant risk factor for joblessness, whereas other SDH factors showed no consistent association. These findings highlight the importance of considering pre-hospitalization employment status when interpreting long-term social recovery after severe COVID-19.
