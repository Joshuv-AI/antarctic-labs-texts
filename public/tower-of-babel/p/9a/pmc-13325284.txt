Arterial calcification due to CD73 deficiency presenting with critical limb-threatening ischemia successfully treated with iliac endarterectomy and distal bypass
Authors: Yusuke Kato, Etsuko Hisanaga, Takuya Koyano, Naoki Konno, Hayato Ikota, Wataru Tatsuishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArterial calcification due to CD73 deficiency is a rare disorder causing progressive medial calcification. Revascularization reports remain scarce. We describe a 67-year-old woman with arterial calcification due to CD73 deficiency and critical limb-threatening ischemia. Because endovascular therapy was unfeasible due to extensive calcification, we performed external iliac artery endarterectomy and anterior tibial artery bypass. Despite near-complete medial calcification, the adventitia allowed secure anastomosis. Postoperative imaging confirmed graft patency, and improved perfusion facilitated forefoot healing. Surgical reconstruction offers a viable option for limb salvage when endovascular therapy is precluded.Keywords: Arterial calcification, Endarterectomy, Arterial bypass, Perfusion, Amputation

Arterial calcification due to CD73 deficiency (ACDC) is a rare autosomal-recessive disease caused by loss-of-function mutations in NT5E, which encodes ecto-5′-nucleotidase CD73.1 CD73 regulates vascular homeostasis by converting extracellular adenosine monophosphate to adenosine, a vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and anticalcific molecule.2,3 CD73 deficiency decreases adenosine bioavailability and upregulates tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase, promoting the degradation of pyrophosphate, an endogenous inhibitor of calcification.2, 3, 4, 5 Patients develop progressive medial arterial calcification, which typically affects the lower extremities and causes severe ischemic symptoms.1,4,6

Few cases of critical limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) associated with ACDC have been reported. The calcification is often circumferential and extensive, making endovascular therapy unsuccessful.6 Surgical revascularization is thus the main treatment option. However, specific strategies, such as optimal inflow vessel selection, technical management of severely calcified vessels, and criteria for anastomotic feasibility, are not adequately documented. We report a genetically confirmed case of ACDC that was treated successfully with external iliac artery (EIA) endarterectomy and distal bypass. This report includes detailed operative findings and postoperative imaging demonstrating durable perfusion restoration.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

Preoperative findings

A 67-year-old woman presented with ulceration and progressive necrosis of the right dorsal foot (Fig 1, A). Conservative local wound care and subsequent antimicrobial therapy for cellulitis did not result in improvement. She had a history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

Clinical and preoperative imaging findings. (A) Preoperative clinical photograph demonstrating dry gangrene involving the dorsum and toes of the right foot. (B) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showing diffuse medial calcification extending from the external iliac artery (EIA) to the popliteal artery. (C) CT angiography demonstrating preserved flow in the anterior tibial artery via collateral circulation.

Physical examination revealed dry gangrene on the dorsum of the right foot, with exposed tendons and violaceous discoloration of the toes. The skin perfusion pressure (SPP) was markedly reduced (plantar SPP, 16 mm Hg).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated diffuse, severe medial calcification extending from both eias to the popliteal arteries (Fig 1, B). The anterior tibial artery showed relatively preserved flow, which was maintained via collateral circulation (Fig 1, C). The common femoral artery (CFA) exhibited severe calcification, marked dilation, and thinning of the adventitia, suggesting that anastomosis may be difficult.

Progressive necrosis of the first three toes raised the suspicion of osteomyelitis, and amputation was considered necessary. However, wound healing was unlikely to occur without adequate revascularization.

Surgical planning

Endovascular therapy was deemed inappropriate because of the extensive calcification. Extensive circumferential calcification made guidewire passage unlikely. Even if wire crossing were achieved, long-segment stent placement across the heavily calcified and tortuous arterial segment was expected to carry a high risk of kinking and subsequent occlusion. The anterior tibial artery, which had minimal calcification and preserved flow, was selected as the distal anastomotic target. Proximally, the CFA was avoided because of severe calcification and adventitial thinning. The common iliac artery was not selected as the proximal site because of insufficient graft length.

Although calcified, the EIA had relatively mild wall thickening; preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings suggested that its diameter was not markedly enlarged, and the adventitial tissue retained sufficient strength. After endarterectomy, a suitable surface for proximal anastomosis could be created. The ipsilateral great saphenous vein (GSV) could be harvested and the contralateral GSV preserved for potential future procedures.

Although preoperative angiography was considered, the patient presented with CLTI requiring urgent revascularization. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated distal runoff to the lower leg; therefore, additional diagnostic angiography was not performed. It was long enough to bypass the EIA into the anterior tibial artery.

Surgical procedure and intraoperative findings

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position. A right lateral abdominal incision was made to expose the common iliac artery, internal iliac artery, and EIA using a retroperitoneal approach (Fig 2, A). The EIA showed moderate medial calcification with limited circumferential thickening (Fig 2, B). The calcified intimal and medial components were removed via endarterectomy, creating a smooth lumen for proximal anastomosis (Fig 2, C).

Intraoperative findings of the right external iliac artery (EIA). (A) Exposure of the right EIA through a retroperitoneal approach. (B) Intraoperative appearance demonstrating circumferential medial calcification of the EIA. (C) After endarterectomy, calcified intimal and medial components were removed and a smooth lumen was created. (D) Completion of proximal anastomosis using a reversed great saphenous vein (GSV) graft.

The entire ipsilateral GSV was harvested using skip incisions along the thigh and leg. The anterior tibial artery was exposed distally and confirmed to have minimal calcification. A reversed GSV graft was anastomosed end-to-side to the EIA and distally to the anterior tibial artery (Fig 2, D). Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed adequate graft flow. The surgical wounds were closed in standard fashion.

Postoperative course

Postoperative CT confirmed graft patency (Fig 3, A). The SPP improved markedly (right plantar SPP, 41 mm Hg medial and 70 mm Hg lateral). One month postoperatively, Lisfranc arthrodesis of the hallux, second metatarsal head resection, and third metatarsophalangeal amputation was performed to control infection. Serial debridement was performed as required. After resolution of infection and formation of healthy granulation tissue, split-thickness skin grafting was performed to achieve complete wound healing (Fig 3, B and C). The patient was discharged 3 months postoperatively. The prolonged hospital stay was required for infection control of the foot ulcer, staged debridement, and subsequent split-thickness skin grafting. In addition, functional decline associated with prolonged wound care made early discharge difficult. At 18 months of follow-up, the patient remains clinically stable without recurrent ischemic symptoms. Single antiplatelet therapy with clopidogrel 75 mg/d has been continued.

(B) Wound appearance after staged debridement and partial forefoot amputation. (C) Result at discharge, demonstrating complete wound healing. Pathological findings

Histopathological analysis of the EIA specimen revealed prominent medial calcification, collagen fiber proliferation, and irregular thickening of the middle layer immediately beneath the inner elastic lamina (Fig 3, D and E). Given these findings and the unusually severe calcification, further evaluation of the underlying systemic etiology was considered. After surgical revascularization and stabilization, genetic testing was performed to investigate the etiology of the extensive medial calcification. Direct sequencing of NT5E revealed a homozygous c.3G>C (p.Met1Ile) mutation in exon 1; the patient's daughter was heterozygous for the same mutation. Flow cytometry revealed markedly reduced CD73 expression, confirming the diagnosis of ACDC.

Discussion

ACDC is a rare disorder resulting from CD73 deficiency. Reduced extracellular adenosine levels increase tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase activity, decrease pyrophosphate levels, and promote vascular smooth muscle cell osteogenic differentiation, causing progressive medial calcification.1,3, 4, 5

Lesions typically involve the lower extremity arteries, and severe CLTI has been reported. Extensive circumferential medial calcification often renders endovascular therapy ineffective.6 Although ACDC has been described radiologically and genetically, reports demonstrating successful revascularization, while providing procedural detail and postoperative imaging, are limited. In this case, calcification from the EIA to the popliteal arteries made wire passage and balloon expansion difficult; surgical bypass was the only viable treatment strategy.7

Selection of a proximal target was challenging. Although aneurysmal dilation was observed in the calcified lower extremity arteries, the risk of rupture was considered low because of severe calcification and poor distal flow. Nevertheless, careful clinical follow-up is planned to monitor for any progressive enlargement. If the ipsilateral GSV had been unsuitable or insufficient in length, an alternative strategy would have been to harvest upper extremity superficial veins to extend the conduit while preserving the contralateral GSV for potential future procedures. The CFA, although conventionally used, exhibited severe calcification, dilation and adventitial thinning, making it unsafe for anastomosis. Furthermore, the CFA demonstrated aneurysmal change with marked thinning of the adventitia, raising concern for potential rupture after endarterectomy and patch angioplasty. The common iliac artery would have required a longer graft than was available. However, the EIA had relatively mild wall thickening and retained sufficient adventitial strength.

In typical atherosclerotic disease, endarterectomy is generally confined to the intimal layer, with the media preserved to maintain arterial wall integrity. However, in this patient, circumferential medial calcification extended beyond the usual cleavage plane, necessitating removal of the intima and media and leaving only the adventitia as the reconstructible layer.

Although this raised concern regarding anastomotic stability and long-term durability, the preserved adventitia provided adequate structural support for a secure suture line. Postoperative imaging and clinical improvement confirmed satisfactory graft flow, suggesting that anatomical reconstruction may be feasible, even when only the adventitial layer can be preserved.

Nevertheless, because the medial layer plays a primary role in resisting arterial wall stress, the long-term durability of adventitia-supported reconstruction remains uncertain. Although no perioperative complications were observed, risks such as structural weakening over time cannot be entirely excluded. Continued postoperative imaging surveillance will, therefore, be essential, and additional case accumulation will be required to determine whether this reconstructive approach can be generalized to other patients with ACDC or similar calcification disorders.

This case emphasizes the importance of individualized surgical planning in ACDC-associated CLTI, particularly when routinely used arteries are unsuitable because of circumferential calcification. In these situations, EIA endarterectomy with adventitia-based reconstruction may be a viable option, provided that intraoperative assessment confirms adequate adventitial integrity and that careful long-term follow-up is ensured.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of CLTI due to ACDC that was successfully treated with EIA endarterectomy and distal bypass. Recognizing ACDC as an underlying cause of medial arterial calcification is important, particularly in patients with atypical or unusually extensive calcification. Tailored surgical strategies can enable limb salvage, even when endovascular therapy is not feasible. Although this approach proved feasible and effective in this case, its long-term durability remains uncertain, and careful postoperative surveillance is essential.
