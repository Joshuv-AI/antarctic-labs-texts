Postnatal outcomes of referred cases with abnormal ultrasound findings of fetal gall bladder and cyst in the fetal liver
Authors: Soo Ran Choi, Hye-Sung Won, Jin Hoon Chung, Mi-Young Lee, Jihye Koh, Yoo Jin Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the postnatal outcomes of referred cases with abnormal findings in the fetal gallbladder (GB) or cysts in the fetal liver.MethodsThis retrospective study included pregnant women referred to a tertiary referral unit, the fetal therapy center at Ulsan Asan Medical Center in Korea, between June 2005 and October 2022. Persistent non-visualization of the fetal gallbladder (NVFGB) was defined as non-visualization at least twice using an ultrasound scan. Antenatal ultrasound findings were obtained from the medical records, and the clinical, radiological, and surgical findings of the babies were reviewed.ResultsA total of 113 referred patients were evaluated. Most enlarged and small GBs were identified as normal or GB stones, sludge, septate GB, or choledochal cysts (CDC). NVFGB was identified postnatally in 77.5% of cases. Persistent NVFGB was identified postnatally as biliary atresia (3/14; 21.4%), hepatic parenchymal disease with cholestasis (2/14; 14.2%), or isolated GB agenesis (6/14; 42.8%). The majority of cysts in the fetal liver cases with visualization of the fetal GB were identified as CDC. Half of the patients (2/4) with CDC who underwent NVFGB were confirmed to have biliary atresia.ConclusionExcept for persistent NVFGB, most abnormal ultrasound findings in fetal GB were associated with good postnatal outcomes. Cysts in the fetal liver with visualization of the fetal GB did not result in serious adverse outcomes. NVFGB, especially persistent NVFGB with or without cysts in the fetal liver, should be followed up closely prenatally and postnatally.

Introduction

The fetal liver, bile duct, and gallbladder (GB) all have a common embryonic origin, arising from the hepatic diverticulum between the 4th and 5th weeks of gestation [1]. Although the fetal liver is always detected using prenatal ultrasound, the bile duct is barely detectable. Fetal GB was observed at 14 weeks of gestation. The visualization rate of the fetal GB increases with gestational age until 34 weeks and then plateaus in >90% of cases. Although non-visualization of the fetal GB (NVFGB) is often transient because it exhibits cyclic contractility [2,3], it may be associated with GB agenesis, biliary atresia (BA), cystic fibrosis, or chromosomal abnormalities [4]. GB agenesis, an isolated finding, does not require consideration of chromosomal abnormalities [1]. The incidence of BA is rare and its detection is complex in utero and usually evident as a slight degree of suspicion. As a result, BA is diagnosed by radiologic examination during the early neonatal period in most cases. Moreover, BA is a serious disease that progresses rapidly to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatic failure, necessitating liver transplantation [5,6].

A cyst in the fetal liver is a rare congenital anomaly involving the biliary tract or hepatic parenchyma, and is identified as a simple hepatic cyst, choledochal cyst (CDC), or cystic BA during the neonatal period. Though these cysts have similar prenatal ultrasound findings, their postnatal management and prognosis vary [7,8].

We aimed to identify relationships between the prenatal abnormal ultrasound findings of fetal GB and a cyst in the fetal liver and postnatal follow-up outcomes in cases referred to the fetal therapy center of a tertiary hospital.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted between June 2005 and October 2022 at the fetal therapy center of the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of Ulsan Asan Medical Center. Pregnant women with abnormal ultrasound findings of an abnormal fetal GB, such as an enlarged or small GB, NVFGB, or cysts in the fetal liver, were included. Medical records and antenatal ultrasound findings obtained at the center were reviewed. After birth, clinical, ultrasonographic, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), and surgical and intraoperative cholangiographic findings were reviewed. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ulsan Asan Medical Center (No. 2024-1333).

A detailed ultrasound examination was performed to detect other structural abnormalities, including those of the fetal hepatobiliary system. For the hepatobiliary system investigation, we initially checked the fetal GB, an eggplant-shaped cystic structure within the fetal liver to the right of the umbilical vein, in the axial plane. Color flow imaging was performed to confirm that the GB was not confused with the umbilical vein. In NVFGB cases, we rechecked the sagittal plane of the fetal abdomen or reevaluated it 2–4 weeks later. Persistent NVFGB was defined as non-visualization of the fetal GB on at least two ultrasound scans. The GB size, shape, septation, and presence of echogenic material in the GB were assessed. In addition, cysts in the fetal liver were assessed for their size, location, and relationship with the GB. Cases were excluded when the fetal GB or hepatobiliary system could not be correctly identified, such as a congenital diaphragmatic hernia, abnormal situs or isomerism, gastroschisis, or omphalocele.

Results

Initially, we considered 366 referred cases with abnormal fetal GB or cyst ultrasonography findings in the fetal liver. However, 92 cases that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. These included major nonisolated anomalies, including congenital diaphragmatic hernia (n=38), abnormal situs or isomerism (n=19), complex cardiac defects (n=13), gastroschisis (n=7), and omphalocele (n=6); chromosomal aneuploidies, including trisomy 21, 18, and 13 (n=5), and four cases of intrauterine fetal death. These exclusions ensured that only patients with isolated abnormal gallbladder or liver cyst findings were included. The remaining 274 patients were divided into two groups: an abnormal fetal GB group (n=214) and a cyst in the fetal liver group (n=60). However, 150 of the 274 patients were excluded because they returned after a detailed ultrasound examination and 11 NVFGB patients were lost to follow-up. The remaining 113 patients were assigned to either the abnormal fetal GB group (69 cases) or the cyst in the fetal liver group (44 cases) (Fig. 1).

Gestational ages at referral of the 113 enrolled cases ranged 18+1 to 37+6 weeks (mean, 27.1±4.7 weeks of gestation), mean gestational age at delivery was 38.1±1.8 weeks, and mean birth weight was 3,017.4±511.7 g. Detailed ultrasound scans were performed in range of one-three times per case.

1. Sent back in which referred as abnormal GB and cyst in the fetal liver

Of the 150 cases sent back after a detailed ultrasound scan, 134 had an abnormal GB and 16 had a cyst in the fetal liver (Table 1).

1) Referrals as an abnormal GB

Of the 134 included cases, 100 were sent back with an enlarged GB, three had GB sludge, one a tortuous GB with common bile duct dilation, and one a combined persistent right umbilical vein on detailed ultrasound. Of the 11 patients referred for a small GB, 10 were deemed normal, and in the remaining patient, the GB was invisible. Of the 23 patients referred for NVFGB, 20 were deemed normal and three were considered to have a combined persistent right umbilical vein. The GB was usually identified immediately by a detailed ultrasound scan, but scans were performed up to three times in two cases.

2) Referrals as a cyst in the fetal liver

Among the 16 cases, nine were identified as CDC with visible GB. Two patients had CDC with a non-visible GB, three had a dilated GB, one had a mesenteric cyst, and one was suspected of having an adrenal cyst.

2. Postnatal outcomes of referred as an abnormal GB

Sixty-nine cases of abnormal GB and 44 cases of cysts in the fetal liver were delivered at the Ulsan Asan Medical Center (Table 2).

1) Referrals as an enlarged GB

Of the 23 patients, 19 were deemed to have normal GB on prenatal and postnatal ultrasonography. Of the remaining four cases, one had an enlarged GB with sludge prenatally and postnatally and showed GB wall thickening with sludge; 3 months later, a GB stone was diagnosed. One patient had echogenic sludge material in the GB prenatally, which gradually disappeared postnatally. The other patient had an L-shaped tortuous GB prenatally, and postnatally, the baby was diagnosed with a septate GB with the possibility of adenomyomatosis. The remaining infant had a prenatally enlarged, tortuous GB. This baby had prominent dilation of the intra- and extrahepatobiliary ducts with a persistently tortuous GB. Although this baby had no clinical problems, MRCP suggested possible early findings of CDC type IV after 2 years of follow-up.

2) Referrals as a small GB

Among the six cases, three were deemed to have normal GB pre and postnatally. One of these had a small GB with a normal common bile duct, the second had a collapsed GB postnatally, and the third was suspected of having a collapsed GB with liver hemangioma prenatally; this baby was later diagnosed as having a portosystemic shunt.

3) Referrals as NVFGB

Of the 40 patients, 13 had a normal GB, 13 had a collapsed GB, and 14 had persistent NVFGB prenatally. However, after birth, normal and collapsed GB were identified in 22 and two patients, respectively. One of these two babies who had a collapsed GB had early stage bile duct obstruction accompanied by hepatic parenchymal disease and increased direct bilirubin levels. The infant did not exhibit an extrahepatobiliary duct by MRCP. The other baby with a collapsed GB had a hyper-vascular hepatic hemangioendothelioma (size: 6.8×5.7×3.5 cm), which decreased progressively to a maximum dimension of 2.0 cm after 3 years of follow-up.

Of the 14 babies referred for NVFGB with persistent NVFGB prenatally, three were diagnosed with BA and an atretic GB and underwent Kasai operation. All three later became candidates for liver transplantation. Two infants had collapsed GB and hepatic parenchymal disease with cholestasis (one was confirmed to have Kabuki syndrome type 1). Six infants were diagnosed with isolated GB agenesis with an intact biliary tree at 3 years to 4 years of follow-up. Two other babies showed findings of GB agenesis after birth, but were finally confirmed to have a small hypoplastic GB after 1 year to 2 years of follow-up. One infant had a collapsed GB with an intact biliary tree.

3. Postnatal outcome of referred as a cyst in fetal liver

Of the 44 cases referred for a cyst in the fetal liver, 40 had a prenatal ultrasound finding of CDC with a visible GB and four had a finding of CDC with a non-visible GB (Table 3).

1) CDC with visualization of the fetal GB on prenatal ultrasound

Of the 40 babies, twenty babies were identified as CDC type I and underwent Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy or CDC excision, and seven babies were identified as CDC type IV and were treated surgically. The cysts ranged in size from 7 to 50 mm. Surgeries were usually performed 5–15, 30, or even 8 months after birth. Seven cases of CDC in which the fetal GB was visualized prenatally had a hepatic cyst and five of the seven babies were closely followed up. However, one of the remaining two babies had a cystic BA with a normal GB on postnatal ultrasound; however, after surgery, the diagnosis was changed to CDC type IV. The hepatic cyst in the remaining baby decreased in size and finally disappeared. An intestinal duplication cyst was identified instead of CDC in four babies, all of whom were treated by resection and anastomosis. GB agenesis was observed in the operative field in one of these babies. The remaining two cases of CDC with prenatal visualization of the fetal GB had normal GB and biliary duct without postnatal CDC.

2) CDC with non-visualization of the fetal GB on prenatal ultrasound

Four patients had CDC with NVFGB on prenatal ultrasonography. Biliary cirrhosis was detected in baby no. 1 during a Kasai operation and the baby received a liver transplant at 6 months. Baby no. 2 showed mild GB dilation with BA. Baby no. 3 had only a small CDC with a visible GB and the cyst disappeared at the 2-year follow-up. Baby no. 4 had CDC type I.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the results of prenatal ultrasound and postnatal final diagnosis in referred cases with abnormal ultrasound findings of the fetal GB and cysts in the fetal liver. Most of the referred cases with an enlarged or small GB were prenatally identified as having a normal GB. However, some patients were diagnosed with septate GB, CDC (type IV), or GB stone after birth and at the 2-year follow-up visit. Of the referred cases of NVFGB, 77.5% had normal GB postnatally. Among the persistent NVFGB cases, 21.4% (three cases) had BA and became liver transplantation candidates, 14.2% had hepatic parenchymal disease with cholestasis, and 42.8% had postnatal isolated GB agenesis. Cysts in the fetal liver with visualization of the fetal GB were identified as CDC in the majority of cases and hepatic cysts or duplication cysts in a minority. Two CDC patients with NVFGB were confirmed to have BA.

The fetal GB maintains a solid structure until the end of the 1st trimester and then becomes eggplant-like in shape and contains bile produced by fetal hepatic cells [9]. GB size increases linearly until 32–35 weeks of gestation and then plateaus [2,10,11]. The shapes of fetal GBs vary and may be enlarged, small, narrow, or curved [10]. Furthermore, the fetal GB exhibits contractility, and the contractility rate increases as gestation progresses, whereas the contractility cycle remains unchanged throughout gestation at 3.1±0.6 hours. The fetal GB volume typically exhibits a sinusoidal pattern [3]. Therefore, if the fetal GB is not visualized at any time during an ultrasound scan, a recheck 1 or 1 and a half hours later is recommended. Moon et al. [2] reported a 100% visualization rate at 17–19 weeks and 25–28 weeks of gestation but a visualization rate of <90% after 35 weeks of gestation.

One study reported that isolated NVFGB was confirmed as transient NVFGB in 60% of cases and GB agenesis in 40% of cases [11]. Another study on the prognosis of an isolated abnormal fetal GB (defined as an enlarged or small GB according to the 90th and 10th percentiles of the normal reference range, respectively) found that it was associated with normal outcomes, except for one case of biliary atresia with a small GB [12]. However, NVFGB was finally visualized in 80.4% of the cases, identified as GB agenesis in 17.4%, and biliary atresia in one case [12]. Studies in France and Israel reported results similar to those of previous studies, except for cases of cystic fibrosis [4,13,14]. They performed amniocentesis in NVFGB patients for genetic and biochemical testing of the digestive enzyme gamma-glutamyl transferase to screen for cystic fibrosis. It may be that cystic fibrosis is more common in Europe than in Asia, as no cases of cystic fibrosis were detected in studies conducted in Hong Kong, China, or the present study [8,12].

In the present study, referred cases with an enlarged or small GB were usually identified as normal, as reported by He et al. [12], with the exception of GB stone, septate GB, and CDC at the 2-year follow-up. Referred cases with NVFGB are usually identified as normal, collapsed, or small GB. However, persistent NVFGB cases have been identified as isolated GB agenesis, hepatic parenchymal disease with cholestasis, or biliary atresia; thus, some of these cases were candidates for liver transplantation. We encountered more unfavorable final diagnoses than in previous studies because we included referred cases that already had abnormal fetal GB ultrasound findings before referral and followed up cases until 4 years after birth.

CDC are rare biliary cystic malformations caused by congenital dilation of the biliary tree. The incidence of CDC is greater in Asian than in Western populations (1 in 1,000 vs. 1 in 100,000 live births per year) [15]. Hepatic cysts are benign, are not connected to the biliary tree, and are commonly located in the right hepatic lobe. The incidence of these cysts during the neonatal period is unclear; however, girls have a three-fold higher risk than boys [15]. However, accurate differential diagnosis of hepatic and biliary cysts using prenatal ultrasound is challenging. CDC are usually managed surgically and have good outcomes, whereas hepatic cysts can be managed nonoperatively by observation. A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that fetal hepatic cysts are benign with a low likelihood of progression; however, biliary cysts progress rapidly and are associated with abnormal liver function and clinical symptoms after birth [16]. Furthermore, 22% of fetuses with biliary cysts may be diagnosed postnatally as having BA [16]. Most BA cases are detected after birth, with clinical symptoms of biliary obstruction. BA is rare in Caucasians with an estimated incidence of >1 per 10,000 live births, whereas it is 1 per 5,000 live births in Asians. In Korea, the overall incidence of BA is 1.06 per 10,000 live births, similar to that reported in Western countries [5,15].

Several studies reported different relationships between BA and NVFGB. Chalouhi et al. [17] suggested that BA should be suspected in NVFGB cases; however, another study concluded that the supposed link between BA and NVFGB is likely to occur and that NVFGB does not predict BA [4]. In another study, the GB was reported that the GB is atretic in biliary atresia but not in cases of CDC without biliary atresia [18].

In this study, most cases of cysts were identified in the fetal liver, and cysts regressed in only a small number of cases. No cases in which the GB was visualized had BA. CDC, hepatic cysts, or duplicated cysts occurred in descending order of occurrence. Of the four cases, including a cyst in the fetal liver with NVFGB, two were confirmed as CDC with BA. One of these patients underwent liver transplantation in addition to the Kasai operation. Altogether, in this study, BA was confirmed more often than that reported in a previous study in cases of persistent NVFGB or in cases of cysts in the fetal liver with NVFGB than in cases with a visualized fetal GB [4]. This might be the same reason as in the case of abnormal ultrasound findings of the fetal GB.

The strengths of this study are that serious cases with additional abnormal ultrasound findings that prevented the identification of the fetal GB and hepatobiliary duct were excluded and babies in the inclusion criteria were followed up for 4 years postnatally.

However, the study was limited by its retrospective design and because we could not calculate the prevalence of abnormal ultrasound findings of the fetal GB and cysts in the fetal liver, as the cases included were referred. Furthermore, because the patients were referred from various clinics and hospitals, the diagnostic criteria used to define enlarged or small GB were unclear.

In conclusion, with the exception of persistent NVFGB, most cases of abnormal ultrasound findings of the fetal GB were associated with good postnatal outcomes. Furthermore, cysts in the fetal liver with visualization of the fetal GB did not result in serious postnatal outcomes during the neonatal period, despite surgical procedures. We recommend that the fetal GB be identified at least once using ultrasound based on its characteristics, although the practice guidelines of the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology recommend that checking for fetal GB on the right side is optional [19]. We conclude that NVFGB with or without a cyst in the fetal liver should be followed up closely prenatally and postnatally.
