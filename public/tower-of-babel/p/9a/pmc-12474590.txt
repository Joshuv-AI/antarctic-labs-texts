Economic Evaluation and Budget Impact Analysis of Indocyanine Green Test for Preoperative Liver Function in Patients with Major Hepatectomy in Thailand
Authors: Unchalee Permsuwan, Pajaree Sriuttha, Chutwichai Tovikkai, Anon Chotirosniramit, Asara Thepbunchonchai, Sunhawit Junrungsee, Worakitti Lapisatepun, Kittipong Chaiyabutr, Tharatip Srisuk, Piyameth Dilokthornsakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality following liver resections. Indocyanine green (ICG) clearance testing provides quantitative liver function assessment to improve perioperative risk stratification. However, its cost poses a concern in resource-limited settings like Thailand. This study aimed to evaluate the cost-utility and budget impact of adding ICG testing to standard diagnosis compared to standard diagnosis alone in patients undergoing first major hepatectomy.MethodsA hybrid model combining a decision tree and Markov model was developed from a societal perspective over a lifetime horizon. Clinical data and cancer treatment costs were derived from 400 real-world patients admitted to four university hospitals. PHLF costs were sourced from the National Health Security Office and utility values were directly collected from patients admitted for major hepatectomy. Primary outcomes included total costs, life years, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). A 3% annual discount rate was applied. A variety of sensitivity analyses were conducted to test parameter uncertainty. A 5-year budget impact analysis was also performed from a payer’s perspective, calculating the net budget impact (NBI) between both strategies.ResultsCompared to standard diagnosis alone, adding ICG testing increased costs by 3600 Thai baht (THB) [102 US dollars ($)] and gained 0.001 QALYs, yielding an ICER of 2,763,973 THB/QALY ($78,321). The probability of cost-effectiveness at the ceiling threshold of 160,000 THB/QALY ($4,534) was 6.3%. The ICER would fall below this threshold if PHLF risk exceeded 10.1% or if ICG test cost decreased by at least 88%. The 5-year NBI was 39.5 million THB ($1.1 million), reduced by 47.3% with dose-sharing.ConclusionAlthough not cost-effective at current thresholds, ICG reduces PHLF-related costs and maintains an acceptable NBI per year.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03341-1.Keywords: Indocyanine green, Hepatectomy, Liver resection, Cost-utility analysis, Budget impact

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Indocyanine green (ICG) testing provides a quantitative assessment of liver function to predict post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF), but its cost is a concern, particularly in middle- and low-income countries.This study evaluates the cost-utility and budget impact of adding ICG testing to standard diagnosis for patients undergoing first major hepatectomy.What was learned from the study?Findings from the cost-utility study indicate that adding ICG testing to standard diagnosis is not a cost-effective strategy; however, it helps reduce PHLF-related costs. The 5-year net budget impact (NBI) was 39.5 million Thai baht (1.1 million US dollar).ICG testing may be cost-effective in patients at high-risk of PHLF. Implementing ICG dose-sharing could optimize the NBI.

Introduction

Liver resection remains the most effective curative treatment for various primary and secondary malignancies. Among primary liver cancers, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common indication for surgical resection, followed by cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), which has a particularly high prevalence and incidence in Thailand [1–3]. For secondary liver malignancies, colorectal liver metastases represent the most frequent indications for liver surgery [4].

The decision to proceed with liver resection is determined by multiple factors, including patient-related risk factors, tumor-related factors, liver function and volume, type and extent of planned resection (major vs minor hepatectomy), all of which directly affect surgical results [4]. Liver resection involving more than four liver segments is classified as major hepatectomy according to the Brisbane terminology [5]. It is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications. A crucial factor in determining postoperative risk is the volume and functional ability of the remaining liver [6].

Complications after hepatectomy encompass post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF), bile leakage, infections, thrombosis, and acute kidney injury. PHLF, while uncommon, is a serious complication that may result in disability or death [7]. The International Study Group of Liver Surgery describes PHLF as compromised functions of the liver’s synthesis, excretion, and detoxification, noted by increased international normalized ratio (INR) and hyperbilirubinemia occurring during or on the fifth day after the surgery. PHLF severity is classified from A to C, influencing treatment choices [8]. Worldwide, the incidence of PHLF varies between 1% and 34% [9, 10], while mortality rates fall between 5% and 10% [11, 12]. A previous Thai study indicated a 38% incidence of PHLF in patients with CCA following hepatectomy [13]. Thus, preoperative liver function assessment is critical to guide treatment decisions and reduce complications and mortality.

In several Asian countries like Japan, Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong, where liver resections are common, the indocyanine green retention ratio at 15 min (ICG-R15) is widely used to predict PHLF. In Thailand, indocyanine green (ICG) is listed as an orphan drug under the category of contrast media and radiopharmaceuticals, with approved indications including retinal angiography and liver function testing [14]. ICG clearance measurement requires the use of a spectrophotometer, which is available only in select hospitals performing complex liver surgeries, such as the National Cancer Institute, medical schools, and certain tertiary hospitals.

Despite its clinical benefit, access to the ICG test is limited. Most patients under Thailand’s Universal Health Coverage Scheme (UHCS), which covers approximately 70% of the population, need to pay out-of-pocket for the ICG test. Currently, there is a lack of economic evidence to support the inclusion of the ICG test in the health benefit package under UHCS. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the cost-utility and budget impact of incorporating the ICG test into standard diagnosis compared with standard diagnosis alone for patients undergoing major hepatectomy in Thailand.

Methods

Overall Study Design

This cost-utility analysis employed a hybrid model consisting of a decision tree model and a Markov model from a societal perspective with a lifetime horizon. A 1-month cycle length was applied to mimic the progression of patients undergoing major hepatectomy, regardless of the underlying disease. The model simulated patients expected to undergo their first major hepatectomy at 64 years of age.

Ethical Approval

This study received local ethical committee approval at each site (Office of Research Ethics, Research Administration Section, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University: SUR-2567-0158; Siriraj Institutional Review Board: Si 457/2024; Research Ethics Committee Rajavithi Hospital: 139/2567; Center for Ethics in Human Research, Khon Kaen University: HE671304). All participants gave informed consent prior to data collection. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. The data used in this analysis was anonymized.

Intervention and Comparator

The ICG test using a spectrophotometer was assessed as an add-on diagnostic tool for assessing liver function prior to major hepatectomy. ICG was administered at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg of body weight, using a formulation of 25 mg/vial. For the base case analysis, an average body weight of 60 kg was applied, resulting in a required dose of 30 mg.

The comparator was standard diagnostic approach, which included routine blood test (such as liver function tests, complete blood count, and alpha-fetoprotein test), clinical scoring systems (including the Child Pugh Turcotte score, albumin-bilirubin grade), and imaging modalities (computer tomography (CT) scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or ultrasonography (US)). These diagnostic methods are routinely used to assess liver remnant function before performing major hepatectomy.

Model Structure and Assumptions

A hybrid model consisting of two phases, as (1) the surgery phase and (2) the post-surgery phase. A decision tree model was applied to evaluate the cost and clinical consequences at the surgery phase. In contrast, a Markov model was applied to evaluate cost and clinical consequences at the post-surgery phase. Patients entered the model at the surgery phase, where two diagnostic strategies were compared: standard diagnosis alone versus standard diagnosis plus the ICG test to guide the major hepatectomy. Following surgery, patients could either develop PHLF or not and could be either alive or dead within a 30-day postoperative period, which defined the surgery phase.

Patients who developed PHLF transitioned into a three-state Markov model consisting of the following health states: (1) liver failure, (2) liver recovery, and (3) death. In each cycle, patients in the liver failure state could either remain in the same health state, recover, or die. Patients without PHLF transitioned into a separate Markov model with two health states: (1) no PHLF and (2) death. The overall model structure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Several assumptions are necessary in this study. First, because the average patient’s body weight was 60 kg, the use of two vials of ICG (25 mg/vial) for a patient was assumed. Any excess medication was assumed to be disposed of. Second, the maintenance cost of a spectrophotometer was assumed to be none, based on our interviews with nurses who are responsible for the ICG test. They reported that spectrophotometer maintenance was rarely required over the past 10 to 15 years of use. Lastly, the CT scan or MRI of the upper abdomen was assumed. The interviewed hepatic surgeons reported that the most common use of CT scan or MRI was for the upper abdomen. Even though there might be some cases that required CT scan or MRI for the whole abdomen or lower chest, these were very rare.

Model Inputs

Transitional Probability

We performed a systematic review to identify the clinical effect of the ICG test compared to no ICG on liver failure rate and its safety; however, no randomized controlled trial was identified. Instead, we performed a retrospective database analysis for the four university hospitals (two were in Bangkok, one in the North, and one in the Northeast) to estimate the clinical effect of the ICG test on liver failure. Patients who underwent their first major hepatectomy were eligible. Patients who received portal vein embolization or experienced obstructive jaundice with total bilirubin > 2 mg/dL were excluded. According to our sample size estimation, a total of 305 patients were required. Thus, we collected data from 400 patients to ensure the appropriate number of subjects when missing data was observed.

We performed a parametric survival analysis with Weibull distribution to estimate the hazard ratio of liver failure between patients receiving ICG and no ICG. A propensity score matching was used to control the effect of confounding factors, including cirrhosis, platelet count, surgery method, blood loss, disease leading to major hepatectomy, gender, age, and body mass index. Costs

Direct medical costs included the cost of ICG and its administration, diagnostic costs, treatment costs for PHLF, treatment costs for cancer, and medical service costs. A cross-sectional database analysis was conducted to estimate the costs using hospital databases or national databases from the National Health Security Office. Data were from patients who were diagnosed as having liver failure (International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th revision, ICD-10; K72.x), hepatocellular carcinoma (C22.0x), colorectal liver metastases (C78.5x), or cholangiocarcinoma (C22.1x) (Table 1). Laboratory costs, including CT scan, MRI, liver function test, alpha-fetoprotein, complete blood count, and ultrasonography, were derived from the Thai standard cost list [18].

Utility

Health utility was collected by an interview of 54 patients using the EQ-5D-5L tool. The tool consisted of five dimensions, namely mobility, self-care, usual activity, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. The interviewers were trained to use the tool before collecting data from patients. Patients were interviewed at two time points, namely 30 days and 3–6 months after surgery. The health utilities are presented in Table S1.

Model Validation

Seven hepatic surgeons clinically validated the model and its inputs. Health economists also validated the model codes in our research team.

The willingness-to-pay (WTP) of 160,000 Thai baht (THB) per QALY ($4533) was used as a cost-effectiveness threshold in accordance with the Thai Health Technology Assessment (HTA) guideline [19].

One-way sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis with 1000 iterations were performed to explore uncertainties around model inputs. The 95% confidence interval, ranges, or ± 20% variation were used to represent the uncertainties. A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) was also generated.

Two scenario analyses were performed. First, we assumed the use of ICG as dose-sharing to reflect the actual practice in some settings that might appoint patients who needed ICG at the same time to share ICG. Second, we varied the probability of PHLF after major hepatectomy. In addition, a threshold analysis was also performed to determine the price of ICG that could result in the ICER of 160,000 THB/QALY. The 3% discount rate was applied according to the Thai HTA guideline [20].

Budget Impact Analysis

A budget impact model from a payer’s perspective was developed to estimate the financial implications of including the ICG test in Thailand’s health benefit package. The model compared two scenarios: the current scenario, in which patients received standard diagnosis alone, and the new scenario, which added the ICG test to the standard diagnostic protocol. A 5-year time horizon was applied without discounting, in accordance with the Thai HTA guideline [21]. An uptake rate of 60% was assumed in the first year of implementation, increasing by 10% annually over the 5 years.

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to estimate the eligible population in Thailand. Thai studies, which provided relevant information, were first selected. Expert opinions were solicited where published data were unavailable. According to this approach, the number of current patients with liver cancer was 36,563 patients, with an incidence of new patients with liver cancer of 27,936 patients/year. Among these, 25% were considered resectable, and of those, 42% underwent major hepatectomy (Table S2). This resulted in the number of eligible patients being 3839 patients at year 1, with 2933 new patients each year.

The costs incurred for budget impact analysis consisted of the costs of ICG and current practice, diagnostic cost, major hepatectomy cost with and without PHLF, treatment cost of PHLF, and cancer treatment cost with and without PHLF. The costs are presented in Table S2.

Results

Cost-Utility Analysis Findings

Base-Case Analysis

The undiscounted total lifetime costs of ICG and no ICG were 694,269 THB ($19,673) and 690,616 THB ($19,569), while the discounted total lifetime costs of ICG and no ICG were 618,105 THB ($17,515) and 614,506 THB ($17,413), respectively (Table 1). The cost of ICG accounted for only 2.4% of the discounted total lifetime cost, while the most important cost driver was the cancer treatment cost, which accounted for 42.9% of the total lifetime cost (Fig. S1). Compared to no ICG, ICG required an incremental cost of 3600 THB ($102).

Patients receiving ICG had 4.175 discounted QALY, while those with standard diagnosis had 4.174 discounted QALY. The incremental QALY for ICG was 0.001 QALY, resulting in the ICER of 2,763,973 THB/QALY ($78,321). The details of base-case analysis results are presented in Table 2.

Sensitivity, Scenario, and Threshold Analysis

One-way sensitivity analysis indicated that the utility of patients with no PHLF was the most influential factor, which could affect the ICER from approximately − 300% to − 60%. The second most influential factor was the cost of spectrophotometry, which could affect the ICER from approximately − 180% to 0% (Fig. S2).

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis revealed that 58.3% of the 1000 iterations fell into the right-upper quadrant. This indicated that there was a higher possibility for ICG to have better QALY, but it required more resources. However, 37.2% of the iterations fell into the upper-left quadrant, indicating higher cost with lower QALYs. The CEAC showed that ICG had 50% probability of being cost-effective at the WTP of 8.5 million THB ($240,861) per QALY, while the probability of being cost-effective at the current threshold of 160,000 THB ($4533) per QALY was 6.3% (Fig. 2).

Cost-effectiveness acceptability curve. ICG indocyanine green, THB Thai baht

A scenario analysis assuming dose-sharing indicated that the discounted lifetime cost of ICG was 616,582 THB ($17,472). In comparison, that of no ICG was 614,506 THB ($17,413), resulting in the incremental cost of 2077 THB ($59). The incremental QALY was the same as the base-case analysis, at 0.001 QALY, resulting in the ICER of 1,594,744 THB ($45,190) per QALY. Another scenario analysis that varied the probability of PHLF after surgery indicated that the ICER decreased when the possibility of PHLF increased. The ICER reached the current WTP if the possibility of PHLF was 10.1% (Fig. S3).

A threshold analysis to determine the price of ICG, which could result in an ICER of 160,000 THB/QALY ($4533), showed that the price of ICG should be reduced from 1926 THB/vial ($55) to 230 THB/vial ($7).

Budget Impact Analysis Results

Our base-case budget impact analysis indicated that the budget of standard diagnosis increased from approximately 630.2 million THB ($17.9 million) in year 1 to 874.9 million THB ($24.8 million) in year 5. In comparison, the new situation using ICG was 637.7 million THB in year 1 ($18.1 million) and 883.7 million THB ($25.0 million) in year 5. The NBI of the overall budget for 5 years was approximately 39.5 million THB ($1.1 million), or the average NBI was 7.9 million THB/year ($0.2 million/year). The details of the budget impact analysis results are presented in Table 2. Breaking down to cost components, the overall budget for ICG and diagnostic tests accounted for 213.5 million THB over 5 years ($6.0 million), while the overall budget for diagnostic tests in standard diagnosis was 149.6 million THB ($4.2 million), resulting in the NBI of 64.0 million THB ($1.8 million). Our one-way sensitivity analysis assuming a constant 100% uptake rate indicated that the overall 5-year NBI was 51.3 million THB ($1.5 million) or an average of 10.2 million THB ($0.3 million). Changing the percentage resectable from 20% to 15–50% showed the overall 5-year NBI ranged from 23.7 to 79.0 million THB ($0.7–2.2 million). Assuming dose-sharing of ICG, the overall 5-year NBI was 20.8 million THB ($0.6 million), representing a 47.3% reduction compared to the base-case budget impact analysis without dose-sharing (Fig. S4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the cost-utility and budget impact analysis of adding ICG to standard diagnostic approach compared to the standard diagnostic approach alone to guide major hepatectomy in Thailand. We found that ICG could slightly improve QALY compared to the standard diagnostic approach, but it required an additional total lifetime cost. Our analysis indicated that ICG was not cost-effective at its current price. The budget impact analysis indicated that, over the 5 years, the NBI of ICG, in addition to the standard diagnostic approach, was approximately 39.5 million THB ($1.1 million) or 7.9 million THB per year.

To our knowledge, this study is the most updated cost-utility of ICG to guide major hepatectomy in Asian countries. Even though we found that ICG was not cost-effective at the current price, our threshold analysis showed that a reduction of approximately 88.0% in the price of ICG would be required for it to be a cost-effective intervention. Policy decision-makers need to use this as the benchmark price for ICG price negotiation in the public–private negotiation process.

According to data from the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System, a total of 62 ICG-related adverse events were reported from 2004 to 2023 [22]. This highlights the need for continued monitoring of ICG’s safety profile. In our study, we collected data from 400 ICG users across four hospitals, and no adverse events were reported. Safety monitoring protocols were implemented in all hospitals that participated in this study. For example, before ICG injection, patients were screened for any foods or medicines that might interact with ICG. After the injection, patients were observed for approximately 1 h to monitor for potential adverse events.

Our one-way sensitivity analysis indicated that the top two influencing inputs were the utility of no PHLF and the utility of liver recovery. Changes in the inputs could affect the ICER from − 300% to 0% from the base-case results. Thus, the validity of the inputs is critical to our findings. In this study, we conducted a prospective data collection by interviewing patients in the real practice using a standard, widely accepted utility measuring tool (EQ-5D-5L) to measure the health utility of the patients in Thailand [23]. Thus, our approach could reflect the actual health utility in Thailand. Our measurement of the utility is credible.

We also attempted to collect the actual input data used in the economic model and BIA model across Thailand. Four university-affiliated hospitals located in different parts in Thailand were selected as the research settings: two large hospitals in Bangkok, one large hospital in the Northeast, and one large hospital in the North. The input obtained from those hospitals should reflect clinical practice in Thailand. In addition, costs of major hepatectomy and liver failure were obtained from the National Health Security Office (NHSO) database, which gathers information on 70% of the Thai population under the Universal Health Coverage Scheme. Thus, the estimated cost and epidemiologic data used in this study are reliable.

Our literature review could not identify randomized controlled studies reporting the effects of ICG on the occurrence of PHLF compared to those without ICG. We only found that the ICG clearance test had a sensitivity of 25–83%, with a specificity of 66.1–93.8% to predict PHLF [24]. Instead, we conducted a primary data collection of 400 patients and performed a propensity score matched survival analysis with a Weibull distribution to estimate the treatment effects of ICG compared to no ICG on PHLF. We found that ICG could reduce the occurrence of PHLF by 45%, but this was not statistically significant. The treatment effect was applied to assess the cost-effectiveness of ICG for guiding major hepatectomy in this study. We believe the approach is acceptable to estimate the treatment effect when evidence from randomized controlled trials is not available; however, further randomized controlled studies determining the effect are still warranted to improve the reliability and validity of our findings.

Because ICG dose is based on patients’ body weight, which is, on average, 60 kg in Thai patients, two vials of ICG are required for one patient; however, only some of the ICG in the second vial is used. Some ICG is therefore wasted and that affects the cost of ICG. If it is listed in the Thai National List of Essential Medicines, the dose-sharing strategy should be implemented to avoid unnecessary loss.

One of our scenario analyses was the variation of PHLF rate after major hepatectomy. We found that if the PHLF rate was approximately 10.1%, the use of ICG could be cost-effective. In our study, we conducted a retrospective data collection to assess PHLF rate after major hepatectomy. We found only 3.5% of patients with no ICG experienced PHLF. However, a recent study in Thailand indicated that the rate of PHLF was approximately 16.7% [16]. The difference might be due to the definition of PHLF used in the studies. However, this also leads to uncertainty in our findings. Policymakers might need to take this uncertainty into account during the decision process.

Our budget impact analysis, conducted according to good practice for budget impact analysis [25], indicated that the total 5-year NBI when adding ICG to the standard diagnostic approach was approximately 39.5 million THB ($1.1 million). However, the NBI was based on an important assumption that the starting percentage uptake was 60% and increased by 10% each year. The increase in percentage uptake rate was based on the trend of ICG use from one large hospital’s data only, which may have some uncertainty associated with the input. We performed the sensitivity analysis by assuming a constant uptake rate from 60% to 100%, resulting in NBI of approximately 30.0–51.3 million THB ($0.8 million to $1.5 million). Other factors that are likely to have an impact on NBI were also explored. We found that implementing the dose-sharing policy would decrease the NBI by almost half. The total 5-year NBI decreased from 39.5 to 20.8 million THB (from $1.1 million to $0.6 million).

Some limitations should be discussed. First, the clinical effectiveness of ICG on the occurrence of PHLF is also based on our primary data collection, which is an observational study by nature. Confounding bias might affect the observed treatment effects. However, we attempted to minimize the bias using a propensity score matching approach, which is the widely accepted statistical method to minimize bias in observational studies. Second, even though we collected real-world local data to reflect direct non-medical costs and health utility among patients with major hepatectomy, only 54 patients were interviewed, which might not comprehensively represent the overall number of patients in Thailand. Lastly, we collected epidemiologic and cost data from the four university hospitals. The settings were high-volume, tertiary-care hospitals. The data might be different from different types of hospitals, such as provincial or regional hospitals, where service systems might differ from the research settings.

Although this study evaluated the value of ICG testing in a risk-stratified population, the advantages of universal testing before major hepatectomy are also worth considering. Universal ICG testing for patients undergoing surgery may lead to earlier identification of at-risk patients and their appropriate management. However, this approach would need further cost-effectiveness studies and practical evaluations in clinical settings, particularly in limited-resource settings.

Even though ICG is not cost-effective at its current price, it should be included in the benefit package because of its clear reduction in cost of PHLF and an acceptable NBI per year. Several strategies should be recommended to help streamline the budget for policymakers. One such strategy might be to select some sub-population who could gain the optimal benefit from using ICG such as patients with high risk of PHLF, e.g., patients with cirrhosis who previously received chemotherapy and are scheduled to undergo major hepatectomy. Another strategy might be to manage eligible patients to receive the ICG test at the same time and enable implementation of the ICG dose-sharing approach: this would reduce unnecessary medication waste, which could save healthcare costs and promote environmental sustainability in healthcare.

Our findings provide essential evidence to support decision-making for the Universal Coverage Benefit Package under the NHSO. Alongside clinical effectiveness, safety, ethical considerations, and other relevant factors, this evidence will help determine whether ICG detected by spectrophotometer should be included in the positive list for reimbursement under Thailand’s Universal Coverage Scheme.

Conclusion

The addition of ICG to standard diagnostic approach is not considered a cost-effective strategy at the Thai ceiling threshold compared to the standard diagnostic approach alone for major hepatectomy at its current price.
