Identifying potential drug targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernia using Mendelian randomization: a multi-omics study
Authors: Chensong Sun, Ga Liu, Taobin Liu, Haixia Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to identify potential drug targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernia. Mendelian Randomization (MR) was applied to integrate data from druggable gene libraries, cis-pQTL data, and cis-eQTL data to analyze postoperative abdominal wall hernia in the FinnGen R11 population. Sensitivity and heterogeneity tests were conducted, along with reverse causality validation, to explore potential drug targets. A colocalization analysis was performed based on three cis-pQTL databases and cis-eQTL databases. Linkage disequilibrium was verified using Summary Data-Based Mendelian Randomization (SMR), and potential drug side effects were assessed using existing databases. Finally, drug target predictions and molecular docking studies were conducted. MR analysis revealed that, within the druggable target range, only one druggable gene, cathepsin S (CTSS) (FDR < 0.05; OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.06–1.20, P = 6.79 × 10e-5), was positively correlated with postoperative abdominal wall hernia. Colocalization analysis showed PPH4 > 0.7 in the cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL gene window of ± 50 kb. A key variant (rs41305070) was identified. Evaluation of drug side effects and molecular docking demonstrated that CTSS holds potential as a promising druggable target. Elevated levels of CTSS increase the risk of postoperative abdominal wall hernia, making CTSS a promising therapeutic target for this condition.Keywords: Postoperative abdominal wall hernia, Mendelian randomization, CTSS, Drug target, pQTL, eQTLSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Biomarkers, Medical research

Introduction

Abdominal wall hernias refer to a condition in which abdominal organs (such as the intestines) protrude through weak areas of the abdominal wall due to increased intra-abdominal pressure. Hernias can occur through natural openings, surgical incisions, or trauma, and their classification varies based on location1–3. Postoperative abdominal wall hernias are very common in the population and are closely related to prior surgeries4. Many specific factors can increase the risk of developing postoperative hernias, such as smoking, age, and wound infections5. In recent years, with the advancements in genomics and proteomics, genetic-based early intervention and optimized surgical techniques for hernia diseases have become possible. A large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) involving 72,805 surgically confirmed inguinal hernia patients and 67,510 controls identified four new susceptibility loci for inguinal hernias: EFEMP1, WT1, EBF2, and ADAMTS66. Another study using hernia ring/neck tissues and hernia sac tissues from hernia patients revealed that the differential expression of connective tissue growth factor, α-smooth muscle actin, CD34, cadherin-11, and fibroblast-specific protein-1 might provide new insights for the study of incisional hernias7. However, there is currently a lack of research exploring the causal relationship between omics technologies and postoperative abdominal wall hernias, and few drug interventions for treating this condition are available.

Mendelian Randomization has rapidly developed as a method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to explore causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. This approach has gained significant attention in hernia-related research8,9. Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) are essential for understanding the genetic control of protein expression and gene transcription, with single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) playing a key role in their regulation. Combining these data helps strengthen evidence for drug target validation and improves the selection of therapeutic targets10,11. Cis-pQTLs, which regulate expression within gene regions, explain the regulatory relationship between genetic variation and protein levels and can complement cis-eQTL data12. The use of cis data provides a more direct explanation of gene expression control and allows for more precise identification of drug targets13.

This study integrates cis-pQTL data from proteomics and genomics to use MR analysis in the search for drug targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernias. Using cis-pQTL data from the INTERVAL study, cis-eQTL data from GTEx V8, and druggable gene libraries as exposure factors for two-sample MR analysis, TNXB and CTSS were identified as potential causal proteins. Following SMR validation, HEIDI testing, and colocalization analysis across multiple datasets, CTSS emerged as a potential drug target. Subsequent pharmacological evaluations and molecular docking studies offered new insights for drug development.

Methods

Research design

The analysis followed these steps: (1) A preliminary screening of druggable targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernia was conducted using a dataset intersection of druggable genes, cis-pQTL, and cis-eQTL summary data. (2) The dataset intersection was used to perform two-step two-sample MR analysis to determine the causal relationship between screened targets and postoperative abdominal wall hernia, aiming to reduce the range of false-positive results. The two-step MR analysis included: (3) The associations between cis-pQTL and postoperative abdominal wall hernia, as well as between cis-eQTL and the same outcome, were analyzed. Colocalization analysis was performed on the cis-pQTL positive results obtained through MR to ensure the robustness of the findings. (4) The stability of the results was enhanced by validating the MR and colocalization findings using additional gene and protein datasets. (5) The positive genes identified through MR analysis were further analyzed using the SMR method. (6) Using the Phenome-Wide Association Study (PheWAS) database, potential side effects of the identified druggable targets on other postoperative conditions and related metabolic disorders were evaluated. Candidate drugs for positive proteins were predicted using DSigDB, followed by scoring. (7) The interaction patterns between the drugs and positive protein targets were evaluated through molecular docking.The specific steps are shown in Fig. 1. To ensure transparency and completeness in reporting, this study adhered to the STROBE-MR guidelines for Mendelian Randomization studies14.

Research design flowchart.

The MR analysis followed three main assumptions: (1) Instrumental variables (IVs) are significantly associated with the exposure; (2) IVs are not associated with confounding factors that influence both the exposure and outcome; (3) IVs affect the outcome solely through their impact on the exposure.

Data selection

1. Druggable gene dataset

Finan et al15. defined druggable genes as those that encode proteins that could potentially be modulated by drug-like molecules, making them suitable targets for therapeutic interventions. GWAS hold great potential for rediscovering known drug targets and providing valuable insights for future drug development. A total of 4,463 druggable genes identified by Finan et al. were used for this study.

2. pQTL dataset

The pQTL data were sourced from the INTERVAL study (SUN_BB)16, the deCODE study17, and Suhre’s research18. The INTERVAL study, used as the discovery cohort, recruited 3,301 healthy participants of European ancestry. This dataset includes 3,282 proteins. A cis-window threshold of ± 1 Mb and a SNP significance threshold of P < 5 × 10e-8 were set to explore the relationship between protein levels encoded by druggable genes and postoperative abdominal wall hernia.

3. eQTL Dataset

The GTEx V8 whole blood dataset from a European population was used. The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project is a large-scale study designed to investigate the relationship between genetic variation and gene expression across multiple human tissues, covering eQTL data for 54 different tissues19. The same cis-window threshold of ± 1 Mb was applied, with a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10e-8.

4. Outcome data

The data for postoperative abdominal wall hernia were obtained from the FinnGen study. FinnGen is a large-scale biobank research project that integrates genetic data from approximately half a million participants with their national health records. Its primary goal is to explore the genetic basis of diseases and advance personalized medicine through the identification of genetic variants associated with various health conditions. The data were sourced from FinnGen R11, which includes 7,161 cases and 255,273 controls. The GWAS summary statistics for postoperative abdominal wall hernia are available on the website (https://r11.risteys.finregistry.fi/endpoints/ABDOM_HERNIA_POSTOP), and all the outcome data come from a European population.

TSMR analysis

The R package “TwosampleMR” was used to analyze the association between drug targets (cis-pQTL) and the GWAS summary statistics for abdominal wall hernia. For quality control, several sensitivity analyses were conducted.

1. Cis-pQTL Data Selection and Instrument Validation

In the first step, cis-pQTL data with a significance threshold of P < 5×10e-8 and a window of 10,000 kb were selected, as previously described.To maximize the number of available instruments for MR analysis, linkage disequilibrium (LD) based on the 1000 Genomes Project European population was set to R2 < 0.1 to select instruments for each gene expression level10,20,21. The F-statistic was calculated using the formula: F = (/SE)^222. IVs with an F-statistic less than 10 were excluded to avoid weak instrument bias. For druggable genes with only one available SNP, the Wald ratio method was used for analysis. If two or more SNPs were available, the inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, and weighted median methods were used, ensuring consistent directional effects across all methods. MR-Egger intercept testing was performed to detect bias due to directional pleiotropy. Cochrane’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity, and FDR correction was applied for multiple testing in discovery analyses10.

2. Cis-eQTL data analysis

In the second step, positive results from the first MR analysis were further analyzed using cis-eQTL data.A stricter threshold was applied for eQTLs to reduce the risk of weak or pleiotropic instruments, given the higher variability and context-dependency of transcriptomic data, Specifically, a window of 10,000 kb was used, and the LD threshold was set at R2 ≤ 0.001, while other parameters remained consistent with those in the first step.

3. Steiger filtering for causal direction

In the third step, Steiger filtering was used to verify the causal direction of individual SNPs and the overall SNP set, enhancing the reliability of the MR analysis results. Steiger filtering validation was conducted for both cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL data in relation to postoperative abdominal wall hernia outcomes23.

Colocalization

The colocalization analysis focused on a smaller region24. Specifically, the probability that cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL within ± 50 kb of the gene window share causal variants with postoperative abdominal wall hernia outcomes was assessed. The “coloc” R package (version 5.2.3) was used for colocalization analysis (https://github.com/chr1swallace/coloc), and the “LocusCompareR” package (version 1.0.0) was used for visual representation of results25. The focus was primarily on posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PPH4), with PPH4 > 0.7 considered strong evidence for shared causal variants21. To strengthen the robustness of the findings, the colocalization results were validated using two independent cis-pQTL datasets.

SMR analysis

The SMR method was used to integrate the GWAS data for postoperative abdominal wall hernia with eQTL and pQTL data to distinguish between pleiotropy and linkage disequilibrium, thus determining the causal relationship between gene/protein expression levels and complex traits26. SMR software version 1.3.1 developed by the Yang Lab at Westlake University (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#ExecutableFiles(version1.3.1)), was used with a significance threshold of P < 5e-8 and MAF > 0.01. SMR results with P < 0.05 and P_HEIDI > 0.05 were considered reliable, indicating that the association between eQTL/pQTL data and postoperative abdominal wall hernia outcomes was not influenced by confounding factors.

PheWAS and drug evaluation

To explore the potential side effects of the identified druggable targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernia, the PheWAS Portal (https://azphewas.com/) was used to conduct phenome-wide association studies at the gene level27. This portal reveals the associations between genetic variations and various clinical phenotypes (diseases and traits). The UK Biobank 470 k (v5) Public dataset, comprising approximately 470,000 participants, was used. A default significance threshold of P ≤ 1e-8, and a suggestive threshold of P ≤ 1e-6 were applied, following the guidelines of the PheWAS database.

To assess the potential of the druggable gene CTSS as a therapeutic target, the drug signature database DSigDB28 was used to predict connections between target genes and drugs or other chemical compounds. The Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) was specifically utilized to rank the predicted drugs in DSigDB by p-value.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking plays a key role in designing ligands compatible with macromolecular binding sites. Sybyl-X 2.1.1 was primarily used for the docking studies29. The 3D structures of drug ligands were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted to mol2 format using OpenBabelGUI 3.1.1 (https://github.com/openbabel/openbabel/releases). The X-ray diffraction structure of Homo sapiens CTSS (PDB ID: 2H7J) was selected from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/).

Results

Selection of gene sets

By cross-referencing druggable genes with the significant cis-eQTL genes from GTEx V8 whole blood data and the cis-pQTL data from INTERVAL study (SUN_BB), a total of 1,037 druggable genes were identified. For duplicate proteins, the data with the larger F-statistic were selected32. Based on the criteria for instrumental variables, 507 druggable genes were ultimately identified for further analysis.These results are shown in Fig. 2 (1).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis

A total of 62,751 SNPs within the pQTL range were included, as listed in Supplementary Table S1. The SNPs used for the final analysis are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

The first-step pQTL two-sample Mendelian randomization results are shown in Fig. 2 (2) and (3), highlighting several results with the most significant p-values. All analysis results are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Only two results had P < 0.05 after FDR correction (P/507), indicating that Tenascin-X (TNXB) (prot-a-3071) and CTSS (prot-a-747) exhibited a promotive effect on postoperative abdominal wall hernia. Cochrane’s Q test (Supplementary Table S4) and MR-Egger regression (Supplementary Table S5) showed no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy for either TNXB or CTSS. Additionally, the results of MR-PRESSO (Supplementary Table S6) further confirmed the robustness of these two positive proteins. Scatter plots, funnel plots, and leave-one-out plots of these positive results are included in the Supplementary Figs. 1–3. The Steiger filtering and directionality tests (Supplementary Table S7) showed consistent results from both individual SNP tests and overall SNP results, confirming that the causal direction is from protein to postoperative abdominal wall hernia.

Cis-eQTL two-sample MR analysis, TNXB could not be analyzed due to insufficient instrumental variables. However, in the whole blood cis-eQTL of CTSS (PDR = 0.003, OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.20–2.17), a significant association with an increased risk of postoperative abdominal wall hernia was identified. Details of the results are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis was performed using cis-pQTL data for CTSS and cis-eQTL data, focusing on the region within ± 50 kb of the gene window for druggable genes to assess the likelihood of shared causal genetic variants. The colocalization results for cis-pQTL from INTERVAL study are shown in Fig. 3A, with PPH4 = 70.0%. The colocalization results for cis-eQTL from GTEx V8 are shown in Fig. 3B, with PPH4 = 71.9%. The combined cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL results indicate that the PPH4 is between 50 and 80%. The top SNP (rs41305070) was shared across both cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL for postoperative abdominal wall hernia, strongly suggesting that CTSS and postoperative abdominal wall hernia share the same causal variant, with rs41305070 as the key SNP.

Discussion

Cis-pQTL data from the INTERVAL study and cis-eQTL data from GTEx V8 were used to conduct Mendelian Randomization analysis, aiming to identify druggable targets for postoperative abdominal wall hernia. The experimental results passed sensitivity and heterogeneity tests. Furthermore, colocalization analysis identified a key SNP (rs41305070), which was shown to be a shared causal variant for both cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL, and the results were validated in other pQTL datasets. The study confirmed a causal relationship between CTSS and postoperative abdominal wall hernia, with elevated CTSS levels increasing the risk of hernia. Drug target evaluations and molecular docking studies were also conducted to explore potential therapeutic interventions.

Surgery is an effective treatment for many diseases, and as medical science progresses, more individuals are undergoing surgical procedures. However, this increase in surgeries is accompanied by complications, and postoperative abdominal wall hernia is one of the most common complications, which is closely related to surgical techniques5 . Proteases play a crucial role in collagen metabolism, which is vital for maintaining the structural integrity of the abdominal wall. Changes in collagen composition and degradation can weaken the abdominal wall and lead to hernia formation33. As a member of the cathepsin family of proteases, CTSS has been proven to play an important role in respiratory diseases, pain, autoimmune diseases, and cancer34.

Although there has been no definitive research directly linking CTSS to the development of abdominal wall hernias, accumulating evidence suggests that CTSS may play a critical role through its involvement in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Changes in the ECM, particularly in the fascia and skin, have been observed in patients with hernias35,36. The ECM is thought to be closely related to the mechanical strength of the abdominal wall in hernia development, and its main component is collagen. The amount of collagen present is a significant factor in the onset of hernias37. CTSS, which is continuously expressed in macrophages and dendritic cells, has been a popular topic in the remodeling of the ECM38. CTSS induces macrophage and vascular smooth muscle cell migration and the degradation of elastin, conversely, deletion of CTSS prevents ECM damage and reduces inflammatory responses39. Furthermore, Elafin, an endogenous human proteinase inhibitor, can inhibit CTSS activity and has been shown to decrease collagen expression in fibroblasts40, highlighting CTSS’s regulatory effect on collagen turnover. Zuo et al41 demonstrated in their experimental study that there is a strong correlation between CTSS and collagen-containing ECM structures, with CTSS degrading collagen 18A1, thereby remodeling ECM activity. The balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) is critical for maintaining ECM function and structure. In microvascular endothelial cells, overexpression of CTSS increases MMP concentrations, leading to ECM degradation42. In incisional hernia tissue samples collected post-surgery, abnormal MMP/TIMP ratios and inflammatory signals result in dysfunctional ECM and abnormal abdominal wall tissues43. In corneal epithelial cells, CTSS exposure at various time points (2, 4, 8, and 24 h) significantly increased MMP-9 gene expression compared to controls, further indicating that CTSS regulates MMP activity and promotes matrix breakdown44. Collectively, these findings suggest that CTSS plays a key role in ECM remodeling, collagen degradation, and MMP regulation, all of which are pivotal processes in hernia formation. This supports the hypothesis that CTSS is causally related to postoperative abdominal wall hernias and highlights its potential as a promising therapeutic target for hernia treatment.

The experimental results consistently demonstrated that both cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL MR analyses indicated a promotive effect of CTSS on postoperative abdominal wall hernias. Additionally, colocalization analyses across four datasets showed PPH4 > 0.7. It is worth noting that there is limited research on rs41305070. In the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/variants/rs41305070), it has been shown to be associated with creatinine levels and the use of beta-blocking agents.To further assess the therapeutic potential of CTSS, 10 drugs linked to CTSS expression using the DSigDB database, and molecular docking studies were performed. Two drugs, valsartan and verteporfin, both of which could downregulate CTSS expression, exhibited good binding activity. Currently, there are no reports linking CTSS and verteporfin. Valsartan is an angiotensin receptor antagonist used as an antihypertensive drug that works by blocking the binding of angiotensin II to its receptor45. Valsartan is also used to treat heart failure46 and acute myocardial infarction47.In a noteworthy double-blind, randomized trial involving 38 patients with glucose metabolism disorders, those receiving 320 mg/d of valsartan for 26 weeks showed a significant reduction in CTSS mRNA expression in abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue, and a marked decrease in adipocyte size compared to the control group48. Although no direct studies have investigated the use of valsartan in hernia treatment, their ability to remodel the ECM and reduce CTSS expression suggests a potential for repurposing in postoperative abdominal wall hernia therapy.

The strength of this study lies in the fact that, to our knowledge, it is the first study to explore the relationship between postoperative abdominal wall hernia and drug targets using a multi-omics approach. By employing methods such as SMR, we reduced the influence of confounding factors. In the process of colocalizing eQTL data with three pQTL datasets, we identified a top SNP (rs41305070) shared across both eQTL and two pQTL datasets. We also evaluated the drug safety and potential of CTSS as a druggable target.

However, there are limitations to this study. First, when conducting LD analyses, we applied a relatively lenient threshold (R2 = 0.1), it may increase the risk of including weaker instruments, which could affect the robustness of the MR analysis. Future studies should consider using a more stringent threshold (e.g., R2 = 0.01) to improve sensitivity and reduce potential bias. Second, this study primarily relies on data from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Future studies should aim to include more diverse populations to determine whether the observed associations hold true across different ethnicities. Third, we only utilized cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data, lacking trans data, which introduces some limitations. Cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data capture genetic effects that are localized to specific genomic regions, but they do not account for the broader, more complex trans-regulatory mechanisms that may be crucial for understanding the full spectrum of genetic influences. Lastly, we included only the GTEx V8 whole blood eQTL dataset, without incorporating more eQTL datasets for validation. The GTEx V8 dataset is comprehensive, but it represents a specific population, and additional datasets could help mitigate any potential population-specific biases and further validate the results. Future work is needed to validate these findings through in vivo functional studies or clinical trials targeting CTSS, to confirm its therapeutic potential for postoperative abdominal wall hernia.

Conclusion

This study identified cathepsin S as a promising therapeutic target for postoperative abdominal wall hernia through Mendelian Randomization analysis combined with multi-omics data. Results from cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL MR analyses consistently showed an association between elevated CTSS levels and increased hernia risk. Colocalization and SMR validations further supported CTSS as a causative factor, identifying rs41305070 as a key SNP shared across datasets. A graphical summary of the study design and key findings is presented in Fig. 6. Subsequent drug evaluations and molecular docking identified valsartan and verteporfin as potential inhibitors of CTSS, with valsartan already approved by the FDA. This multi-omics approach highlights CTSS as a viable target for therapeutic intervention in postoperative abdominal wall hernia, paving the way for targeted drug development.
