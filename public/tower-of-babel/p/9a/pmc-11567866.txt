Factors associated with cognitive reserve according to education level
Authors: Yeshin Kim, Yaakov Stern, Sang Won Seo, Duk L Na, Jae‐Won Jang, Hyemin Jang, Cognitive Reserve Research Group of Korea Dementia Association
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe investigated distinctive factors associated with cognitive reserve (CR) based on education level.METHODSAmong 1247 participants who underwent neuropsychological assessment, amyloid positron emission tomography, and brain magnetic resonance imaging, 336 participants with low education (≤6 years) and 697 with high education (≥12 years) were selected. CR was measured as the difference between the predicted and observed value of cognitive function based on cortical thickness. Multiple linear regression was conducted in each group after controlling for age and sex.RESULTSIn the low‐education group, low literacy, long sleep duration(>8 h/day), and diabetes were negatively associated with CR, whereas cognitive and physical activity were positively associated with CR. In the high‐education group, cognitive activity was positively related to CR, whereas low literacy, long sleep duration (> 8 h/day), and depression were negatively related to CR.DISCUSSIONThis study provides insights into different strategies for enhancing CR based on educational background.Highlights Factors associated with cognitive reserve (CR) varied according to the education level.Diabetes and physical activity were associated with CR in the low‐education group.Depression was related to CR in the high‐education group.Low literacy, sleep duration, and cognitive activity were associated with CR in both groups.Dementia‐prevention strategies should be tailored according to educational level. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cognitive reserve, education, risk factors

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of dementia is increasing rapidly, causing enormous economic and social burdens worldwide. 1 , 2 Although Alzheimer's disease (AD), characterized by the accumulation of amyloid and hyperphosphorylated tau, is the most common cause of dementia, 3 , 4 many other pathologies contribute to neurodegeneration with consequent cognitive impairment. Cognitive reserve (CR) is defined as the adaptability of cognitive processes that helps to explain differential susceptibility of cognitive abilities or day‐to‐day function to brain aging, pathology, or insult. 5 Despite the presence of the same amount of AD pathology, there can be different individual manifestations of the disease, including differences in onset of cognitive impairment and disease progression. 6 Individuals with high CR can compensate for brain pathology, and may therefore have delayed onset of dementia, even with substantial pathological burdens. 7 , 8 , 9 To measure CR, the socio‐behavioral proxies, including education years, have been widely used. 5 Recently, residual approaches have been used to quantify CR rather than proxies. This method applies statistical models that use brain factors (e.g., cerebral volume, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 cortical thickness, 11 or tau accumulation 10 , 11 ) as predictors for cognition. CR is considered as the residual, calculated as the difference between predicted cognition and observed cognition that is unexplained by predictors. 5 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Among various risk factors, higher education is most notably related to higher CR and a reduced risk of dementia. The risk of dementia decreases by 7% with each additional year of education. 15 , 16 Considering that differences in education reflect differences in many other health‐related factors including socioeconomic status (SES), we could expect that low‐ and high‐education groups might have different risk factors for dementia. However, these distinct risk factors have not been thoroughly investigated, and low‐education groups have been particularly under‐represented in previous research. For example, many previous CR studies included individuals with relatively high levels of education, with the average education years ranging from 9.0 years to the 16 years observed in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. 15 Therefore, exploring distinctive risk factors related to CR between the two education level groups is essential to understanding and narrowing the disparities in dementia prevention and care.

In this study, our objective was to examine whether different factors are associated with CR in the low‐ and high‐education groups, categorized by ≤6 years and ≥12 years of education, utilizing a large multi‐center cohort dataset. To achieve this, we required a CR measure that did not rely on life exposure proxies such as education. Instead, we quantified CR using the residual approach. 5 We measured CR as residuals calculated as the difference between predicted cognition and observed cognition that is unexplained by cortical thickness. Although various neuropathologic markers can be used, as in many previous studies, our study used cortical thickness, which is directly associated with cognition as the most downstream biomarker. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 After calculating CR, we investigated the relationship between known risk or protective factors for dementia and CR. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 These factors encompass socioeconomic factors (literacy, occupational experience, and income), health‐related behaviors (sleep duration, social activity, cognitive activity, physical activity, nutrition, alcohol, and smoking), vascular disk factors (presence of hypertension and diabetes, and body mass index [BMI]), and psychologic factors (depression, anxiety, and quality of life). If the factors influencing CR vary based on education level, this would have clinical implications for the development of distinct dementia‐prevention strategies contingent on education levels.

METHODS

Study participants

We included participants from a nationwide multi‐center cohort, the Establishment of Precision Medicine Platform for Early Diagnosis of Mild Cognitive Impairment (PREMIER) consortium study that comprised 25 memory clinics in Korea. For this cohort, all participants were 40 years of age or older and were required to have a study partner to provide an independent evaluation of functioning. All participants had to agree to undergo brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), neuropsychological tests, blood examination for biomarkers, amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), and/or lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid amyloid and tau. In addition, all participants and caregivers were required to complete a detailed questionnaire. The general exclusion criteria were: inadequate visual and auditory acuity for neuropsychological testing; diagnosis of psychiatric diseases including schizophrenia, alcohol addiction, previously diagnosed mental disability, and uncontrolled major depressive disorder; cancer diagnosis within 2 years or diagnosis of unstable illnesses such as uncontrolled dyspnea; medical history of neurosurgery; and subcortical vascular cognitive impairment, defined as having severe white matter hyperintensities on MRI.

Of 1597 recruited participants, including those with cognitively unimpaired (CU), amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI), and Alzheimer's disease dementia (ADD), between January 2018 and December 2020, a total of 350 participants were finally excluded due to missing data on neuropsychological battery, MRI, and quantifiable amyloid PET. A comparison of basic demographic characteristics between included and excluded participants is provided in Table S1. Then, 1247 participants were divided into two groups based on their educational attainment: a low‐education group (≤6 years) and a high‐education group (≥12 years). Two hundred fourteen participants with educational attainment of >6 years and <12 years were excluded (Figure 1).

Flow diagram for study participants ADD, Alzheimer's disease dementia; CU, cognitively unimpaired; MCI, mild cognitive impairment.

CU participants fulfilled these criteria: (1) subjective memory complaints by participants or caregivers, (2) no objective cognitive dysfunction on any cognitive domain as assessed by scores from evaluations, (3) no history of medical diseases likely to affect cognitive function, and (4) no significant impairment in activities of daily living (ADLs) based on Korean version of instrumental ADL scales (K‐IADL). All patients diagnosed with aMCI met both the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) core clinical criteria for aMCI. 21 These patients also met these modified Petersen's criteria 22 : (1) subjective memory complaints by patients or caregivers, (2) normal activities of daily living based on clinical findings and the K‐IADL, 23 and (3) objective memory dysfunction (Z score of –1.0 standard deviation [SD] below those of age‐ and education‐matched norms). Participants with ADD met the NIA‐AA core clinical criteria for probable ADD. 24

The institutional review board of Kangwon National University Hospital approved this study (approval number: [KNUH‐2022‐10‐011]), and the study followed the ethical guidelines specified in the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the data used in this study were retrospective and de‐identified, the requirement for obtaining informed consent from the participants was waived.

Neuropsychological tests and measurement of a cognitive composite score

All participants underwent neuropsychological tests using the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery (SNSB) that is comprised of tests for attention, memory, language, frontal‐executive function, and visuospatial function. 25 , 26 We calculated composite scores by adding all cognitive domain scores in the following manner. 25 , 27 The Boston Naming Test (BNT; score range: 0–60) was used to determine language function. The Rey Complex Figure Test (RCFT; score range: 0–36) was used to measure visuospatial function. Memory score was computed by adding the scores from verbal memory tests (Seoul Verbal Learning Test immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition scores) and visual memory tests (RCFT immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition scores). Maximum memory score was 144. The frontal‐executive function score was derived by summing scores from the category word generation test, phonemic word generation test, and Stroop color reading test. Maximum frontal‐executive function score was 55.

MRI protocols and measurement of cortical thickness

All participants underwent brain MRI, which included three‐dimensional (3D) T1 and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) as standard protocols using a 3.0T MRI scanner (Philips 3.0T Achieva, SIEMENS 3T Skyra, and GE 3T Discovery MR750/MAGNETOM Trio). The T1‐weighted and T2 FLAIR data were collected using a standard isotropic voxel size of 1 mm3 and transferred to the central lab for imaging analyses. Image processing was performed using the Computer Imaging, Visualization, and Evaluation of Tissues anatomic pipeline. 28 Native T1‐weighted images underwent registration to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) anatomic pipeline. 29 This was followed by the intensity‐based non‐uniformities correction using the N3 algorithm. 30 Subsequently, the images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and background using the Intensity‐Normalized Stereotaxic Environment for Classification of Tissues algorithm. 28 The ventricular volume (lateral, third, and fourth ventricles) and extracerebral CSF volume were obtained using Automated Nonlinear Imaging Matching and Anatomical Labelling parcellation on native space. 29 We used the Constrained Laplacian‐Based Automated Segmentation with Proximities algorithm to automatically extract the surfaces of the inner and outer cortex. 31 The Euclidean distance between the linked vertices of these areas was determined as cortical thickness. 32 However, the thickness was calculated in native brain space rather than Talairach space, owing to the limitations of linear stereotaxic normalization. The MR volume in the native space was transformed into the stereotaxic space using a linear formation matrix and was reconstructed with the inverse transformation matrix. 33 Finally, the global thickness of the cortical surface was calculated for each subject.

Amyloid PET acquisition and quantification of amyloid uptakes

All participants underwent either 18F‐florbetaben (FBB) PET or 18F‐flutemetamol (FMM) PET. Imaging acquisition was performed according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Briefly, a 20‐min emission PET scan in dynamic mode (consisting of 4×5 min frames) was performed 90 min following injection of a mean dose of 311.5 MBq FBB or a mean dose of 197.7 MBqFMM. Three‐dimensional PET images were reconstructed in a 128×128×48 matrix with a 2×2×3.27 mm voxel size using the ordered‐subset expectation maximization algorithm (FBB, iteration = 4 and subset = 20; FMM, iteration = 4 and subset = 20). To quantify global amyloid uptakes on FBB and FMM PET, we used Centiloid (CL) values by replicating the image processing steps described in the previously published study. 34

Measurement of CR

To capture CR, we used the residual approach. 5 , 11 , 12 , 13 Using the total data set of 1247 participants, we predicted each individual's cognitive composite score as a dependent variable from linear regression model with global cortical thickness as independent variable (as described in the previous section), after controlling for age, sex, and global amyloid uptake represented by CL value as covariates. Then we obtained residuals, which represent the difference between predicted cognitive score and actual cognitive score, from the prediction model. We defined this as CR (= CR_rsd, actual cognition–predicted cognition) (Figure 2). Representative cases from Figure 2 are demonstrated in Figure 3.

Definition of the low‐education and high‐education groups

To define the education groups, we categorized participants into two groups based on education duration: the low‐education group had 6 years or less of education (elementary school graduate), and the high‐education group had 12 years or more of education (high school graduate). First, the criterion for low education, primary education for 6 years, holds particular significance considering the socio‐cultural context in Korea. In the country, the policy of compulsory primary education was completed in 1971. Therefore, considering the age range of the elderly population visiting memory clinics in Korea, a significant portion of the elderly population, particularly residing in rural areas, likely has an education level of primary education or below, as they grew up during the transitional period when primary education was becoming compulsory. For high education, we set the cutoff at 12 years or more, encompassing individuals who have completed high school education, allowing for unrestricted participation in most social activities (it is generally understood that in Korea, completion of the 12‐year education curriculum is a prerequisite for most office‐related occupations). We excluded participants with an education level between 6 and 12 years from the main study, considering them as a “gray zone” that does not adequately reflect the characteristics of the polarized education level groups. We examined the relationship between CR and several variables, separately in the low‐ and high‐education groups.

Variable assessments

The dependent variables were obtained from self‐reported and caregiver‐reported questionnaires. These included socioeconomic factors (literacy, occupational experience, and income), health‐related behaviors (sleep duration, social activity, cognitive activity, physical activity, nutrition, alcohol, and smoking), vascular disk factors (presence of hypertension and diabetes, and BMI), and psychologic factors (depression, anxiety, and quality of life). Low literacy was defined as the ability to read only simple materials and to write only names and addresses or less. Hypertension was defined by self or caregiver reports or by current use of antihypertensive medication, and diabetes was defined by self or caregiver reports, by a specialist's diagnosis, or by current use of diabetic medications or insulin. BMI was calculated as weight (in kg) divided by height (in meters) squared, and participants were classified based on recommendations into four BMI subgroups: <18.5 kg/m2, underweight; 18.5–23 kg/m2, normal; 23–25 kg/m2, overweight; and >25 kg/m2, obese. 35 A group with normal weight (BMI 18.5‐23 kg/m2) was used as a reference group. Participants were asked about occupational experience (yes or no). As a health‐related behavior, total sleep duration per day was obtained and categorized as <7 h, 7–8 h, and >8 h per day. Another health‐related behavior, cognitive and social activity, was investigated and measured using a 16‐item questionnaire we developed to assess the duration and frequency of various cognitive and social activities within the past year. Then the duration of each activity was calculated based on the time participants spent engaging in that activity over a week. Physical activity (PA), another health‐related behavior, was measured by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which assessed the duration (time per day) and frequency (days per week) of different types of PA, including vigorous PA, moderate PA, walking, and sedentary activity in the preceding 7 days. According to the IPAQ scoring protocol, responses were converted to Metabolic Equivalent Task minutes per week (MET‐min/week). 36 The dietary pattern was investigated using the Mini Dietary Assessment Index (MDA), a 10‐item questionnaire to assess nutrition and overall dietary quality. The items in the questionnaire included encouraged food elements (e.g., milk, meat, vegetables, and fruits), limited food elements (e.g., fried foods, high‐fat foods, and foods high in salt and sugar), and dietary lifestyle factors (e.g., regulation and variety). Each element was rated on a 3‐point scale (1, 3, 5). The total score of the MDA ranged from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating a healthier dietary pattern. Alcohol intake (units/week) and smoking (pack‐years) were also calculated. Psychological factors were measured using a short version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15, range 0–15), the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (GAI, range 0–20), and the Geriatric Quality of Life‐Dementia scale (GQOL‐D, range 15–60). The GQOL‐D is a 15‐item questionnaire assessing the quality of life (QoL) on a scale of 1 to 4 for each item.

Statistical analysis

A comparison of demographics and clinical characteristics between the two education groups was performed using the independent‐sample t‐tests and chi‐square tests, as appropriate. Univariate analyses were conducted to investigate clinical factors associated with CR in each education group. Factors found to be significant in univariate analyses at a level of p < 0.05 were chosen for inclusion in the multiple linear regression analyses, with CR as the dependent variable and the identified factors as independent variables. Age and sex were included as covariates. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) and R studio (version 2023.12.0+369). p‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 1247 participants were categorized into three groups according to years of education, ≤6 (low‐education, N = 336; 26.9%), >6 and <12 (N = 214; 17.2%), and ≥12 (high‐education, N = 697; 55.9%). The characteristics of the total and each group are presented in Table S2. In this study, we included the low‐ and high‐education groups only (N = 1033), and characteristics of included study participants are presented in Table 1. The mean (SD) years of education were 4.6 (2.2) and 14.3 (2.6) years for the low‐ and high‐education groups, respectively. The mean CR_rsd were −34.4 (67.5) in the low‐education group and 22.0 (69.4) in the high‐education group (p < 0.001). In the low‐education group, there was a comparatively greater proportion of female participants (83.9 vs 56.8%, p < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of low‐literacy participants (17.6 vs 2.3%, p < 0.001) compared to the high‐education group. Table 1 shows the disparities between the two groups regarding all structural and social determinants of health (SSDoH). The low‐education group had a lower proportion of occupational experiences (57.6 vs 74.4%, p < 0.001) compared to the high‐education group. Moreover, the low‐education group had lower levels of engagement in social (5.0 vs 6.8 hours/week, p = 0.003), and cognitive (3.2 vs 11.0 h/week, p < 0.001), than the high‐education group. In addition, the low‐education group had lower alcohol consumption (5.6 vs 9.7 units/week, p = 0.003) and smoking (3.7 vs. 6.6 units/week, p = 0.001), but more vascular risk factors. These factors included hypertension (57.9% vs 44.9%, p = 0.001) and diabetes (26.8% vs 18.1%, p = 0.001). The low‐education group also had higher scores for depression (4.0 vs 3.5 points, p < 0.001) and anxiety (6.1 vs 5.1 points, p < 0.001) and a lower QoL score (7.9 vs 9.0 points, p < 0.001) compared to the high‐education group.

DISCUSSION

In this study we investigated clinical factors associated with CR in the low‐ and high‐education groups of a large multi‐center cohort. The major findings in this study were the following. First, the two education level groups had distinct baseline characteristics. Second, within the low‐education group we found that low literacy, long sleep duration (>8 h/day), and diabetes were negatively associated with CR, whereas frequent cognitive activity was positively associated with CR. Third, in the high‐education group, cognitive activity was positively associated with CR, whereas low literacy, long sleep duration (> 8 h/day), and depression were negatively associated with CR. Taken together, our study findings demonstrated that CR is associated with different modifiable risk factors according to the education level. This supports the consideration of education level in dementia‐prevention strategies.

Our first major finding was that the low‐ and high‐education groups had different baseline characteristics. The low‐education group was older and more likely to be female. This can be attributed to the historical context of elderly Koreans, especially females, who had limited access to education during both the Japanese colonial period (1910‐1945) and the Korean War (1950‐1953). The absence of early‐life education and low literacy may be strongly connected to an individual's SES in mid‐ and late life, affecting their occupational attainment and income. These factors can also influence the regulation of healthy behaviors and vascular risk factors, 37 , 38 particularly due to a lack of awareness regarding modifiable risks for dementia. As a result, the low‐education group demonstrated lower engagement in social, cognitive, and physical activities and had a higher prevalence of vascular risk factors. In addition, this group reported higher levels of depression and anxiety and a poorer quality of life. All of these components are categorized under SSDoH. These contrasting characteristics support that the well‐known association between low education and dementia may be linked to various other risk factors, including lower SES, higher vascular disease risks, and worse health‐related behaviors, associated with lower levels of education. 37 , 38 , 39 Unexpectedly, an interesting finding in our study was that the low‐education group consumed less alcohol, smoked less, and had better nutrition scores than the high‐education group. Alcohol and smoking habits are also related to social activity, and the relationship between alcohol and dementia risk has been inconsistently reported. 40 Better nutrition scores in the low‐education group might be attributed to living in a rural area and consuming less processed food. Our second major finding was that in the low‐education group, having long sleep duration(>8 h/day) and diabetes were negatively associated with CR, whereas more frequent engagement in cognitive and physical activity were positively associated with CR. Among these various factors, low literacy is the distinct characteristic of the low‐education group. Previous studies demonstrated that low literacy was linked to higher dementia prevalence and incidence and lower cognitive function. 41 , 42 Illiteracy can affect an individual's cognition in various ways. Socially, illiteracy can be a significant barrier to obtaining employment, resulting in lower SES. Regarding its effect on the brain, distinct brain‐activation patterns underlying cognitive tasks have been observed in literate and illiterate individuals. 43 A previous study demonstrated functional and structural differences between the brains of literate and illiterate individuals. Moreover, even among individuals who achieved literacy later in life, greater anatomic connections were observed compared to those who remained illiterate. 44 This suggests that being literate, even if only in late life, could be a potential modifiable strategy to increase CR and prevent dementia.

Among vascular risk factors, diabetes was negatively associated with CR only in the low‐education group. It has been known that diabetes contributes to CR via various mechanisms. Diabetes has been reported to be associated with clinical AD, 45 , 46 , 47 and insulin signaling changes may be related to AD pathophysiology as well, 48 but findings are inconsistent. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 In addition, diabetes might cause neuronal damage independently from the Aβ pathology and 53 also contributes to decreased cognitive function without significant structural change. 54 We consider that the deteriorating effect of diabetes on CR only in the low‐education group may result from limited access to optimal diabetes management due to lower income or health care availability. With a higher diabetes prevalence in this group, it might be possible that these individuals are more susceptible to both diabetes development and poor control. However, future research, including objective measurements such as blood glucose level, is required to provide further insights into this explanation.

Of interest, we found that physical activity was associated with CR only in the low‐education group. The relationship between physical activity and cognition has been reported inconsistently in previous studies 40 Although some studies found no significant associations, others observed that higher physical activity levels are linked to cognition. However, previous studies did not stratify the education level. Our result suggests that encouraging physical activity might have a greater impact on cognition in the low‐education group.

The third major finding was that in the high‐education group, more engagement in cognitive activity was positively related to CR, whereas long sleep duration and depression showed negative association with CR. Depression showed a significant association with CR only in the high‐education group. The relationship between depression and dementia is complex. 47 , 55 Depression is related closely to cognitive function and is one of the most common psychiatric symptoms in patients with AD. 56 , 57 In addition, there are reports that depression is related to CR. 57 , 58 However, studies examining the impact of education level are lacking, indicating a need for further research.

Notably, we found that frequent cognitive activity was associated with CR in both groups. Cognitive activity is a protective factor for dementia, 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 and late‐life cognitive activity is associated with a lower risk of AD and slower cognitive decline in highly educated individuals. 63 However, our study found that regardless of education levels, engagement in cognitive activities in later life was still positively associated with CR. This highlights the importance of cognitive activity for dementia prevention, even among individuals with low education.

Long sleep duration (>8 h/day) was negatively associated with CR in both groups. Sleep is related to AD, 64 but optimal sleep duration remains inconclusive. In previous meta‐analyses, both longer (>8 h/day) and shorter (<7 h/day) sleep duration compared to 7–8 h/day were associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment or AD. 64 The rapid cognitive decline in longer sleepers 65 , 66 might be related to concurrent general medical conditions such as depression or neurological diseases, 64 which limit physical or cognitive activities.

This study had several limitations. First, the study relied on cross‐sectional data. CR was assessed at a single time point, although CR, as measured by the residual approach used here, can change over time due to experiences or health conditions. Second, our questionnaire focused on late‐life factors, neglecting mid‐life health‐related lifestyles that impact CR. 16 Third, the study findings could not be replicated using the independent sample data set. Thus, future study, particularly using longitudinal study design and mid‐life factors, is warranted to validate our study findings. Fourth, participants excluded for missing data were older, less educated, and more likely cognitively impaired compared to those included. Such individuals were prone to drop out, possibly due to health issues, lack of support, or misunderstanding the purpose of tests. While acknowledging this inclusion bias, we mitigated its impact by stratifying education levels, as our primary study outcome focused on identifying different risk factors according to education levels. Finally, this study was not equipped to explain the mechanisms underlying the correlation between factors and CR. More research is required to explore the mechanisms behind the relationship between each factor and CR.

Nevertheless, our study is noteworthy due to its large population from a nationwide cohort that underwent systematic evaluation for dementia. The findings addressed the gap in research by exploring factors associated with CR across different education levels. Specifically, we included individuals with low education, an understudied population. Understanding different modifiable risk factors for CR according to the education level could help us develop personalized strategies to enhance CR and narrow health care disparities.
