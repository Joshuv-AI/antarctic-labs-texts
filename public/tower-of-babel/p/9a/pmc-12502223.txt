Chemical-mediated effects of Chaetoceros affinis on colony formation of Phaeocystis globosa: induced release of allelopathic compounds
Authors: Dayong Liang, Huanxin Li, Hua Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhaeocystis globosa is a significant cause of harmful algal blooms in temperate and tropical regions, with outbreaks typically occurring after spring diatom blooms. A previous study revealed that Chaetoceros affinis promotes the formation of P. globosa colonies. However, the composition of its extracellular metabolites and the underlying molecular mechanisms driving colony formation remain unclear. This study utilized C. affinis and its filtrate as stress conditions to stimulate P. globosa. Results indicated that both C. affinis and its filtrate induced a life-history transition in P. globosa. The abundance of solitary cells decreased, whereas colony abundance, colony diameter, and the number of colonial cells increased. Additionally, the filtrate induced significant alterations in the metabolic pathways of P. globosa cells. Photosynthesis was enhanced; genes related to DNA replication were downregulated, while those involved in fatty acid synthesis, amino acid synthesis, and polysaccharide synthesis were upregulated, and its degradation were downregulated. The reduced concentrations of 3-hydroxy-4-methoxycinnamic acid, abietic acid, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol, and oleamide in the filtrate favored algal growth, thereby promoting colony formation. Nonetheless, the specific metabolites responsible for colony formation warrant further characterization in future studies. This study offers new insights into the role of chemical interactions in regulating the alternation processes between diatoms and P. globosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04378-6.Keywords: Phaeocystis globosa, Chaetoceros affinis, Allelopathy, Transcriptome, Metabolic compound

Introduction

Phaeocystis, a genus widely distributed across both tropical and polar marine ecosystems, plays a crucial role in global carbon and sulfur cycles, while also contributing significantly to regional food webs [26, 46]. Among the colony-forming species, Phaeocystis globosa is particularly notable for its frequent association with harmful algal blooms along coastlines [39]. The life cycle of P. globosa is complex, involving polysaccharide-based colonies and multiple types of solitary cells [27, 35]. Solitary cells typically range from 3 to 10 μm in diameter, while colonies can grow up to 3 cm in size [21]. Notably, colony formation is one of the most distinctive phenotypic traits of P. globosa [34]. This colony structure confers significant ecological advantages in environments favorable to algal bloom outbreaks, as its considerable size and robust outer covering provide protection against grazing and degradation by zooplankton, bacteria, and viruses [1, 13]. Furthermore, colonies enhance the survival of enclosed cells by reducing mortality rates.

In European coastal waters, P. globosa blooms typically occur during spring [18, 27]. Similarly, an extensive bloom of P. globosa was observed in February 2019, following a diatom bloom in January in the coastal waters of the South China Sea [10]. The succession from diatoms to P. globosa is often linked to nutrient imbalances in the water column, particularly an excess of nitrate and phosphorus relative to silicate [18]. Diatoms generally dominate under silicate-replete conditions due to their higher intrinsic growth rates. However, once silicate becomes limited, P. globosa can proliferate and form blooms [19,47]. Contrarily, some studies indicate that P. globosa can outcompete diatoms even under silicate-sufficient conditions, suggesting additional factors may influence the competition dynamics between these taxa [2]. Such inconsistencies highlight the need to investigate other mechanisms underlying the occurrence of algal blooms.

Biologically, P. globosa blooms frequently succeed diatom blooms, and this natural phytoplankton succession may be driven by two primary factors. First, diatoms facilitate colony formation by providing attachment substrates. During P. globosa blooms, the formation of small colonies is often associated with adhesion to certain Chaetoceros species, which may serve as a material basis for colony development [35]. Second, allelopathic interactions between diatoms and P. globosa contribute to species succession. Previous studies suggest that diatoms release specific chemical compounds that influence P. globosa colony formation [49]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which diatom-derived chemical signals regulate colony formation remain unclear, and the exact nature of these chemical signals has yet to be elucidated.

In this study, filtrates from Chaetoceros affinis, a diatom species characterized by clustered chains of cells with thick, curved, sickle-shaped terminal seta, were used to stimulate colony formation in P. globosa. C. affinis is widely distributed and often co-occurs with P. globosa in natural aquatic environments, making it a representative model organism. To assess the impact of C. affinis and its secreted chemicals on colony formation, three experimental groups were established: a mixed culture group, a filtrate culture group, and a blank control group. Transcriptomic analyses were performed to elucidate the molecular pathways involved in allelopathic regulation of colony formation. Furthermore, the composition of the filtrate was analyzed using high-resolution mass spectrometry to identify potential chemical signal components. As diatoms and Phaeocystis are critical constituents of coastal phytoplankton communities, this study provides novel insights into the mechanisms governing their blooms and succession dynamics.

Materials and methods

Algae collection

The P. globosa strain used in this study was isolated during an algal bloom event in Beibu Bay, China, on September 10, 2017 (N 21°10.12; E 108°37.12” to “21°10.12’ N, 108°37.12’ E). Similarly, C. affinis was collected from the same region in February 2019 (N 21°10.12; E 108°37.12). Both samples were preserved in sterile seawater supplemented with f/2 medium (Guillard and Ryther, 1962) to maintain viability. Species identification was performed using 18 S rDNA sequencing to ensure taxonomic accuracy. Cultivation was conducted in natural seawater enriched with f/2 medium under controlled conditions: temperature 20 °C, light intensity 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and a 12:12 h light-dark cycle.

Experimental design

To investigate the impact of C. affinis on the growth and colony formation of P. globosa, cultures were established in 500 mL polystyrene bottles and divided into two groups (n = 5). The control group contained only P. globosa, while the treatment group included both P. globosa and C. affinis. Cell volumes were calculated using geometric models based on cell dimensions and converted to carbon biomass using established equations [23]. Both cultures were adjusted to achieve a target carbon concentration of approximately 0.13 g mL−1 before initiating experiments to ensure equivalent initial conditions. The solitary cells of P. globosa were obtained by filtering the culture through a 20 μm nylon mesh to remove colonies, followed by centrifugation at 100 × g for 3 min to collect solitary cells. The initial cell density of P. globosa was 1 × 10⁴ cells mL−1 (solitary cells), while C. affinis pre-filtration had a density of 5 × 10³ cells mL−1.

To assess the effect of C. affinis filtrate, cultures were also set up in 500 mL polystyrene bottles with two groups (n = 5). The C. affinis filtrate was prepared by vacuum filtration (pressure < 100 mm Hg) of exponentially growing cultures (Day 5) through 0.1 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membranes (Millipore, Cat. No. SLGP033RB). The filtrate was sterilized and stored at 4 °C for less than 24 h before use. In the control group, 50 mL of fresh medium was added daily. A graduated sterile pipette was used for precise 50 mL volume transfer. The addition process maintained constant culture volume through simultaneous removal of 50 mL old medium prior to fresh medium addition. In the treatment group, 25 mL of fresh medium and 25 mL of C. affinis filtrate were added. The filtrate was obtained by vacuum-filtering C. affinis culture through a 0.1 μm membrane (< 100 mm Hg). The cultivation conditions were identical to those described above.

Microscopic analysis

In this study, the Olympus BX61 microscope (Tokyo, Japan) was used to quantify the abundance of solitary cells, colony count, colony diameter, and the number of cells per colony. Samples were collected using a wide-mouth pipette and fixed in acidic Lugol’s solution every day (Tang, 2003). After fixation, the samples were transferred to a 1 mL Sedgwick-Rafter chamber for further analysis. For the measurement of isolated cell abundance, at least 200 cells per replicate group were counted by randomly selecting fields of view under an inverted microscope. For colony measurements, colonies were first placed in a 24-well plate and allowed to stand for 1 h. Then, 30 colonies from each sample were randomly selected to measure their diameter and the number of cells per colony [50].

Transcriptome analysis

Algal samples were collected from both the control and filtrate-treated groups on day 8. Three biological replicates were sequenced per group. The samples were first centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min to pellet the cells. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of RNA extraction buffer. To remove potential genomic DNA contamination, all samples were treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No. EN0521) at 37 °C for 30 min. RNA quality evaluation, library construction, sequencing, and data filtering were performed by Biodio Gene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, producing paired-end reads (150 bp). The raw reads were pre-processed for quality control (QC) by removing low-quality reads (Q-score < 20) and adapter sequences using Cutadapt (v2.10) before alignment. A de novo transcriptome assembly was conducted using Trinity (v2.11.0). Differential gene expression was analyzed using the DESeq R package (version 1.10.1) [7], which employs a negative binomial distribution model. To control for false positives, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a threshold of |log2 fold change| >1.5 and p < 0.05. For functional annotation, the assembled transcripts were translated into protein sequences using TransDecoder (v5.5.0) before annotation. Functional classification was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) annotation through the Blast2GO software (v5.2) [5], which provides functional categorization of the DEGs. Pathway analysis was conducted using the KEGG database with KOBAS (v3.0), which maps DEGs to known biological pathways. For GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, a p-value cutoff of 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05 were applied to identify significantly enriched categories and pathways.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Compounds were extracted from the filtrate of C. affinis. The sample was treated by adding 1000 µL of a methanol/acetonitrile/water mixed solution (2:2:1, v/v/v). The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and evaporated under vacuum to remove solvents. The dried extract was then dissolved in 100 µL of an acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) mixed solution, preparing it for LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (1290 Infinity LC, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 6600, AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). The analysis was carried out on a 2.1 mm × 100 mm ACQUITY UPLC BEH column (1.7 μm particle size, Waters, Ireland). The mobile phase consisted of two solutions: solution A, which was 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide solution, and solution B, which was acetonitrile. The gradient elution conditions started with 85% solution B, Held for 1 min, followed by a linear decrease to 65% solution B over 11 min. The solution B content was then rapidly reduced to 40% in 0.1 min, and Held for 4 min. Finally, the solution B content was increased back to 85%, and the column was equilibrated for 5 min to ensure stable separation and accurate analysis. In the LC-MS/MS analysis, compound identification was based on matching with the METLIN database and mzCloud database, with stringent criteria for mass error (≤ 5 ppm) and fragment ion match score (≥ 80%). Additionally, the retention time of each compound was compared with that of standards or reference retention times in the database, allowing a deviation range of ± 0.2 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM, USA). To assess significant differences among the control, short-term, and long-term treatment groups, t-tests were applied at each sampling point, such as for solitary cells abundance, colony abundance, and colony diameter. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

The effect of C. affinis and filtrate on P. globosa

No significant differences in solitary cells abundance were observed between the control and co-culture groups during the first three days (Fig. 1a). However, solitary cells abundance in the co-culture group significantly decreased compared to the control group from Day 4 onward (p < 0.05), reaching only 3 × 10⁴ cells mL⁻¹ by Day 10 (Fig. 1a). While solitary cells abundance declined, the number of colonies increased progressively over time (p < 0.05), peaking at approximately 1,254 colonies mL⁻¹ on Day 8 (Fig. 1c). The colony abundance in both the control and treatment groups remain relatively stable for the first 8 days and then suddenly increase significantly in the last two days. The allelopathic compounds in C. affinis filtrate likely required time to reach critical concentrations in the culture medium before significantly altering colonial behavior. Even after sufficient allelochemical accumulation, P. globosa cells may have undergone a period of metabolic adjustment before transitioning to colonial growth. At Day 10, the number of cells within colonies and the colony diameters were significantly higher in the co-culture group compared to the control group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1b). These findings suggest that C. affinis promotes colony formation in P. globosa.

Similarly, when C. affinis filtrate was added, P. globosa growth exhibited patterns comparable to those observed in the co-culture group. No significant differences in solitary cells abundance were detected during the first three days; However, solitary cells abundance in the filtrate treatment group were significantly lower than those in the control group from Day 4 onward (p < 0.05), reaching only 3.5 × 10⁴ cells mL⁻¹ by Day 10 (Fig. 1d). Despite the decline in solitary cells abundance, the number of colonies increased, with colony cell counts and diameters also significantly higher than those in the control group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1e, f). These results suggest that chemical compounds in the C. affinis filtrate play a pivotal role in facilitating P. globosa colony formation.

Transcriptome profiling

In this study, we performed transcriptomic analysis to investigate the gene expression changes in P. globosa under C. affinis filtrate treatment. The raw read counts obtained from each sample ranged from 42,019,784 to 52,897,700 (Table S1). After removing low-quality reads and adapter sequences, more than 40 million high-quality clean reads were retained. These clean reads were then de novo assembled, resulting in the identification of 50,453 unigenes with an average length of 1,169 bp and an N50 of 1,486 bp.

Differential gene expression (DEGs) analysis identified 9,368 DEGs in the filtrate treatment group, with 5,343 genes upregulated and 4,025 genes downregulated (Fig. S1). To further explore the biological significance of these DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed. The results showed that the DEGs were mainly involved in biological processes such as organic compound biosynthesis, cell biosynthesis, and nitrogen-containing compound synthesis (Fig. 2). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the DEGs were enriched in metabolic pathways, photosynthetic antenna proteins, and carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (Fig. 3, S2).

Further analysis of DEGs in KEGG pathways revealed significant upregulation of genes associated with the photosynthetic apparatus, including light-harvesting complex, photosystem I, and photosystem II components, such as LHCA5 (10.9-fold), psbI (5-fold), and psaE (3.5-fold). Genes involved in photosynthetic carbon fixation, such as prkB (3.3-fold) and pgk (3.8-fold), were also significantly upregulated. Additionally, genes linked to fatty acid biosynthesis, including ACACA (4.2-fold), fabF (3.3-fold), fabG (2.9-fold), fabZ (5.4-fold), and MECR (2.3-fold), showed marked upregulation. Similar trends were observed in genes related to amino acid biosynthesis, such as hisD (2.5-fold), livD (10.1-fold), glyA (2.3-fold), thrC (3-fold), argH (9.7-fold), and proC (12.9-fold). Genes involved in polysaccharide synthesis, such as UAP1, GME, and gmd, were also upregulated, whereas modA and xylS—encoding mannosyl-oligosaccharide alpha-1,3-glucosidase—were downregulated by 2.8-fold and 3.2-fold, respectively. Conversely, key genes associated with the DNA replication pathway, including pola1 (−3.2-fold), Prim1 (−3.3-fold), MCM2 (−2.3-fold), and lig1 (−2.9-fold), were significantly downregulated.

Compound composition of C. affinis metabolites

A total of 79 differential compounds were identified in the C. affinis filtrate compared to sterile seawater using UHPLC, including 36 known compounds (Table 1). The dominant class among the identified compounds was glycerophospholipids, represented by eight types. Other major compounds included organooxygen compounds (3), organonitrogen compounds (2), benzene and substituted derivatives (2), fatty acyls (2), and flavonoids (2). Of these compounds, 21 were significantly reduced, while 15 showed increases. The greatest decrease was observed in 1-palmitoyl-2-linoleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (−3.7-fold), whereas the most pronounced increase was detected for diclofenac (4.6-fold).

Discussion

P. globosa is a dominant phytoplankton species that frequently forms blooms in temperate and tropical coastal waters [38]. Alternating diatom-P. globosa blooms are common in natural ecosystems. Diatoms are generally recognized as influencing phytoplankton succession, community structure, and function by releasing metabolites that often inhibit the cell membrane integrity, photosynthetic activity, biochemical synthesis, and growth of competing species [29, 41, 42]. In contrast, this study demonstrated that both C. affinis and its extracellular filtrate promote colony formation in P. globosa. Furthermore, transcriptome analysis revealed molecular changes in the metabolic pathways of P. globosa induced by C. affinis filtrate (Fig. 4). Finally, the chemical composition of the filtrate was analyzed, allowing us to identify potential compounds responsible for stimulating colony formation.

Schematic illustration of the key molecular processes occurring in P. globosa cells treated with C. affinis filtrate

Promotion of P. globosa colonies by C. affinis and its filtrate

The inhibitory effects of diatoms on competitors’ growth have been extensively documented [16, 52], with allelopathy often being a key competitive strategy. However, our findings reveal that C. affinis and its extracellular metabolites instead stimulate P. globosa colony formation. In natural marine environments, solitary cells and newly formed small colonies of P. globosa frequently adhere to the surfaces of spiny diatoms such as C. affinis [28, 35]. These diatoms may provide a stable substrate, facilitating the rapid formation of initial colonies in complex oceanic environments. In our experiments, neither nutrient limitation nor pH changes were observed, suggesting that the release of specific extracellular compounds by C. affinis contributes to colony formation. The colonies of P. globosa consist of cells uniformly distributed in a mucus-like matrix. Colony size and cell density are determined by the secretion of dissolved polysaccharides and by cell proliferation within the colony [21, 34]. It is plausible that extracellular metabolites of C. affinis contain compounds that promote colony formation, stimulate cell division within colonies, and enhance gelatinous matrix production. Ecologically, the stimulation of P. globosa colony formation may indirectly challenge diatoms by increasing zooplankton grazing pressure on them, as colonial P. globosa cells are less accessible to grazers [9, 13]. For P. globosa, however, the benefits of colony formation, such as enhanced protection against predation, likely outweigh any associated drawbacks. For example, the polysaccharide matrix of P. globosa colonies stores nutrients [20, 37] and may provide an organic nutrient source for diatoms under nutrient-limiting or dark conditions. Sazhin et al. [36] also suggested that P. globosa colonies serve as habitats for small diatoms and offer organic substrates that promote diatom growth. This indicates a potential symbiotic relationship between P. globosa and certain diatoms.

Transcriptomic changes induced by C. affinis filtrate

The observed proliferation of P. globosa colonies following C. affinis filtrate exposure suggests profound modifications in critical metabolic pathways (Fig. 3). Our transcriptomic investigation identified marked upregulation of photosynthetic genes, revealing a coordinated response at multiple levels of the photosynthetic apparatus. Specifically, we detected increased expression of: (1) light-harvesting complex proteins (FCPD, LI818, LHCA5), which facilitate efficient photon capture and provide photoprotection [30]; (2) core photosystem components (psaE for PSI; psbC, psbI, psbU for PSII) that maintain electron transport efficiency and structural integrity [15, 17]; and (3) key carbon fixation enzymes (PEPC, PEPCK) associated with C4-like metabolic pathways [31]. This comprehensive upregulation likely represents an adaptive strategy, where enhanced light energy conversion (as demonstrated by LHC protein correlations in Yadavalli et al., [51] couples with improved carbon assimilation through C4 mechanisms [11] to boost overall photosynthetic performance under allelochemical stress. Consequently, the C. affinis filtrate appears to stimulate P. globosa’s photosynthetic machinery, leading to more efficient energy acquisition and carbon fixation that ultimately supports colony formation and growth.

Enhanced photosynthesis enables cells to absorb increased energy, which is redirected from cell proliferation to other metabolic pathways, including amino acid synthesis, fatty acid synthesis, and polysaccharide synthesis. DNA polymerase α, the sole enzyme in eukaryotic cells capable of de novo DNA strand synthesis, is critical for initiating SV40 DNA replication in vitro [4]. In this study, the expression of genes encoding this enzyme, pola1 and POLA2, was significantly downregulated. Similarly, microchromosome maintenance (MCM) proteins, which form a heterohexameric complex (MCM2–7) acting as a licensing factor for DNA replication to ensure complete and accurate genome duplication during the S-phase [45], also showed reduced expression. In contrast, acetyl-CoA carboxylase/biotin carboxylase 1, a rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid biosynthesis, was markedly upregulated. This observation aligns with findings by Cordonier et al. [6], who demonstrated that soraphen A inhibition of acetyl-CoA carboxylase impeded lipid accumulation and adipocyte differentiation in 3T3-L1 cells. Furthermore, key genes in the amino acid synthesis pathway, including hisD, TRP-1, and thrc, were significantly upregulated, indicating an enhanced rate of intracellular amino acid production. UDP-sugars, critical precursors for various glycosylation reactions, are primarily synthesized through de novo and salvage pathways in plants [25]. These UDP-sugars can be further interconverted into other nucleotide sugars by specific enzymes [32]. Among the three known UDP-sugar diphosphorylases, UDP-N-acetylgalactosamine diphosphorylase was significantly upregulated in this study, suggesting increased intracellular polysaccharide biosynthesis. Conversely, the gene GANAB (mannosyl-oligosaccharide alpha-1,3-glucosidase), responsible for converting oligosaccharides into glucose [43], exhibited downregulation. This combination of increased polysaccharide synthesis and reduced polysaccharide degradation likely contributed to decreased extracellular polysaccharide secretion.

Chemical composition of promoting P. globosa colonies

Comparison of the chemical compositions of the C. affinis filtrate and the seawater medium revealed significant differences. Algal surface properties enable the adsorption of various metals and organic pollutants [24], and the reduction of harmful substances may facilitate colony formation in natural marine environments. For instance, compounds such as 3-hydroxy-4-methoxycinnamic acid, abietic acid, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol, and oleamide, which showed reduced relative concentrations in this study, have been reported to inhibit algal growth [12]. Specifically, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol disrupts electron transfer between the primary electron acceptor of photosystem II and the plastid quinone pool, ultimately deactivating the reaction center of photosystem II and suppressing plant growth [33]. Similarly, Kaya et al. [14] demonstrated that oleamide increased water clarity, reduced chlorophyll a level, and sharply decreased ammonia nitrogen levels, thereby inhibiting the growth of Microcystis spp. In this study, however, P. globosa colony size increased even with the addition of just the filtrate. This prompted us to focus on the upregulated compounds in the filtrate. Among these, diclofenac emerged as the most upregulated metabolite and is one of the most frequently detected pharmaceutical contaminants in aquatic environments [44]. Although diclofenac and its primary metabolite, 4-hydroxydiclofenac, have known adverse effects on marine mussels [40], other upregulated compounds, such as quercetin 3-glucoside and asperuloside, possess antioxidant and antiviral properties [22, 48]. Given the limited research on the effects of these compounds on algae, further experimental investigations are required to determine their roles in promoting colony formation.

Implication

The occurrence of diatom and P. globosa blooms is predominantly governed by physical, chemical, and biological factors or their interactions [3, 8]. The chemical regulatory effects of diatoms on P. globosa reported in this study do not fully replicate the complex dynamics of field competition. For instance, the nutrient concentrations used in our experiments (f/2 medium) were higher than typical levels in natural waters. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the potential for extracellular metabolites to influence the competitive outcomes between diatoms and P. globosa. If diatom communities are predominantly composed of species that promote colony formation, this could enable P. globosa to outcompete diatoms. The stimulatory effects of certain diatoms on colony formation may further enhance the growth rates of P. globosa relative to diatoms, particularly under non-limiting nutrient conditions [2]. Our findings provide insights into the chemical-mediated interactions between diatoms and bloom-forming haptophytes, which may have significant consequences for marine food web dynamics and bloom development. Future studies should aim to identify and characterize the specific allelopathic compounds involved and explore their effects under more natural, complex community settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that C. affinis and its extracellular filtrate act as potent inducers of life-cycle transition in P. globosa, shifting its growth strategy from solitary cells to colonial forms. The observed increase in colony abundance, diameter, and cell numbers, coupled with a decline in solitary cells, suggests an adaptive response to allelochemical stress. Transcriptomic and metabolic analyses revealed that C. affinis filtrate enhances photosynthetic activity while suppressing DNA replication and catabolic processes, while upregulating biosynthetic pathways for fatty acids, amino acids, and polysaccharides—key components for colony formation. The reduced levels of specific metabolites (3-hydroxy-4-methoxycinnamic acid, abietic acid, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol, and oleamide) in the filtrate further support their role in modulating P. globosa growth dynamics. While these findings highlight the importance of chemical interactions in diatom-Phaeocystis dynamics, the precise metabolites driving colony induction remain to be fully characterized. This work provides a foundation for future studies on allelopathy-mediated phytoplankton interactions and their ecological implications in marine ecosystems.
