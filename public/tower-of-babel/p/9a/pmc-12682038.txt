Dataset of surface images for automated inspection of machined workpieces
Authors: Virginia Riego del Castillo, Lidia Sánchez González
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset comprises 593 high-resolution RGB images of machined workpiece surfaces, acquired using an industrial borescope integrated with a microscope camera and adjustable white LED illumination. Images are captured at a resolution of 2592 × 1944 pixels and 300 pixels per inch, providing detailed visualization of both inner and outer surfaces. The dataset is organized into four distinct workpiece categories (Piece01 to Piece04), with each image manually annotated by experts to indicate the presence or absence of surface defects, including wear consistent with quality standards. Images are systematically arranged within a hierarchical directory structure, facilitating straightforward access by workpiece and defect status.The acquisition system was designed to ensure controlled illumination and imaging conditions, enabling consistent capture of surface detail critical for automated defect analysis. This expertly labeled dataset supports the development and benchmarking of computer vision algorithms for surface inspection in manufacturing, with a particular focus on defect detection in components made from the same material under varying wear conditions. The comprehensive coverage and structured organization promote reproducibility and facilitate tailored investigations into machining quality and automated inspection methodologies. All dataset materials are publicly accessible to enable further research and industrial application.Keywords: Surface analysis, Workpiece quality, Milling, Wear identification

Background

The emergence of collaborative robots is affecting a transformation within the domain of manufacturing, including the inspection of machined workpieces. Traditionally, operators have relied on visual and manual checks, such as the inspection of edges for the presence of burrs. However, these methods are time-consuming, subjective, and lack consistency. The use of instruments such as roughness meters has been demonstrated to enhance the precision of measurements. However, this approach concomitantly results in a reduction in the efficiency of the process. The utilisation of computer vision for the automation of inspection processes has been demonstrated to engender a reduction in the time and financial expenditure associated with such endeavours, whilst concomitantly enhancing the consistency and reliability of the outcomes [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. This, in turn, facilitates the adherence to rigorous quality standards.Research has explored the fields of defect detection and surface roughness prediction. However, despite these efforts, reliable identification of surface defects remains challenging. As defined by ISO 13715, imperfections can lead to increased production costs and a deterioration in the quality of the finished product. The digital processing of microscopic images holds considerable potential for the automation of such inspections. Unlike existing datasets, the present dataset includes images of workpieces and their defects on both internal and external surfaces. The equipment used to collect these samples, as well as its operating procedure, was proposed in [8], with the image acquisition carried out in 2019.

Data Description

The dataset was acquired using an industrial borescope equipped with a high-resolution microscope camera, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This vision-based system incorporates adjustable white LED illumination to optimize lighting conditions and ensure adequate capture of surface details on machined workpieces. Images are recorded in RGB format at a resolution of 300 pixels per inch (ppi), with dimensions of 2592 × 1944 pixels, providing detailed visual information suitable for defect analysis. The acquisition system can be consulted in detail in [2,8].

Acquisition vision-based system formed by a borescope and a microscope camera [2].

In the context of this dataset, a defect refers to any observable irregularity or anomaly on the surface of a machined workpiece that deviates from the expected uniform texture and machining pattern. These defects may include scratches, grooves, burrs, corrosion spots, cracks, or heterogeneous surface textures that could adversely affect the component’s functional performance, aesthetic quality, or adherence to manufacturing tolerances. For this dataset, workpieces featuring a variety of surface finishes were considered, as these were specifically produced to evaluate the performance of the machine described in the referenced study [9].

Both inner and outer surfaces of the machined parts were inspected using this setup, examples of which are presented in Fig. 2, showing four representative workpieces. Each image underwent meticulous manual labeling by experts through visual inspection to classify surface conditions based on wear and defect presence. The resulting dataset is divided into subsets, including one focused on surface wear classifications. This comprehensive and precisely curated dataset enables the development and evaluation of automated surface inspection algorithms under real manufacturing conditions.

Workpieces used to generate images of the dataset.

The dataset comprises a total of 593 images as can be shown in Fig. 3, systematically categorized based on both the identity of each machined workpiece and the presence or absence of surface defects. The four workpieces are labeled Piece01, Piece02, Piece03, and Piece04. Each image has been meticulously annotated by experts to indicate surface condition, enabling a structured partition between defective and non-defective cases. Distribution analysis reveals notable variances among the workpieces. Piece02 constitutes the largest subset, containing a significantly higher number of images with defects compared to those without. Piece03 and Piece04 present a more balanced distribution between defect and no-defect categories, each hosting a moderate count of images. Piece01, by contrast, includes the fewest images in both groups. This heterogeneous distribution reflects the real-world variability inherent in the manufacturing process and serves to ensure that supervised learning models trained on this dataset are exposed to diverse scenarios.

Distribution of images of the dataset by workpiece and category.

The annotation process was carried out under a collaborative expert review protocol, designed to ensure label quality and consistency. In the initial phase, quality experts established clear definitions and explanations for the criteria distinguishing defective from non-defective surfaces, leading to the development of a standardized framework for surface labelling. Following this, a single experienced annotator independently labelled all images according to the predefined criteria. A second expert then reviewed the annotations to verify their accuracy. To quantitatively assess the agreement between annotators beyond chance, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was computed, resulting in a high value of 0.8793, indicating strong inter-rater reliability. Cohen’s kappa measures the extent to which annotators agree while adjusting for agreement that could occur by chance. Additionally, the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) was calculated, yielding a value of 0.8848, which provides a balanced measure of classification quality by considering true and false positives and negatives. MCC is especially informative for imbalanced datasets, offering a robust single-number summary of annotation performance. Together, these metrics confirm the robustness of the labelling process and the consistency of the classification criteria. The detailed calculations and results of both analyses are available for consultation at the following GitHub repository link[10]. In cases where discrepancies arose, they were largely attributed to human error or fatigue rather than ambiguity in the criteria. To address these instances, a collaborative resolution process was implemented, with both experts discussing disagreements until a consensus was reached on the correct classification. This dual-review approach minimized the influence of human fallibility, enhanced label reliability, and ensured that the resulting dataset provided a robust foundation for the development and benchmarking of automated surface inspection methods.

Fig. 4 presents a set of representative microscopic images capturing the surface condition of machined workpieces in two categories. The first row displays samples of surfaces without defects as classified by expert visual inspection; these images exhibit relatively uniform textures and consistent machining marks, indicative of proper manufacturing processes and minimal surface wear. In contrast, the second row illustrates surfaces with defects, where irregularities such as scratches, grooves, or heterogeneous textures are clearly visible, reflecting typical wear or surface damage encountered during machining operations.

Samples of surfaces without defects (first row) and defects (second row).

The dataset displays an imbalanced distribution of images across the four workpieces, with Piece02 containing a significantly higher number of defective samples compared to the others, and Piece01 having the fewest overall images. This imbalance is indicative of real-world manufacturing variability; however, it presents a challenge in the training of machine learning models, including the potential for bias towards more prevalent classes. It is also noteworthy that the non-defective (good) images manifest a high degree of visual similarity, while the defective samples encompass a broader spectrum of surface irregularities and defect types. This variability contributes to the observed imbalance in the dataset, yet at the same time enhances its representativeness. Consequently, researchers are encouraged to carefully consider data balancing, augmentation, or weighting strategies when developing models with this dataset. Furthermore, the dataset under consideration does not include predefined train, validation, or test splits. It is recommended that users adopt a strategy of data splitting, whereby separate workpieces are utilised for training (i.e. some pieces are used for training, while others are used for validation or testing). This approach facilitates a more robust assessment of model generalisation to unseen component geometries and surface conditions.

There are a number of solutions for the handling of imbalanced datasets, each of which has its own advantages. Undersampling involves reducing the majority class samples to match the size of the minority class. This adjustment is evident in the data, where the distribution was modified from 440 defects and 153 OK images to a balanced 153 each following undersampling. While this approach assists in balancing the data, it may potentially result in the loss of valuable information. Oversampling, a data augmentation technique for images, has been shown to increase the minority class samples by generating new, varied examples. This process preserves all data, but may increase training time and risk overfitting. Cross-validation with stratified splits is employed to ensure that each fold maintains the class proportions, thereby enhancing the reliability of model evaluation, as evidenced by our training sets comprising approximately 352 defects and 122 OKs, in conjunction with the balanced test sets. The implementation of these complementary strategies has been demonstrated to enhance dataset balance, model robustness, and generalizability. The detailed implementation and results of these methods can be found in the GitHub repository [10].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset presented herein served as the foundation for the experimental studies described in Book Chapter [1]. The collection comprises 593 high-resolution RGB images, acquired using an industrial borescope equipped with a microscope camera and adjustable white LED illumination. Both the inner and outer surfaces of machined workpieces were systematically imaged under controlled conditions, capturing fine surface details essential for reliable defect detection and classification.

For reproducibility and ease of access, there are code available to load dataset which is publicly available via the corresponding GitHub repository: https://github.com/uleroboticsgroup/computer_vision_workpieces. Users can use as a guide to load images and follow possible experiments. Alternatively, the images can be accessed by following the directory structure detailed in the previous section, which organizes data by workpiece and classification category. This organization facilitates targeted dataset handling for training and validation purposes in various research and industrial scenarios.

A previous version of this dataset was initially hosted on the OpenScayle platform (https://open.scayle.es/dataset/workpieces-image-dataset) as a compressed ZIP file. However, since this repository does not support DOI generation or dataset versioning, a Zenodo record has been created to ensure permanent accessibility and proper citation. In this new version, folder names have been revised and standardized in English, and the full directory tree can be consulted directly within the Zenodo interface.

Limitations

Despite the fact that the dataset provides a valuable resource for advancing automated inspection of machined workpieces, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. Initially, the image collection was conducted utilising a singular acquisition setup, employing a designated type of borescope, microscope camera, and lighting configuration. This reliance on a singular material and inspection system may impose constraints on the applicability of models trained on the dataset, potentially limiting their use to other materials, machining processes, or optical configurations. Secondly, the fixed illumination and optical conditions, while ensuring consistency, do not capture the variability typically encountered in industrial environments. In such environments, ambient light, camera positioning and surface reflectivity can all have a significant effect on image quality and defect detectability. Thirdly, the dataset demonstrates class imbalance, with certain workpieces – particularly Piece02 – containing a higher number of defective samples than others. This heterogeneity has the potential to introduce bias in machine learning models if it is not meticulously managed during the training process. It is evident that the generalisability of the dataset is constrained, as the current version represents a mere four workpieces of a single type of machined material. In order to comprehensively evaluate the robustness of automated inspection approaches developed with this dataset, it is essential to conduct broader validation on diverse geometries, finishes, and manufacturing processes.
