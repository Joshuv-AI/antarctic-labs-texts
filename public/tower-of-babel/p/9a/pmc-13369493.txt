Health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Changfu Chen, Miao Zhan, Hongying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) requires sustained self-management, and health literacy may play an important role in patients’ ability to understand, implement, and maintain disease-management behaviors. However, the effectiveness of self-management interventions incorporating health literacy–sensitive components in COPD remains unclear. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions for adults with COPD. Health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions were defined as self-management programs incorporating explicitly identifiable strategies intended to reduce literacy-related barriers or to support patients in obtaining, understanding, appraising, or applying COPD-related health information in daily self-management. Nine databases were searched from inception to August 30, 2025. Thirty-one randomized controlled trials involving 4759 participants were included. Outcomes included 6 min walk distance (6MWD), forced expiratory volume in 1 s as a percentage of predicted value (FEV₁% predicted), St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea score, COPD Assessment Test (CAT), and self-management-related outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, and pooled effects were calculated using fixed- or random-effects models according to heterogeneity. Compared with usual care, self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with improvements in 6MWD (MD = 44.81 m, 95% CI 17.48–72.14; p = 0.001), FEV₁% predicted (MD = 7.43, 95% CI 6.31–8.55; p < 0.00001), SGRQ total score (MD = −6.80, 95% CI −9.80–−3.80; p < 0.00001), mMRC score (MD = −0.40, 95% CI −0.61–−0.19; p = 0.0002), CAT score (MD = −3.78, 95% CI −5.49–−2.07; p < 0.0001), and self-management-related outcomes (SMD = 1.50, 95% CI 1.34–1.67; p < 0.00001). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for several outcomes, particularly 6MWD, SGRQ, mMRC, CAT, and self-management-related outcomes. Health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions were associated with improvements in exercise capacity, pulmonary function, symptom burden, dyspnea, health-related quality of life, and self-management-related outcomes in patients with COPD. However, the evidence should be interpreted with caution because eligible interventions were defined by the presence of explicitly identifiable health literacy–sensitive strategies embedded within self-management programs, rather than by isolated health-literacy enhancement alone, and substantial heterogeneity and variable methodological quality were present across studies. More rigorously designed and standardized trials are needed to clarify the specific contribution of health literacy–sensitive strategies and their long-term effectiveness in COPD care.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global public health challenge and one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. It imposes a substantial disease burden through persistent respiratory symptoms, recurrent exacerbations, progressive functional decline, increased healthcare utilization, and reduced quality of life1,2. Although pharmacological treatment has advanced considerably, many patients continue to experience symptom burden, activity limitation, and poor disease control. This indicates that COPD management should not rely solely on clinician-directed medical treatment, but also requires sustained patient participation in day-to-day disease management1,3.

Self-care provides an important theoretical framework for understanding the active role of patients in chronic illness management. According to the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness proposed by Riegel et al.4, self-care is a dynamic and multidimensional process comprising self-care maintenance, self-care monitoring, and self-care management. In COPD, these behaviors include medication adherence, symptom monitoring, recognition of exacerbations, physical activity, pulmonary rehabilitation, smoking cessation, and appropriate use of healthcare resources. Therefore, strengthening patients’ self-care capacity is essential for improving long-term COPD management and health outcomes3,5.

Health literacy (HL) is closely related to effective self-care. HL refers to the ability to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information to make appropriate health decisions6. It is increasingly recognized not only as an individual capability but also as a modifiable determinant shaped by health communication, healthcare systems, and social context7. With the increasing digitalization of healthcare, this concept has expanded to include eHealth literacy and mobile health literacy, which reflect patients’ ability to seek, evaluate, exchange, and use digital or mobile health information and services effectively8,9. For patients with COPD, limited HL may affect their understanding of disease-related information, correct use of inhaled medications, recognition of symptom deterioration, adherence to rehabilitation plans, and implementation of self-management behaviors. Accordingly, self-management interventions that incorporate health literacy–sensitive strategies may help patients better understand, apply, and sustain disease-management behaviors in daily life5,10.

Health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions can be understood as self-management programs that are deliberately designed or adapted to reduce literacy-related barriers and to make health information more understandable, actionable, and applicable to daily disease management. Conventional COPD self-management interventions are generally defined as structured, patient-tailored, and multifaceted programs that provide ongoing support to help patients change health behaviors, develop disease-management skills, and take greater ownership of their health11. By contrast, health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions place additional emphasis on how health information is communicated, reinforced, and translated into patient action. This distinction is consistent with the Health Literate Care Model, which proposes integrating health literacy strategies into chronic care and self-management support to improve patient engagement in care12. It is also consistent with health literacy universal precautions and health-literate organization approaches, which emphasize reducing the cognitive and navigation demands placed on patients and using strategies such as plain-language communication, teach-back, show-me methods, written action plans, and navigation support to help patients understand and use health information13,14. Therefore, in the context of COPD, health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions are not limited to providing disease education or behavioral advice; rather, they incorporate identifiable strategies intended to support patients in obtaining, understanding, appraising, and applying COPD-related information in real-life self-care situations10–14.

Although self-management interventions have been widely investigated in COPD, evidence regarding interventions incorporating health literacy–sensitive components remains fragmented3,10. Existing randomized controlled trials vary considerably in intervention components, delivery formats, duration, theoretical basis, and outcome measures. Some interventions include simplified educational materials, individualized coaching, action plans, teach-back methods, digital support, repeated feedback, or other strategies intended to improve patients’ understanding and use of health information. However, their overall effects on self-management-related outcomes, exercise capacity, pulmonary function, dyspnea, symptom burden, and health-related quality of life in COPD have not been systematically synthesized. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on self-management-related outcomes, exercise capacity, pulmonary function, dyspnea, symptom burden, and health-related quality of life in adults with COPD.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria:(i)Participants: adult participants aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as defined by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria or by physician diagnosis based on spirometric evidence.(ii)Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English or Chinese.(iii)Interventions: the intervention group received a COPD self-management intervention incorporating one or more explicitly identifiable health literacy–sensitive components. In this review, health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions were defined as self-management programs that were deliberately designed or adapted to reduce literacy-related barriers and to make COPD-related health information more understandable, actionable, and applicable to daily disease management. Eligible interventions were required to meet both of the following criteria: (a) the program aimed to support COPD self-management behaviors, such as medication use, inhaler technique, symptom monitoring, exacerbation recognition, physical activity, pulmonary rehabilitation, smoking cessation, oxygen therapy, or appropriate healthcare use; and (b) the program included at least one identifiable strategy intended to support patients in obtaining, understanding, appraising, or applying COPD-related health information in real-life self-care situations.Eligible health literacy–sensitive strategies included, but were not limited to, the following categories: (1) information accessibility strategies, such as plain-language, simplified, pictorial, culturally tailored, or individualized educational materials; (2) comprehension confirmation and reinforcement strategies, such as teach-back, show-me methods, repeated feedback, individualized coaching, skill demonstration, or inhaler-technique training; (3) actionability and decision-support strategies, such as written action plans, symptom diaries, exacerbation-response guidance, medication-use guidance, self-monitoring tools, or goal-setting support; and (4) navigation, continuity, or digital-support strategies, such as telephone follow-up, mobile-app support, digital reminders, telehealth feedback, peer support, or coordinated hospital–community–home management designed to help patients use health information in daily care. Interventions consisting solely of routine education, general self-management advice, exercise training, or pulmonary rehabilitation without an identifiable health literacy–sensitive component were not considered eligible. Exclusion criteria

Literature search and retrieval strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following electronic databases: CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, SinoMed, Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Ovid MEDLINE. The search covered all studies published up to August 30, 2025. Two reviewers (CFC and MZ) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (HYZ). The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Appendix 1.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (CFC and MZ) independently extracted data using a standardized data extraction form and cross-checked all extracted information for accuracy. The extracted data included the first author, publication year, country, sample size (intervention/control), mean age and baseline characteristics of participants, detailed descriptions of the health literacy–sensitive self-management intervention components, implementation methods, personnel involved, reinforcement frequency, control conditions, intervention duration, follow-up duration, and outcome data for all predefined endpoints, such as means, standard deviations, and event counts where applicable. In addition, we extracted information on whether the included studies assessed health literacy, self-management ability, self-care, self-efficacy, adherence, or related process outcomes, when reported.

When data were missing or incompletely reported, attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors for clarification. If additional information could not be obtained, relevant data were extracted from tables or figures where feasible, or estimated according to established methodological guidance. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (HYZ).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 1). The assessment covered the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was judged as having low, high, or unclear risk of bias according to the criteria recommended in the Cochrane Handbook. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer(HYZ). The literature management process was conducted using NoteExpress.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were primarily performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3. For continuous outcomes measured using the same scale, pooled effect estimates were calculated as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used when studies assessed the same construct using different measurement instruments, such as self-management-related outcomes. For dichotomous outcomes, where applicable, pooled effect estimates were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the chi-squared test and the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low or moderate (I² ≤ 50%); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. When substantial heterogeneity was identified, potential clinical and methodological sources were explored through subgroup analyses where data permitted. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out analyses and, where applicable, by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias. When necessary, standard errors or 95% confidence intervals were converted into standard deviations (SDs) using Cochrane-recommended methods. All analytical methods and decisions were prespecified in the review protocol.

For outcomes including at least 10 studies, publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots. When visual inspection suggested funnel plot asymmetry, Egger’s regression asymmetry test was additionally conducted as a quantitative assessment. Egger’s test was performed using R software version 4.5.2 with the meta package. Because Egger’s test may be influenced by substantial between-study heterogeneity, differences in intervention components, and the limited number of included studies, its results were interpreted as exploratory and considered together with funnel plot inspection and clinical/methodological heterogeneity.

This review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251180797).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published randomized controlled trials and did not involve new human participants or collection of individual participant data. Ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this secondary analysis. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained in the original primary studies where applicable.

Ethical approval and consent for publication

This meta-analysis used publicly available, anonymized datasets. All included studies had prior ethical approval and informed consent where applicable. No additional consent for publication was required.

Risk of bias assessment

In the 31 studies included in this meta-analysis, most showed a low risk of bias in random sequence generation and allocation concealment. However, there were significant issues with blinding. Studies34,43,44 were at higher risk of performance bias because neither the participants nor the researchers were blinded, potentially affecting the intervention’s effectiveness. Moreover, studies29,32,43 had higher detection bias risks because outcome assessors were not blinded, raising concerns about the subjective interpretation of the results. Attrition bias was an issue in studies43,44 due to high attrition rates and insufficient explanations of the reasons for participant dropout, which might compromise the generalizability of the findings. Studies like34,43 also showed unclear attrition risks due to inadequate reporting. Selective reporting bias was identified in studies22,39, where predefined outcomes were inconsistently reported, introducing potential reporting bias. These issues are detailed in Figs. 2, 3, highlighting the need for methodological improvements in future studies.Fig. 2Risk of bias graph in 31 studies.Fig. 3Risk of bias summary assessments for each bias risk item in 31 studies.

Meta-analysis results

6MWD

The 6 min walk distance (6MWD) is widely used to assess exercise tolerance and functional capacity in patients with COPD. A total of 10 studies16,18,22,25,29,34,35,37,42,44 involving 1,455 participants were included in the meta-analysis. Because substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 99%, p < 0.00001), a random-effects model was applied. The pooled results showed that self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with greater improvement in 6MWD compared with the control group (MD = 44.81 m, 95% CI [17.48, 72.14], p = 0.001), indicating a beneficial effect on exercise capacity (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on 6MWD in patients with COPD.

Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on 6MWD in patients with COPD.

To further explore the potential source of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was conducted according to intervention delivery format, including face-to-face, mixed, and digital/remote-dominant interventions (Fig. 5). In the face-to-face subgroup, the pooled effect showed a significant improvement in 6MWD (MD = 50.08 m, 95% CI [37.66, 62.50], p < 0.00001), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). In the mixed-intervention subgroup, the pooled result also favored the experimental group (MD = 39.41 m, 95% CI [18.15, 60.68], p = 0.0003), although substantial heterogeneity remained (I² = 84%). In the digital/remote-dominant subgroup, the pooled effect was not statistically significant (MD = 55.27 m, 95% CI [−14.38, 124.92], p = 0.12), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%).The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.68).Fig. 5Subgroup analysis of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on 6MWD by intervention delivery format.

FEV₁% predicted

FEV₁% Predicted is a key indicator of pulmonary function in patients with COPD and is commonly used to reflect the degree of airflow limitation. In this meta-analysis, 9 studies22,29,30,34,35,39,40,43,44 involving 1,077 participants were included. Statistical heterogeneity across studies was relatively low (I² = 28%, p = 0.20); therefore, a fixed-effect model was used for the pooled analysis. The results demonstrated that self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with an increase in FEV₁% Predicted compared with the control group (MD = 7.43, 95% CI [6.31, 8.55], p < 0.00001), suggesting that these interventions contributed to improved lung function in patients with COPD (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on FEV₁% predicted in patients with COPD.

In the integrated-care subgroup, the pooled estimate showed a significant benefit in favor of the experimental group (MD = 7.80, 95% CI [5.29, 10.30], p < 0.00001), and heterogeneity was not detected (I² = 0%). SGRQ total score

The St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) total score is an established measure of health-related quality of life in patients with COPD, covering symptoms, activity limitation, and psychosocial impact. In the present meta-analysis, 16 studies15,19,20,23,24,30–36,38,40,42,45 involving 2,835 participants were included. Considerable heterogeneity was identified across studies (I² = 95%, p < 0.00001), and therefore a random-effects model was adopted. The pooled analysis indicated that self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with lower SGRQ total scores compared with the control group (MD = −6.80, 95% CI [−9.80, −3.80], p < 0.00001), suggesting a significant improvement in health-related quality of life among patients with COPD (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on SGRQ total score in patients with COPD.

Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on SGRQ total score in patients with COPD.

To further examine whether the intervention effect differed according to the inclusion of pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training in the experimental program, a subgroup analysis was conducted (Fig. 9). In the subgroup of interventions including pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise, the pooled result showed a statistically significant reduction in SGRQ total score (MD = −8.58, 95% CI [−11.45, −5.71], p < 0.00001), although substantial heterogeneity remained (I² = 91%). By contrast, in the subgroup of interventions without pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise, the pooled effect was not statistically significant (MD = −2.09, 95% CI [−7.07, 2.91], p = 0.29), with high heterogeneity still present (I² = 79%). mMRC

The modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale was used to assess the severity of breathlessness in patients with COPD. A total of 14 studies15,16,18,19,24,27,28,32,37,38,41,43–45 involving 2652 participants were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled analysis showed that self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with lower mMRC scores compared with the control group (MD = −0.40, 95% CI [−0.61, −0.19], p = 0.0002), indicating an improvement in dyspnea. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 95%, p < 0.00001) (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on mMRC dyspnea score in patients with COPD.

In the face-to-face subgroup, the pooled effect showed a significant reduction in mMRC scores (MD = −0.66, 95% CI [−0.81, −0.51], p < 0.00001), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 48%). Self-management outcome

A total of 7 studies17,18,23,28,31,41,44 were included in the meta-analysis of self-management outcome, comprising 893 patients with COPD, including 446 in the experimental group and 441 in the control group. Because different studies used different measurement instruments to assess self-management, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the pooled effect size. The overall meta-analysis showed that self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive components were associated with improved self-management-related outcomes in patients with COPD compared with usual care (SMD = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.34–1.67, P < 0.00001). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies (I² = 97%, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 14).Fig. 14Forest plot of the effect of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions on self-management-related outcomes in patients with COPD.

Publication bias

Publication bias and small-study effects were visually assessed using funnel plots for outcomes including 10 or more studies, namely 6 min walk distance (6MWD), St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) total score, modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score, and COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score (Fig. 16A-D). Overall, most studies were distributed in the upper and middle parts of the plots, while some degree of asymmetry was observed across several outcomes. To further assess funnel plot asymmetry quantitatively, Egger’s regression asymmetry test was conducted for all outcomes with at least 10 included studies. The Egger’s test results were as follows: 6MWD, p = 0.6765; SGRQ total score, p = 0.7177; mMRC score, p = 0.0338; and CAT score, p = 0.1014. Discussion

Principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 31 randomized controlled trials involving 4759 patients with COPD to evaluate the effects of health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions. Overall, the findings suggest that self-management interventions incorporating explicitly identifiable health literacy–sensitive components were associated with improvements in exercise capacity, pulmonary function, dyspnea, symptom burden, health-related quality of life, and self-management-related outcomes. These results indicate that adapting COPD self-management programs to make health information more understandable, actionable, and applicable may provide multidimensional benefits for patients with COPD. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution because many included interventions were complex, multicomponent programs in which health literacy–sensitive strategies were embedded within broader self-management, rehabilitation, digital health, or integrated-care models. Therefore, the present findings support the effectiveness of self-management interventions with health literacy–sensitive features, but they do not establish the independent effect of health literacy enhancement alone.

A key conceptual issue in this review is the distinction between health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions and conventional self-management interventions. Conventional COPD self-management programs generally aim to improve patients’ knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing their disease through education, behavioral guidance, rehabilitation, and follow-up support11. In contrast, health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions place additional emphasis on how COPD-related information is communicated, reinforced, and translated into patient action. This distinction is consistent with the Health Literate Care Model, which emphasizes integrating health literacy strategies into chronic care and self-management support to improve patient engagement12. It is also consistent with health literacy universal precautions approaches, which emphasize reducing communication and navigation barriers and helping patients understand and use health information13,14. In this review, eligible interventions were required to include at least one identifiable strategy intended to reduce literacy-related barriers or to support patients in obtaining, understanding, appraising, or applying health information in real-life self-care situations. Such strategies included simplified or pictorial materials, individualized guidance, teach-back or feedback methods, skill demonstration, action plans, symptom-monitoring tools, digital reminders, navigation support, and continuity-based follow-up. This operational definition helped distinguish health literacy–sensitive self-management from routine education or general rehabilitation alone, while also reflecting the practical reality that health literacy strategies are often integrated into multicomponent COPD management programs.

The improvement in 6 min walk distance suggests that health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions may enhance functional exercise capacity in patients with COPD. This finding is clinically relevant because impaired exercise tolerance is a major manifestation of COPD and is closely related to daily activity limitation, independence, and quality of life1. The observed effect may be explained by several interacting mechanisms. Literacy-sensitive education may help patients better understand exercise prescriptions, breathing techniques, pacing strategies, symptom thresholds, and the importance of regular physical activity. In addition, repeated feedback, goal setting, self-monitoring, and follow-up support may improve adherence to exercise or rehabilitation plans. Many included studies also incorporated pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training, which can directly reduce physical deconditioning and improve functional performance. Therefore, the observed improvement in 6MWD should be understood as the result of comprehensive self-management support, in which health literacy–sensitive strategies may have facilitated patient engagement, comprehension, and adherence.

The pooled analysis also showed a favorable effect on FEV₁% predicted, suggesting that these interventions may contribute to improvements in pulmonary function-related outcomes. Although COPD is characterized by largely irreversible airflow limitation, better self-management may still influence lung function-related indicators through improved inhaler technique, medication adherence, respiratory training, smoking-related behavior, and earlier recognition of symptom deterioration1,3. Health literacy–sensitive strategies may be particularly relevant in this process because correct inhaler use, symptom monitoring, and adherence to treatment plans require patients to understand and apply health information accurately6,10. Nevertheless, this finding should not be overinterpreted. FEV₁ is influenced by baseline disease severity, spirometric measurement conditions, pharmacological treatment, intervention duration, and disease progression. Moreover, because most interventions combined education with behavioral, rehabilitative, or follow-up components, the specific contribution of health literacy–sensitive strategies to pulmonary function cannot be determined with certainty.

The reductions in CAT and mMRC scores indicate that health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions may reduce symptom burden and dyspnea. These patient-reported outcomes are particularly important because they reflect patients’ perceived disease impact and daily functional limitations. Interventions that present information in an understandable, tailored, and actionable manner may help patients recognize symptom changes, use medications correctly, apply breathing strategies, avoid triggers, and seek timely care during exacerbation risk. Written action plans, symptom diaries, digital reminders, and repeated follow-up may also help patients respond more appropriately to worsening symptoms. These mechanisms may explain the observed improvements in symptom-related outcomes. However, CAT and mMRC are subjective measures and may be affected by patient expectations, frequency of contact with healthcare providers, and lack of blinding in behavioral intervention trials. Therefore, although the direction of effect was generally favorable, the magnitude of improvement should be interpreted in the context of methodological limitations.

Health-related quality of life, measured by the SGRQ total score, also improved following intervention. This finding is consistent with the broader goal of COPD self-management, which is not only to improve physiological indicators but also to help patients live more effectively with a chronic and progressive disease3,11. Health literacy–sensitive approaches may improve quality of life by strengthening patients’ understanding of disease management, increasing confidence in daily decision-making, and supporting sustained behavior change5,6. The subgroup findings suggested that quality-of-life benefits were more apparent when interventions included pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training. This may be because exercise-based components directly improve activity tolerance, reduce deconditioning, and decrease fear of exertion, whereas literacy-sensitive education helps patients understand why exercise is important, how to perform it safely, and how to maintain it at home. Therefore, interventions combining understandable information with active rehabilitation may have synergistic effects on physical limitation, symptom perception, and health-related quality of life. By contrast, interventions without exercise or pulmonary rehabilitation may still improve medication use, symptom recognition, and exacerbation response, but they may have a smaller direct effect on activity-related quality-of-life domains.

The subgroup analyses provide further insight into possible mechanisms underlying differences in intervention effects. First, delivery format may influence the extent to which patients can translate information into self-management behaviors. Face-to-face interventions showed more consistent benefits for some outcomes, particularly mMRC and 6MWD. Direct contact allows healthcare professionals to assess comprehension, demonstrate breathing or inhaler techniques, correct errors immediately, and adapt explanations to patients with different literacy levels. These features may be especially important for older patients or those with limited health literacy13,14. By contrast, digital or remote-dominant interventions may be more dependent on patients’ eHealth literacy, access to technology, and sustained engagement8,9. Limited digital skills, poor usability, or insufficient feedback may weaken the effect of remote interventions. Mixed models may combine the accessibility of remote follow-up with the skill-building advantages of face-to-face contact, but their effects are likely to vary according to the intensity, frequency, and quality of reinforcement.

Second, the inclusion of pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training may partly explain differences in SGRQ and CAT outcomes. Exercise and rehabilitation components can directly improve physical conditioning and activity tolerance, while literacy-sensitive strategies can improve patients’ understanding of rehabilitation goals, safe symptom thresholds, and home-based exercise instructions. This combination may promote better adherence and reduce anxiety related to activity-induced breathlessness. Third, the integrated-care subgroup analysis for FEV₁% predicted did not show a clear difference between integrated and non-integrated models. One possible explanation is that lung function is relatively less responsive to behavioral intervention than patient-reported outcomes and is strongly influenced by baseline disease severity, pharmacological treatment, and measurement conditions. Integrated care may be more likely to affect continuity of care, early exacerbation management, adherence, and healthcare utilization than to produce large short-term changes in spirometric indices.

The duration-based subgroup analysis for self-management-related outcomes suggested larger standardized effects in interventions lasting 6 months or less. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. Shorter interventions may produce early gains after intensive education, coaching, and reinforcement, particularly when outcomes are measured soon after the intervention. In contrast, longer interventions may face challenges related to declining adherence, reduced contact intensity, disease progression, or behavioral fatigue over time. Differences in measurement instruments and small-study effects may also contribute to the observed subgroup difference. Therefore, although intervention duration may be one source of heterogeneity, the result should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future studies should examine not only intervention length, but also the timing, frequency, intensity, and sustainability of literacy-sensitive reinforcement.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed for several outcomes, including 6MWD, SGRQ, mMRC, CAT, and self-management-related outcomes. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in intervention content, delivery format, intensity, duration, follow-up frequency, healthcare setting, patient characteristics, and outcome measurement. Some interventions emphasized education and written materials, whereas others incorporated pulmonary rehabilitation, digital support, motivational interviewing, action plans, home follow-up, or integrated hospital–community management. The subgroup analyses suggested that delivery format, inclusion of pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training, integrated-care elements, and intervention duration may partly explain between-study differences, but no single factor fully accounted for the observed heterogeneity. Accordingly, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as average effects across a diverse group of interventions rather than as the expected effect of a single standardized intervention model.

From a practical perspective, these findings support the incorporation of health literacy–sensitive strategies into COPD self-management programs. Healthcare professionals should not only provide COPD-related knowledge, but also ensure that patients can understand, recall, and apply that information in daily care12,13. Plain-language communication, pictorial or simplified materials, teach-back methods, inhaler technique demonstration, written action plans, individualized counseling, symptom-monitoring tools, digital reminders, and repeated feedback may help reduce the gap between receiving information and implementing self-care behaviors. However, implementation should be adapted to local resources, patient characteristics, baseline health literacy, digital accessibility, and healthcare delivery models. For patients with limited health literacy or limited digital skills, additional face-to-face support and repeated reinforcement may be necessary.

This review has several strengths. First, it focused on randomized controlled trials, which strengthens the internal validity of the evidence compared with observational designs. Second, it included multiple clinically relevant outcomes, covering exercise capacity, pulmonary function, dyspnea, symptom burden, health-related quality of life, and self-management-related outcomes. Third, the review examined a broad range of intervention formats, including face-to-face, mixed, digital, and integrated-care approaches, thereby providing a comprehensive overview of current evidence. Finally, subgroup analyses, funnel plot assessments, and Egger’s regression tests for outcomes with at least 10 studies were conducted to explore heterogeneity and potential small-study effects.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although we refined the operational criteria for health literacy–sensitive interventions, the definition remained broader than interventions specifically designed to improve health literacy as a primary outcome. Many included interventions were general self-management or pulmonary rehabilitation programs that incorporated identifiable literacy-sensitive features, rather than interventions targeting health literacy alone. Second, substantial heterogeneity across studies limits the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Third, the methodological quality of included trials varied, and incomplete blinding, attrition, and selective reporting may have influenced the results, particularly for subjective outcomes such as dyspnea, symptom burden, health-related quality of life, and self-management ability. Fourth, self-management-related outcomes were measured using different instruments, requiring the use of standardized mean differences, which may reduce clinical interpretability. Fifth, not all studies measured health literacy, adherence, or self-efficacy as process outcomes, making it difficult to clarify the mechanisms through which the interventions produced clinical benefits. Finally, possible publication bias or small-study effects cannot be excluded. Egger’s regression test indicated statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry for mMRC but not for 6MWD, SGRQ, or CAT. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously because the number of studies for each outcome was relatively limited and substantial heterogeneity was present in several analyses.

Future research should address these limitations by using clearer operational definitions and standardized reporting of health literacy–sensitive intervention components. Trials should distinguish between health literacy, self-efficacy, adherence, and self-management behaviors, and should examine whether changes in these intermediate outcomes mediate improvements in clinical and patient-reported outcomes. More rigorous randomized trials with adequate allocation concealment, blinded outcome assessment where feasible, larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and prespecified protocols are needed. Future studies should also compare different delivery formats, including digital, face-to-face, and hybrid models, and evaluate their effectiveness among patients with different levels of baseline health literacy, disease severity, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare resources.

In summary, health literacy–sensitive self-management interventions appear to be associated with improvements in multiple outcomes among patients with COPD. The findings suggest that making health information more understandable, actionable, and applicable may strengthen patients’ capacity for long-term disease management. Nevertheless, because the available evidence is characterized by multicomponent interventions, substantial heterogeneity, and variable methodological quality, conclusions regarding the independent effect of health literacy enhancement should remain cautious. More standardized and mechanism-focused research is needed to determine how health literacy–sensitive strategies can be optimally integrated into COPD self-management care.

Conclusion

Self-management interventions with explicitly identifiable health literacy–sensitive components were associated with improvements in exercise capacity, pulmonary function, symptom burden, dyspnea, health-related quality of life, and self-management-related outcomes in patients with COPD. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because most included interventions were multicomponent programs incorporating health literacy–sensitive features rather than interventions targeting health literacy alone, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across several outcomes. Future trials should use clearer operational definitions, standardized intervention reporting, and direct assessment of health literacy-related process measures to clarify the specific contribution of health literacy–sensitive strategies in COPD management.
