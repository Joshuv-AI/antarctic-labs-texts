NN-xTB: density functional accuracy at semi empirical speed with neural network extended tight binding
Authors: Yufan Xia, Albert Thie, Joshua Soon, Giuseppe M. J. Barca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of molecular structure, energetics, and reactivity requires quantum chemistry methods that remain too costly for large-scale and high-throughput modeling. Semi-empirical tight-binding approaches are much faster, but their largely element-fixed parameters under-resolve local chemical-environment effects, limiting accuracy. We introduce a neural-network extension of extended-tight binding that learns bounded, environment-dependent shifts to Hamiltonian parameters, increasing chemical adaptivity while preserving self-consistency, charge and spin treatment, and correct long-range behavior. We show that, across benchmarks spanning main-group thermochemistry, noncovalent interactions, molecular forces, vibrational spectra and supramolecular complexes, this approach achieves density functional theory accuracy at near tight-binding cost. On the GMTKN55, the weighted mean absolute error decreases from 25.0 to 3.78 kcal/mol relative to GFN2-xTB; molecular force errors are lowest on eight of ten rMD17 molecules and improve by 10–40% relative to leading machine-learned interatomic potentials; and vibrational frequency errors decrease from 200.6 to 12.7 cm−1, with runtime increasing by less than 20%.

Introduction

Fast, general, and interpretable quantum mechanics remains a central goal in atomistic modeling. Kohn–Sham density functional theory (DFT)1 delivers reliable accuracy, but its relatively high computational cost limits routine use in large systems, long time-scale simulations, or screening workflows. Modern semi-empirical quantum chemistry methods2—most prominently the extended tight-binding (xTB) family3–5—bridge much of this gap by retaining near-DFT accuracy and generalization through a physically motivated Hamiltonian and self-consistency while achieving orders-of-magnitude lower time-to-solution. In particular, GFN2-xTB4 and, more recently, g-xTB5 extend earlier xTB variants with anisotropic multipole electrostatics and dispersion corrections—and in g-xTB, range-separated exchange and higher-order tight-binding terms—yielding robust structure and property predictions at low computational cost.

Yet the practical efficiency of xTB is coupled to a pervasive structural constraint in its parameterization: most of its empirical parameters are tabulated on a per-element (and/or shell) basis and are therefore applied uniformly to all atoms of the same element, irrespective of their molecular context. Only narrow environment adaptation surrogates are typically available—coordination-number corrections, self-consistent charge updates, and charge-aware dispersion as in D46. This means that the majority of parameter values remain essentially element-fixed.

From a tight-binding perspective, however, the local chemical environment is expected to perturb precisely those quantities that enter the xTB Hamiltonian: on-site (shell) energy levels and their coordination slopes, effective hardness and its charge derivative, Slater-type exponents controlling overlap decay and hopping scales, short-range exchange terms, anisotropic multipole electrostatics (and associated damping radii), and dispersion prefactors via polarizability changes. If these remain largely element-fixed, with only modest charge/coordination heuristics, the model under-resolves genuine environment dependence in covalency, polarization/anisotropy, and short-range exchange, thereby placing a practical ceiling on achievable accuracy.

In parallel, work on Machine-Learned Interatomic Potentials (MLIPs) has progressed quickly, driven by E(3)/SE(3)-equivariant message-passing architectures (e.g., NequIP, MACE, Allegro/PaiNN/GemNet/GotenNet families) and, more recently, large-scale pretrained networks (e.g., UMA trained on OMol25)7–14. Equivariance ensures that scalar and tensor outputs transform correctly under rotations and translations and are invariant to atom indexing, which greatly improves sample efficiency and physical consistency9,15. These models can match—in well-covered domains—DFT accuracy and accelerate dynamics by orders of magnitude, especially when run on Graphics Processing Units (GPUs).

However, most MLIPs replace the electronic structure model with a high-capacity black-box regressor. As a consequence, they typically do not provide orbitals, densities, multipoles, or linear/nonlinear response properties, and their asymptotic long-range behavior (electrostatics, induction, dispersion) must be approximated or added ad hoc16–19. Reliability degrades under extrapolation—charged or open-shell species, strong long-range interactions, and unseen chemistries, where multiple studies and reviews report marked error growth and even unstable dynamics20–24. Addressing these gaps generally requires broadening the training distribution and retraining or fine-tuning, which entails expensive data generation, curation, and re-validation21,25–27.

One may therefore consider the feasibility of a methodological synthesis that would preserve the interpretability, asymptotic fidelity, and self-consistency of an explicit semi-empirical quantum Hamiltonian, while endowing its most rigid components—the physically motivated but empirically fixed parameters—with disciplined, chemical-environment-dependent variability. Rather than replacing the Hamiltonian with a black-box surrogate or adding post-hoc Δ-energy corrections27–38, the focus is to intervene precisely at the narrow locus where empiricism enters—hardness, shell energies and overlap scalings that encode covalency, short-range exchange repulsion and anisotropy terms—and to let those quantities respond to the molecular context in a manner that is data-informed, symmetry-respecting and chemically constrained.

The underlying rationale for this approach is that element-fixed parameters provide robustness but insufficient sensitivity to the local chemical environment, whereas black-box regressors provide adaptivity but forfeit many of the inductive biases and cross-observable consistency of an explicit Hamiltonian.

The synthesis pursued here keeps the Hamiltonian explicit and confines learning to a few Hamiltonian parameters where empiricism has historically bound accuracy. Accordingly, energies, forces, and response properties are obtained from a single self-consistent field (SCF) formulation in which asymptotics are preserved analytically, and symmetry, extensivity, and size-consistency are enforced by construction. Since parameter updates are embedded in the Hamiltonian and propagate through the SCF solution, the framework achieves variational coherence: all calculated properties are mutually consistent manifestations of the same modified electronic state.

This stands in contrast to Δ-energy models, which reshape the potential-energy surface without redefining the Hamiltonian28,30,34,36. Such corrections can improve energies and forces (if the correction is differentiated), but they leave the underlying density unchanged, so properties that depend directly on that density remain unaffected. The distinction is therefore structural: Hamiltonian-level adaptation reorganizes the electronic state and all observables derived from it; Δ-corrections addenda act only on the potential-energy surface defined by that state.

There are also reasons to expect more robust behavior in data-sparse regimes. When the multipolar form of electrostatics, the dispersion model, and SCF charge redistribution remain explicit, the method inherits the semiempirical Hamiltonian guardrails for extreme charge states, unusual coordination, and large-distance limits. Constraining learning to a low-dimensional, physics-motivated parameter space and tethering those parameters to chemically reasonable ranges reduces the scope for pathological failure under extrapolation while capturing first-order environmental dependence that fixed constants miss. Related efforts have explored data-driven reparameterization of semiempirical and tight-binding Hamiltonians39,40, further motivating this operator-level approach.

In this work, we introduce a Hamiltonian-preserving, context-adaptive variant of xTB in which an equivariant neural network predicts small, bounded adjustments to a compact, physically-motivated subset of GFN2-xTB parameters. The modified Hamiltonian operator is then solved self-consistently, so energies, forces, and density-dependent observables arise from a single electronic state (variational coherence) while xTB’s analytic long-range behavior and charge/spin treatment are retained. We train on complementary datasets: forces (SPICE/MACEOFF23), vibrational information (VQM24), and relative/association energies (GMTKN55, DES15K) and evaluate on held-out splits across GMTKN55 (thermochemistry, noncovalent interactions, barrier subsets), DES15K (ion-ion, ion-neutral interactions), L7 (supramolecular assemblies), rMD17/MACEOFF23 (force fidelity against strong equivariant MLIPs), equilibrium geometries (W4–11–GEOM), VQM24 (frequencies), and 3BPA (temperature-shift robustness)41–46. Across these benchmarks, the model consistently shows improved performance over fixed-parameter xTB and matches or surpasses leading MLIPs on multiple benchmarks, while maintaining electronic observables and asymptotic correctness at near-xTB cost. In the following section, we summarize the applied methodology succinctly in order to interpret the figures and then present quantitative benchmarks, robustness analyses, and computational overhead.

GFN2-xTB in brief

GFN2-xTB is a density-functional extended tight-binding model with a compact atom-centered basis and self-consistent charge. Learning Hamiltonian parameters

Rather than predicting energies and forces, the neural component outputs bounded shifts to a compact subset of GFN2-xTB parameters. An E(3)-equivariant encoder, built using Multilayer Atomic Cluster Expansion (MACE)8 layers with a radial cutoff of rc = 5 Å produces atom-specific node encodings za(Z, R) and a pooled global encoding z = ∑aza. Assembly, self-consistency, and differentiation

Given {pa}, pglob, we assemble S(p), H(0)(p), and G[ ⋅ ; p], solve Eq. (2) to self-consistency with updated multipolar and D4 coefficients, and evaluate Eq. (4). Training and evaluation use an SCF convergence threshold of 10−6; level-shifting and damping are applied automatically for small-gap cases. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation through a customized PyTorch autograd implementation allows for implicit differentiation through the SCF fixed point. This enables us to obtain gradients, second- and third-order derivatives47.

Training targets, datasets, and splits

We perform supervised training for NN-xTB on complementary targets that constrain different derivatives of the potential energy surface (PES) and relative energetics. Evaluation is performed on held-out test sets and benchmarks designed to probe fidelity and generalization.

We use the SPICE and MACEOFF23 datasets for supervised training on per-atom forces at the ωB97M-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVPPD level41,42. SPICE provides a broad coverage of organic fragments of all charges; MACEOFF23 contributes large-scale, neutral subsets. These include PubChem, DES370K monomers, dipeptides, solvated amino acids, and water clusters, which are widely used to compare against strong E(3)/SE(3)-equivariant MLIPs under identical filtering. In addition, to assess whether the improved force predictions translate into reliable structural relaxation, we evaluate geometry optimization performance on a subset of the W4–11–GEOM dataset, comparing the optimized structures obtained with NN-xTB and GFN2-xTB against reference geometries48.

We use VQM24 for supervised training on fundamental frequencies at ωB97X-D3/cc-pVDZ for small organic and inorganic species (C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br)43. Following the dataset protocol, we use the supplied GFN2-xTB geometries but target the DFT frequencies, thereby shaping the curvature of the PES while keeping geometry bias fixed. This training signal discourages unphysical minima/flat directions and improves normal-mode spectra without decoupling from energies and forces (all derived from the same SCF state).

We use GMTKN55 to supervise reaction and association energies across main-group thermochemistry, barrier heights, and intra- and intermolecular noncovalent interactions, with CCSD(T)/CBS or composite references44. This anchors relative energetics across chemically diverse regimes and complements derivative-based supervised learning. In addition to the primary GMTKN55 benchmark, we evaluate NN-xTB on several targeted tests designed to probe distinct physical regimes that are either weakly represented or absent from GMTKN55. These include strongly ionic dimers using the DES15K dataset, the large supramolecular complexes in the L7 dataset, and the bond dissociation behavior for the H2 molecule49–51.

A universal NN-xTB model is trained with system-wise splits of 80%/10%/10% or 85%/5%/10% for SPICE, VQM24, DES15K, and GMTKN55, stratified by the molecule and reaction subset to prevent leakage41,43,44. Target quantities are normalized per-subset using median absolute deviation. This version of NN-xTB serves as a model that is generally applicable.

To compare against strong equivariant MLIPs under standard conditions, we train molecule-wise models on the rMD17 dataset, using 1000 frames per molecule with a 950/50 train/validation split and held-out test data45. We report the MAE and RMSE per component, where applicable.

To probe robustness beyond the training distribution, we follow the standard 3BPA protocol: train on 500 configurations at 300 K and evaluate on 1000 to 2000 configurations at 600/1200 K. This broadens conformational coverage and stresses long-range electrostatics and dispersion under the broadened ensembles.

As total charge and spin multiplicity handling is retained, ionic and open-shell species are treated natively as in xTB. We therefore also train and test on a charge-diverse SPICE subset (H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, I; neutral and charged species) to verify that force fidelity on charged systems is comparable to neutral-only performance under the same protocol41.

Relative energetics benchmark

We quantify the impact of Hamiltonian-level parameter adaptation on relative energetics using the GMTKN55 benchmark suite44. Training uses approximately an 85%/5%/10% (train/val/test) split system-wise, stratified by subset; we report MAEs and WTMAD-2 metrics on the entire GMTKN55 dataset to match the GFN2-xTB, g-xTB, and scheme of other DFT methods. The WTMAD-2 value of NN-xTB calculated for the entire GMTKN55 dataset is 3.78 kcal/mol—falling in the DFT accuracy range, while the WTMAD-2 value of GFN2-xTB on the same set is 25.0 kcal/mol, and that of g-xTB is 9.3 kcal/mol. For the train, validation, and test splits, NN-xTB achieves WTMAD-2 values of 3.42, 7.04, and 6.78 kcal/mol, respectively. On the test set, GFN2-xTB yields a WTMAD-2 value of 28.04 kcal/mol.

To test whether the train-test gap reflects model capacity or data scarcity, we trained an evaluation-trained variant of the model, NN-xTB[all-GMTKN55], on all GMTKN55 energies while leaving the forces and vibrational-frequency datasets unchanged. This model attains a WTMAD-2 of 2.18 kcal/mol on GMTKN55, substantially better than the original NN-xTB. Since the evaluation set is used for training, this number is not a fair benchmark but an upper bound that isolates representational limits from data limits. The gain indicates the architecture has sufficient capacity and that the original gap is driven primarily by the limited size/coverage of the energy training set.

It is worth noting that the weight factor for each subset is kept unchanged as calculated from the entire GMTKN55 set, to prevent fluctuation of the weight factor due to different subset sizes in the data splits. Detailed per-subset WTMAD-2 metrics for GMTKN55 on the training, validation and test sets, including also metrics for NN-xTB[all-GMTKN55] and comparisons to other DFT methods, are reported in Supplementary Table 4 for reference.

Vibrational frequencies benchmark

Harmonic vibrational frequencies probe the second derivatives (curvature) of the PES and therefore provide a stringent test of whether Hamiltonian–level adaptation improves local shape consistently with the forces and energies derived from the same electronic state. We evaluate on the VQM24 test split (C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br; ~8.36 × 105 configurations) where reference frequencies are computed at ωB97X–D3/cc–pVDZ and the geometries are fixed to GFN2-xTB–optimized structures43. Using the supplied geometries isolates curvature errors from geometric biases and mirrors the parametrization setting of xTB methods.

For each structure, we remove translational/rotational modes, project out numerical noise below ~5 cm−1, and report MAE in cm−1 over all remaining harmonic modes. The NN-xTB Hessian is obtained by differentiating the same assembled Hamiltonian via reverse-mode autodifferentiation, ensuring variational coherence with the energies and forces. Reverse-mode auto-differentiation requires a small number of graph passes and is dominated by the modest-basis SCF cost.

Table 4(b) reports mean absolute errors on the VQM24 test set. Relative to fixed-parameter GFN2-xTB, NN-xTB reduces the mean absolute frequency error from 200.6 to 12.7 cm−1 (> 90% reduction).

As shown in Fig. 5, improvements are significant across the entire spectrum. Frequency-binned MAEs indicate substantial gains in the low- and mid-wavenumber ranges ( < 500 cm−1), which are typically dominated by collective and torsional modes. Geometry optimization benchmark

Accurate forces should translate into directionally correct optimization steps and reliable convergence to low-energy structures. We test this by running BFGS geometry optimizations via the ASE interface on 102 molecules from the W4–11–GEOM dataset within the element scope of NN-xTB52,53. Each starting geometry is obtained by displacing all atoms in random directions with a standard deviation of 0.05 Å. We use the model trained on SPICE with both neutral and charge-diverse data, without any geometry-specific fine-tuning.

Transferability under distribution shift

To evaluate robustness beyond the training distribution, we follow the standard protocol of train-benchmark using the 3BPA dataset46. The 3BPA system (3-(benzyloxy)pyridin-2-amine; 44 atoms) exhibits broad torsional flexibility and multiple weak intramolecular contacts, making it a stringent test of force fidelity under conformational drift. We train on 500 configurations sampled at 300 K and evaluate on held-out sets at 300, 600, and 1200 K. The temperature shift broadens the explored conformational ensemble and stresses long-range electrostatics/dispersion and many-body couplings not seen during training.

Table 4d summarizes force RMSE at each temperature for recent, strong E(3)/SE(3)-equivariant baselines and for NN-xTB trained at 300 K7–9,54. At the training temperature (300 K), NN-xTB attains 6.1 meV/Å, improving over the best baseline (MACE, 8.8 meV/Å) by ~31%.

Under the distribution shift, NN-xTB maintains a lower error: a 16.8 meV/Å at 600 K (vs 21.8 for MACE; ~23% reduction) and a 35.4 meV/Å at 1200 K (vs 62.0; ~43% reduction). Improvements relative to other models(Allegro, NequIP, BOTNet) are of comparable magnitude.

The 3BPA test isolates thermal broadening on a single flexible neutral molecule; it does not probe chemical extrapolation, such as elements or bonding motifs absent from training. Nevertheless, it is a widely used measure of out-of-distribution behavior for force fields. Extensions to charged/open-shell flexible systems are straightforward in NN-xTB as charge and spin enter the SCF natively. This can be seen in the results for the charge-diverse SPICE subset, as errors remain comparable to the performance on the neutral set.

Dipole moments

To evaluate whether Hamiltonian-level parameter adaptation perturbs density-derived properties outside the training objective, we benchmarked molecular dipole moments on a CCSD(T)/CBS reference set55. The base unified NN-xTB model (without dipole supervision) shows a modest error increase relative to GFN2-xTB (0.61 D versus 0.45 D MAE), reflecting the expected trade-off when optimizing for forces and relative energies. Adding a dipole loss term recovers competitive performance (0.33 D), confirming that the framework can incorporate additional property targets without architectural changes. Detailed benchmarking is provided in Supplementary Information Section III.B.

Discussion

We presented NN-xTB, a Hamiltonian-adaptive framework that augments the explicit GFN2-xTB operator with small, bounded, environment-dependent shifts to a compact set of physically interpretable parameters, predicted by an equivariant neural-network model. This preserves interpretability, analytic long-range behavior, and native charge/spin treatment, while restoring the local chemical sensitivity that element-fixed parameters underresolve. Across complementary validation axes—including relative energetics, forces, vibrational curvatures, structural relaxation, and challenging interaction regimes—NN-xTB delivers consistent and substantial improvements, as summarized below.

On GMTKN55, NN-xTB yields a WTMAD-2 of 3.78 kcal/mol, improving from 25.0 kcal/mol for GFN2-xTB and from 9.3 kcal/mol for g-xTB; incorporating a stratified subset of DES15K further strengthens the description of strongly ionic dimers, yielding MAEs of 0.29 and 0.36 kcal/mol on the DES15K train and test sets, respectively, compared with 1.68 and 1.63 kcal/mol for GFN2-xTB. For forces, NN-xTB achieves the lowest MAE on eight of ten rMD17 molecules and yields 10–40% RMSE reductions on large MACEOFF23/SPICE subsets relative to strong equivariant MLIP baselines; on a charge-diverse SPICE split, the held-out force RMSE is 16.7 meV/Å, comparable to neutral-only results under the same protocol. For vibrational spectra (VQM24), the mean absolute error decreases from 200.6 cm−1 for GFN2-xTB to 12.7 cm−1 (>90% reduction), indicating that the curvature of the potential energy surface is corrected consistently with the forces and energies derived from the same electronic state. Structural relaxation is also improved: in geometry optimization benchmarks on W4–11–GEOM molecules, the geometry RMSD decreases from 0.022 Å for GFN2-xTB to 0.009 Å for NN-xTB, while the energy-based metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| {E}_{{{{\rm{geo}}}}}^{{\prime} }|$$\end{document}∣Egeo′∣ improves from 0.023 to 0.007 kcal/mol. Performance gains extend to challenging regimes beyond the primary training targets: on the L7 supramolecular benchmark, NN-xTB reduces the MAE from 2.83 kcal/mol for GFN2-xTB to 1.82 kcal/mol, and under distribution shift (3BPA), NN-xTB sustains lower force RMSEs than competing MLIPs at elevated temperatures, achieving 6.1, 16.8, and 35.4 meV/Å at 300, 600, and 1200 K, respectively. Since the SCF dominates runtime, adding the neural-network module increases wall time modestly (typically < 20%), preserving the speed advantage of semi-empirical QM over DFT.

The improvements demonstrated above apply to the chemical domain represented in the training data, which includes the main-group elements H, C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br, and I and spans the vast majority of organic and drug-like molecules; furthermore, the relative energy training covers an additional 12 elements contained in the GMTKN55 dataset. NN-xTB augments the existing GFN2-xTB Hamiltonian through bounded parameter shifts (Eqs. (8, 10)), and when the neural correction layer encounters regimes that were not represented in the training data, the correction in our software is set up to reduce to the baseline GFN2-xTB parameterization (Δp = 0). In this sense, NN-xTB preserves the periodic-table applicability of the underlying xTB framework while introducing environment-adaptive parameterization within the trained chemical domain.

This fallback behavior is a deliberate choice reflecting the fact that certain regimes have limited availability of representative reference data. A simple illustrative example is radical cations/anions (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{H}}}}}_{2}^{+}$$\end{document}H2+) that contain jointly charged and open-shell electronic structure and are absent from the present training datasets; similar situations arise for other radical cations or strongly open-shell species such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CH}}}}}_{3}^{+}$$\end{document}CH3+, NO+, or molecular radical intermediates encountered in photochemistry and plasma chemistry. In such cases, the learned correction layer is not expected to provide systematic improvements, and the current implementation defaults safely to the GFN2-xTB description. Extending NN-xTB to these regimes, therefore, requires the same principle that underlies all semiempirical methods: generating suitable reference data and training the corresponding parameter corrections while preserving the Hamiltonian structure. Ongoing work is focused on constructing targeted reference datasets for open-shell systems and conditioning the neural parameter-prediction layers explicitly on total charge and spin (Q, M).

Several natural extensions follow. First, this study is built on the GFN2-xTB Hamiltonian, and we plan a drop-in extension to g-xTB (range-separated exchange and higher-order TB terms) to strengthen the fixed-parameter baseline and test whether Hamiltonian-level adaptivity compounds those advances. Second, while total charge and spin already enter the SCF procedure, we will explicitly condition the parameter shift prediction layers on (Q, M) to capture spin- and charge-state–dependent local responses; combined with targeted reference datasets for radical and open-shell species, this will extend the adaptive parameterization to regimes currently falling back to baseline GFN2-xTB. Third, for dispersion, D4 relies on environment-aware pair coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{6}^{AB},{C}_{8}^{AB}$$\end{document}C6AB,C8AB and the three-body C9 (Axilrod–Teller–Muto) built from atom-in-molecule polarizabilities and partial charges with BJ-type damping; we will add tailored pairwise descriptors to predict small, bounded modifiers of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({C}_{n}^{AB})$$\end{document}(CnAB) and/or damping radii/factors on top of D4, allowing context-aware refinement of long-range association while retaining charge awareness and the analytic form. Finally, a fast, multi-GPU-accelerated, fused implementation of the NN and semi-empirical QM components is underway in the Extreme-scale Electronic Structure System (EXESS)56, employing mixed-precision kernels for the encoder and SCF primitives (overlap/orthogonalization, Fock updates, density mixing) with stability safeguards; this aims to deliver a low-latency implementation at large scale and enable routine dynamics and screening.

NN-xTB shows that Hamiltonian-level adaptation—learning where empiricism historically limits semi-empirical QM—can close much of the gap to DFT on relative energetics, reach state-of-the-art force fidelity, and reduce vibrational errors by an order of magnitude, all while preserving interpretability, asymptotics, and native charge/spin robustness. Extending the approach to g-xTB, enriching (Q, M) conditioning, and incorporating pair-aware dispersion refinements are natural next steps toward a broadly applicable, physically grounded, and GPU-native quantum-accurate engine for molecular simulation and design.

Methods

This section details the architecture and mathematical formulation of the proposed Hamiltonian-adaptive framework, comprising (i) an E(3)-equivariant encoder that infers atomistic environment descriptors, (ii) a hierarchy of parameter-shift prediction networks (PSPNs) that map those descriptors to bounded, context-dependent Hamiltonian parameters, and (iii) a semi-empirical physical module that embeds the learned parameters into an explicit, differentiable GFN2-xTB Hamiltonian solved self-consistently. We then discuss how differentiation through both the neural and quantum components is achieved using implicit auto-differentiation at the SCF fixed point. Finally, we discuss the technical details of our training procedure.

Neural network module

Physics module and connection

As outlined in the Results section, we retain the standard GFN2-xTB Hamiltonian as the explicit quantum-mechanical core of our hybrid framework and make it differentiable end-to-end. Below, we detail the implementation choices needed for clarity and reproducibility. We describe how the learned parameters are injected, how the operator is assembled, and how derivatives are obtained through the SCF fixed point. A complete description of the underlying GFN2-xTB formalism is provided in Ref. 4; the full list of adjustable atom- and shell-specific parameters targeted by the PSPNs is enumerated in Supplementary Note 1.

Environment-dependent parameter shifts predicted by the PSPNs are applied to parameter baselines as in Eqs. (8, 9). Our implementation supports three parameter scopes, which map directly to the relevant parts of the GFN2-xTB operator.

For a given R, Z, Q, M, we first compute node descriptors {za} and the pooled descriptor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{{{{\bf{z}}}}}$$\end{document}z¯ (Eq. Note that, within this loop, global PSPN outputs are not re-evaluated for a fixed geometry; all parameter shifts are treated as functions of R and are recomputed only when R changes.

In the hybrid architecture, the neural network predicts geometry-dependent parameter shifts pθ(R) that modify the GFN2-xTB Hamiltonian. Evaluating these expressions explicitly is impractical. We therefore rely on automatic differentiation (AD) to apply the chain rule exactly through the full computational graph that includes both the neural and quantum components. In practice, differentiation through the physical model is implemented using a specifically developed version of the dxTB package47, which extends GFN2-xTB4 with full support for reverse-mode AD across all parameters selected for machine learning. Our implementation directly computes ∂E/∂R, ∂E/∂p, and their mixed partials, while the neural component supplies ∂pθ/∂R and ∂pθ/∂θ through standard PyTorch backpropagation. Since all dependencies are captured in a single differentiable graph, forces and curvatures are obtained by a single or double gradient pass, respectively, at a computational cost comparable to standard SCF gradient evaluation.

To verify correctness, we benchmarked the AD-derived forces and frequencies against finite-difference references using a central five-point stencil (step size 10−3 Å). Force discrepancies remain below 10−5 Hartree/Bohr, and frequency differences are within a few cm−1, confirming that the AD gradients accurately capture all dependencies.

Regarding SCF and basis settings, all calculations use the GFN2-xTB minimal valence basis of contracted Slater-type orbitals57, with adaptive Broyden mixing and a 100-iteration cap. The SCF convergence threshold is 10−4 Hartree during early training and 10−8 Hartree for fine-tuning and inference. Level shifting and damping are applied automatically for small-gap systems. The charge Q and spin multiplicity M determine both SCF occupations and global PSPN inputs, ensuring consistent treatment of charge- and spin-dependent terms across the hybrid framework.

Training methodology

We train the hybrid model by minimizing a composite, physics-targeted objective function that jointly supervises (i) Cartesian forces, (ii) harmonic vibrational information, and (iii) relative energies (from GMTKN55). This multi-objective formulation mirrors the goals of semi-empirical parameterization while exploiting the end-to-end differentiability of the Hamiltonian-level adaptation, yielding a single model that remains balanced across diverse chemical tasks.

Unless stated otherwise, we use a unified NN-xTB model trained jointly on the broad multi-task datasets described above: force data from SPICE/MACEOFF23, vibrational data from VQM24, and relative-energy data from GMTKN55 augmented with the DES15K subset. The W4–11–GEOM geometry optimization benchmark, the L7 supramolecular benchmark, and the H2 dissociation test are used strictly for evaluation and are not included as supervised training targets. A full summary of dataset sizes, system-size ranges, train/test splits, and reference levels of theory is provided in Supplementary Note 2.

For benchmark-specific comparisons, however, we follow the standard protocol used in the MLIP literature and train separate NN-xTB models on the prescribed benchmark splits. This applies in particular to the per-molecule rMD17 models and the 3BPA temperature-shift benchmark, where direct comparison requires using the same train/validation/test partitions and target definitions as prior studies7,8,45,46,54. In those cases, the same NN-xTB architecture is used, but the objective is restricted to the corresponding benchmark target, typically, forces.

Our multi-objective loss function for the unified model takes the form: 26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{rcl}{{{\mathcal{L}}}} &=& \frac{{w}_{{{{\rm{force}}}}}}{{N}_{I}}{\sum }_{i=1}^{{N}_{I}}{\left\Vert {\widehat{{{{\bf{F}}}}}}_{i}-{{{{\bf{F}}}}}_{i}^{{{{\rm{ref}}}}}\right\Vert }^{2}+\frac{{w}_{{{{\rm{freq}}}}}}{{N}_{J}}{\sum }_{j=1}^{{N}_{J}}{\left({\widehat{\omega }}_{j}-{\omega }_{j}^{{{{\rm{ref}}}}}\right)}^{2}\\ & &+\frac{{w}_{{{{\rm{energy}}}}}}{{N}_{K}}{\sum }_{k=1}^{{N}_{K}}{\left({\widehat{E}}_{k}-{E}_{k}^{{{{\rm{ref}}}}}\right)}^{2}\end{array}$$\end{document}L=wforceNI∑i=1NIF^i−Firef2+wfreqNJ∑j=1NJω^j−ωjref2+wenergyNK∑k=1NKE^k−Ekref2 where the three terms represent the mean squared errors for forces, vibrational frequencies, and non-covalent interaction energies, respectively, each weighted by wforce, wfreq, and wenergy. Before weighting, each loss term is normalized based on a pre-training evaluation to bring all components to a comparable scale (typically around 10). This normalization ensures that no single loss dominates the total during training, allowing for more flexible weighting to focus on specific targets as desired.

For optimization, we employ the AdamW algorithm58. During the final combined model training, we use a weight decay of 10−8; for other models, a weight decay of 10−4 is used for better regularization. Training starts with an initial learning rate of 0.001, and we apply a reduce-on-plateau scheduling scheme, with a patience parameter of 10 and a decay factor of 0.5. Training is conducted with distributed data parallelism and a per-device batch size equivalent to 32. When the learning rate scheduler reaches the minimum learning rate of 10−9, and the validation loss does not improve, the training is stopped.

Training with neural networks and tracking higher-order derivatives introduces additional computational overhead. To make it feasible to train on millions of examples, we use distributed training to accelerate the process. Given that the GNN model is difficult to partition across multiple GPUs but is efficient on a single device, we adopt the Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) strategy, which speeds up training primarily by distributing the data. DDP synchronizes and averages the gradients for each model parameter across all GPUs via an allreduce operation before each optimizer step, ensuring consistency and stable convergence. As a result, the effective batch size is scaled by the number of GPUs. Due to the efficiency of the backend communication, the wall time per batch does not significantly increase despite gradient aggregation across devices.
