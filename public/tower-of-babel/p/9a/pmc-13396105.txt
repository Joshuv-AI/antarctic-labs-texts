M6A-modified lncRNA HULC promotes osteosarcoma progression by stabilizing GLUT1 mRNA through interaction with TAF15
Authors: Bo Liu, Yang Yue, Chaoqun Cen, Yong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe overexpression of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) HULC is recognized as an important contributor to osteosarcoma (OS) progression. However, its precise roles and mechanisms in OS remain unclear, necessitating further investigation.MethodsThe expression levels of HULC, methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), TATA-box-binding protein-associated factor 15 (TAF15), and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) were measured using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). RNA in situ hybridization and subcellular localization analyses were conducted to determine the spatial distribution of HULC. Methylation of HULC by METTL3 was assessed using methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assays. Functional assays, including CCK8 and transwell assays, were conducted to evaluate cell viability, migration, and invasion. Immunofluorescence staining of E-cadherin and N-cadherin was performed to assess the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Glycolysis was evaluated through extracellular acidification rate, oxygen consumption rate, glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP levels. Protein levels of glycolysis-related markers, TAF15, and GLUT1 were analyzed by Western blotting (WB). Xenograft and lung metastasis models were used to examine the roles of HULC and METTL3 in OS tumorigenesis and metastasis in vivo, and the interactions between TAF15 and HULC or GLUT1 were investigated using RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and RNA pull-down assays, while fluorescence in situ hybridization confirmed the co-localization of HULC and TAF15.ResultsElevated HULC expression was found to be correlated with poor prognosis in OS patients. In addition, METTL3-mediated m6A modification stabilized HULC expression, which promoted OS cell viability, migration, EMT, and glycolysis, and these effects could be reversed upon METTL3 downregulation. In vivo, METTL3 knockdown reduced tumor growth and lung metastasis primarily by decreasing HULC expression. Mechanistically, HULC enhanced GLUT1 mRNA stability by interacting with TAF15. Silencing TAF15 or GLUT1 mitigated the effects of HULC overexpression, suppressing OS cell viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis.ConclusionM6A modification of HULC promotes OS progression by stabilizing GLUT1 mRNA through interaction with TAF15, highlighting a potential regulatory axis in OS pathogenesis and providing insights into therapeutic strategies targeting METTL3, HULC, or TAF15.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01229-y.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a primary malignant bone tumor originating from mesenchymal tissue and is ranked as the second most common primary bone tumor [1]. Despite significant advancements in medical technology (neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy) that have improved the overall survival rates of patients with OS [2, 3], the efficacy of the standard-of-care is often limited by the development of chemoresistance and the high propensity for metastasis, leading to poor prognosis in advanced or recurrent cases [4, 5]. Recent advances have underscored metabolic reprogramming, particularly the hyperactivation of aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), as a hallmark of OS that fuels its aggressive phenotype and contributes to therapy resistance [6, 7]. Consequently, targeting key regulators of glycolysis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to circumvent conventional treatment limitations and inhibit OS progression. In this context, elucidating the molecular drivers of glycolytic flux in OS is of paramount translational importance.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), defined as RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, have been demonstrated to play pivotal roles in regulating fundamental biological processes such as tumor proliferation and metastasis [8, 9]. Among these, the lncRNA highly upregulated in liver cancer (HULC) was first identified as a critical molecule in human hepatocellular carcinoma [10]. Subsequent studies revealed that HULC functions as an oncogene in OS [11], where its expression is significantly elevated in tumor tissues, and its downregulation suppresses tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [12, 13]. Furthermore, it has been reported that HULC facilitates glycolysis by interacting with key glycolytic enzymes such as LDHA and PKM2, highlighting its potential role in metabolic regulation [14]. However, whether HULC directly modulates cell glycolysis to drive OS progression remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

RNA methylation, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A), represents an important post-transcriptional modification that plays an essential role in the regulation of gene expression and has been implicated in tumorigenesis [15, 16]. Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), the catalytic subunit of the m6A “writer” complex, has emerged as a significant regulator in the development of various malignancies, including OS [17]. For instance, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of ZBTB7C has been shown to promote OS cell growth [18], while its regulation of MALAT1 through m6A modification facilitates both proliferation and metastasis of OS cells [19]. Nevertheless, whether METTL3 contributes to OS progression by regulating the m6A modification of HULC remains to be determined.

Interactions between lncRNAs and specific proteins often play a crucial role in enabling lncRNAs to regulate various biological processes [20]. TATA-box-binding protein-associated factor 15 (TAF15), a member of the TET/FET protein family, is known to participate in transcription, splicing, and RNA transport by binding to RNA molecules [21, 22]. TAF15 has been reported to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of Ewing sarcoma and is considered a diagnostic marker for this disease [23, 24]. Glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) is the most important energy carrier for transporting glucose across cellular membranes, including the blood-brain barrier, thereby ensuring energy supply to the brain [25]. In tumor cells, GLUT1 enhances glycolysis and contributes to the development and progression of various malignancies [26, 27]. In this study, we observed that HULC binds to TAF15, which, in turn, interacts with GLUT1. However, whether HULC modulates the TAF15/GLUT1 axis to influence OS progression remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that METTL3-mediated modification of HULC promotes OS progression by enhancing glycolysis through the regulation of the TAF15/GLUT1 axis. Overall, these findings offer promising insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OS and provide potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Detailed information regarding all reagents, and antibodies, used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Clinical tissues and cell culture

Paired OS tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumorous bone tissues were collected from 30 patients who underwent surgery at The Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had histologically confirmed OS and had provided written informed consent. Patients with a history of other cancers were excluded from the study. Tissue specimens from each patient were obtained in a single procedure, and the clinical characteristics of these patients are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Human OS cell lines, including U2OS and HOS (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or EMEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, respectively. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was used to authenticate all cell lines.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples and OS cell lines using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). PCR amplification was conducted with SYBR Green (Takara) using the synthesized cDNA and specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 3. β-actin served as the reference gene, and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Subcellular localization analysis

The nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA from OS cells was isolated using the PARIS Kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative expression of HULC in the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions was determined by qRT-PCR, with GAPDH and U6 used as controls for the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments, respectively.

Cell transfection

OS cells were transfected with overexpression plasmids for HULC (oe-HULC) or TAF15 (oe-TAF15), small hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting METTL3 (sh-METTL3), TAF15 (sh-TAF15), or GLUT1 (sh-GLUT1), and the respective negative controls (oe-NC and sh-NC), using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

MeRIP

OS cells were transfected with sh-NC, sh-METTL3, or HULC wild-type (WT) or mutant-type (MUT) methylation transcripts. Total RNA was extracted and fragmented using an mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen) and fragmentation buffer. Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) was performed using the Magna MeRIP m6A Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fragmented RNA was incubated with anti-m6A or anti-IgG antibodies conjugated to protein A/G magnetic beads. The enrichment of m6A-modified HULC in the co-precipitated RNA was quantified by qRT-PCR. All data were analyzed after normalizing the signal of the IP product to the corresponding Input signal to control for the effect of the target RNA abundance.

RNA stability assay

OS cells were transfected with sh-NC, sh-METTL3, or sh-HULC and treated with 5 µg/mL actinomycin D solution (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). The cells were collected at indicated time points. The relative RNA levels of HULC and GLUT1 RNA was analyzed using qRT-PCR.

CCK8 assay

OS cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 48 h. Following incubation, 10 µL of CCK8 reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Transwell assay

OS cells were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts with serum-free medium, which were either pre-coated with Matrigel (for invasion assays) or left uncoated (for migration assays). A complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers. After 24 h of incubation, cells on the upper surface of the inserts were removed, and the migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

OS cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% goat serum for 30 min at 37 °C, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-N-cadherin (ab18203, Abcam) and anti-E-cadherin (ab40772, Abcam), followed by secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594 or 488 (ab150080 or ab150077, Abcam). Their nuclei were stained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Aerobic glycolysis and seahorse extracellular flux assays

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in OS cells were measured using the XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Billerica, MA, USA). Glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP levels were quantified using the Glucose Assay Kit (ab65333, Abcam), L-Lactate Assay Kit (ab65331, Abcam), and ATP Assay Kit (ab83355, Abcam), respectively, following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from OS cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime) and quantified. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies (all from Abcam), including anti-GLUT1 (ab115730, 1:10000), anti-HK2 (ab209847, 1:1000), anti-LDHA (ab52488, 1:5000), anti-TAF15 (ab134916, 1:10000), anti-Twist (ab313483, 1:1000), anti-Snail (ab216347, 1:1000), and anti-β-actin (ab8227, 1:1000), followed by secondary antibodies (ab205718, 1:50000). Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Beyotime), and band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Animal experiments

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old; Vital River, Beijing, China) were housed under controlled temperature and light conditions with free access to water and food. Randomization of animals to treatment groups was performed prior to treatment initiation. As described previously [28], OS xenograft tumor models and lung metastasis models were established using BALB/c nude mice. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, and conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

For the xenograft model, U2OS cells stably transduced with lentivirus constructs expressing oe-NC, lentivirus oe-HULC, lentivirus sh-NC, or lentivirus sh-METTL3 (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for vector details) were injected into the axillae of mice (n = 5 per group). Tumor volume was measured weekly and calculated as (length×width2)/2. All mice were sacrificed at day 28, prior to any tumor reaching the permitted maximum size (maximum diameter ≤ 20 mm). Excised tumors were then weighed.

For the lung metastasis model, U2OS cells stably transfected with oe-NC, oe-HULC, sh-NC, or sh-METTL3 were injected into the tail veins of mice (n = 5 per group). After 5 weeks (to allow macroscopic nodule formation while minimizing excessive tumor burden), the mice were sacrificed using carbon dioxide following sodium pentobarbital anesthesia as per humane endpoints, and lung tissues were collected for analysis.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tumor tissues collected from the mice were processed into paraffin sections, which were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and blocked with goat serum, followed by incubation with primary antibodies, including anti-Ki67 (1:200, ab15580, Abcam), anti-E-cadherin (1:500, ab40772, Abcam), anti-N-cadherin (1:50, ab76011, Abcam), and anti-GLUT1 (1:500, ab115730, Abcam), and then incubation with secondary antibody (1:20000, ab205718, Abcam). Afterward, the sections were stained with hematoxylin, developed with DAB, and sealed with neutral gum. Images were acquired under a microscope, and the staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The integrated optical density (IOD) of positive staining was calculated for each marker.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Lung tissue paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin (Beyotime). After washing with distilled water, the sections were counterstained with 1% Eosin Y (Beyotime). The sections were analyzed under a microscope for histopathological evaluation.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

OS cells transfected with sh-NC or sh-HULC were lysed in RIP lysis buffer, and the lysates were incubated overnight with magnetic beads pre-coated with anti-TAF15 (ab134916, Abcam) or anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam). The RNA-protein complexes were purified, and HULC and GLUT1 RNA enrichments were analyzed using qRT-PCR. All data were analyzed after normalizing the signal of the IP product to the corresponding Input signal to control for the effect of the target RNA abundance.

RNA pull-down assay

Biotin-labeled HULC sense or antisense probes were generated by in vitro transcription using PCR-amplified templates corresponding to the HULC transcript (NR_004855). The purified probes were immobilized onto streptavidin magnetic beads and incubated with OS cell lysates overnight at 4 °C. Bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by WB for TAF15.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-IF staining

OS cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The cells were hybridized with FAM-labeled HULC RNA probes (FISH, synthesized by RiboBio) and anti-TAF15 (1:500, ab134916, Abcam) and Cy3-labeled secondary antibody (1:500, A10520, Invitrogen) following the protocol provided by the FISH Kit (RiboBio). After DAPI staining of the nuclei, images were captured and analyzed using a confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, and multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves. Correlations between gene expression levels were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. All measurements and outcome assessments were performed by investigators blinded to treatment group allocation. Group information was held by an independent investigator and revealed only after data collection was complete. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High HULC expression was observed in OS tissues

To investigate the role of HULC in OS, we analyzed its expression in 30 paired OS tissues and histologically non-neoplastic tissues. qRT-PCR assay revealed that HULC expression was significantly upregulated in OS tissues compared to non-neoplastic tissues (Fig. 1A). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that OS patients with high HULC expression exhibited lower survival rates (Fig. 1B). To explore the subcellular localization of HULC in OS cells, we performed FISH and subcellular localization assays, which indicated that HULC was mainly localized in the cytoplasm of U2OS and HOS cells (Fig. 1C-D). These findings suggest that high HULC expression may contribute to OS progression.

METTL3 stabilized HULC through m6A modification

Considering the significant role of m6A modifications in OS progression, we predicted the potential m6A modification sites in HULC using the SRAMP website. Expression analysis by qRT-PCR revealed that the m6A writer METTL3 was upregulated in OS tissues and positively correlated with HULC expression (Fig. 2A-B). To further examine the relationship between METTL3 and HULC, METTL3 expression was knocked down in OS cells using sh-METTL3 (Fig. 2C). After METTL3 knockdown, HULC expression was markedly reduced in OS cells (Fig. 2D).

To determine whether METTL3 regulated HULC via m6A modification, we evaluated the m6A level of HULC in METTL3-downregulated cells. HULC wild-type (WT) and mutant-type (MUT) transcripts were used to determine the effect of mutation at a high-confidence m6A modification site (located at position 112 of the sequence). MeRIP assays showed that the m6A modification level of HULC was significantly reduced in cells with METTL3 knockdown and that the HULC MUT transcript exhibited a lower m6A modification level compared to the WT transcript (Fig. 2E-F). Besides, the m6A enrichment level of HULC in the MUT group was significantly lower than that in the WT group in sh-METTL3 transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that the regulation of HULC by METTL3 is dependent on this m6A modification site. RNA stability analysis following actinomycin D treatment revealed that METTL3 knockdown decreased the stability of HULC RNA (Fig. 2G). These findings indicate that METTL3 stabilizes HULC expression through m6A modification, highlighting its regulatory role in OS progression.

Silencing METTL3 abolished HULC-mediated OS progression

The transfection of sh-METTL3 was used to reduce METTL3 mRNA and protein expression in U2OS cells(Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). To determine whether METTL3 regulates HULC to mediate OS progression, U2OS and HOS cells were co-transfected with oe-HULC and sh-METTL3. The results showed that the transfection with oe-HULC significantly increased HULC expression, but this effect was reversed by sh-METTL3 (Fig. 3A). Functional assays were then performed to evaluate the impact of co-transfection on OS cell behavior, and we observed that the overexpression of HULC enhanced cell viability, migration, and invasion in both U2OS and HOS cells, while these effects were completely abolished by METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 3B-C). Furthermore, overexpression of HULC significantly reversed the inhibitory effects of METTL3 knockdown on HULC expression and cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 4A-B).

IF staining for epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers revealed that METTL3 silencing in HULC-upregulated cells increased E-cadherin expression and decreased N-cadherin expression (Fig. 3D). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that HULC overexpression upregulated the levels of EMT-related proteins Twist and Snail, whereas METTL3 knockdown reversed these changes (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To examine the role of METTL3 in glycolysis, we measured ECAR and OCR. The results showed that HULC overexpression increased ECAR and decreased OCR in U2OS and HOS cells, and that these effects could be mitigated by METTL3 silencing (Fig. 3E-G). Similarly, METTL3 knockdown reversed the effects of HULC overexpression on glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP levels (Fig. 3H-J). In addition, HULC overexpression increased the expression of glycolysis-related proteins GLUT1, HK2, and LDHA, while METTL3 knockdown eliminated these effects (Fig. 3K). These results suggest that METTL3 downregulation suppresses the viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis of OS cells by reducing HULC expression.

METTL3 knockdown reduced OS tumor growth and lung metastasis via HULC

To further validate the regulatory relationship between METTL3 and HULC in vivo, a xenograft tumor model was established. The overexpression of HULC was found to promote tumor growth, as indicated by increased tumor size, volume, and weight, and these effects were significantly attenuated by METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 4A-C). Analysis of the tumor tissues revealed that HULC expression was elevated in the oe-HULC group, but both HULC and METTL3 expression levels were reduced in the oe-HULC + sh-METTL3 group (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, IHC staining of tumor sections demonstrated that HULC overexpression increased the number of Ki67-positive cells and upregulated N-cadherin and GLUT1 expression, while decreasing E-cadherin expression. These effects were reversed by METTL3 silencing (Fig. 4E). In addition, a rescue experiment that maintained high HULC levels under METTL3 knockdown was performed. The results showed that knockdown of METTL3 significantly inhibited tumor size, volume and weight, as well as downregulated HULC expression and the protein levels of METTL3, GLUT1, Ki67, N-cadherin, and upregulated E-cadherin levels. However, overexpression of HULC reversed the effects of METTL3 knockdown on tumor growth and the expression of these indicators, without affecting METTL3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4C-G).

To explore the impact of METTL3 and HULC on metastasis, a lung metastasis model was constructed. The results showed that HULC overexpression facilitated lung metastasis, while METTL3 knockdown abrogated this effect (Fig. 4F-G).

These findings indicate that METTL3 downregulation reduces HULC expression, thereby inhibiting OS tumor growth and lung metastasis in vivo. Notably, we also found that this salvage effect of HULC overexpression was not complete, which may be because METTL3 is also involved in the regulation of other downstream target genes.

HULC bound with TAF15 protein

TAF15, a protein highly translocated in sarcomas, has been implicated in carcinogenesis [29]. Using the RPISeq online tool, we predicted a potential binding relationship between TAF15 and the lncRNA HULC. To experimentally validate this relationship, we conducted RIP and RNA pull-down assays. The RIP assay showed significant enrichment of HULC in the immunoprecipitated complexes with anti-TAF15, confirming their interaction (Fig. 5A). Consistently, the RNA pull-down assay demonstrated that TAF15 was pulled down by the HULC probe (Fig. 5B). Further, FISH-IF analysis revealed co-localization of HULC with TAF15 in the cytoplasm of OS cells (Fig. 5C). In addition, oe-HULC increased both mRNA and protein levels of TAF15, whereas TAF15 knockdown reversed these changes (Fig. 5D-F). These findings indicate that HULC binds to the TAF15 protein.

HULC interacts with TAF15 to stabilize GLUT1 mRNA

GLUT1 is considered to be an oncogene involved in OS progression [30, 31]. In this study, we examined GLUT1 expression and analyzed its relevance to the HULC expression in OS tissues. Our qRT-PCR analysis revealed that GLUT1 expression was significantly upregulated in OS tissues and positively correlated with HULC expression (Fig. 6A-B). Given that TAF15, as an RNA-binding protein (RBP), potentially interacts with GLUT1, we hypothesized that HULC might regulate GLUT1 expression by binding to TAF15.

To test this hypothesis, RIP and RNA pull-down assays were performed. The RIP assay demonstrated significant enrichment of GLUT1 by anti-TAF15 in U2OS and HOS cells (Fig. 6C), and RNA pull-down analysis confirmed the interaction between TAF15 and GLUT1 (Fig. 6D). Further, qRT-PCR showed that oe-TAF15 increased both TAF15 and GLUT1 mRNA levels. However, sh-GLUT1 abolished the oe-TAF15-induced increase in GLUT1 expression without affecting TAF15 expression (Fig. 6E).

Actinomycin D-based RNA stability assays revealed that HULC knockdown reduced GLUT1 mRNA stability (Fig. 6F). Additionally, RIP assays showed decreased GLUT1 enrichment in anti-TAF15 immunoprecipitates upon HULC silencing (Fig. 6G). Moreover, the overexpression of HULC promoted the mRNA and protein levels of both TAF15 and GLUT1, while GLUT1 knockdown reversed the effect on GLUT1 without altering TAF15 expression (Fig. 6H-I). Conversely, HULC knockdown suppressed GLUT1 expression, which was rescued by TAF15 or GLUT1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 6). Collectively, these results indicate that HULC interacts with TAF15 to stabilize GLUT1 mRNA and influence OS progression.

Downregulation of TAF15 or GLUT1 reversed HULC-mediated promotion of OS cell progression

To explore the role of the HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 axis in OS cell progression, U2OS and HOS cells were co-transfected with oe-HULC and shRNAs targeting TAF15 or GLUT1 (sh-TAF15 or sh-GLUT1). Functional assays demonstrated that the downregulation of TAF15 or GLUT1 reversed the oe-HULC-induced enhancement of cell viability, migration, and invasion (Fig. 7A-B). IF staining revealed that HULC overexpression suppressed E-cadherin expression and promoted N-cadherin expression, and these effects could be abolished by silencing TAF15 or GLUT1 (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

Currently, OS treatment primarily involves surgical resection combined with adjuvant chemotherapy [32]. However, the prognosis for patients with recurrent or primary OS remains poor, with a long-term survival rate of less than 30%, largely due to the aggressive nature of the disease and the emergence of resistance to chemotherapy [33, 34]. The Warburg effect participates in regulating OS cell proliferation and invasion [35, 36], and studies have confirmed that targeting aerobic glycolysis can affect tumor growth [37]. Therefore, elucidation of the regulatory mechanism of HULC in OS glycolysis is necessary for developing the targeted therapeutic regimens. Our study unveils a novel oncogenic axis wherein METTL3-mediated m6A modification stabilizes lncRNA HULC, which in turn recruits the RNA-binding protein TAF15 to stabilize GLUT1 mRNA, thereby accelerating glycolysis and malignant progression in OS. This METTL3/HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 cascade provides a multi-layered regulatory framework connecting epitranscriptomic modification, non-coding RNA function, and metabolic reprogramming in OS pathogenesis.

LncRNA HULC has been shown to be aberrantly upregulated in OS tissues [38]. Previous studies have reported that elevated HULC expression correlates with clinical stage, poor overall survival, and increased metastasis in OS patients [12]. In particular, Li et al. revealed that HULC overexpression promotes OS cell proliferation and metastasis [39]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that high HULC expression is associated with poor prognosis in OS patients. Functional analyses indicated that HULC overexpression enhanced OS cell viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis. Furthermore, in vivo experiments confirmed that HULC upregulation accelerated OS tumor growth and facilitated lung metastasis. However, previous investigations into HULC’s mechanism in OS have primarily focused on its function as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) or its direct interaction with glycolytic enzymes. Our work significantly extends this understanding by identifying a distinct, RBP-dependent mechanism. Our study demonstrates that HULC predominantly localizes in the cytoplasm and exerts its pro-glycolytic effect not merely through enzyme binding but by acting as a molecular scaffold that recruits TAF15 to stabilize GLUT1 mRNA. This reveals a layer of post-transcriptional regulation for a key metabolic transporter, offering a fresh perspective on how lncRNAs fine-tune the Warburg effect beyond direct metabolic enzyme modulation.

The regulation of lncRNA stability by m6A modification is an emerging theme in cancer biology. METTL3 expression was elevated in OS tissues, where it increased LEF1 mRNA levels through m6A modification, contributing to OS cell proliferation and metastasis [40]. Additionally, Zhou et al. found that METTL3-mediated m6A modification of the lncRNA DANCR contributed to OS progression [41]. While these studies established the importance of METTL3 in OS, the upstream regulation of oncogenic lncRNAs themselves by m6A in this context was less explored. Our study fills this gap by establishing HULC as a direct and functional downstream target of METTL3 in OS. Interestingly, the role of m6A in regulating RNA fate is context-dependent, often involving specific “reader” proteins that can lead to either stabilization or degradation [42]. Our observation that METTL3-mediated m6A modification stabilizes HULC aligns with similar stabilizing effects observed for other transcripts in cancers [43], but contrasts with degradation-promoting roles elsewhere. Our study showed that METTL3 was overexpressed in OS tissues and could stabilize HULC RNA through m6A modification. Furthermore, METTL3 downregulation abolished the pro-tumor effects of HULC upregulation on OS cell viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis in vitro, as well as tumorigenesis and lung metastasis in vivo. These findings underscore the role of METTL3-mediated HULC stabilization in promoting OS progression. Our data demonstrate that METTL3-mediated m6A modification stabilizes HULC RNA, as evidenced by reduced HULC abundance upon METTL3 knockdown. Using SRAMP prediction and MeRIP validation with a mutant transcript (HULC-MUT), we identify a functional m6A modification site critical for this stabilization. While this site is essential, HULC may harbor additional m6A sites contributing to its regulation.The stabilization mechanism likely involves m6A “reader” proteins that protect HULC from degradation. In many cancers, IGF2BP family members recognize m6A-modified transcripts and enhance their stability. We hypothesize that a similar reader protein (e.g., IGF2BP1/2/3) binds to m6A-modified HULC in OS, although this requires future experimental validation.

TAF15, a known proto-oncogene, can stabilize target mRNAs and has been implicated in the progression of various tumor types [44]. For instance, TAF15 has been shown to be upregulated in gastric cancer, and its downregulation inhibited cell proliferation and metastasis [45]. Additionally, lncRNA SNHG4 was found to enhance RNF14 mRNA stability by recruiting TAF15, thereby accelerating colorectal cancer progression [46]. Similarly, Pan et al. reported that lncRNA TRPM2-AS facilitated colorectal cancer cell proliferation by enhancing TAF15-mediated TRPM2 mRNA stability [47]. These findings collectively suggest that lncRNAs often interact with TAF15 to stabilize their target mRNAs, contributing to tumor progression. Our findings reveal that HULC employs an analogous strategy in OS, thereby integrating HULC into this established mechanistic paradigm. This is the first report of the interaction between HULC and TAF15 and to implicate TAF15 in OS progression. More importantly, we identify GLUT1 as the critical downstream mRNA stabilized by the HULC-TAF15 complex.

Glucose is the primary energy substrate for tumor cells, and the GLUT1-mediated regulatory mechanism plays a vital role in tumor development [48]. GLUT1 has been shown to be upregulated in OS tissues, where its elevated expression enhances glycolysis, thereby driving OS cell proliferation [30]. While prior research emphasized transcriptional control or protein activity of GLUT1, our study unveils a novel post-transcriptional regulatory axis governing its expression. The positive correlation between HULC and GLUT1 in clinical samples further supports the pathophysiological relevance of this axis. Further analysis revealed that HULC interacts with TAF15 to stabilize GLUT1 mRNA, and functional experiments confirmed that downregulation of TAF15 or GLUT1 reversed the pro-tumor effects of HULC overexpression, including enhanced OS cell viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis. This underscores the functional indispensability of the entire axis and positions TAF15 and GLUT1 as viable therapeutic targets downstream of HULC.

Our study not only delineates a novel oncogenic axis in OS but also unveils its considerable translational potential. Firstly, the METTL3/HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 cascade presents multiple layers for therapeutic intervention. Small-molecule inhibitors targeting METTL3 catalytic activity are under active development for other cancers and could be repurposed for OS. Similarly, disrupting the HULC-TAF15 interaction with oligonucleotide-based strategies (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs against HULC) or targeting the GLUT1 transporter with specific inhibitors (e.g., BAY-876 or WZB117) may offer novel avenues to cripple OS glycolysis and progression. Secondly, the components of this axis hold promise as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers. The correlation of high HULC, METTL3, and GLUT1 expression with poor patient survival suggests their potential utility in risk stratification. Compared to established OS biomarkers like alkaline phosphatase (ALP) or lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which are tissue-nonspecific, the HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 axis offers a more disease-specific molecular signature linked directly to the pathogenic metabolic shift. Furthermore, while existing metabolic targets in OS research often focus on enzymes like PKM2 or LDHA, our work highlights the upstream regulatory node (METTL3-mediated RNA methylation) and a critical glucose transporter (GLUT1) as equally viable targets. Future studies should validate these components in larger, independent cohorts and explore the efficacy of combinatorial targeting of this axis with conventional chemotherapy to overcome drug resistance.

Despite the compelling evidence, certain limitations of our study warrant consideration and point to future directions. Our study primarily focused on elucidating the role of TAF15 within the HULC-driven regulatory axis. While our functional rescue experiments clearly show that TAF15 knockdown reverses the effects of HULC overexpression, indicating its indispensability in this pathway, we did not systematically characterize the phenotype of TAF15 knockdown alone. Future studies will comprehensively profile the tumor-suppressive effects of TAF15 knockdown in OS models. At present, the clinical cohort supporting the prognostic relevance of HULC in this study is relatively small (n = 30). Expanding the clinical analysis to a larger, independent cohort to evaluate the combined prognostic power of METTL3, HULC, TAF15, and GLUT1 as a multi-gene signature would strengthen the translational potential of our findings. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether HULC binds directly or indirectly to TAF15, and the precise binding site for HULC on TAF15 remains to be mapped. Whether HULC binding induces a conformational change in TAF15 that enhances its affinity for GLUT1 mRNA is an intriguing hypothesis, but elucidating such allosteric regulation would require structural studies beyond our current RIP and RNA pull-down data. Concerning the stabilization mechanism, we propose that the HULC-TAF15 complex acts as a protective scaffold on GLUT1 mRNA. By binding simultaneously to TAF15 and potentially to sequences or structures near the TAF15-binding site on GLUT1 mRNA, HULC could enhance the recruitment or retention of TAF15, thereby increasing the local concentration of this stabilizing factor on GLUT1 transcripts and protecting them from decay machineries; however, direct evidence for this model is currently lacking. It is also entirely plausible that the HULC-TAF15 complex co-regulates a network of mRNAs involved in OS progression. Future transcriptome-wide studies (e.g., TAF15 RIP-seq with HULC perturbation) are warranted to identify the full repertoire of mRNAs co-regulated by this complex, which may extend beyond glycolysis to other cancer hallmarks such as cell cycle control or survival signaling. In addition, rescue experiments involving overexpression of TAF15 or GLUT1 in the context of METTL3 or HULC deletion are timely and warranted, which will help us to further refine and solidify the proposed mechanistic chain.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the METTL3/HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 axis is a critical driver of OS progression via reprogramming glycolysis. Our results showed that METTL3-mediated m6A modification of lncRNA HULC promotes OS progression by enhancing cell viability, metastasis, EMT, and glycolysis through the stabilization of GLUT1 mRNA, facilitated by the interaction between HULC and TAF15. The identification of the METTL3/HULC/TAF15/GLUT1 axis provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OS and highlights a potential target for the development of novel molecular therapies with significant clinical implications.
