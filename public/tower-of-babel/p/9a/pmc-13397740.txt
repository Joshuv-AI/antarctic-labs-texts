A grounded theory study of interpretive experiences in cyberostracism among university students
Authors: Jianning Lyu, Ling Zhang, Ziyi Zhang, Mingsheng Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the widespread use of social media, social exclusion in digital contexts (e.g., “being left on read” or being ignored in group chats) has become increasingly prevalent among university students. Previous research has primarily focused on its negative psychological consequences, while the process through which individuals interpret and construct the meaning of exclusion experiences remains underexplored. This study aimed to develop an interpretive framework of how university students experienced and negotiated ambiguous digital nonresponse within cyberostracism contexts using a grounded theory approach.MethodsA Straussian grounded theory approach was adopted. Using purposive sampling, theoretical sampling, and snowball sampling, 22 university students (aged 18–24) with experiences of cyberostracism were recruited for semi-structured interviews. Data were analyzed through open, axial, and selective coding using constant comparative analysis until theoretical saturation was achieved.ResultsA five-stage interpretive model was developed, including (1) digital ostracism situations, (2) relational uncertainty and interpretive negotiation, (3) emotional responses, (4) behavioral adjustment, and (5) narrated changes in self-perception. Uncertainty emerged as a central interpretive experience through which participants made sense of ambiguous digital nonresponse. Participants’ interpretations were shaped by relational closeness, communication expectations, prior interaction history, and platform norms.ConclusionsThis study provides a process-oriented interpretive account of how university students experience and negotiate cyberostracism in digitally mediated contexts. Rather than proposing a confirmed causal mechanism, the findings contextualize existing ostracism theories by showing how ambiguous digital nonresponse is interpreted within relational and communication contexts.Keywords: Cyberostracism, Ambiguous digital nonresponse, Relational uncertainty, Digital communication, Grounded theory

Background

In the digital age, patterns of social interaction are undergoing profound transformations. With the widespread use of social media and instant messaging platforms, individuals increasingly establish and maintain social relationships through digital channels. In this context, both the feedback process of social interaction and the ways in which relationships are maintained have changed substantially [1]. At the same time, social exclusion, a traditional interpersonal phenomenon, has gradually shifted into digital environments and manifests in new forms, such as being “left on read,” experiencing a lack of interaction, or being ignored online [2]. This phenomenon has been conceptualized as cyberostracism, referring to individuals’ experiences of being ignored or excluded in online contexts [3, 4].

Previous studies have demonstrated that cyberostracism exerts significant effects on individuals’ emotional states and basic psychological needs, including belongingness, self-esteem, sense of control, and meaningful existence [5–7]. Cyberostracism refers not merely to delayed or absent digital responses, but to situations in which individuals subjectively perceive online nonresponse or ambiguous digital nonresponse as signals of social exclusion, relational distancing, or interpersonal disregard. Accordingly, the present study focuses not on objective response delay itself, but on participants’ subjective interpretation of ambiguous digital nonresponse as potentially conveying exclusion-related relational meaning. Compared with face-to-face interactions, cyberostracism is often characterized by greater ambiguity and uncertainty, as individuals lack access to nonverbal cues to infer others’ intentions, thereby increasing cognitive load and intensifying negative experiences [8]. Recent research has further suggested that post-pandemic digital communication environments are increasingly characterized by communication overload, digital exhaustion, and delayed responsiveness, which may be related to ambiguous online nonresponse in everyday interaction contexts [9]. In some cases, digitally ambiguous nonresponse may not necessarily reflect intentional interpersonal rejection, but rather communication fatigue, attentional fragmentation, or reduced responsiveness within digitally saturated environments. Previous research has also suggested that individuals differ in their sensitivity to social exclusion cues. Social Monitoring System (SMS) theory proposes that when belongingness needs are threatened, individuals become more attentive to subtle interpersonal signals and relational information [10, 11]. In digitally mediated interactions, where communication cues are often incomplete or ambiguous, this heightened social monitoring may increase individuals’ sensitivity to delayed responses, message nonresponse, and interactional uncertainty. Accordingly, cyberostracism is not only a byproduct of changing social interaction formats but also represents a critical challenge for psychological adaptation in contemporary society.

However, existing research has predominantly focused on the outcomes of cyberostracism, such as its effects on emotion, behavior, and mental health, while paying limited attention to the processes through which individuals interpret and make sense of exclusion experiences [12]. Prior work suggests that individuals’ subjective interpretations of exclusion play a crucial role in shaping their subsequent emotional and behavioral adjustment strategies [13]. This indicates that exclusion experiences were described not only in relation to situational factors, but also through participants’ ongoing interpretive appraisal and relational interpretation.

From a theoretical perspective, the need-to-belong theory [6] and the temporal need-threat model [14] provide important frameworks for understanding social exclusion. These theories emphasize that exclusion threatens fundamental psychological needs and elicits a range of emotional and behavioral adjustment strategies. However, these models primarily explain exclusion from outcome- or stage-based perspectives and offer limited insight into how individuals interpret exclusion experiences within specific contexts [15]. Furthermore, the high level of ambiguity inherent in digital interactions, along with the interplay between online and offline contexts, poses additional challenges to the explanatory power of traditional theories in digital environments [8].

Against this background, the present study adopts a qualitative perspective and employs a Straussian grounded theory approach to explore university students’ subjective experiences of cyberostracism and the processes through which these experiences are interpreted and constructed [16]. Specifically, this study aims to explore how university students interpret ambiguous digital nonresponse and negotiate the relational meaning of perceived exclusion within digitally mediated interactions. The study focuses on the interpretive processes through which participants attempted to understand the relational significance of ambiguous digital interactions and perceived nonresponse. By developing an interpretive framework grounded in participants’ experiences, this study seeks to complement existing theories of social exclusion by providing a contextualized understanding of cyberostracism in digitally mediated social environments.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design based on a Straussian grounded theory approach to explore the meaning-making process in university students experiencing cyberostracism in digital contexts [17, 18]. Grounded theory emphasizes the inductive development of theoretical models from empirical data and is particularly suitable for examining complex interpretive experiences and their underlying processes [16]. Following the Straussian analytical framework, data were analyzed through open coding, axial coding, and selective coding, through which categories were systematically identified, linked, and integrated into a theoretical model [19]. The study followed a Straussian grounded theory approach, emphasizing systematic coding procedures and category development grounded in data rather than interpretive co-construction.

Participants and sampling

Particular attention was also paid to variations in relational context, including differences in relationship type, relationship closeness, interaction frequency, and perceived communication expectations across interpersonal relationships. These relational contexts included interactions involving romantic partners, close friends, classmates, and casual acquaintances, which were considered important sources of interpretive variation during the analytical process.

The present study does not assume that all ambiguous digital nonresponse reflects actual interpersonal rejection. Rather, the analysis focuses on how participants subjectively interpreted and negotiated the relational meaning of such experiences within specific interpersonal and situational contexts.

The sample size was determined based on the principle of theoretical saturation. A total of 22 university students (coded as P01–P22) were included in the study, comprising 11 males and 11 females, with a mean age of 20.6 years. Participants were drawn from diverse academic backgrounds, including humanities, science and engineering, and medical disciplines.

Theoretical saturation was considered to have been reached when no new conceptual properties or inter-category relationships emerged from the data. Among the 22 participants, data from the first 18 participants primarily contributed to the development of the emerging interpretive model, while interviews conducted during the later stage of data collection were used to further refine emerging categories and examine whether additional conceptual variation would emerge. Compared with earlier participants, later participants were intentionally selected to provide greater variation in relationship type, communication expectations, perceived intentionality of nonresponse, and interpretations of ambiguous digital interaction cues, thereby enabling refinement of emerging interpretive categories. Interviews with the final four participants largely reinforced the existing interpretive framework, and no substantially new categories or theoretical relationships were identified. As data collection and analysis progressed, theoretical sampling strategies were gradually incorporated to refine emerging categories and explore conceptual variation. Later participants were recruited based on emerging analytical categories identified during the ongoing coding process. In particular, subsequent recruitment focused on increasing conceptual variation in participants’ experiences of relational closeness, perceived intentionality of nonresponse, communication expectations, and interpretations of ambiguous digital interaction cues. For example, later participants included individuals describing experiences involving close friends, romantic partners, classmates, and relatively low-obligation interpersonal relationships, in order to further explore how relational context shaped the interpretation of digitally ambiguous nonresponse. Data collection continued until no substantially new categories or interpretive relationships emerged across subsequent interviews, suggesting that theoretical saturation had been adequately achieved.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. An initial interview guide was developed based on the research questions and relevant literature, then reviewed by one to two peer researchers and a supervisor, and subsequently refined through pilot interviews. The final interview guide focused on key experiences of cyberostracism, including specific incidents in which participants felt ignored or excluded in social media or instant messaging contexts, their attributional reflection and emotional responses, coping behaviors, and the perceived impact of these experiences on their self-related perceptions. During the coding process, the research team engaged in ongoing analytic discussions to reduce potential researcher bias and to ensure that category development remained grounded in the data.

All interviews were conducted in relatively quiet environments and lasted approximately 30–60 min. With informed consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. To enhance the richness and accuracy of the data, probing and clarification strategies were employed during the interviews to encourage participants to elaborate on key experiences and provide detailed accounts of their subjective perceptions. In total, approximately 182,000 words of interview transcripts were obtained.

Data analysis was conducted using Nvivo 11 and followed a Straussian grounded theory approach. Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently, and a constant comparative method was used to iteratively compare and refine emerging concepts and categories [16, 19]. Specifically, during open coding, interview transcripts were analyzed line by line to identify initial concepts. During axial coding, these concepts were integrated to establish relationships between categories and to develop a structured category system. During selective coding, all categories were systematically integrated around a central interpretive category to construct a coherent theoretical model. As analysis progressed, a relatively stable category structure had been established after coding the first 18 participants. The subsequent four interviews did not yield new conceptual properties or inter-category relationships, indicating that theoretical saturation had been achieved. Therefore, the final sample size was 22 participants.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to established ethical guidelines for psychological research. Prior to data collection, all participants were fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, as well as their rights, and provided written informed consent. Participants were explicitly informed that their participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty, and that their decision would not result in any negative consequences.

Given that the interview topics involved potentially negative interpersonal experiences (e.g., being ignored or excluded), the researchers maintained a sensitive and empathetic stance during interviews, avoided leading questions, and provided emotional support when necessary to minimize potential psychological discomfort. After the interviews, participants were provided with information on emotional support resources where appropriate.

All data were anonymized during processing. Participants’ names and identifiable information were replaced with codes to ensure confidentiality. Audio recordings and transcripts were used solely for research purposes, securely stored by the research team, and not disclosed to third parties. Data were stored or disposed of in accordance with relevant data protection regulations. The study reporting followed established qualitative research reporting standards [20].

Trustworthiness of the findings

The trustworthiness of the findings was enhanced through multiple qualitative strategies, including participant feedback, constant comparative analysis, reflexive discussion, consensus-based coding discussion, and ongoing analytic review [21]. The study also drew on Maxwell’s qualitative validity framework [22], particularly regarding descriptive validity and interpretive validity, as well as established qualitative criteria for credibility and reflexivity.

After the preliminary analysis was completed, a subset of participants (n = 10) was invited to review summaries of the emerging categories and interpretive findings. Participants were encouraged to provide open-ended feedback on whether the findings adequately reflected their experiences and interpretations of cyberostracism in digitally mediated interaction contexts. Most participants considered the analytical interpretations to be generally consistent with their experiences. Several participants further elaborated on the emotional ambiguity and relational uncertainty involved in digital exclusion situations, particularly regarding relationship closeness, communication expectations, and offline relational contexts. These reflections contributed to the refinement of category descriptions and interpretive relationships within the developing model.

Throughout the analytical process, constant comparative analysis was used to compare interview data, initial concepts, emerging categories, and theoretical relationships across participants and coding stages. Analytic memos were written to document evolving interpretations, category refinements, and reflexive considerations.

To enhance analytic rigor, several transcripts and preliminary coding structures were independently reviewed by multiple researchers. When discrepancies in coding or category interpretation arose, the researchers revisited the original interview materials and resolved differences through consensus-based discussion. This process was used to refine category boundaries, strengthen interpretive coherence, and ensure that the emerging grounded theory model remained closely grounded in participants’ narratives [21]. Reflexive discussions were conducted throughout the study to examine how the researchers’ disciplinary backgrounds, assumptions, and interpretive perspectives might influence data interpretation and category development [23].

Results

Participant characteristics and data overview

A total of 22 university students (aged 18–24) were included in the study. All participants reported having experienced various forms of cyberostracism in digital contexts (e.g., being “left on read” or ignored in group chats). The dataset comprised approximately 182,000 words of interview transcripts, which were analyzed using a constant comparative method. Theoretical saturation was considered to have been reached when no new conceptual categories emerged from additional data. The characteristics of the sample are consistent with prior research identifying university students as a population with high exposure to cyberostracism in digital social environments [5, 8].

Data analysis and coding process

The core of grounded theory lies in the systematic development of theoretical constructs through coding procedures applied to empirical data. Following a Straussian grounded theory approach, the interview data were analyzed through three sequential stages: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding.

Throughout the analysis, a constant comparative method was employed to iteratively compare data across participants. In combination with theoretical sampling, this process enabled the continuous refinement of categories and their interrelationships. The analysis proceeded until the category structure stabilized and no new conceptual properties emerged, indicating that theoretical saturation had been achieved. Ultimately, this process facilitated the inductive development of a theoretical model grounded in participants’ lived experiences.

Open coding

During the open coding stage, interview transcripts were analyzed line by line, and meaning units relevant to the research questions were conceptualized and assigned initial codes. Through a systematic examination of the entire dataset, a total of 86 initial concepts were identified and further grouped into 18 preliminary categories. The coding may suggest that university students’ experiences of digital exclusion can be broadly organized into several domains. First, participants demonstrated recognition of exclusion situations, such as being “left on read” or experiencing a lack of interaction. Second, their experiences involved attributional reflection, including rumination, self-attribution, and other-attribution. Third, participants reported distinct emotional experiences, such as anxiety, shame, and perceived neglect. Fourth, they described various behavioral adjustment strategies, including attempts to increase interaction or reduce contact. Finally, participants reported long-term psychological impacts, such as decreased self-evaluation and heightened social sensitivity. Axial coding

During the axial coding stage, the concepts generated in open coding were systematically integrated and reorganized to establish relationships among categories. Through constant comparative analysis, the findings suggest that experiences of digital exclusion were described by participants as ongoing interpretive experiences involving relational uncertainty, emotional discomfort, and changing interpersonal responses. Specifically, five core categories were identified: (1) digital exclusion situations; (2) relational uncertainty and interpretive appraisal; (3) emotional responses; (4) behavioral adjustment strategies; and (5) narrated changes in self-perception. Overall, participants’ narratives suggested a frequently described interpretive pattern in which ambiguous digital nonresponse was associated with relational uncertainty, attributional reflection, emotional discomfort, behavioral adjustment, and ongoing negotiation of self-worth under relational uncertainty. Within this interpretive pattern, uncertainty was frequently described as an important condition shaping how participants interpreted relational meaning and responded to ambiguous digital interactions.

During axial coding, several initially separate concepts, including self-doubt, repeated message checking, and relational rumination, were progressively reorganized into broader interpretive categories related to relational uncertainty and interpretive appraisal.

Selective coding

During the selective coding phase, the categories identified in axial coding were systematically integrated to clarify the relationships among them and to further identify a central category. Through constant comparative analysis and theoretical sampling, the study identified a central interpretive category concerning narrated changes in self-perception under conditions of relational uncertainty. Participants frequently described ongoing efforts to understand their relational significance and interpersonal value when encountering ambiguous digital nonresponse, suggesting that cyberostracism experiences involved active interpretive negotiation rather than passive reactions to exclusion situations. Instead, participants described ongoing interpretive and behavioral efforts to manage relational uncertainty and perceived changes in self-worth. Within this process, uncertainty functions both as an immediate consequence of exclusion situations and as a key process that appeared related to subsequent psychological responses.

Based on this central interpretive category, the present study developed an interpretive model of cyberostracism experiences in digitally mediated contexts. Rather than implying a fixed causal or temporal progression, the model illustrates a frequently described interpretive pattern in which ambiguous digital nonresponse was associated with relational uncertainty, attributional reflection, emotional discomfort, behavioral adjustment strategies, and ongoing negotiation of self-worth under relational uncertainty. Specifically, digital exclusion situations (e.g., being “left on read” or experiencing a lack of interaction) serve as the initial interpretive condition, eliciting uncertainty regarding the state of the relationship. Individuals then engage in dynamic interpretive appraisal processes, oscillating between self-attribution and other-attribution, which were often accompanied by emotional responses such as anxiety, shame, and perceived rejection. These emotional responses, in turn, motivate individuals to adopt behavioral strategies—either approach-oriented or avoidance-oriented—to regulate the relational context. Over time, participants described these ongoing interpretive experiences as being associated with narrated changes in self-perception, particularly fluctuations in perceived self-worth and heightened sensitivity to subsequent exclusion-related cues.

Importantly, participants’ narratives suggested that cyberostracism experiences were not interpreted as simple linear events, but rather as ongoing and recursive interpretive experiences. Some participants described that prior experiences of ambiguous digital exclusion appeared to influence how they later interpreted subsequent interpersonal uncertainty, sometimes increasing sensitivity to potentially exclusion-related cues and negative attributional interpretations. On the other hand, offline relationship quality was described by participants as a contextual influence within this process: stable offline relationships can buffer the negative impact of uncertainty, whereas unstable relationships may amplify exclusion experiences.

Compared with prior research, the present study contributes to the literature by providing a process-oriented interpretive perspective on cyberostracism in digital contexts. The findings suggest that uncertainty, relational interpretation, emotional responses, and behavioral adjustment strategies were closely interconnected within participants’ narratives of ambiguous digital nonresponse. Discussion

Main findings

The present study employed a grounded theory approach to develop an interpretive framework of how university students experienced and negotiated perceived social exclusion in digitally mediated interaction contexts. The findings suggest that participants experienced cyberostracism as an ongoing and context-dependent experience involving relational uncertainty, interpretive appraisal, emotional discomfort, behavioral adjustment strategies, and changes in self-perception.

Participants frequently described digitally ambiguous nonresponse, such as being “left on read,” as generating uncertainty regarding relational meaning and interpersonal standing. These experiences were often associated with ongoing interpretive efforts involving self-attribution, other-attribution, emotional discomfort, and interpersonal coping responses, suggesting that attributional interpretation played an important role in how ambiguous digital nonresponse was cognitively appraised. Rather than emerging as direct and uniform consequences of exclusion experiences, emotional and behavioral adjustment strategies appeared to vary according to participants’ interpretations of relational ambiguity and perceived interpersonal significance.

Over time, repeated experiences of ambiguous digital exclusion were associated with n arrated changes in self-perception and heightened sensitivity to subsequent exclusion cues. In the present study, these self-related changes primarily referred to fluctuations in perceived self-worth, relational significance, and interpersonal self-evaluation described by participants in digitally mediated interactions. Some participants described becoming increasingly attentive to ambiguous interactional signals following repeated experiences of perceived exclusion, suggesting a potentially recursive interpretive pattern within ongoing interpersonal interactions.

Furthermore, the findings highlight three notable characteristics of cyberostracism experiences in digital contexts. First, participants’ interpretations of digital nonresponse appeared to vary across different relational contexts and levels of interpersonal closeness. Second, the findings suggest that participants described cyberostracism as an ongoing meaning-negotiation experience rather than a fixed sequence of psychological reactions. Third, online exclusion experiences were frequently understood in relation to broader offline relational experiences, indicating that digitally mediated exclusion was continuously interpreted within ongoing interpersonal contexts.

Interpreting cyberostracism in ambiguous digital contexts

Rather than proposing a fundamentally new model of social exclusion, the present study provides a process-oriented interpretive perspective with a particular focus on how ambiguous digital nonresponse was subjectively interpreted and negotiated by university students within cyberostracism experiences in contemporary online interaction contexts. Classic theories, such as belongingness theory [6], Williams’s temporal need–threat model [14], and Smart Richman and Leary’s multimotive model [12], have provided important explanations for exclusion-related psychological responses. The present findings overlap in several respects with the reflective stage described in Williams’s temporal need–threat model, particularly regarding individuals’ efforts to interpret exclusion experiences, regulate emotional responses, and restore relational belonging following perceived exclusion. However, this study focuses more specifically on how these interpretive experiences unfold within ambiguous digital interaction contexts, where individuals often have difficulty determining whether nonresponse reflects rejection, relational distancing, situational busyness, or ordinary communication habits [8, 10].

The findings suggest that uncertainty played an important role in how participants interpreted ambiguous digital nonresponse. In the present study, uncertainty primarily refers to participants’ immediate relational uncertainty when attempting to infer the relational meaning and interpersonal intention underlying ambiguous digital nonresponse. Rather than conceptualizing uncertainty as a stable psychological construct, the present study treats uncertainty primarily as an interpretive condition emerging from ambiguous digital interaction cues.This finding is broadly consistent with previous cyberostracism research, which has identified ambiguity and uncertainty as defining characteristics of digitally mediated exclusion experiences [8]. Rather than identifying uncertainty as a novel psychological mechanism, the present study provides a more detailed interpretive account of how participants experienced, interpreted, and negotiated relational uncertainty within ambiguous digital interaction contexts. When encountering unclear social cues, such as being “left on read,” participants often described attributional reflection, relational interpretation, and ongoing efforts to understand the interpersonal meaning of ambiguous digital interactions. These interpretive processes were frequently associated with emotional discomfort and changes in interpersonal coping strategies.

The findings also suggest that repeated experiences of ambiguous digital exclusion were often associated with participants’ ongoing negotiation of self-worth and relational value. Narrated changes in self-perception were described not merely as direct outcomes of exclusion experiences, but as part of a continuing interpretive process through which participants attempted to understand their relational standing in digitally mediated interactions. Some participants further described becoming increasingly sensitive to subsequent exclusion cues following repeated experiences of ambiguous nonresponse, suggesting a potentially recursive interpretive pattern within ongoing interpersonal interactions. Prior research has also suggested that perceived abandonment and heightened uncertainty may intensify emotional distress, interpersonal vigilance, and defensive reactions following exclusion-related experiences. Although these findings were primarily observed in intergroup contexts, they further support the possibility that uncertainty-related interpretations may be related to heightened sensitivity and defensive responding in digitally mediated exclusion experiences [24]. This recursive interpretive tendency is broadly consistent with prior evidence that online exclusion may heighten social monitoring and sensitivity to subsequent interpersonal cues [25].

Importantly, the present findings should be understood as a process-oriented interpretive account derived from participants’ retrospective narratives rather than as evidence of confirmed psychological mechanisms or fixed temporal pathways. Taken together, the present study provides a more contextualized interpretive understanding of cyberostracism within ambiguity-rich digital communication environments. Rather than proposing a fundamentally distinct exclusion model, the findings illustrate how participants described and negotiated experiences of relational ambiguity, interpretive uncertainty, and ongoing efforts to understand relational meaning within digitally mediated exclusion experiences. These interpretive experiences appeared to vary across relational contexts, communication expectations, and individual sensitivities to interpersonal uncertainty.

Distinct relational experiences in digital contexts

Participants described several characteristics of digitally mediated exclusion that appeared distinct from their offline interpersonal experiences, particularly in terms of ambiguity and persistence. Due to the absence of immediate feedback and nonverbal cues in digital interactions, individuals often find it difficult to accurately infer others’ intentions, which participants frequently described as increasing feelings of uncertainty, rumination, and negative attribution. These results not only support prior findings that cyberostracism is associated with increased cognitive load [4], but also extend this line of research by clarifying its underlying interpretive experiences: situational ambiguity participants described heightened feelings of uncertainty, which in turn intensifies interpretive appraisal and emotional responses [26]. In addition, the traceability of digital interactions enables individuals to repeatedly revisit exclusion cues, which participants often described as contributing to prolonged emotional discomfort and repeated reflection on exclusion-related experiences. This is consistent with prior evidence suggesting that digital exclusion heightens social monitoring and attentional allocation [27].

Importantly, participants’ interpretations of digital nonresponse appeared to vary substantially across relational contexts. Being ignored by a romantic partner, close friend, classmate, or casual contact often carried different relational meanings and emotional implications. Participants frequently described that the perceived significance of nonresponse depended not only on the absence of reply itself, but also on prior relationship closeness, communication expectations, interaction frequency, and perceived relational obligations [28]. These findings may also be understood in relation to attribution theory and research on rejection sensitivity. Participants often differed in how they explained ambiguous digital nonresponse, with some tending toward self-attribution (e.g., interpreting nonresponse as reflecting personal inadequacy or relational devaluation), while others emphasized situational explanations, such as busyness, communication habits, or platform-related interaction norms. Prior research has suggested that individuals high in rejection sensitivity may be more likely to interpret ambiguous interpersonal cues as signals of rejection or exclusion, particularly under conditions of relational uncertainty [29]. In digitally mediated interactions characterized by reduced social cues and delayed responsiveness, such individual differences may further appear related to how ambiguous nonresponse is cognitively appraised and emotionally experienced. Recent intergroup research has similarly suggested that individuals’ responses to inclusion and exclusion cues may vary depending on identity-related motivations and belongingness needs. Although these studies were conducted in intergroup contexts, they further support the idea that exclusion-related experiences are not interpreted uniformly, but are filtered through individuals’ relational concerns, identity relevance, and need for belonging [30].

In addition, participants sometimes considered alternative explanations for ambiguous nonresponse, including academic workload, platform-related communication habits, delayed message checking, emotional avoidance, or differences in online interaction norms.This relational uncertainty may also be related to characteristics of digitally mediated interaction itself. Previous research on the online disinhibition effect has suggested that reduced social accountability, asynchronous communication, and diminished nonverbal cues in online environments may increase individuals’ likelihood of delayed responsiveness or interactional disengagement [24]. Participants’ awareness of these online communication characteristics sometimes appeared to be related to how they interpreted ambiguous digital nonresponse, particularly when attempting to distinguish between intentional rejection and situational or platform-related communication patterns. These findings suggest that cyberostracism experiences were not interpreted uniformly, but were continuously negotiated within broader interpersonal and situational contexts.Not all participants interpreted ambiguous digital nonresponse as exclusion-related. Some participants described delayed responses as reflecting ordinary communication habits, academic busyness, or low relational obligation, particularly in relationships characterized by weaker emotional expectations or infrequent interaction. Some participants also described experiences of communication fatigue and digitally overloaded interaction environments, particularly in the context of frequent online communication following the pandemic period [9]. Taken together, these findings suggest that individuals may differ substantially in how ambiguous exclusion cues are interpreted, depending on relational expectations, belongingness concerns, identity relevance, and broader interpersonal motivations.

Interpretive patterns of behavioral adjustment strategies

At the behavioral level, individuals primarily adopt approach-oriented and avoidance-oriented strategies when confronted with digital exclusion, consistent with the approach–avoidance framework [13]. Importantly, the present study further demonstrates that these strategies are not fixed but were dynamically related to interpretive appraisal processes. Specifically, when individuals engage in self-attribution, they are more likely to attempt to repair relationships through increased interaction. In contrast, when they attribute exclusion to others, they are more likely to adopt avoidance strategies to minimize further psychological harm. These findings suggest that behavioral adjustment strategies were closely intertwined with participants’ ongoing interpretive appraisal and attempts to manage relational uncertainty. In addition, exclusion experiences were described as shaping cognitive resource allocation, such as attentional shifts [19], further supporting the adjustment nature of behavioral adjustment strategies.

An integrated online-offline perspective

The present study further demonstrates that the effects of digital exclusion do not occur in isolation but were related to offline relationship quality as a contextual condition. Stable offline relationships may provide interpretive frameworks that buffer the negative impact of digital exclusion, whereas unstable relationships may increase the likelihood that ambiguous cues are interpreted as rejection, thereby amplifying psychological distress. Participants also described important differences in how digitally ambiguous nonresponse was interpreted across different relational contexts. For example, being “left on read” by a romantic partner or close friend was often experienced as more emotionally salient and relationally threatening than similar nonresponse from casual acquaintances or weak social ties. Participants frequently interpreted ambiguous nonresponse in relation to relationship closeness, communication expectations, relational history, and perceived interpersonal importance. These findings suggest that cyberostracism experiences were highly context-dependent and embedded within ongoing relational dynamics rather than experienced as uniform interpersonal events. This finding extends prior research by moving beyond the traditional separation of online and offline contexts, highlighting that exclusion experiences should be understood as the result of cross-contextual integration. Notably, similar interpretive patterns of exclusion have also been identified in organizational settings [31], suggesting a degree of cross-contextual consistency in how exclusion experiences are processed.

Practical implications for psychological intervention

The findings of the present study may provide several tentative implications for psychological support and intervention among university students in digitally mediated interaction contexts. Because many participants described recurrent self-attribution, rumination, and uncertainty-related interpretation following ambiguous digital nonresponse, the findings may suggest the potential value of helping individuals reflect on maladaptive attribution tendencies and rigid interpretive patterns in online interpersonal interactions. Participants also described a range of behavioral adjustment strategies, including withdrawal, repeated checking behaviors, and interpersonal avoidance, which may indicate the potential importance of promoting more flexible and adaptive coping responses in digitally ambiguous interaction situations [25, 32].

Given that many participants described heightened distress in relationships involving strong emotional expectations, the findings may further highlight the potential importance of stable offline interpersonal support and relational belonging. In addition, because uncertainty and relational uncertainty were frequently described throughout participants’ narratives, the findings may also suggest the potential relevance of helping individuals develop greater tolerance for interpersonal ambiguity within digitally mediated communication contexts.

Importantly, these implications should be understood as tentative interpretive considerations derived from qualitative participant narratives rather than empirically validated psychological or educational intervention strategies.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample was limited to university students within a specific sociocultural context, which may restrict the transferability of the findings to other age groups and cultural environments. In addition, participants who chose to participate in the interviews may have been more likely to report salient or emotionally meaningful experiences of digitally ambiguous nonresponse, potentially contributing to self-selection bias.

Second, the present study relied on retrospective interview narratives, which may have been associated with recall bias and participants’ current emotional interpretations of past interpersonal experiences. Although efforts were made to encourage open-ended and reflective discussion, participants’ responses may also have been affected by social desirability or self-presentational concerns.

Third, the study primarily relied on qualitative interview data and did not incorporate triangulation with actual digital interaction records, behavioral communication data, or longitudinal observation of online interpersonal interactions. As a result, the findings should be understood as participants’ retrospective interpretive accounts rather than as direct evidence of objective interpersonal processes or confirmed psychological mechanisms.

In addition, although the study attempted to distinguish cyberostracism from ordinary online nonresponse by emphasizing participants’ subjective interpretations of exclusion-related meaning, the conceptual boundary between ambiguous digital nonresponse and perceived cyberostracism remains context-dependent and may vary across interpersonal situations, communication norms, and relational expectations.

Future research may further examine how digitally ambiguous nonresponse is interpreted across different cultural contexts, relational structures, and communication environments. Quantitative, longitudinal, or mixed-method approaches may also help further explore the interpretive variability and contextual complexity of cyberostracism experiences. In addition, individual difference variables, such as attachment style and rejection sensitivity, may also influence how individuals interpret and respond to digitally ambiguous interpersonal interactions.

Rather than proposing a fixed psychological mechanism or confirmed temporal pathway, the present study provides a process-oriented interpretive account of how cyberostracism experiences were subjectively understood and negotiated within ambiguity-rich digital environments. By emphasizing relational ambiguity, interpretive uncertainty, and online–offline relational contexts, the findings contribute to a more contextualized understanding of cyberostracism in contemporary digital communication settings.

Conclusion

Using a grounded theory approach, the present study explored how university students interpreted experiences of perceived social exclusion within digitally mediated interaction contexts. The findings suggest that participants experienced cyberostracism as an evolving interpersonal process involving relational uncertainty, emotional discomfort, interpretive appraisal, and behavioral adjustment strategies. Participants frequently described ambiguous digital nonresponse as difficult to interpret, particularly in relationships involving strong emotional expectations. Importantly, the present findings should be understood as a context-dependent interpretive account derived from participants’ retrospective narratives rather than as evidence of fixed psychological mechanisms or developmental consequences. The study highlights how experiences of ambiguous digital nonresponse were shaped by relational context, communication expectations, and individual interpretations within contemporary online social environments.
