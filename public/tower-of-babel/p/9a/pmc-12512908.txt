Distinct lung microbiota community states are associated with pulmonary nontuberculous mycobacterial disease prognosis
Authors: Yu Chen, Qing Miao, Rong Bao, Hong Qu, Jiajin Shen, Na Li, Sichun Luan, Xiaoyu Yin, Jue Pan, Bijie Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (PNTM) is rising, but the available treatments have limitations. Currently, the understanding of the ecology of the airway microbiota in PNTM is limited, especially regarding community structure, dynamics, and their relationship with clinical outcomes.MethodsWe used metagenomic sequencing to characterize the lung microbiota in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). We evaluated the prognosis of patients with PNTM through respiratory specimen cultures and chest CT scans.ResultsPNTM exhibit distinct airway microbiota characteristics compared to controls, however, no significant differences were observed in NTM species. A Dirichlet multinomial mixture model was used to identify two distinct community types (pneumotypes) and investigate their association with host immunity and prognosis. At the 13-month median follow-up, pneumotype 1 (including Mycobacterium, opportunistic pathogens, and anaerobes) presented a lower probability of sustained culture conversion (hazard ratio = 0.29; 95% confidence interval = 0.12–0.73; P = 0.009) than pneumotype 2, indicating a worse prognosis. Based on microbial community abundance and interactions, Ralstonia (NetMoss score = 1.0; log2FoldChange = 3.6) and Dolosigranulum (NetMoss score = 0.6; log2FoldChange = 1.4) emerged as prominent taxa associated with the shift from pneumotype 1 to pneumotype 2, which correlated with differences in clinical outcomes.ConclusionOur research indicates that distinct baseline microbial characteristics in PNTM patients are associated with prognosis. Furthermore, we identified candidate microbes driving changes in the PNTM microbial community state, serving as potential therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04420-7.

Introduction

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) cause pulmonary diseases and extrapulmonary infections and are ubiquitous in soil, water, and manmade environments [1, 2]. Recent studies have shown that the incidence and prevalence of pulmonary nontuberculous mycobacterial disease (PNTM) are increasing worldwide [3].

Clinical management of PNTM is extremely challenging, especially in terms of treatment initiation and prognosis determination [4, 5]. This variability in NTM pathogenicity complicates disease states (such as colonization, latent infection, post-primary activation, and progression), resulting in different prognoses across host populations. Moreover, the lack of effective anti-NTM drugs results in an overall cure rate for PNTM (30–68%) [6] that is comparable to those of multidrug-resistant (MDR)-TB (average 60%) [7] and extensively drug-resistant (XDR)-TB (30–50%) [8]. Owing to this complexity, clinical practice related to PNTM remains largely unstandardized. The lack of objective criteria limits the identification of high-risk patients or those who would not benefit from therapy, leading to similar mortality rates and clinical improvement between treated and untreated patients [9].

Changes in the microbial community structure of the airways are associated with short-term fluctuations in patient clinical status and may be related to the long-term progression of lung diseases [10]. However, it remains unclear whether the lower respiratory tract communities in PNTM have different types that influence the ecology, functionality, and pathogenicity of the airway microbiota. Identifying biomarkers associated with prognosis could help identify high-risk patients and optimize treatment decisions, ensuring maximal therapeutic benefits for patients while minimizing potential adverse effects of multidrug therapy. For instance, the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease airway microbiome delineates two neutrophilic subtypes: Haemophilus-dominant (elevated IL-1β/TNF-α) and balanced-microbiota (elevated IL-17 A), informing personalized antimicrobial or immunomodulatory interventions [11]. Therefore, we aimed to determine the indicative characteristics of a patient subpopulation that would benefit from therapy. Clarifying such characteristics would allow for precise medicine administration and appropriate antimicrobial prescription.

We characterized the microbial community of PNTM through metagenomic sequencing and accurately classified the baseline microbiota. Furthermore, we compared the characteristics of respiratory microbiota and co-occurrence networks among different pneumotypes, exploring their relationship with prognosis. Finally, by employing dimensionality reduction and pseudo-time series analysis, we identified key species driving microbial community changes, offering insights for the development of novel antimicrobial agents.

Materials and methods

Study design

We retrospectively analyzed 169 BALF metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) datasets from Zhongshan Hospital, Shanghai, China between October 2018 and October 2021. This dataset includes 69 patients with PNTM, 46 with PTB, 17 with non-mycobacterial infections, and 37 from a non-infected control group (primarily comprising cancer patients) (for a flowchart of study design, see Fig. 1). Among the 69 PNTM patients, 39 were identified as infected with Mycobacterium avium complex, 12 with Mycobacterium abscessus, 11 with Mycobacterium kansasii, and 7 with other mycobacterial species.

Ethical statement

All samples in this study were obtained from routine clinical practice at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients, authorizing the use of their clinical data for research purposes and publication. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. B2020-411), and all procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. As this study does not constitute a clinical trial, clinical trial registration was not required.

Clinical data collection and diagnostic criteria

Patient demographic, clinical, laboratory, radiological, treatment, and outcome data were collected from electronic medical records. These data included age, sex, body mass index, C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), white blood cells, neutrophils, inflammatory factors, and lymphocyte count. Diagnosis of PNTM was based on criteria set by the American Thoracic Association/American Infectious Diseases Association [12]. Other infectious diseases were diagnosed according to standard clinical and microbiological criteria, using symptoms and laboratory test results from culturing, pathological examination, serology testing, and PCR. We conducted systematic follow-up evaluations in PNTM patients, including both microbiological and radiological assessments.

Flow cytometry and cytokine profiles

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Sweden) from whole blood and resuspended in 0.5 mL PBS for immunophenotyping using fluorescently conjugated antibodies against CD3-FITC, CD4-APC, CD8-APC-Cy7, CD56-PE, and CD19-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences), with appropriate isotype controls for gating. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10.6.2, BD Biosciences). Concurrently, plasma cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-2R, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α) were quantified using a cytometric bead array (CBA) assay (QuantoBio) according to the manufacturer’s standardized protocol.

Metagenome sequencing

We followed standard BALF collection procedures. All specimens were collected and immediately stored in a −80 freezer. All BALF samples were subjected to metagenomic sequencing (MS). DNA was extracted from BALF using the TIANamp Micro DNA kit (DP316, Tiangen Biotech). DNA libraries were constructed with the end-repair method and then sequenced on the BGI Sequencer platform (BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China).

Bioinformatic pipeline analysis

First, data quality control was performed. Low-quality and short (< 35 bp) reads were removed from raw data using fastp [13]. Remaining reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler method to remove sequences of human origin. Filtered sequencing reads were taxonomically classified through alignment to the RefSeq microbial reference genome database (accessed via http://ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/) using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) [14].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.0) and SPSS (version 23.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous covariates were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs) if normally distributed, and medians ± interquartile ranges (IQRs) if non-normally distributed. Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA, the Mann-Whitney U test, or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Group composition was compared using PERMANOVA.

First, the abundance table was normalized. Alpha and β-diversity indices (based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) of microbial communities were calculated using the vegan package [15]. To compare lung microbiota community structure across patients in different groups, β-diversity was estimated and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Microbial ecological network analysis was performed using Spearman’s correlations and significant correlations (P < 0.05) were visualized in Gephi (version 0.10.1). Taxonomic composition was analyzed with the DESeq2 package [16]. A random forest classifier with five-fold cross-validation was used to distinguish between patient groups. Recursive feature elimination was used to select the strongest features for the model. A Dirichlet multinomial mixture (DMM) model was used to cluster community samples [17]. Multivariate Cox regression and competing risk models were used to assess associations between the lung microbiota and outcomes. Significance was set at P < 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed. Pseudotime analysis infers low-dimensional microbial trajectories, enabling lung microbiome studies where serial sampling is challenging. Existing evidence shows it effectively reveals gut/vaginal microbiota transitions, complementing longitudinal findings [18, 19]. We used Slingshot to capture the potential dynamic changes in the microbial community of PNTM lungs in a low-dimensional space [20], and analyzed species abundance changes along the trajectory using tradeSeq [21]. Furthermore, the GeneSwitches algorithm was used to identify key species associated with trajectories from patients that have never been infected to those with various PNTM disease states [22]. GeneSwitches applied logistic regression to model the presence or absence of each genus along the pseudo-time trajectory and estimated its switching time, defined as the pseudo-time point at which the predicted probability of presence crosses 0.5. Model fit was evaluated using McFadden’s Pseudo R², with higher R² values indicating stronger pseudo-time association. Genera were ranked and visualized according to their switching times, providing an intuitive view of key taxa dynamics during community transitions. The NetMoss score was calculated for each node to assess its importance in the network transition between groups [23]. NetMoss quantitatively assessed the contribution of each node to network differences by comparing module-level centrality changes between co-occurrence networks of different states (e.g., uninfected vs. DMM1 or DMM2). Specifically, it identified genera with substantial changes in network position and connectivity, highlighting potential drivers of ecological shifts. Co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman correlations (|r| >0.3, p < 0.05), and NetMoss scores were computed using default parameters from the official implementation (https://github.com/hclimente/netmoss).

Results

Comparison of airway microbiome profiles

The 169 participants included 69 PNTM, 46 PTB, 17 non-mycobacterial infected, and 37 non-infected patients. Their detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table S1.

Using MS, we identified 3717 species of bacteria, 474 species of fungi, and 97 species of DNA viruses in 169 BALF samples. After filtering out low-prevalence (< 10% of all samples) species, 1292 species remained for subsequent analyses. We assessed α-diversity using the Shannon index. The PNTM group had lower bacterial diversity than the PTB and non-infection group but higher diversity than the non-mycobacterial infection group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). Mycobiome and virome Shannon indices did not differ significantly between the four patient groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 2A).

Next, we compared β-diversity using PCoA. Bacterial and fungal community compositions differed significantly between the four groups (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations, bacterial level, P = 0.001; fungal level, P = 0.001; Fig. 2B). The results of all pairwise comparisons are presented in Table S2. Average relative abundances of bacterial, fungal, and viral genera differed across all four groups (Fig. 2C and D, Table S3).

The dysbiotic state in PNTM differs from PTB and is independent of NTM species

We compared microbial genus and species compositions across the two patient groups with mycobacterial infections. The relative abundance of genera Burkholderia, Acinetobacter, Stenotrophomonas, Escherichia, Delftia, and Alternaria was lower in the PNTM group than in the PTB group (FDR < 0.05; Figure S1A). Additionally, the PNTM group had lower relative abundance in 224 species than the PTB group but higher relative abundance in 10 species (Figure S1B). Of the latter, 70% (7/10) were NTM-associated (P < 0.05, |log FC| >1). Based on lung microbiota profiles, machine learning models can effectively distinguish between NTM- and TB-caused infections (Figure S1C and D).

Subsequently, we used Spearman’s correlations to generate a co-occurrence network including high-frequency (> 50%) species. The network heterogeneity index was 0.88, indicating a significant difference between the PNTM and PTB groups. The PNTM co-occurrence network consisted of 81 nodes and 815 edges, whereas the PTB network consisted of 193 nodes and 6911 edges (r > 0.3, P < 0.05). Moreover, PNTM had significantly more positive co-abundance relationships in the Mycobacterium interaction network than PTB (n = 137 vs. n = 1). We found that PNTM exhibited co-abundance with most genera and lower network diversity than PTB (Fig. 3A and B).

NTM infections exhibit pathogen diversity, with patients further categorized into Mycobacterium abscessus (MAB), Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Mycobacterium kansasii, and unknown groups (failed species identification). No statistically significant differences were detected among the four groups in terms of microbial abundance and community composition (Fig. 3C and D). The dysbiotic features of the lung microbiome in PNTM were independent of specific species, with commensal bacteria playing a crucial role in regulating lung microbiome dysbiosis.

Microbial pneumotypes linked to PNTM outcome

To further delineate the shared characteristics of the baseline microbiota in PNTM patients, we categorized the microbiota into different pneumotypes. We selected 60 PNTM patients with the most comprehensive microbiota and clinical data. Genera present in over 50% of the samples were retained, and the Dirichlet Multinomial Mixture (DMM) model was applied to classify the samples. This probabilistic model distinguishes between samples based on genus-level contributions. After seven independent runs, the model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) indicated that the optimal number of clusters was two (Figure S2). Accordingly, patients were classified into DMM1 and DMM2. DMM1 was enriched in the genera Aspergillus, Prevotella, Streptococcus, Ralstonia, Pseudomonas, and Mycobacterium, whereas DMM2 was enriched in Ralstonia, Aspergillus, Bradyrhizobium, Rhizobium, Prevotella, and Neisseria (Fig. 4A). The two groups differed significantly in community composition (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.30, P = 0.001; Fig. 4B). The relative abundances of key contributing microbial taxa differed significantly between the two groups (Figure S3). Furthermore, DMM1 and DMM2 differed significantly in natural killer cell (CD56+/CD16+) count and bronchiectasis prevalence at baseline levels (Table S4, Fig. 4C and D).

Next, we explored the relationship between pneumotypes and outcomes. We evaluated the outcome based on culture conversion rates of respiratory specimens (at least three consecutive negative mycobacterial cultures collected a minimum of 4 weeks apart). Nine patients without follow-up respiratory specimens were excluded. The sampling date for the first negative culture result was the date of culture conversion. During the follow-up period (median: 13 months), patients with DMM2 pneumotype had significantly higher culture conversion rates than patients with DMM1 pneumotype (63.6% vs. 31.0%, log-rank test: P = 0.0037; Fig. 4E and F). The data met the proportional hazards assumption (Figure S4). The DMM1 group had a lower probability of sustained culture conversion (no positive cultures after three consecutive negative cultures) than the DMM2 group, according to both univariate (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.12–0.71, P = 0.006; Table 1) and multivariate (HR = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.12–0.73, P = 0.009; Table 1) Cox regression, indicating that DMM was an independent predictor of culture conversion.

According to previous study results [24, 25], sex, age, pulmonary cavity, and ESR are clinical indicators associated with the prognosis of PNTM. In our multivariate Cox regression analysis, sex and pneumotype were identified as significant predictors. Therefore, we included pneumotype and sex as variables in Model 1, while Model 2 included sex, age, pulmonary cavity, and ESR. After five-fold cross-validation, Model 1 (AUC6M = 0.841, 95% CI = 0.822–0.861; AUC12M = 0.829, 95% CI = 0.810–0.852; AUC18M = 0.922, 95% CI = 0.909–0.935) was a better predictor of culture conversion rate than Model 2 (AUC6M = 0.666, 95% CI = 0.633–0.699; AUC12M = 0.715, 95% CI = 0.689–0.744; AUC18M = 0.547, 95% CI = 0.512–0.582) in patients at 6 months, 12 months, and 18 months (Fig. 5). We also used CT imaging as a prognostic outcome. Model 1 demonstrated improved predictive performance after incorporating patient microbiome data. However, pneumotype had no significant effect on the improvement or progression of CT imaging outcomes (Figure S5).

Dynamic changes in pneumotypes and key driving species analyzed using trajectory analysis

We aimed to investigate whether there are orderly dynamic changes among pneumotypes over time and explore their potential driving factors. We performed trajectory analysis on individuals from the uninfected, DMM1, and DMM2 groups, with uninfected individuals designated as the starting point, and obtained a pseudotime for this process. The three groups were visualized using UMAP dimensionality reduction, with pseudotime mapped onto each individual. Analysis revealed dynamic changes in the microbial community transitioning from the uninfected state to the DMM1 pneumotype, and then to the DMM2 pneumotype (Fig. 6A). To identify key species associated with pneumotype transitions, we employed GeneSwitches analysis to assess changes in genus-level abundance along pseudotime and further visualized these changes using heatmaps (Figs. 6B and C). During the transition from the uninfected state to the DMM1 pneumotype, we observed decreases in the abundance of Rothia, Veillonella, and Propionibacterium. As the DMM1 pneumotype transitioned to DMM2, we noted increases in the abundance of Ralstonia, Bradyrhizobium, and Rhizobium, while the abundance of Streptococcus and Prevotella decreased.

Dynamic changes in pneumotypes are associated with disturbances in airway interactions

If we consider only abundance, we might overlook the role of certain species with low abundance but significant relationships. Therefore, to identify key taxa driving pneumotype transitions due to intra-group interactions, we first computed interactions between genera within each group and conducted module analysis. Integration of bacterial and fungal microbiota revealed a significant weakening of intra-group interactions from uninfected individuals to DMM1 pneumotype, and then to DMM2 pneumotype (Fig. 7A). Module analysis demonstrated the conservation of three bacterial-fungal interaction modules across all three groups (Fig. 7B). Module M4, characterized by bacteria–bacteria interactions, was exclusively detected in the non-infected group, suggesting its potential role in maintaining a stable microbial network under non-disease conditions. Specifically, the M1 module consistently expanded from uninfected to both DMM1 and DMM2, while the M2 and M3 modules exhibited substantial interaction initially, stabilizing thereafter. Additionally, intra-group bacterial-bacterial interactions decreased and then recovered from uninfected to DMM1 to DMM2, bacterial-fungal interactions increased and then recovered, while fungal-fungal interactions continuously decreased. To identify taxa driving inter-group interaction shifts, we computed NetMoss scores for each node. Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Ascomycota were positively associated with NTM infection, whereas Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and a subset of Proteobacteria were linked to the transition from DMM1 to DMM2 pneumotype (Fig. 7C). Additionally, decreased relative abundances of Neisseria, Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, Eubacterium, Staphylococcus, Fusobacterium, Leptotrichia, Porphyromonas, and Rothia were associated with the transition from DMM1 to DMM2. Notably, sustained increases in Ralstonia and Dolosigranulum were associated with the transition to the DMM2 pneumotype (Fig. 7C), which was linked to a more favorable prognosis.

Discussion

Lung infections involve complex ecological communities, where pathogens coexist with symbiotic microorganisms [25, 26], and there exists inter-patient heterogeneity. Previous studies have not comprehensively evaluated the characteristics of lung microbiota and their association with host status. Our study used metagenomic sequencing to explore the relationship between lung microbiota and PNTM prognosis. Comparative analysis of the abundance and interactions of bacteria, fungi, and viruses revealed that the lung microbiota in PNTM patients exhibited reduced diversity, with microbial species predominantly showing positive correlations. Symbiotic microorganisms may also play a crucial role in the pathogen-host interaction process.

In clinical practice, the utilization of clustering algorithms as a precise method of disease classification has precedence [11, 27]. We have identified two distinct types of pulmonary dysbiosis, referred to as pneumotypes, which exhibit variances in host immune response and airway structure. Here, using Cox proportional hazards regression, we found a significant correlation between pneumotypes and prognosis. Cross-validation demonstrated the superiority of pneumotypes over a prediction model based on previously defined risk factors [25]. The better-prognosis pneumotype DMM2 was enriched with Ralstonia and environmental microbiota. While Ralstonia pickettii can activate ethionamide against mycobacteria in vitro [28], its in vivo therapeutic role requires validation. In contrast, DMM1 has a worse prognosis and is characterized by higher mycobacterial burden, along with abundant opportunistic pathogens (Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, Aspergillus) and anaerobes such as Prevotella. DMM1 is characterized by a lower number of NK cells. In the cystic fibrosis patient cohort, the number of NK cells correlates with the bacterial burden of NTM [29]. Within pulmonary macrophages and granulomas, NTM induces a hypoxic microenvironment and promotes anaerobic growth. Hypoxia decreases the number of monocyte-derived macrophages in the lungs and generates persistent inflammation [30]. Anaerobic bacteria ferment and degrade respiratory mucins, resulting in the production of amino acids and short-chain fatty acids [31]. These compounds enhance neutrophil-mediated inflammatory responses and serve as nutrients for aerobic pathogens [32]. Additionally, bacteria such as the Burkholderia cepacia complex use T6SS to secrete Rac1-, Cdc42-, and RhoA-targeting effectors into macrophages, thus avoiding microbicidal mechanisms and persisting in phagocytes [33]. Moreover, ecological dysbiosis in patients with PNTM probably alters the ecological niche of Aspergillus, which ranges from colonization to infection. Coinfection with NTM and Aspergillus leads to poor prognosis and increased mortality [34, 35]. Notably, IL-10 was significant in univariate but not multivariate analysis. Studies show innate myeloid (IM)-sensed IL-10 maintains lung homeostasis by preventing dysbiosis-induced inflammation [36]. The DMM1 pneumotype is associated with higher abundance of pathobionts, suggesting potential underlying associations between IL-10 and pneumotype status. A deeper understanding of the functional interactions between the microbiota and host immunity is crucial for guiding personalized patient management. Our findings contribute to the identification of high-risk patients through pneumotypes in the era of microbiome, thus balancing therapeutic efficacy and adverse reactions to combined antimicrobial treatments.

Previous studies have also explored the significance of inflammatory cytokines in BALF in the context of mycobacterial infections. Persistent elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 has been associated with cavity formation in tuberculosis, disease progression in NTM pulmonary disease, and increased mortality [37, 38]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), produced by the gut microbiota, modulate pulmonary immune responses via the gut-lung axis [39]. In both tuberculosis and NTM infections, gut dysbiosis may lead to reduced SCFA production, which has been linked to more severe lung damage and delayed treatment response [40, 41]. In future studies, we plan to integrate host-derived data, such as cytokine profiles, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, to further validate and elucidate the significance of the microbial markers identified in this study.

In fact, the microbial dysbiosis in patients with pulmonary diseases continues to dynamically change in response to alterations in airway structure and host immunity [42]. However, continuous monitoring of BALF in PNTM patients is challenging. Although changes in microbial composition occur in high-dimensional space, microbial dynamics processes (such as transitioning from a healthy state to NTM infection) may follow relatively common patterns, thus allowing inference through low-dimensional trajectories in high-dimensional space.

Previous studies have provided insights into the dynamic changes of microbial communities through several pseudo-temporal methods [19, 43]. Using a pseudo-temporal algorithm with uninfected patients as the starting point, we discovered that the trajectory of lung microbiota in PNTM patients transitions from the uninfected state to DMM1 pneumotype before transforming into DMM2, rather than directly transitioning from the uninfected state to either DMM1 or DMM2 pneumotype. Considering the host characteristics of the two pneumotype, we propose that DMM2 pneumotype reshapes the microbiota during host-microbiota interactions, alleviating the inflammatory state.

Further exploration based on species abundance and interactions within the microbiota elucidated key species driving the transition of lung microbiota from uninfected to DMM1 and from DMM1 to DMM2 pneumotype. The sustained increase of Ralstonia and Dolosigranulum plays a notable role in the transition from DMM2 to DMM1 pneumotype. Studies on lung microbiota in mice infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis have shown a negative correlation between Ralstonia and Mycobacterium abundance, with Ralstonia inhibiting Mycobacterium growth in vitro [44]. Dolosigranulum, a lactic acid bacterium commonly found in the upper respiratory tract, interacts directly with bacterial respiratory pathogens and modulates host immunity and inflammatory responses, thereby exerting beneficial effects in the human respiratory tract [45]. Previous studies have successfully identified key substances from bacteria antagonistic to Staphylococcus aureus in the microbiota as novel antimicrobial agents [46]. For NTM, particularly multi-drug resistant Mycobacterium abscessus, further research is needed to identify small molecule targets against NTM from antagonistic or beneficial bacteria and their representative microbiota.

The prognosis of pulmonary infections results from the complex interplay among pathogens, commensal microbiota, host factors, and treatment regimens [47]. In our pseudotime analysis, the more pathogenic pneumotype DMM1 emerged earlier than DMM2, while patients with a DMM2-type microbiota had better outcomes. We cannot overlook the role of host factors and NTM infection in reshaping the lung microbiota. The host continuously influences microbial communities through immune responses, metabolic state, and nutrient availability, while NTM infection further alters the local microenvironment, driving microbial succession. For example, the host immune system can directly regulate airway microbiota through secretory IgA, antimicrobial peptides, and cytokines such as IL-17, or indirectly via pattern recognition receptors like Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which modulate local inflammation and microbial selection [48]. Therefore, investigating pneumotype transitions and their association with treatment outcomes may help elucidate the pathogen–host immunometabolic mechanisms underlying PNTM prognosis.

Our study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Thus, a larger sample size and external validation cohort are required to verify pneumotype stability and the relationship with patient outcome. Second, we did not measure inflammatory factors in BALF, but such data would have provided a more realistic representation of host immune response. We recommend their inclusion in future research to enhance the validity of our proposed diagnostic method. On the other hand, epidemiological surveys indicate a higher prevalence of PNTM in females than in males, which was also observed in our study. Follow-up of PNTM patients via bronchoscopy for non-diagnostic purposes was challenging, making it difficult to obtain continuous BALF samples. Some PNTM patients had underlying lung disease, and prior infections with P. aeruginosa or S. aureus may have shaped their microbiota. Including such cases could better reveal ecological transitions and disease heterogeneity in PNTM.

In conclusion, we found that the shared characteristics of the lung microbiota in PNTM patients are associated with patient prognosis. Against the backdrop of widespread NGS application, this finding aids in identifying high-risk patients. Furthermore, we identified key species driving the transition of the lung microbiota towards a favorable pneumotype, which could serve as potential targets for future antimicrobial drug development.
