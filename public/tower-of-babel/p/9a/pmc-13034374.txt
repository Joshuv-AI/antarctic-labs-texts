Development and validation of the Barriers to Mental Health Service Utilization Scale (BMHSUS) in Bangladesh: an in-depth analysis of sociodemographic influences on access barriers – insights from the National Mental Health Survey
Authors: Md Rizwanul Karim, Md Faruq Alam, Niaz Mohammad Khan, Shahnaz Akter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health service utilisation (MHSU) in Bangladesh remains limited due to cultural, social and structural barriers, including stigma, reliance on traditional healing practices, concerns about medication and restricted access to care. This study aimed to develop and validate a culturally appropriate tool to assess barriers to MHSU and to examine sociodemographic, clinical and psychosocial factors associated with these barriers.MethodsData were drawn from the National Mental Health Survey of Bangladesh (August 2019–May 2020), a multicentre, community-based study using stratified cluster random sampling across 496 primary sampling units (PSUs). A total of 7238 adults were included. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted to validate the Barriers to Mental Health Service Utilization Scale (BMHSUS). Binary logistic regression identified predictors of perceived barriers.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis validated a four-factor, eight-item BMHSUS with strong psychometric properties. Overall, nearly three-quarters of participants reported at least one barrier to mental health service utilisation, with social concerns (47%) and perceived stigma (39%) emerging as the most prevalent obstacles. Females had significantly lower odds of reporting barriers than males (adjusted OR (AOR)=0.49; 95% CI 0.38 to 0.61), and urban residents were less likely than rural residents to report barriers (AOR=0.84; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.94). Individuals with depressive (AOR=1.48; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.90) and anxiety disorders (AOR=1.75; 95% CI 1.28 to 2.39) were more likely to experience barriers. Higher stigma-related scores for relationship disruption, anxiety and hygiene concerns were associated with increased barriers, whereas favourable beliefs about treatability and recovery reduced the odds (AOR=0.89; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.90).ConclusionsThe BMHSUS is a reliable and culturally valid tool for assessing barriers to MHSU in Bangladesh. Addressing stigma, geographic inequities and disorder-specific vulnerabilities is essential to improving access to mental health services and informing evidence-based policy and intervention strategies.Keywords: Mental Health, Public Health, Health Services Accessibility, Epidemiologic Research Design, Preventive Medicine

Introduction

Mental health issues represent a significant public health concern worldwide, with an estimated one in eight people affected by a mental health disorder.1 Despite the increasing recognition of mental health as a critical component of overall well-being, the utilisation of mental health services remains low, particularly in low- and middle-income countries such as Bangladesh. Barriers to mental health service utilisation (MHSU) are multifaceted, encompassing individual, cultural, structural and systemic factors.2–5

Cultural beliefs and practices play a crucial role in shaping perceptions of mental health and the utilisation of related services.6,7 To date, a significant segment of Bangladeshi society views mental health problems through religious and spiritual lenses, leading many individuals to seek help from traditional healers or religious figures rather than from formal healthcare providers, which often delays or prevents appropriate treatment. Deeply rooted stigma further compounds this issue, as people with mental illness—and their families—face labelling, social exclusion and fear of discrimination, resulting in concealment and avoidance of care. In addition, concerns about psychiatric medication remain a significant barrier, collectively limiting timely and effective MHSU.3,5,8–10 Many individuals are apprehensive about the side effects of psychotropic drugs, perceiving them as addictive or harmful. Misinformation and a lack of understanding about treatment modalities contribute to a widespread reluctance to accept pharmacological interventions. These concerns are exacerbated by inadequate communication between healthcare providers and patients regarding treatment’s benefits and potential side effects.11,12

Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, occupation, marital status, residence and mental health condition, significantly influence barriers to MHSU. Younger individuals may be more open to seeking mental health services due to increasing awareness through education and social media. However, older generations often rely on traditional practices or dismiss mental health concerns altogether, perceiving them as a sign of weakness or lack of resilience. Gender norms and expectations play a crucial role. Women in Bangladesh often face restricted mobility and limited autonomy, which can hinder their ability to seek care. Conversely, men may avoid seeking mental health services due to societal expectations of masculinity and fear of appearing vulnerable. Individuals in low-income or informal employment may face financial constraints and lack the flexibility to attend appointments. Conversely, those in high-stress occupations may encounter stigma within the workplace, discouraging them from seeking help.4,13–18 Married individuals may have better support networks and be more likely to seek help due to encouragement from partners. Conversely, unmarried and divorced individuals might experience greater social stigma or lack the necessary support to access care. Urban residents often have greater access to mental health services but may still face stigma and concerns about confidentiality. Rural populations face additional barriers, including limited availability of services, lack of awareness and traditional beliefs. The nature and severity of mental health conditions also influence service utilisation. Individuals with severe conditions such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder may be more likely to seek treatment due to pronounced symptoms. However, those with mild or moderate disorders, such as anxiety or depression, often delay seeking help, perceiving their condition as less serious or manageable without professional intervention.4–6,19,20

The development and validation of a context-specific tool to assess barriers to MHSU is essential for addressing the mental health crisis in Bangladesh. Such an instrument enables the systematic identification of critical obstacles across population groups, supports the design of targeted and efficient interventions and facilitates monitoring of changes over time. By capturing the complex interplay of demographic, cultural, psychosocial and treatment-related factors that influence access to care, this tool can inform strategies to reduce stigma, enhance awareness and strengthen service utilisation, ultimately improving mental health outcomes.21–23

The study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive tool to assess perceived barriers to mental health service use and to explore how sociodemographic and psychosocial factors influence these barriers among the Bangladeshi population.

Methods

Design and sampling

The National Mental Health Survey was a multicentric, community-based cross-sectional study that used stratified cluster random sampling from primary sampling units (PSUs), as defined by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS). The survey was conducted from August 2019 to May 2020, targeting all adult Bangladeshi men and women aged 18 years and above. Based on a 95% CI, a 5% sampling error and a 16.1% prevalence of mental disorders reported in the National Mental Health Survey (2010), the estimated sample size was set at 208. The final targeted sample size was 8928 adults, considering both genders, five age groups, urban and rural contexts, a design effect of 2 and an estimated 20% non-response rate. The survey employed a nationally representative sampling frame, comprising all PSUs, which were defined as Enumeration Areas from the 2011 Bangladesh Population and Housing Census. Each PSU represents a geographic cluster of approximately 100–220 households and includes detailed information on location, urban-rural classification and estimated household counts, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the country. The survey was conducted in 496 randomly selected PSUs, evenly distributed across all eight divisions and all 64 districts of the country (online supplemental figure 1).

Sampling method

At the first sampling stage, stratification was applied to select an equal number of urban and rural PSUs from each of the eight administrative divisions specified by the BBS. PSUs were defined as the enumerated areas from the 2011 Census conducted by the BBS. In 2017, these 496 PSUs were updated for the Global Adult Tobacco Survey, making them suitable for this survey to ensure national representation. Specifically, 62 PSUs were chosen from each division, with 31 designated as urban and 31 as rural. In the second stage, 18 households were systematically selected from each sampled PSU using the fractional interval technique, ensuring equal probability. The selected households were randomly assigned as ‘male’ or ‘female’ to achieve balanced gender representation. Finally, one individual was randomly chosen from the eligible males and females within each participating household using an Android application. All Bangladeshi nationals by birth, aged 18 years and older, who were not suffering from major illnesses and provided informed written consent for the interview were included in the study (online supplemental figure 1).

Data collection

Data collection occurred in two steps: initially, enumerators used programmed Android tablet computers, followed by paper-and-pencil forms completed by psychiatrists. Data from the tablets was transferred to a server via the cloud, while manual data entry from paper forms was also conducted. After manual data entry, records were reviewed for completeness and consistency, resolving discrepancies between paper forms and the electronic database through cross-checking by the study team. An acceptable level of non-response was recorded at each stage of data gathering. After discrepancies were resolved and missing variables addressed, 7238 completed data sets were deemed suitable for further analysis, resulting in an overall completion/response rate of 81.07% (online supplemental figure 2).

Data collection instrument

This study aims to develop a novel instrument for evaluating MHSU barriers, leveraging insights derived from the National Mental Health Survey data. The National Mental Health Survey included an interviewer-administered questionnaire that comprised sections on sociodemographic information, mental health condition screening and diagnostic tools, a stigma scale and a scale measuring attitudes towards mental health service-seeking.

Day’S Mental illness stigma scale

The Day’s Mental Illness Stigma Scale (DMISS) consists of 28 items that reflect seven reliable dimensions of stigma. These dimensions are anxiety, relationship disruption, hygiene, visibility, treatability, professional efficacy and recovery, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.71 to 0.90. Participants rated their agreement or disagreement with each statement on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The scale was developed to assess stigma towards individuals experiencing mental illness, depression, bipolar mood disorder and schizophrenia. Overall, the scale exhibits strong psychometric reliability and captures both emotional and cognitive aspects of stigma related to mental illness.24

Inventory of attitudes toward seeking mental health services

The Inventory of Attitudes Toward Seeking Mental Health Services (IASMHS) is a 24-item measure constructed to assess attitudes towards seeking professional psychological assistance. It comprises three subscales: Psychological Openness, Help-Seeking Propensity and Indifference to Stigma. The Psychological Openness subscale evaluates individuals’ willingness to acknowledge psychological challenges and consider seeking help. The Help-Seeking Propensity subscale assesses perceived readiness and ability to seek professional support, and the Indifference to Stigma subscale examines the degree to which individuals are concerned about social perceptions associated with receiving psychological services.

The IASMHS was developed as an improvised version of the Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help Scale, originally introduced by Fischer and Turner in 1970.21–23 Modifications include the replacement of gendered pronouns with gender-neutral alternatives, the substitution of technical terms with accessible language, the specification of ‘psychological problems’ in place of generic terminology, and the revision of the original four-point rating scale to a five-point Likert scale. The IASMHS demonstrates robust internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients reported as 0.87 for the total scale, 0.82 for Psychological Openness, 0.76 for Help-Seeking Propensity and 0.79 for Indifference to Stigma. Test-retest reliability coefficients indicate stability over time, with values of 0.85 for the full scale, 0.86 for Psychological Openness, 0.64 for Help-Seeking Propensity and 0.91 for Indifference to Stigma.25,26

The research team conducted eight focus group discussions (FGDs), stratified by sex and 16 key informant interviews from eight PSUs near the study areas to explore the criteria for identifying barriers to seeking mental health services. The discussions were recorded, translated into English and transcribed under the supervision of experienced researchers. Key statements and concepts reflecting respondents’ views on barriers to mental health services utilisation were then extracted. Based on expert opinions and insights from eight FGDs, five items—addressing beliefs about spiritual influences on mental disorders, the role of traditional healers, marriage reducing mental problems, concerns about psychotropic drug side effects and challenges in discontinuing mental health medications—were added to the IASMHS scale items to enhance its comprehensiveness and cultural relevance.

Statistical analysis

In the initial phase, data were input, screened and assessed for outliers, normality and multicollinearity using SPSS V.23 and Jamovi (V.2.3.28). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was employed to test model fit based on the factorial model derived from exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Structural and cultural validity were assessed using standardised factor loadings and fit indices to evaluate the consistency of the factorial structure. Although the χ2 test is sensitive to large sample sizes and may indicate misfit even for well-specified models, multiple fit indices were therefore used to provide a more robust evaluation of model adequacy. Model fit was assessed using χ², the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR). RMSEA values <0.08 or <0.05, CFI >0.90 and TLI >0.90 or >0.95 are considered acceptable, while SRMR should be <0.08. Other fit indices, such as Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Relative Fit Index (RFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI) and Parsimony Normed Fit Index (PNFI), were also evaluated for model adequacy.27,28 Average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) were computed to assess the theoretical and empirical relationships of the construct. Cronbach’s alpha, MacDonald’s omega and Guttman’s λ2 were used to evaluate reliability, with acceptable values for alpha between 0.70 and 0.95 and omega ≥0.70.

Descriptive statistics were presented in tables and graphs. To examine the association between barriers to mental health service utilisation and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, bivariate analyses were conducted, and p values along with crude ORs were reported. To identify predictors of barriers to MHSU while adjusting for sociodemographic factors, clinical characteristics and stigma towards mental illness, binary logistic regression analysis was employed. The final statistical results were presented with adjusted ORs (AORs) and 95% CIs for the AOR.

Exploratory factor analysis

A total of 29 items, comprising 24 from the IASMHS scale and five derived from FGDs and expert recommendations, were subjected to EFA using parallel analysis in Jamovi V.2.3.28. Before conducting EFA, the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was evaluated. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of sampling adequacy was 0.73, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.6. At the same time, Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity indicated statistical significance, confirming the factorability of the correlation matrix.

The scree plot displayed a noticeable inflection point after the fourth component, and based on Cattell’s scree test, four components were retained for further analysis. Varimax rotation was performed to facilitate interpretation, revealing eight items loading strongly (>0.5) and predominantly onto one of the four components, with loadings ranging from 0.62 to 0.97.29 Collectively, the four-component solution explained 62.08% of the total variance, distributed as follows: component 1 (18.77%), component 2 (16.99%), component 3 (13.56%) and component 4 (12.76%) (table 1).

The Barriers to Mental Health Service Utilization Scale (BMHSUS) was therefore finalised with eight items categorised into four dimensions. The first dimension, ‘cultural barrier’ (α=0.84), included two items addressing beliefs about mental health and spirituality. The second dimension, ‘perceived stigma’ (α=0.80), comprised two items related to perceptions of stigma and the social burden of mental illness. The third dimension, ‘Medication concern’ (α=0.66), included two items focused on perceptions and concerns about psychotropic medication. The fourth dimension, ‘social concern’ (α=0.68), contained two items assessing perceived social barriers to seeking mental health services. A comparison of the final eight-item BMHSUS scale with the IASMHS 24-item scale is provided in online supplemental appendix 1.

Confirmatory factor analysis

As outlined by Malhotra et al, CFA is employed to verify whether the number of factors and the loadings of observed variables conform to theoretical expectations.30 Assessing the validity and reliability of the scale is crucial to confirm the proposed model and accurately represent the constructs through the observed variables. Maximum likelihood is commonly used in CFA and SEM but requires large samples, continuous indicators and multivariate normality. Significant non-normality was detected using Mardia’s skewness and kurtosis statistics (p>0.05), which can bias parameter estimates and standard errors. Thus, CFA was conducted using the weighted least squares estimation (WLSMV) method to assess the relationships between observed variables and their latent constructs.31 Structural and cultural validity was assessed through standardised factor loadings and fit indices to ensure the consistency of the model with the targeted population.

The four-factor solution derived from the EFA was further evaluated for theoretical validity, using four subscales: cultural barrier, perceived stigma, medication concern and social concern. The proposed four-factor model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data, meeting the recommended thresholds for fit indices: GFI=1.00, AGFI=0.99, NFI=0.99, TLI=0.98, CFI=0.99, RFI=0.98, IFI=0.99, PNFI=0.50, RMSEA=0.04 and SRMR=0.02 (table 2).

Structural and cultural validity

The National Mental Health Survey of Bangladesh identified a four-factor structure for the BMHSUS through EFA, requiring further theoretical validation. CFA was conducted to verify the scale’s structure, focusing on four subscales: cultural barrier, perceived stigma, medication concern and social concern. Factorial validity was assessed by evaluating standardised factor loadings and fit indices for consistency and reliability. The initial analysis of the 24-item IASMHS plus five additional items from expert input and FGDs revealed low factor loadings for several items. After removing weaker items, a revised eight-item model was tested, showing factor loadings exceeding the accepted threshold of 0.60. Model fit for both versions was evaluated using absolute fit indices (SRMR, RMSEA, CFI, TLI, etc) and relative fit indices (GFI, AGFI, etc), with adequacy determined by established benchmarks. The refined eight-item model revealed a strong fit across all indices, confirming its structural and cultural validity. The final model consists of four latent factors and eight observable variables, which meet the standardised threshold for factor loadings (>0.60).

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent validity establishes that latent constructs are theoretically related, while discriminant validity ensures that they are empirically distinct. In this study, both forms of validity were evaluated using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which examines the AVE and CR. Convergent validity for the BMHSUS was assessed by computing CR and AVE, with thresholds of ≥0.60 for CR and ≥0.50 for AVE. Discriminant validity was confirmed by comparing the square root of each construct’s AVE to its correlations with other constructs.32 The results indicated that the square root of the AVE for each latent variable exceeded its correlations with other factors, providing evidence of robust discriminant validity (online supplemental appendix 2).

Internal consistency

The BMHSUS scale demonstrated good internal consistency,33 with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78. The alpha values for the four dimensions were 0.84, 0.80, 0.66 and 0.68, respectively.

Results

Measuring perceived barriers to MHSU in Bangladesh

The validated measure of perceived barriers to MHSU consists of eight items evenly divided across four factors, with each factor addressing two key aspects of the construct. Each item on the validated BMHSUS was rated using a five-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from 0 (disagree) to 4 (agree), including intermediate values of 1 (somewhat disagree), 2 (neutral) and 3 (somewhat agree). Subsequently, responses were dichotomised into ‘barrier absent’ (scores ≤2) and ‘barrier present’ (scores >2), with a score of 2 representing the neutral category. Two items from each factor are combined to ensure precision in assessing specific dimensions. The total scores for each factor were further grouped into two categories: ‘barrier absent’ (score=0) and ‘barrier present’ (score≥1). Finally, all four factors (cultural barrier, perceived stigma, medication concern and social concern) were combined to determine the overall barriers to MHSU, with a total score of zero indicating the absence of barriers and any score greater than zero indicating the presence of at least one barrier. The extent of barriers to MHSU was presented in two ways: by items and by factors.

Scale item-wise barriers: Individual scale items (eg, ‘traditional healer’, ‘spiritual’, ‘blotted life’, ‘shame’, ‘lifetime treatment’, ‘drug effect’, ‘social attitude’ and ‘embarrassment’) are shown along the x-axis with their corresponding percentages of cases on the y-axis. The highest reported barriers are ‘embarrassment’ (36%), ‘social attitude’ (33%) and ‘shame’ (32%). The lowest barriers include ‘drug effect’ (21%) and ‘lifetime rx’ (22%) (figure 1).

Sex: the study identified clear gender differences across all dimensions of barriers to MHSU. Male respondents consistently reported higher levels of cultural barriers, perceived stigma, medication-related concerns and social concerns than females, with the most pronounced disparities observed for perceived stigma and social concerns domains. Overall, these findings highlight that men experience significantly greater psychosocial barriers to accessing mental health services, while gender differences in cultural and medication-related barriers were comparatively less marked (online supplemental appendix 3).

As the BMHSUS comprises four distinct domains, substantial variation was observed in the distribution and associations of sociodemographic characteristics across these dimensions, with patterns most pronounced in the cultural barriers, perceived stigma and social concern domains (online supplemental appendix 3). Predictors of barriers to MHSU

The binary logistic regression analysis identified several sociodemographic, clinical and perceptual factors independently associated with barriers to MHSU. Compared with males, females had significantly lower odds of reporting barriers (AOR=0.49; 95% CI 0.38 to 0.61; p<0.001). Urban residents were also less likely to report barriers than rural residents (AOR=0.84; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.94; p=0.003). Age and marital status were not significantly associated with barriers in any category. With respect to religion, participants identifying with Buddhism or Christianity had significantly lower odds of reporting barriers compared with Muslims (AOR=0.37; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.70; p=0.002), while no significant difference was observed for Hindus. Occupational status showed a limited effect: those in service occupations had lower odds of reporting barriers than homemakers (AOR=0.73; 95% CI 0.55 to 0.95; p=0.019), whereas other occupational groups did not differ significantly.

Clinical characteristics demonstrated notable associations. Participants with depressive disorders (AOR=1.48; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.90; p=0.002) and anxiety disorders (AOR=1.75; 95% CI 1.28 to 2.39; p=0.001) had significantly higher odds of reporting barriers compared with those without mental disorders. A family history of mental disorder was associated with lower odds of barriers (AOR=0.70; 95% CI 0.52 to 0.96; p=0.025), while the presence of a mental health condition in the family was not statistically significant.

Among psychosocial and perception-based variables, higher scores for relationship disruption and anxiety were associated with increased odds of barriers (AOR=1.01; 95% CI 1.004 to 1.013; p<0.001), as were concerns related to hygiene (AOR=1.05; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.07; p<0.001). In contrast, more favourable perceptions regarding treatability and recovery were associated with lower odds of barriers (AOR=0.89; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.90; p<0.001). Perceived visibility was not significantly associated with barriers (table 4).

Discussion

The study validates a theoretically grounded four-factor structure by employing CFA and complementary statistical methods, addressing key dimensions of cultural barriers, perceived stigma, medication concerns and social concerns.

CFA supported the four-factor structure identified in the exploratory analysis, demonstrating excellent model fit (RMSEA=0.04, TLI=0.98, CFI=0.99, GFI=1.00). The BMHSUS also showed strong psychometric performance, with adequate CR and evidence of convergent validity (CR ≥0.60; AVE ≥0.50), while discriminant validity was confirmed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion.32 The findings indicated that the latent constructs within the BMHSUS were conceptually linked yet empirically distinct, reinforcing the scale’s capacity to consistently measure different dimensions of barriers to MHSU in Bangladesh. The BMHSUS scale also demonstrated good internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78.33

The analysis of barriers to MHSU provided granular insights into the prevalence of challenges experienced by the Bangladeshi population. At the individual item level, social concerns such as ‘embarrassment’ (36%) and ‘social attitude’ (33%) were the most significant barriers, reflecting societal stigma and attitudes towards mental health. Dimension-wise, social concerns (47%) and perceived stigma (39%) emerged as the most prominent factors, emphasising the critical need to address societal and cultural influences on mental health perceptions3,7,20,34 (figure 1).

The study highlights that a complex interplay of sociodemographic, clinical and psychosocial factors shapes barriers to MHSU. Variations by gender, residence, religion, occupation and clinical status, together with the influence of family history and individual perceptions of mental illness, underscore the multidimensional nature of access to care. These findings provide a clear framework for discussing how structural, personal and contextual factors jointly influence help-seeking behaviour.

The gender disparities in barriers to mental health align with existing literature on gendered experiences. Women were about two times less likely to report barriers to MHSU compared with men (AOR=0.49), who faced greater cultural barriers (35% vs 30%) and perceived stigma (53% vs 26%). These findings suggest that traditional masculine norms may hinder men’s help-seeking behaviours. Furthermore, men reported more social concerns (58% vs 37%) (Appendix 3), highlighting the impact of societal expectations.15–18,21 Urban residents had 16% lower odds of reporting barriers to MHSU compared with rural residents (AOR=0.84; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.94; p=0.003). This finding highlights the substantial influence of geographic disparities in mental health resources and healthcare infrastructure on access to these services.35 Age and marital status were not significantly associated with MHSU barriers in any category.36 Participants identifying as Buddhists or Christians had 63% lower odds of reporting barriers to MHSU compared with Muslims (AOR=0.37; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.70; p=0.002). In contrast, no significant differences were found among Hindu participants, a finding supported by the existing literature.37 Individuals in service occupations had 27% lower odds of reporting barriers to MHSU compared with unemployed homemakers (AOR=0.73; 95% CI 0.55 to 0.95; p=0.019). No significant differences were observed in other occupational categories. In contrast, unemployment was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of mental disorders, particularly anxiety and depression. Unemployed individuals faced greater barriers, which can be attributed to economic instability, social marginalisation and a lack of mental healthcare coverage.38

According to data, participants with depressive and anxiety disorders had 48% and 75% higher odds, respectively, of reporting barriers to MHSU,39,40 while those with a family history of mental illness had 30% lower odds41; the presence of a current mental health condition in the family was not significantly associated.

Greater stigma towards mental illness, as assessed by DMISS—capturing factors such as relationship strain, elevated anxiety and concerns related to hygiene—was associated with a modest increase in the likelihood of facing barriers. In contrast, stronger positive beliefs regarding the treatability and potential for recovery were linked to an approximate 11% reduction in those odds. Perceived visibility, however, showed no significant association with barriers.42

Strength and limitations

A notable strength of this study is its substantial sample size of 7238 participants, which enhances the generalisability of the results across various sociodemographic groups in Bangladesh. Furthermore, the employment of the validated BMHSUS ensures the use of reliable and standardised measures for assessing perceived barriers to MHSU. However, a key limitation of this study is its reliance on self-reported data, which may be prone to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationship between sociodemographic factors and perceived barriers to MHSU (online supplemental appendix 4).

Implications and recommendations

The study’s findings have significant implications for public health interventions and policy development:

Addressing stigma: The high prevalence of perceived stigma and social concerns underscores the need for awareness campaigns to reduce societal prejudices and normalise mental health help-seeking.

Tailored interventions: Disparities in barriers to MHSU based on gender, residence, occupation and psychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depression underscore the need for targeted interventions tailored to the specific needs of diverse demographic and clinical groups.

Scale utility: The BMHSUS provides a robust framework for future research and intervention planning, enabling a deeper understanding of barriers to MHSU in diverse settings.

Conclusion

This study confirms the BMHSUS as a reliable and culturally valid tool for assessing barriers to MHSU in Bangladesh. Barriers are widespread and primarily driven by social concerns, stigma and cultural beliefs, with marked disparities across gender, residence, religion, occupation and clinical status. These findings underscore the need for targeted, equity-oriented strategies to reduce stigma, improve access and guide evidence-based mental health policy and practice.
