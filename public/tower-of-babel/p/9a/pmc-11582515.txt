Augmentation of Tendon and Ligament Repair with Fiber‐Reinforced Hydrogel Composites
Authors: Robert N Kent, Alice H Huang, Brendon M Baker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review highlights the promise of fiber‐reinforced hydrogel composites (FRHCs) for augmenting tendon and ligament repair and regeneration. Composed of reinforcing fibers embedded in a hydrogel, these scaffolds provide both mechanical strength and a conducive microenvironment for biological processes required for connective tissue regeneration. Typical properties of FRHCs are discussed, highlighting their ability to simultaneously fulfill essential mechanical and biological design criteria for a regenerative scaffold. Furthermore, features of FRHCs are described that improve specific biological aspects of tendon healing including mesenchymal progenitor cell recruitment, early polarization to a pro‐regenerative immune response, tenogenic differentiation of recruited progenitor cells, and subsequent production of a mature, aligned collagenous matrix. Finally, the review offers a perspective on clinical translation of tendon FRHCs and outlines key directions for future work.Keywords: biomaterial scaffolds, fiber‐reinforced hydrogel composites, tendon regeneration, tenogenic differentiation, tissue engineering

Introduction

Tendons and ligaments are critical components of the musculoskeletal system, responsible for transmitting forces between muscles and bones or bone to bone, respectively. Unfortunately, they are prone to injury and degeneration, with nearly half of all musculoskeletal injuries reported in the US involving tendons or ligaments.[ 1 ] Moreover, current surgical and conservative treatment strategies result in comparably poor outcomes with regard to pain, diminished function, and elevated risk of re‐injury.[ 2 , 3 ] There is a clear need for better therapies for tendon and ligament injury, motivating the development of biomaterials designed to enhance tendon healing and improve long‐term function. Throughout this review, we will focus primarily on prior work investigating new biomaterials for tendon repair and regeneration, recognizing that many of these strategies should have direct relevance to ligaments, as well, given their structural and functional similarities.

The tendon repair field has widely recognized the need for influencing the biochemical milieu at the site of injury, motivating early strategies of injection or suture‐based delivery of drugs or biologics.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] However, these approaches failed to provide the spatiotemporally controlled cues necessary for regenerating the functional architecture of native tendon, essential to its mechanical performance.[ 8 , 9 ] To address this key structural requirement, initial strategies explored the use of woven fibrous scaffolds made of synthetic materials such as poly(glycolic acid) or poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA). Even though these woven scaffolds approached the mechanical properties of native tendon, their stiffness elicited adverse biological responses, including the promotion of chondrogenic or osteogenic trans‐differentiation in vivo.[ 10 ] Hydrogels have therefore recently gained popularity as they allow for fine tuning of the mechanical and biological features of the microenvironment that have been shown to enhance tenogenic differentiation in vitro.[ 11 , 12 ] However, these materials are typically orders of magnitude below the stiffness regime of native tendon and are therefore generally unsuitable for the mechanically demanding environment of a tendon injury site.

Fiber‐reinforced hydrogel composites (FRHCs), incorporating both polymeric fibers and hydrogel‐based materials, thus represent a promising approach to simultaneously provide the cellular and biomechanical features critical for tendon repair. By incorporating reinforcing fibers, such as those composed of collagen, silk, or synthetic materials into a hydrogel matrix, the resulting composite can provide improved tensile strength, stiffness, and toughness compared to hydrogels alone, while also providing tenogenic microenvironmental cues. Furthermore, incorporation of aligned fibers into these materials can mimic the aligned structure of native tendon tissue,[ 13 ] expedite tendon progenitor cell (TPC) recruitment,[ 14 , 15 ] promote tenogenic differentiation,[ 16 ] and template aligned matrix deposition that reflects the organization of native tendon.[ 17 ]

In this review, we discuss recent advances in the development of FRHCs for tendon repair. We explore various scaffold compositions and resulting mechanical properties, as well as the methods of fabrication and characterization of these composites. Additionally, we examine the in vitro and in vivo performance of these composites, including their ability to promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, as well as their efficacy in enhancing tendon healing and regeneration. Finally, we discuss the challenges and future directions for the development of FRHCs for tendon repair, including hurdles associated with clinical translation of these materials.

Biomaterials for Tendon Repair

Tendon tissue is composed of collagen (mostly type I, 65–80% dry mass) and elastin (1–2% dry mass) ensheathed in a small leucine‐rich proteoglycan (SLRP) ground substance predominantly comprised of decorin.[ 8 , 18 , 19 ] In healthy tendon, large, aligned collagen fibrils on the order of 100 nm are largely responsible for the tensile properties of the tissue, with human Achilles tendon exhibiting a failure strain of 80 MPa and a Young's modulus of 800–1000 MPa.[ 20 ] To restore these architectural and mechanical properties following tendon injury, it is critical that an implanted scaffold directs a regenerative rather than fibrotic cellular response.

The ideal biomaterial for tendon repair should therefore satisfy two major categories of design criteria: mechanical and biological. The first category involves the mechanical performance of the graft upon implantation, which has been the primary focus of early attempts at developing biomaterial scaffolds to augment load‐bearing, connective tissue repair.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] Properties like compressive/tensile strength, fatigue strength, resistance to creep deformation, and frictionless gliding against adjacent tissues are important for early restoration of the load bearing function of the tissue, especially in light of the known clinical benefits of early weightbearing and controlled range of motion.[ 27 , 28 ] However, despite achieving improved restoration of Achilles tendon function in the short term, these implants result in long‐term complications related to scaffold rupture and poor integration with the native tendon.[ 25 , 29 ] Problems with these fibrous scaffolds generally stemmed from neglecting biological design criteria, which limited the ability of the graft to integrate with the native tissue and encourage cell‐mediated remodeling of the scaffold into functional tendon‐like matrix over time. Consideration of factors like the capacity for cell recruitment, biochemical cues engendering tenogenic differentiation of recruited cells, and degradability required for cell‐mediated remodeling of the scaffold and matrix synthesis are essential to the long‐term performance of implants and the resulting formation of new tendon tissue that functions comparably to the native tendon.

Fiber‐Reinforced Hydrogel Composite Mechanics

FRHCs show great promise for achieving the biological goals of a tendon scaffold due to their highly tunable composition. For example, the bulk hydrogel component of an FRHC can be comprised of natural materials such as collagen or fibrin, which generally allow for enhanced cell‐mediated remodeling and better integration with the native tendon.[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] Alternatively, synthetic materials like poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG),[ 33 ] polyvinyl alcohol (PVA),[ 34 ] or functionalized dextran[ 14 ] require additional modifications to present appropriate microenvironmental cues driving integration and remodeling (e.g., cell‐degradable crosslinks and ligands enabling cell adhesion), but they allow for more precise tuning of these cues. Synthetic materials also pose the added benefit of improved scalability from a manufacturing perspective.

The fibrous component can similarly be formed from a range of natural or synthetic materials, and many innovative fabrication approaches have been recently established to achieve precise control over fiber geometry, mechanics, and overall network architecture in 3D (Table 1 ). Fibers as large as 300 µm in diameter (recapitulating the upper length‐scale of tendon fascicles) have been fabricated by crosslinking a polymer solution in a Teflon mold,[ 35 ] but diameters more akin to those of collagen fibrils (20–500 nm)[ 36 ] that hierarchically assemble to form fascicles can be achieved through techniques like solution electrospinning (SES),[ 16 , 37 ] melt electrowriting (MEW),[ 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 ] or meltblowing[ 42 ] (Table 1 and Figure 1 ). Topographical features like fiber anisotropy and crimp of the fibrous component can also be achieved either during fiber fabrication[ 16 , 43 , 44 ] or during crosslinking of the composite material.[ 13 , 34 , 45 ] In native tendon, the crimped morphology of collagen fibers imbues tendon with a non‐linear stress‐strain response, potentially shields fibers from mechanical damage,[ 46 ] and is thought to be responsible for the auxetic behavior of the tissue.[ ] While crimped fibers have not been explored in an FRHC, thermal treatment of poly‐L‐lactide fibers,[ 45 ] MEW with PCL,[ 48 ] and tuning hydrophilicity in electrospun DexVS fibers[ 44 ] have been used to achieve scaffolds with control over fiber crimp. Future work should therefore explore the impact of a crimped fibrous component on FRHC mechanics. Indeed, FRHCs enable myriad combinations of natural or synthetic bulk and fibrous components, providing an unprecedented level of tunability in these composite scaffolds toward engineering a tenogenic microenvironment.

While fiber reinforcement does improve the stiffness and strength of a hydrogel,[ 51 , 52 , 53 ] the mechanical properties of FRHCs are generally inferior compared to native tendon, likely due to the relatively low volume fraction composed of fibers. Most FRHCs developed for connective tissue repair applications have tensile moduli on the order of 100–101 MPa,[ 16 , 32 , 35 , 39 , 50 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 ] which pales in comparison to the tensile modulus of native tendon (102–103 MPa)[ 59 , 60 ] (Figure 2 ). No et al. recently described a composite of a PVA/gelatin hydrogel with ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene fibers possessing a tensile modulus of ≈1.2 GPa and yield strength of ≈80 MPa, comparable to that of native tendon.[ 34 , 60 ] This was achieved in part due to the interpenetrating network formed within PEGDM fibers when encapsulated in a PEGDM hydrogel.[ 34 ] Despite these initial mechanical properties, however, constructs explanted from injured rat patellar tendons after 8 weeks exhibited moduli and strengths of 10 MPa and 750 kPa, respectively, at the scaffold‐tendon interface, mirroring the previously reported failure mechanism observed in tendons repaired with purely fibrous scaffolds (Table 1).[ 25 ] Despite their mechanical shortcomings, all of the above FRHCs were able to encourage cell infiltration, tenogenic differentiation, and aligned collagen deposition, supporting the biological efficacy of these constructs. Therefore, the clinical indication of FRHCs for tendon repair may need to be restricted to serve as an adjunct to suture repair, for example, to obviate the demand for mechanical performance immediately upon implantation. As the field moves forward, we will need to weigh the pros and cons of tendon‐mimetic mechanical properties upon implantation versus following a period of scaffold‐augmented healing.

A final tradeoff to consider from a material perspective is the strategy of implantation, as this greatly impacts interfacial strength with the adjacent tendon tissue. While many of the proposed fabrication methods require that a whole construct be formed, implanted as a solid, and sutured to the adjacent tendon, a subset of FRHCs can be crosslinked in situ.[ 14 , 61 , 62 ] These constructs are typically formed by introducing a crosslinking molecule to a solution of bulk hydrogel backbone containing a suspension of cell‐adhesive fibers (Figure 2). In addition to facilitating percutaneous delivery, in situ crosslinking allows the FRHC to conform and in some cases adhere to the irregular geometry of a ruptured tendon stub, theoretically expediting cell recruitment and tissue integration. Injectable FRHCs cannot achieve the level of control over 3D fibrous architecture achievable for those fabricated prior to implantation, but topographical anisotropy of in situ polymerized FRHCs can still be tuned to a degree (Figure 2). For example, Hiraki et al. incorporated superparamagnetic nanoparticles within the fibrous component of an FRHC to achieve topographical anisotropy during in situ gelation under a 650 mT magnetic field.[ 13 ] The fiber alignment attained through this method promoted spreading and co‐alignment of embedded murine tendon progenitor cells in vitro, establishing promising evidence that magnetic alignment of fibers during in situ crosslinking may be adequate for driving the formation of organized and anisotropic tissue during tendon healing. It is important to note, however, that few existing FRHCs are injectable. Solid constructs fabricated prior to implantation comprise the bulk of the tendon FRHC literature, given the relative ease of controlling 3D topographical organization of the fibrous component. However, these constructs require suturing to the adjacent tendon and tend to suffer from poor interfacial strength after ≥2 months of healing.[ 30 , 34 ] A particularly innovative approach toward achieving a strong scaffold‐tendon interface over time was demonstrated by Qu et al., who showed that PLGA microsphere‐mediated release of collagenase resulted in better tissue‐scaffold integration through partial degradation of the tendon stub.[ 63 ]

Engineered Cues for Effective Tenogenesis and Cell‐Mediated Repair

The ideal tendon healing response involves four key phases: 1) the immediate immune cell response and subsequent inflammation, 2) progenitor cell recruitment/proliferation, 3) tenogenic differentiation of recruited progenitors, and 4) extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling.[ 8 ] A hematoma forms immediately following tendon rupture, then in the subsequent hours to days, neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages invade the resulting fibrin clot to phagocytose dead cells and damaged ECM proteins, at the same time releasing pro‐inflammatory and chemotactic factors.[ 9 ] The stromal cell recruitment phase occurs over the first 2 weeks following injury, during which TPCs and other mesenchymal progenitors migrate into the defect. Many putative tissue origins and lineages have been reported to contribute to this progenitor pool,[ 65 , 66 , 67 ] with the tendon fascicles, epitenon, and paratenon (largely perivascular) all previously demonstrated to contain progenitor cells that can activate upon injury, hone to the injury site, and participate in the repair process.[ 66 , 68 , 69 , 70 ] Recruited TPCs differentiate and synthesize a provisional matrix predominately composed of type III collagen,[ 9 ] then over the subsequent weeks to months, type I collagen is secreted and remodeled to form the neotendon.[ 8 , 9 ] Unfortunately, this remodeling often leads to the formation of relatively isotropic, hypercellular, hypervascularized scar tissue that has been associated with chronic pain and increased risk of re‐rupture.[ 71 ] Indeed, the Achilles epitenon has been shown to house distinct populations of progenitors that are biased toward either a scleraxis (Scx)+ tenogenic (pro‐regenerative) or α‐smooth muscle actin+ myofibroblastic (pro‐fibrotic) phenotype[ 69 ] (Figure 3 ). The mechanobiological, topographical, soluble, and immunomodulatory cues potentially presented by an FRHC would be critical in appropriately guiding this fate switch to optimally impact the resulting composition, mechanical properties, and function of the regenerated tissue. To address aberrant scar tissue formation in the endogenous tendon repair process, the biological goals of a tendon FRHC are highlighted in the following sections.

Typical versus ideal tendon wound healing response. Following injury, a persistent pro‐inflammatory response occurs, whereby M1‐like macrophages invade the wound site and secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α and interleukin (IL)−1. Myofibroblastic differentiation of invading stromal progenitors results in scar tissue formation in lieu of functional tissue regeneration. Ideally, early polarization to an M2‐like phenotype and tenogenic differentiation of stromal progenitors would lead to more complete tendon regeneration.

Tendon Progenitor Cell Recruitment

A FRHC therapeutic for improving tendon healing must either deliver or efficiently recruit the TPCs required for synthesizing, remodeling, and maintaining the regenerated tissue. Cell implantation may be a promising approach, with seminal work in this field showing that implanting a pre‐contracted, mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)‐laden collagen construct in a rabbit Achilles tendon defect resulted in improved biomechanical and structural properties of the regenerated tissue.[ 72 , 73 , 74 ] Moreover, in vitro pre‐culture of tissue grafts affords opportunities for priming cells with tenogenic cues (e.g., growth factors or uniaxial cyclic stretch).[ 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 ] In FRHCs, cells can be encapsulated stochastically[ 16 ] or patterned via pre‐adhesion to the fibrous component.[ 32 , 35 ] Pre‐cellularized scaffolds offer distinct advantages, especially in older patients where age‐related changes result in diminished TPC abundance or differentiation potential and increased TPC senescence.[ 81 ] Additionally, the use of autologous cells in these patients is hindered by the rapid phenotypic drift that occurs during in vitro expansion of human tenocytes and TPCs, and there are numerous regulatory hurdles and significantly higher costs inherent to cell‐based therapies.[ 82 , 83 ] For younger patients with relatively potent TPC pools, and until viable strategies are established for generating universal donor sources of mesenchymal or induced pluripotent stem cells that can be differentiated into TPCs or tenocytes,[ 84 ] acellular biomaterial strategies that can effectively recruit (i.e., drive migration and proliferation of) endogenous TPCs to the injury site are needed.

FRHCs allow for modular inclusion of topographical and soluble cues to encourage TPC migration into the injury site. Inclusion of fibrous topography has been shown to drive TPC migration and proliferation into both natural and synthetic hydrogels,[ 14 , 15 ] likely through contact guidance effects whereby cells sense and respond to the anisotropic mechanics and ligand presentation conferred by a fibrillar matrix[ 85 ] (Figure 4 ). For example, Xie et al. developed a collagen/poly(lactic acid) hybrid fibrous scaffold that improved proliferation of rat rotator cuff and Achilles tendon‐derived cells via incorporation of a cell‐adhesive collagen yarn.[ 15 ] Additionally, our own studies examining FRHCs composed of non‐degradable, cell‐adhesive dextran vinyl sulfone fibers embedded in a bulk hydrogel also formed from dextran vinyl sulfone, but crosslinked with protease‐cleavable peptides, demonstrated that contact guidance cues enhance TPC migration from the epitenon of mouse Achilles tendons[ 14 ] (Figure 4). The impact of this pro‐migratory cue may be further improved with an aligned fibrous topography. While the relationship between fiber alignment and TPC recruitment has not been studied directly in FRHCs, fibrillar matrix alignment is well‐established in other cell types to influence the organization of focal adhesions and dictate cell polarity, thereby directing cell migration.[ 86 , 87 ]

Tunable physical microenvironmental features of an FRHC can address key phases required for proper tendon healing. Tunable crosslink density and degradability, in addition to fiber‐guided cell migration, can be used to control the recruitment of TPCs. Fiber composition and anisotropy can influence macrophage polarization. Finally, aligned, cell‐adhesive fibrous topography drives tenogenic specification and templates deposition and maturation of aligned, de novo collagen fibrils.

Chemotactic and mitogenic cues can also be incorporated and delivered by FRHCs to recruit endogenous TPCs to the repair site. Previous work has shown that transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β1,[ 89 , 90 ] platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF)‐AB,[ 63 ] PDGF‐BB,[ 91 ] connective tissue growth factor (CTGF),[ 92 , 93 ] stromal‐derived cell factor (SDF)−1,[ 94 ] and periostin[ 30 ] can encourage migration and proliferation of TPCs or MSCs. In terms of biomaterial delivery applications, the most direct approach has been to mix soluble factors into a hydrogel solution prior to crosslinking.[ 90 , 95 ] While straightforward to implement, this strategy typically results in a rapid and transient release of chemotactic factors over the course of a few days. Recognizing the advantages of sustained or delayed delivery of chemotactic cues that promote TPC recruitment over the 2 weeks following injury, prior work has also explored incorporation of micro‐ and nano‐scale delivery vehicles toward this end. Tarafder et al. used a double‐emulsion technique to encapsulate CTGF in PLGA microspheres, which they 3D printed into polycaprolactone microfibers to form an implantable mesh. Gradual hydrolytic degradation of these microspheres following implantation yielded sustained delivery of CTGF to a rat rotator cuff defect, promoting TPC recruitment and resulting in a strong fibrocartilaginous interface at the enthesis.[ 93 ] Furthermore, Hettiaratchi et al. recently demonstrated the utility heparin‐based delivery vehicles for sustained release of a wide range of heparin‐binding soluble factors.[ 96 , 97 , 98 , 99 , 100 ] Our group successfully applied this heparin‐based soluble factor delivery technology to achieve TPC recruitment into a synthetic FRHC via sustained release of PDGF‐BB (Figure 1E).[ 14 ] Given the modular nature of FRHCs, chemokines or chemokine‐laden delivery vehicles can be incorporated into the fibrous and/or bulk components of an FRHC to deliver these critical, early pro‐migratory cues to the tendon injury site.

Driving Tenogenic Differentiation

To promote robust tendon regeneration, a key requirement of the engineered microenvironment within an FRHC is its ability to promote tenogenic differentiation of recruited cells. Despite the extremely high stiffness of native tendon at the tissue‐scale, TPCs and MSCs are sensitive to matrix elasticity and tend to favor tenogenic differentiation in or on softer materials.[ 49 , 101 ] Islam et al. seeded human MSCs onto compacted collagen sheets of varying stiffness, finding that 100 MPa substrates resulted in upregulation of chondrogenic and osteogenic markers while softer (1 and 10 MPa) substrates yielded upregulation of SCX and COL1A1.[ 49 ] Stiffer microenvironments have also been strongly associated with myofibroblastic differentiation, with previous work implicating more efficient activation of latent TGF‐β1[ 102 ] and elevated Rho‐associated kinase signaling.[ 103 , 104 ] Together, these findings suggest that the bulk substance of an FRHC should possess relatively low stiffness (likely lower than that of native tendon) to effectively support tenogenic differentiation of recruited or delivered cells.

FRHCs can provide anisotropic, cell‐scale physical cues provided by fibers that can be tuned independently of the bulk stiffness, allowing for uniaxial cell spreading and organization without the drawback of aberrant trans‐differentiation common to stiffer, purely fibrous scaffolds.[ 10 ] Fibrous topography is typically required to achieve an elongated morphology in recruited cells; furthermore, a spindle morphology is characteristic of tenocytes in healthy tendon, and appears to be necessary for a mesenchymal progenitor cell to undergo tenogenic differentiation in post‐developmental contexts.[ 105 , 106 ] Collective alignment of the fibrous topography is thought to be another critical differentiation cue within a tendon FRHC. While many groups have provided evidence supporting a pro‐tenogenic effect of topographical anisotropy on 2D substrates and in purely fibrous 3D settings,[ 11 , 49 , 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 , 109 , 110 ] our recent work suggests that fiber alignment in a synthetic FRHC minimally influences tenogenic differentiation but instead drives aligned de novo collagen deposition.[ 111 ] While cells likely engage multiple fibers simultaneously in pure fibrous 2D and 3D environments, we suspect that engagement to a single fiber in an FRHC is sufficient to encourage a spindle morphology, regardless of collective organization of the fibers. Nonetheless, several prior studies have utilized aligned fibrous components in tendon FRHCs (although variations in alignment were not explored), and the presence of aligned topography in an FRHC appears to be compatible with robust tenogenesis in each case.[ 16 , 34 , 112 , 113 ] As such, at least from a cell recruitment and matrix remodeling perspective, alignment of the fibrous topography appears to be a fundamental requirement for a tendon FRHC.

Aside from static mechanical properties of the construct, uniaxial cyclic strain has been established as another important physical mediator of tenogenic differentiation. Some degree of loading on the tendon during healing is needed for appropriate anabolic matrix remodeling that supports restoration of tensile strength of the repaired tissue.[ 27 , 114 ] In vitro and in vivo models have also demonstrated the importance of externally applied load in upregulating tenogenic genes during healing[ 75 , 76 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 115 ] and development.[ 116 , 117 ] For example, using a bioartificial tendon formed from murine MSCs cultured in a collagen hydrogel between two rigid posts, Scott et al. showed that subjecting these constructs to 10% cyclic strain at 0.1 Hz for 2 h per day resulted in the upregulation of Scx and Col1a1.[ 76 ] Successful mechanical integration of an FRHC with adjacent tendon stubs following implantation or in situ gelation would be critical to transmitting dynamic mechanical tensile cues to embedded or recruited cells during healing. Suture fixation is generally employed for solid scaffolds,[ 34 , 112 ] but other approaches have been explored for tendon tissue engineering. Freedman et al. developed an interpenetrating network of alginate, polyacrylamide, and chitosan that can adhere to wet tendon intraoperatively and interpenetrate the native ECM over time.[ 118 ] Our group employed a Michael‐type addition chemistry that proceeds between a vinyl sulfonated dextran backbone and a dithiolated peptide crosslinker[ 14 ]; as this chemistry is not selective to exogenous peptides, covalent bonds also form between the in situ crosslinked gel and thiol‐containing proteins in the ECM of the adjacent tissue.

Pro‐tenogenic soluble cues can be delivered by FRHCs via the same approaches outlined above for chemokine release. Myriad growth factors including TGF‐β1,[ 119 , 120 ] 2,[ 77 , 121 ] and 3;[ 77 , 93 ] insulin growth factor 1;[ 119 ] growth differentiation factor 5;[ 121 ] PDGF‐AA;[ 65 ] PDGF‐BB;[ 122 , 123 ] bone morphogenic protein 12;[ 119 ] CTGF;[ 124 ] and basic fibroblast growth factor[ 119 ] have been shown to improve tenogenic differentiation of TPCs and MSCs in previous work. Inorganic, soluble tenogenic cues have also been investigated, with No et al. incorporating strontium‐hardystonite (Sr‐HT) nanoparticles into the bulk of a tendon FRHC.[ 34 ] Although Sr‐HT delivery did not increase tenogenic expression, this work introduces a potential means of supplementing growth factors with inorganic soluble cues toward more robust tenogenesis (Figure 4).

Templating Mature and Organized Collagenous Matrix Deposition

Although FRHCs can achieve low stiffness regimes that are conducive to tenogenic differentiation, their inadequate mechanical properties relative to native tendon also necessitate the rapid deposition and organization of de novo collagen to achieve functional mechanical properties of the regenerated neotendon. Compared to native tendon, where fibrils exceeding 100 nm in diameter comprise ≈50% of its dry mass, restoration of native fibrillar ultrastructure is limited following tendon injury, such that fibers >100 nm in diameter are completely absent, even after 14 months of healing.[ 20 ] To restore a fibrillar architecture more similar to native tendon, Wang et al. showed that periostin delivery with a collagen scaffold drove the formation of larger collagen fibrils.[ 30 ] In addition to biochemical cues, physical cues that support the production and maturation of collagen fibrils and template collagen alignment are likely critical microenvironmental design parameters for effective tendon FRHCs (Figure 4).

The anisotropic topography of aligned FRHCs is crucial to the templating of aligned matrix deposition from implanted or recruited TPCs. Isotropic hydrogels such as fibrin have been shown to enable the deposition of type I collagen,[ 125 , 126 ] but subsequent alignment of this matrix is believed to require externally applied loads and gradual, cell‐mediated matrix remodeling.[ 127 ] Cells in anisotropic fibrous scaffolds and FRHCs containing aligned fibers, on the other hand, excel at generating aligned collagenous ECM independently of externally applied loads.[ 16 , 17 , 34 ] For example, the fiber‐reinforced PVA hydrogel designed by No. et al contained densely aligned, mature collagen fibrils after 8 weeks of implantation in a rat patellar tendon defect.[ 34 ] As an adjunct to the uniaxial strain imposed on an implanted construct in vivo, aligned topographical cues likely further enhance matrix production and remodeling.

Fiber density in a synthetic FRHC was recently shown to increase type I collagen deposition by tendon progenitor cells in a dose‐dependent fashion.[ 111 ] However, this study also noted diminishing returns in alignment of de novo collagen above 2.5 vol/vol% fibers. Fiber diameter and mechanical properties may also influence matrix deposition and remodeling, but this relationship likely varies with cell source and phenotype. Two groups utilized SES to generate aligned PLGA fibers of varying diameter and studied the resulting behavior of seeded cells; while El Khatib et al. observed higher expression of type I collagen and tenomodulin in ovine amniotic epithelial stem cells on 1.3 versus 2.5 µm fibers,[ 108 ] Erisken et al. saw the opposite in end‐differentiated, human rotator cuff tenocytes on 0.7 versus 1.8 µm fibers.[ 128 ] Future work is needed to evaluate the interplay between fiber properties, expression of matrix proteins and critical mediators of remodeling like tenomodulin, and maturation and organization of de novo collagenous matrices within FRHCs.

Immunomodulation

Crosstalk between stromal and immune cells, particularly macrophages, plays a key role in cell differentiation and matrix remodeling responses during connective tissue repair.[ 129 , 130 , 131 , 132 , 133 ] This topic, as it pertains to both tendinopathy and tendon healing, has been extensively reviewed elsewhere.[ 134 , 135 ] Briefly, macrophages, which exist on a spectrum spanning M1 (pro‐inflammatory) to M2 (pro‐regenerative) phenotypes, can be described as either “M1‐like” or “M2‐like” with regard to their expression of relevant signals guiding stromal cell behavior during tendon healing.[ 136 ] Immediately following tendon injury in humans, M1‐like macrophages invade the wound site to degrade ECM, phagocytose debris, and secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines cause tendon fibroblasts to secrete additional pro‐inflammatory cytokines, upregulate matrix metalloproteases, and downregulate genes associated with matrix production.[ 137 ] In adult mice, at ≈4 weeks following injury, M2‐like macrophages begin to predominate, which secrete anti‐inflammatory cytokinesin addition to promoting stromal cell proliferationand matrix production.[ 138 , 139 ] However, at this point in the healing process, increased TGF‐β1 production by M2‐like macrophages may contribute to scar tissue formation and heterotopic ossification.[ 132 , 140 ] Recently, Arvind et al. found that neonatal C57BL/6 mice, which exhibit more complete tendon regeneration compared to their adult counterparts, show increased secretion of pro‐M2 cytokines at 3 days post‐injury, with markedly elevated M2‐like macrophages at the wound site by 14 days.[ 141 ] Collectively, these studies suggest that, while an early inflammatory response is necessary for robust tendon healing, the repair process may be improved by encouraging rapid polarization to an M2‐like phenotype (Figure 3).

FRHCs can influence macrophage polarization through a range of encoded microenvironmental cues. Although this interaction has not been studied directly in FRHCs designed for tendon repair, specific physical and biochemical features of hydrogels and pure fibrous scaffolds have been previously established for promoting M2 polarization. In hydrogels, for example, softer materials tend to favor M2 polarization. Srindharan et al. cultured THP‐1‐derived macrophages in type I collagen‐functionalized polyacrylamide gels of varying stiffness, showing that stiffer (323 kPa) and softer (< 88 kPa) substrates drove M1 vs. M2 polarization, respectively.[ 142 ] Increased fibril density in collagen gels is also associated with M1 polarization of macrophages, although the relative contributions of increased fibril density and concomitant increases in substrate stiffness remains uncharacterized.[ 143 ] Using electrospun poly(L‐lactic acid) scaffolds, Cai et al. found that inclusion of silk fibroin fibers drove M2 polarization in vitro and also improved biomechanical properties of rat Achilles tendons at 3 and 6 months post‐injury[ 144 ] (Figure 4). The anisotropic mechanical cues provided by fibers may help macrophages adopt a spindle morphology, which is also associated with an M2‐like phenotype.[ 145 ] Collective fiber alignment has not been shown to directly influence macrophage polarization, but it can desensitize tenocytes to pro‐inflammatory cytokines secreted by M1‐like macrophages, potentially posing an additional, pro‐regenerative role of aligned topography in FRHCs[ 146 ] (Figure 4). It should also be noted that many of the soluble factor delivery strategies outlined above can be applied to cytokine delivery for temporally controlled macrophage polarization. This application has not been studied for tendon regeneration, but Spiller et al. utilized biotinylated, decellularized bone scaffolds to achieve sequential delivery of M1‐ followed by M2‐polarizing cytokines, enhancing vascularization of bone scaffolds in vivo.[ 147 ] A similar, sequential polarization from an M1‐ to an M2‐like macrophage phenotype may be applicable to FRHCs designed for tendon repair as well.

Conclusion and Future Outlook

FRHCs offer a promising strategy to positively influence the innate, adult tendon healing response by effectively recruiting tendon progenitor cells, dynamically modulating the immune environment, driving tenogenic differentiation of recruited progenitors, and templating aligned, collagenous matrix deposition. The tunable and modular integration of mechanical, topographical, and soluble cues within FRHCs significantly heightens their overall promise for effectively guiding cell behavior throughout multiple phases of tissue repair. Moreover, the potential for in situ crosslinking of FRHCs offers advantages in terms of percutaneous delivery and adaptation to the irregular geometry of ruptured tendon stubs.

However, there are numerous translational hurdles that must be overcome prior to clinical application of FRHCs for tendon regeneration. The mechanical properties of FRHCs, while advantageous with respect to the biological goals of tendon repair, remain mechanically inferior compared to native tendon. Balancing the trade‐off between mechanical performance and biological effectiveness is therefore a key consideration in the continued development and clinical application of FRHCs. Mass transport poses another important challenge. FRHCs have been validated in vitro and in small animal models, but the length scales of unrepaired human tendon defects would likely require the use of porogens or pre‐vascularized constructs to ensure sufficient nutrient delivery and waste exchange. These limitations may necessitate a more focused application (e.g., as an adjunct to suture repair or with limb immobilization), rather than a standalone solution for mechanically demanding scenarios.

Delivery of RNA molecules, such as small interfering RNA or messenger RNA, has been shown to modulate gene expression levels associated with inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cell proliferation, addressing several key aspects of tendon healing.[ 148 , 149 , 150 ] However, RNA therapy also faces several challenges, including delivery efficiency, off‐target effects, and potential immune responses. Given that ensuring effective delivery of RNA molecules to target cells within the tendon while minimizing systemic side effects remains a significant hurdle, future work should explore incorporation of RNA therapy into FRHCs given its potential for localized delivery and additional control over these biological processes.

The future of FRHCs for tendon repair holds great promise for addressing the shortcomings of current treatment strategies. However, ongoing research is needed to 1) further optimize cell recruitment; 2) incorporate timed soluble factory deliver vehicles for immunomodulation, stromal cell recruitment, and tenogenic differentiation; 3) identify and deliver physical and biochemical cues that promote tenogenesis while preventing myofibrogenesis; 4) improve integration with the native tendon; and 5) explore effective routes toward clinical translation. As this promising class of biomaterials continues to rapidly evolve and improve, we are optimistic that FRHCs offer new avenues for improving patient outcomes following tendon injury.
