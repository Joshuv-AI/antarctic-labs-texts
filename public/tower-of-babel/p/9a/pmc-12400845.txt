Upfront use of temporal myofascial flap for repair of traumatic injury and prevention of CSF leaks: illustrative cases
Authors: Aliana N Rao, Samuel L Ricci, Sami Pathak, Theodore Hannah, Robert Ziechmann, Philip Villanueva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTraumatic brain injury involving destruction of the mastoid bone leading to a large defect in the middle fossa floor or posterior fossa dura can necessitate delayed or multistep repairs or reconstruction of the middle fossa floor because of a CSF leak and rhinorrhea. Delayed repair may be associated with CNS infection. Reconstruction using a temporalis myofascial flap has been described in delayed reconstruction cases with CSF leak. The authors report a novel concept of upfront repair.OBSERVATIONSTwo patients with gunshot wound injuries involving the mastoid and middle fossa floor or posterior fossa dura underwent decompression and simultaneous early reconstruction with a temporalis myofascial flap sutured to the middle fossa dura. Both patients avoided prolonged CSF leak and had no complications from this approach.LESSONSUpfront repair of traumatic defects of the middle fossa floor or posterior fossa dura involving the mastoid air cells may avoid a prolonged CSF leak and its complications. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25412 Keywords: temporal myofascial flap, CSF leak, myoplasty, case reportABBREVIATIONS: GCS = Glasgow Coma Scale, GSW = gunshot wound, ICP = intracranial pressure, OR = operating room

Up to 20% of gunshot wounds (GSWs) to the head involve the temporal bone and mastoid.1–4 If the projectile breaches the middle fossa dura, CSF otorrhea or rhinorrhea may ensue. Although minor leaks can seal spontaneously, defects larger than 5 mm, multiple defects, and those involving the tegmen require operative repair.5–9 Rapid identification of a CSF leak and management are important to prevent what can be a fatal infection.10,11 Most research regarding myofascial flaps reports the use of the technique after the first operation as a means of addressing a subsequent CSF leak.7,12,13 However, there are no cases in the literature to date reporting upfront use of the temporal myofascial flap at the time of the initial surgery.

Among various options, a pedicled temporalis myofascial flap offers unique advantages for ballistic skull base defects because of its flexibility, the muscle’s rich blood supply, and the lack of denervation during transfer.14–16 Transposition through the mastoid or zygomatic root delivers ample bulk to obliterate dead space, buttress dural repairs, and deliver immunocompetent tissue to a field heavily contaminated by bone fragments and environmental debris.17,18 Contemporary data supporting early, single-stage reconstruction following traumatic head injuries have emerged, showing that it shortens hospital stays and does not increase infection, provided that well-vascularized tissue is interposed between the cranial cavity and aerodigestive tract.19–22 One case series using upfront temporalis coverage for skull base defects reported low rates of persistent CSF leakage or intracranial infection and very low donor site morbidity.17

A temporalis myofascial flap is created through rotating the muscle inferiorly and suturing it directly to the dural edges of the middle fossa floor or native posterior fossa dura, as applicable.23 An advantage of this techniques is the formation of an immediate watertight closure that can prevent or limit CSF leaks and infections.23 This technique is prophylactic against CSF leaks and diminishes return visits to the operating room (OR) for leaks and/or infection.12

Our case series demonstrates the use of this technique in two GSW victims. We describe their presenting pathology, the operative experience, and their outcomes.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 35-year-old female presented after being shot multiple times, with one trajectory involving the mastoid and the middle fossa floor on the left (Fig. 1). The patient presented with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15 and no focal neurological deficit. Initial head CT imaging showed no surgical lesion, a small left temporal hemorrhagic contusion, and a thin left convexity subdural hemorrhage, with only 3 mm of rightward midline shift. The patient required intubation and sedation for emergency repair of a left brachial arterial injury by the trauma team. A right frontal intracranial pressure (ICP) monitor was placed as there was no examination to follow at this point. ICP progressively increased, triggering repeat imaging showing increasing edema and midline shift > 5 mm. This prompted us to perform a craniectomy and create a temporal myofascial flap, given the middle fossa floor defect. Two grams of cefazolin was prophylactically administered, which is consistent with the antibiotic regimen at our institution for cranial cases.

Axial (left) and coronal (right) CT scans of the head demonstrating the left temporal bullet entry site.

A standard trauma hemicraniectomy flap was made. We found a defect in the temporal and middle fossa floor from the injury, unroofing multiple mastoid air cells. The air cells were waxed. Given that the middle fossa floor was comminuted, the decision was made for a temporalis myofascial flap. The temporalis muscle fascia was sewn to the middle fossa floor dura with a simple running Prolene suture.

The patient’s postoperative course was uncomplicated, and ICP was easily managed after craniectomy.

At the 3-month follow-up, the myofascial flap was well functioning without any wound dehiscence, infection, or CSF leak. The patient had right-sided weakness that persisted after discharge, but speech is relatively clear and fluent.

Case 2

A 34-year-old man presented after being shot in the right side of the head, with the entrance wound just behind the ear (Fig. 2). The projectile fractured the mastoid bone and posterior portion of the mastoid sinus with in-driven bone fragments but remained in the epidural space. On presentation, the patient had a GCS score of 8 and was promptly intubated. The right pupil was 6 mm and sluggishly reactive, whereas the left pupil was 4 mm and briskly reactive. CT angiography demonstrated traumatic occlusion of the right transverse sinus, sigmoid sinus, and jugular bulb as well as a complex comminuted fracture of the right mastoid bone. Given the sinus involvement on imaging, diagnostic angiography was performed to evaluate the venous sinuses and cerebral arterial vasculature prior to open debridement. The patient was then taken to the OR on hospital day 2 for debridement of the open depressed fracture. On the day of surgery, a CSF leak was evident as CSF otorrhea.

Axial (left) and coronal (right) CT scans of the head demonstrating the right retromastoid entry site.

The patient was brought to the OR and had a lumbar drain placed in the L4–5 space preoperatively as an additional precaution given the preoperative leak. One gram of vancomycin was prophylactically administered. We elected for this over our typical antibiotic, cefazolin, given the lumbar drain insertion immediately before the procedure.

A lateral suboccipital craniectomy was performed. Bone fragments, dural bone margins, and necrotic cerebellar tissue were removed carefully. Vancomycin powder and wax were packed into the open mastoid air cells to minimize infection and prevent leakage. The temporalis muscle was mobilized for the myofascial flap and tacked down to the intact posterior fossa dura with a Prolene suture. The defect was additionally sealed with dural sealant.

Again, the postoperative course was uncomplicated, and there was no further CSF leak.

At the 2-month follow-up, there was no CSF leak. The patient has a left homonymous hemianopsia from his injury, but no other complications from the surgery.

Technique

The temporalis muscle is incised vertically and detached superiorly to expose the dura and middle fossa floor (Fig. 3). After completion of the intracranial work and securing of the dural closure, the temporalis myofascial flap is repositioned into the defect. The muscle edges are then sutured directly to the floor of the middle fossa dura, ensuring a secure fixation. Through this, we are able to use the temporalis muscle to cover the mastoid defect and provide protection against a CSF leak.

Temporalis myofascial flap technique during craniectomy. A: The temporalis is divided with a single vertical incision (red dashed line) to preserve the anterior portion for reconstruction in eventual cranioplasty. B: The temporalis is elevated and a craniectomy is performed. C: Suturing of the posterior pedicle of the temporalis muscle flap over the middle fossa floor defect, with attachment to the remaining middle fossa floor dura.

Discussion

Observations

We present a novel approach of utilizing a temporal myofascial flap during the initial decompressive craniectomy in patients with traumatic GSW injuries to the head. Implementing this technique at the time of the initial surgery may reduce the need for return trips to the OR for subsequent reconstruction.

Upfront temporalis myofascial coverage is known to provide a watertight barrier to prevent CSF leaks in other contexts. Kawsar et al. documented a 0% CSF leak rate in six high‑risk skull base tumor resections when they rotated the flap at the index surgery.12 We extend that same principle to ballistic trauma: in GSWs that pulverize the mastoid and lacerate dura, we immediately rotate the temporalis muscle to buttress the lateral skull base, close dural rents, and restore contour, thereby combining watertight reconstruction, structural support, and preservation of dynamic facial function without increasing infection risk.18,24

Surgeons usually treat middle cranial fossa fractures without using the immediate temporalis myofascial flap, reserving it for secondary repair if a postoperative CSF leak appears. We take a proactive approach: during the initial debridement, we mobilize the temporalis muscle, advance its tendon, and anchor it to the orbicularis oris, creating a vascularized barrier that aims to prevent CSF egress and eliminate the need for later reconstruction.25

Unlike free gracilis flaps, the temporalis myofascial flap requires no microvascular vascular anastomosis because the temporalis muscle remains attached to its native blood supply.26,27 Eliminating an anastomosis removes the risk of flap thrombosis and the need for labor-intensive postoperative monitoring, thereby shortening operative time and reducing ICU or step-down resource use. These practical advantages make upfront repair with the temporalis myofascial flap an appealing alternative for patients who cannot tolerate prolonged surgery or the high surveillance demands of free flap reconstruction.23,24

Given the adaptability of the temporalis muscle to act as a muscle or fascial flap, its use in middle fossa procedures with concern for CSF leakage is highly advantageous.17,28 The temporalis muscle and its fascia can be used in multilayered closure techniques, which are associated with higher success rates in CSF leak repair compared with single-layer repairs.5 The muscle or fascia acts as a robust barrier, reinforcing the closure of dural defects and reducing the risk of persistent CSF leakage.

Potential complications include, but are not limited to, wound infection, CSF leakage, seroma, herniation, and seizure. However, both patients in this case series had no complications following repair with their temporal myofascial flap. This aligns with previously reported low complication rates associated with this technique.17,28

Lessons

Currently, there are no cases in which the temporalis myofascial flap is used for traumatic injuries in the middle fossa. Upfront use of the temporalis myofascial flap offers an avenue for patients to achieve both surgical decompression and reconstruction within the same operation, thus limiting infection risks and return visits to the OR.
