EZB‐type diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma cell lines have superior migration capabilities compared to MCD‐type
Authors: Marwa Sherif, Hendrik Schäfer, Sonja Scharf, Vivienne van Oostendorp, Aresu Sadeghi Shoreh Deli, Andreas G Loth, Matthieu Piel, Martin‐Leo Hansmann, Thomas Oellerich, Falko Fend, Leticia Quintanilla‐Martinez, Sylvia Hartmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDiffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) represents the most prevalent aggressive B‐cell lymphoma. The group is heterogeneous and the outcome is variable. A variety of approaches have been employed with the objective of improving the stratification of DLBCL patients according to their prognosis, based on the cell of origin. Recently, distinct genetic subtypes of DLBCL have been identified. Given the importance of cell migration in immune cells, the objective of this study was to ascertain whether different genetic subtypes of DLBCL exhibit disparate migration abilities. MCD‐ and EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines were subjected to testing to ascertain their basal velocity in straight microchannels and their ability to overcome tight constrictions of 2 μm. The EZB‐type cell lines showed superior basal migration velocity and constriction passage time, and a similar trend was observed in live cell imaging of native human DLBCL tissue. In addition, MCD‐type DLBCL exhibited significantly elevated levels of nuclear lamin A/C, which is responsible for the stiffness of the nuclear envelope and could thus explain the disparate migration behaviours observed among these subtypes. Our study suggests that different genetic subtypes of DLBCL may not only influence the outcome after therapy but also the motility of the tumour cells.Keywords: diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, microchannel, migration

INTRODUCTION

Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most frequent form of B‐cell Non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. It can be classified into the activated B‐cell‐like (ABC) and germinal centre B‐cell‐like (GCB) subtypes, which are categorized by their cell of origin. 1 In ABC DLBCL, autoantigens drive BCR‐dependent activation of NF‐κB. 2 , 3 The prognosis for patients with DLBCL varies depending on the subtype, particularly patients with the GCB subtype have shown a more favourable prognosis compared to those with the ABC subtype. 4 , 5 Additionally, individual patient factors, disease stage and genetic mutations significantly influence prognosis and treatment decisions. 6 In 2018, distinct genetic subtypes of DLBCL were identified. 7 , 8 These corresponded to either 5 clusters C1–C5 8 or four specifically named subtypes 7 : MCD, which includes MYD88 and CD79B mutations; BN2, which encompasses BCL6 and NOTCH2 mutations; N1, characterized by NOTCH1 mutations; and EZB, based on EZH2 mutation and BCL2 translocation. In 2020, two more subtypes were defined 9 : A53, associated with TP53 mutation and deletion, and ST2 with SGK1 and TET2 mutations. MCD and N1 are dominant in ABC DLBCL, while EZB and ST2 are dominant in GCB DLBCL. The prognosis of different genetic subtypes varies; for instance, MCD has a worse prognosis than the EZB type. 9

Cell migration is a basic mechanism vital for any immune cell. Usually, normal immune cells are more efficient migrators than neoplastic immune cells. 10 Various molecular mechanisms contributing to the migratory behaviour of DLBCL have already been uncovered through research. These include pathways such as STAT3‐mediated amoeboid migration, the activation of the WNT pathway by FBN1, 11 RELN, PLAGL2 as well as several others. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Cell migration can be inhibited or promoted by factors secreted by stromal cells and extra‐cellular matrix components. Additionally, cell migration reflects the tumour cells' ability to disseminate.

The objective of this study was to determine whether the various genetic subtypes of DLBCL exhibit different migration behaviours. The investigation specifically examines the migratory behaviour of well characterized cell lines from the following subtypes of DLBCL: MCD (TMD8, HBL1, OCI‐Ly10), BN2 (Riva) and EZB (OCI‐Ly1, SUDHL4, WSU‐DLCL2). 9 For the assessment of cell motility, we used cell lines as obtaining native tissue of DLBCL with comprehensive molecular characterization is rare and only a few primary DLBCL cases could be studied. Additionally, primary tissue includes bystander cells that can impede the measurement of neoplastic cells.

METHODS

In vitro model of DLBCL subtypes using microfluidics microchannel

For assessment of the motility of the DLBCL subtypes, the following cell lines were studied: MCD type: TMD8 (CVCL_A442) and HBL‐1 (CVCL_4213); EZB type: SU‐DHL‐4 (CVCL_0539), SU‐DHL‐6 (CVCL_2206), WSU‐DLCL2 (CVCL_1902) and OCI‐Ly1 (CVCL_1879); BN2: RIVA/Ri‐1 (CVCL_1885). The identity of all cell lines was authenticated by short tandem repeats (STR) profiling. All cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chips with different types of microchannels were produced in moulds provided by Dr. Matthieu Piel, Institut Curie. 16 Straight channels with a diameter of 4 μm and height of 10 μm were tested and determined to be most appropriate in size. A 3 mm hole was punched in the PDMS on each side of the microchannels to serve as a reservoir for the cells. 1.26 × 106 cells were resuspended in 30 μL of RPMI medium containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% foetal calf serum. 6 μL of this cell suspension was then added to the punch and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were allowed to enter the fibronectin‐coated microchannels with undirected baseline migration. Cell motility was monitored using a phase‐contrast microscope (Lumascope LS720, Etaluma, Carlsbad, CA, USA) under incubator conditions for 20 h, with time‐lapse images taken every 7 min in straight channels. To study how efficiently cells pass constrictions, microchannels with 12 × 12 μm size and 2 μm constrictions were chosen. Cells in videos from microchannel experiments were segmented using a custom script as previously described. 17 Passage time of constrictions was manually evaluated.

Case selection

Seven primary DLBCL were examined for tumour cell motility in native tissue sections (Tables 1 and 2). In these cases, the molecular subtype was determined by next‐generation sequencing, and these cases were also stained for lamin A/C. All cases were obtained from the University Hospital of Frankfurt. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Goethe University Hospital (No. 20‐876aV).

In addition, an independent series of 12 DLBCL from the CNS (‘MCD‐like’), all negative for CD10 and positive for BCL6 and MUM1, and seven DLBCL transformations from follicular lymphoma (FL) (‘EZB‐like’, 6/7 positive for CD10, all positive for BCL6 and negative for MUM1) were analysed for their nuclear lamin A/C content. No motility data were available for these cases.

Lamin A/C fluorescence immunostaining and analysis

Cells from all cell lines were embedded in a low‐melting agarose and cut into thick sections of around 50 μm. Sections were incubated with a monoclonal anti‐lamin A/C antibody (1:200; sc‐7292, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Dallas, USA) as previously described. 17 The VectaFluor Excel Antibody Kit Dylight 594 (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) was used for detection. A Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) was used for 3D imaging. The settings were as follows: HC PL APO 63×/1.3 GLYC CORR, Cs2; lasers: 405 nm DMOD Compact, Red 594 nm. The presented pixel size was 130 nm in each co‐ordinate direction. Furthermore, the z‐stack had a size of 0.13 mm and a total of 30 μm thickness was analysed.

The analysis of the lamin A/C content per nucleus was performed using Fiji software. 18 The DAPI and lamin A/C channels of the image were processed separately. First, we identified the nuclear areas by segmenting the DAPI pixels. The following steps were performed consecutively: Gaussian blur, manual threshold setting, deletion of small and large objects, and computation of the region of interest. Each connected component in the region of interest was considered to be a nucleus object. Second, the lamin channel was segmented by applying a Gaussian blur and setting a manual threshold. The ‘Analyse Particles’ function was then used to measure the fraction of lamin‐positive pixels per nucleus object.

Live cell imaging

For 4D imaging, native thick sections from seven primary cases of DLBCL were analysed as previously described. 10 The native tissue was stained with a fluorescent antibody against CD20 (B cells, Clone L26, AlexaFluor 488 ThermoFisher).

The tissue slices were imaged with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a 37°C thermostatic chamber. The temperature was constant, and oxygen was controlled by self‐written appropriate software. For dynamic imaging, the tissue slices were secured with a stainless steel slice anchor (Warner Instruments) and perfused at a rate of 0.8 mL/min with a solution of RPMI 1640 without phenol red, bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. For four‐dimensional analysis of cell migration, stacks of 10–12 sections (z step = 5 μm) were acquired every 20 s for 15 min at depths up to 80 μm.

Next generation sequencing

The primary cases studied for motility of the tumour cells were subtyped by next generation sequencing in the Institute of Pathology, University Hospital Tübingen. All cases were screened for mutations with the oncomine lymphoma III panel from ThermoFisher including 78 genes, encompassing the complete sequence of BCL2, BCL6, CARD11, EZH2, IRF4, PIM1, PRDM1 and TNFAIP3. For CD79B and MYD88 hotspot regions were covered.

Statistics and text editing

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the data. In the absence of a Gaussian distribution, a Mann–Whitney test was used for pairwise comparisons, otherwise a paired t‐test. For multiple comparisons, a Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post‐test for multiple comparisons was used in the absence of normality; otherwise, a one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post‐test for multiple comparisons was used.

Suggestions for text editing were generated by artificial intelligence (DeepL). All suggestions were checked manually by the authors for accuracy.

RESULTS

DLBCL cell lines of EZB‐type exhibit higher motility compared to MCD‐type cell lines

The migration velocity of different DLBCL cell lines in straight microchannels of 10 μm height and 4 μm width was assessed. The BN2 subtype Riva cell line exhibited the highest velocity at a median of 6.67 μm/min (Figure 1A). The EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines SU‐DHL‐4 (median 5.50 μm/min), WSU‐DLCL2 (median 2.62 μm/min) and OCI‐LY1 (median 2.34 μm/min) were faster than the MCD‐type cell lines TMD‐8 (median 1.70 μm/min) and HBL1 (median 1.01 μm/min) (Figure 1A,C,D). Only the EZB‐type cell line SU‐DHL‐6 exhibited a lower baseline velocity, with a mean of 1.36 μm/min. When considering all time‐lapse movies taken from the EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines (SU‐DHL‐4, WSU‐DLBCL2, SU‐DHL‐6 and OCI‐Ly1), the median velocity was significantly higher than that of the MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines TMD8 and HBL‐1 (medians 3.14 for EZB‐type vs. 1.34 μm/min, for MCD‐type, Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.0001, Figure 1B).

EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines are superior to MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines in the passage of tight constrictions

In the dissemination of malignant lymphomas, it may be necessary to overcome tight constrictions between other cells. To model this, we used microchannels with 2 μm constrictions. Each microchannel contained five constrictions of uniform size, connected in series. Interestingly, only a few cells from all the cell lines studied were observed to successfully overcome all five consecutive constrictions. The study focused on the first constriction in the series and analysed the time required for cells to pass through it. Most EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines, such as OCI‐Ly1, SU‐DHL‐4 and SU‐DHL‐6, were able to quickly pass through the first constriction with median times of 24.50, 14.00 and 14.00 min per passage, respectively (Figure 2A,D). However, despite also originating from the EZB‐type, WSU‐DLCL2 required a significantly longer median passage time of 252 min. Both HBL‐1 and TMD8 cell lines of the MCD‐type took a longer time to pass the first constriction (medians of 49.00 and 24.50 min). When comparing the total passage time for all cell lines, the EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines had significantly shorter passage times through constrictions compared to MCD‐type DLBCL (median 28.00 vs. 52.50 min, p < 0.001, Mann–Whitney test, Figure 2B,C). The BN2‐type cell line RIVA did not pass constrictions at all and was thus excluded from this analysis.

The nuclear stiffness of MCD‐type DLBCL cells may impede the spread of cancerous cells

The size of the nuclei can affect the passage through a constriction. To measure nuclear sizes, we embedded cell lines in an agarose gel and used DAPI staining on 3D thick sections of the cells by confocal microscopy. However, we found no significant difference in nuclear area between DLBCL cell lines of the EZB‐type compared to those of the MCD‐type (mean areas 80.18 vs. 85.64 μm2, Figure S1).

In addition, an excessively rigid nucleus may also affect the ability to pass through a constriction. Increased levels of lamin A/C result in a stiffer nuclear membrane and are crucial for the mechanical stability of the cell nucleus. The amount of lamin A/C per nucleus in % area was assessed. Both MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines, TMD8 and HBL‐1, had denser nuclear lamin A/C (means of 17.16% and 13.54%, Figure 3A,D) compared to the EZB cell lines (means of 3.74% for OCI‐Ly1, 11.69% for SU‐DHL‐4, 5.22% for SU‐DHL‐6 and 3.30% for WSU‐DLCL2, Figure 3E). When comparing all cells from MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines with all cells from EZB‐type cell lines, the MCD‐type cell lines showed a significantly higher lamin A/C density (medians of 18.72% vs. 3.18%, p < 0.0001, Mann–Whitney test, Figure 3B). To extend our analysis beyond cell lines, we also examined lamin A/C density in primary DLBCL. As only a small number of primary DLBCLs were available for motility analysis (EZB n = 4, MCD n = 1), we supplemented these groups with additional cases. Due to the unknown mutation profile of the additional primary tumours, we evaluated 12 primary DLBCL from the CNS, which are typically MCD‐like. For the EZB type, seven secondary follicular lymphoma transformations were selected as they typically correspond to EZB type DLBCL. In this cohort of primary DLBCL, the 13 MCD‐like cases had significantly higher levels of lamin A/C than the 11 EZB‐like cases (mean 8.06% vs. 3.24%; p = 0.0461, unpaired t‐test, Figure 3C,F,G).

Mutations affecting motility associated genes in primary DLBCL

The mutation profiles of primary MCD‐type and EZB‐type DLBCL from Wright et al. 9 were screened for mutations in motility‐associated genes. The fold change in the number of mutations per gene between MCD‐type and EZB‐type cases relative to all cases in the study was calculated and is shown in Figure 4. DYNLT1, ARHGEF10, KIF2B, NCKAP1, XIRP1, MAP1A and MYO1B had more than 2‐fold more mutations per case in EZB‐type DLBCL, whereas WDR63, STMN4 and PLXNB3 had more than 2‐fold more mutations per case in MCD‐type DLBCL. Thus, differences in the number of mutations affecting different motility‐associated genes were observed in primary MCD‐ and EZB‐type DLBCL.

Motility of primary DLBCL

Next, we were interested in the motility of primary DLBCL tumour cells. We grouped seven DLBCL cases into GCB‐type and non‐GCB‐type according to the Hans classifier and according to the molecular subtypes of Wright et al. 9 The median tumour cell velocity of four cases of GCB‐type and three cases of non‐GCB‐type DLBCL according to Hans et al. 19 was almost the same (median velocity in GCB‐type 2.10 μm/min vs. 2.52 μm/min in non‐GCB type, not significant, Mann–Whitney test, Figure 5). When molecular subtyping was applied, four cases were classified as EZB and one case as MCD (Table 1). The median tumour cell velocity of the four EZB cases was higher than that of the MCD case (2.89 vs. 1.85 μm/min, Figure 5). One further case was classified as ST2‐type and one case could not be classified due to poor DNA quality. However, due to the small number of cases, this difference was not significant.

DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to investigate whether genetic subtypes of DLBCL result in differences in tumour cell motility. Our findings demonstrate that MCD‐ and EZB‐type DLBCL cell lines exhibit differences both in baseline migration and the ability to pass through constrictions. Our hypothesis was that DLBCL cells that are disseminated at the time of diagnosis, leading to a worse outcome, would have greater migratory capabilities. Contrary to our expectations, MCD‐type DLBCL, which usually has a worse prognosis than EZB‐type DLBCL, showed a lower migratory capacity compared to EZB‐type DLBCL. A high migration capacity with low nuclear lamin A/C levels may be unfavourable for the survival of cancer cells, which may be one reason why MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines show increased lamin A/C levels and a decreased migratory capacity. 20

Various factors have been identified as contributing to the migration capacity of aggressive B‐cell lymphoma cells. For Burkitt lymphoma, both CD47 and HDAC6 were identified as important factors in dissemination. 21 , 22 Additionally, the re‐expression of DLC1 in Burkitt lymphoma cells had a significant inhibitory effect on migration. 23 Furthermore, RELN, a secreted extra‐cellular matrix protein that is thought to control cell–cell interactions critical for cell positioning and migration, was also shown to be essential in the migration of B‐cell lymphoma cells. 12 Various factors have been identified to contribute to the migration of neoplastic cells in DLBCL, including PLAGL2, 13 HK2, 14 DEPTOR, 24 HMGA2, 25 and the LncRNAs SNHG5 15 and PCAT1. 26 Most of these studies did not analyse the migratory behaviour of lymphoma cells in relation to their cell of origin or genetic subtype. Lu et al. 27 observed the effects of HGAL, a protein that is specifically expressed in germinal centre B (GCB) cells and GCB‐cell derived lymphomas. However, this study found an interaction between HGAL and myosin II in podosomes, and an inhibitory effect of HGAL on cellular migration in the presence of IL‐6. This may explain how GCB cells are retained within the GC, but it contradicts our findings.

The distinct movement patterns of cells from DLBCL subtypes may also depend on different mutated genes. In the primary MCD‐type DLBCL of Wright et al., 9 a higher number of mutations were found in WDR63, a gene that inhibits Arp2/3‐dependent Actin polymerization, which should also result in higher migratory capability and thus again contradict our results. 28 Another gene that was also more frequently mutated in MCD‐type DLBCL was PLXNB3, 29 which has been shown to be responsible for invasiveness and migration in breast cancer and thus could explain our results.

Migration data obtained from primary DLBCL cases showed the same trend as cell lines when cases were classified according to their mutation profiles. However, no such trend was observed when the Hans immunohistochemical classifier 19 was applied. This suggests that it is time for molecular classification to be used in routine practice, as the Hans classifier is not sufficient to correctly recognize the different molecular groups.

However, it is difficult to compare these data because the microchannel experiments did not include bystander cells and also lacked chemokine gradients that may be present in primary tissue. Several studies have shown that the microenvironment is important in DLBCL 4 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 and the bystander cells certainly influence the migratory ability of the neoplastic cells, which was neglected in the present study. Finally, we observed increased expression of the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C in the nuclei of MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines and primary DLBCL cases. Since lamin A/C increases nuclear stiffness, 34 increased expression of lamin A/C may explain the reduced ability of MCD‐type DLBCL cell lines to squeeze into tight microchannels.

In conclusion, our study observed differences in the migratory behaviour of different genetic subtypes of DLBCL cell lines and a similar trend in a small group of primary DLBCL, suggesting that the genetic subtype influences the migratory behaviour. Further studies demonstrating this effect also in a large cohort of well‐characterized primary DLBCL cases are warranted.
