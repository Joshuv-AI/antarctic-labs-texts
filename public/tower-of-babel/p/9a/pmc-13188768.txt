A practice-based implementation strategy bundle for integrating behavioral health in primary care: a matrixed multiple case study
Authors: Gretchen Buchanan, Kelley McCall, Lorella Palazzo, Michelle Bagwell, Shivani Patel, Shravya Allena, Byron J. Powell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntegrated behavioral health (IBH) is an evidence-based approach to addressing mental health in primary care settings. It typically involves augmenting a primary care team with a behavioral health professional (e.g., psychologist, licensed clinical social worker) who sees patients for brief, focused concerns; can function as a bridge to specialty care; and provides medical providers with support and education about patient behavioral health concerns. IBH has slowly begun to disseminate across the United States, but practices encounter significant implementation barriers. How IBH is implemented has not been systematically studied outside of trials, yet this study could provide a rich foundation for selecting and optimizing implementation strategies that can increase the speed of IBH scale-up.MethodsUsing rapid ethnographic assessment (REA), we conducted site visits, interviews, and surveys at a stratified sample of five primary care clinics in three different healthcare systems in two Midwestern states. We coded the data in a primarily deductive analysis approach using Stephens’ IBH Cross-Model Framework (CMF) to characterize the IBH intervention; the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) to characterize implementation barriers and facilitators; and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy of implementation strategies. Using a matrixed multiple case study design, we identified implementation strategies that clinics found effective in implementing and sustaining IBH.ResultsDespite geographic variability, the clinics primarily served low-income and-resource populations. A strong pattern emerged regarding common IBH aspects targeted for internal implementation support, associated barriers, and strategies used for them. Successful implementation strategies included accessing additional funding for start-up, creating new clinical teams, revising professional roles, promoting adaptability, facilitating relay of data to clinical providers, and purposely reexamining the implementation process. The Normalization Process Theory (NPT) mechanisms of coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring were all clearly identified in successful implementations.DiscussionImplementation of IBH is an ongoing process of implementing, maintaining, and improving many specific processes. People with knowledge of the IBH practice model and desire to implement and sustain it are critical, as are policies and programs that support initial and ongoing implementation, and an organizational culture that embraces IBH as its standard of practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00893-3.

Background

Mental and behavioral health issues have a profound impact on quality of life and are a contributing factor to early mortality [1–4]. Yet, about half of people with mental health issues don’t receive treatment; minoritized individuals and the uninsured have even lower rates of treatment, resulting in worse outcomes [5–9]. A lack of trauma-informed or culturally-competent care, rigid attendance rules, less insurance access, stigma, and other barriers contribute to this reduced access [7, 10–14].

Integrated Behavioral Health (IBH)

Obtaining mental healthcare in the U.S. can be challenging, so the medical primary care system has become the de facto mental health system. [15]. However, primary care providers (PCPs) are not usually equipped to adequately address behavioral health (i.e., mental health and substance use) [16]. One evidence-based solution is IBH, which augments the primary care medical team with a behavioral health professional (e.g., licensed clinical social worker, licensed marriage and family therapist, or psychologist) who sees patients for brief, focused concerns; can function as a bridge to specialty care; and provides PCPs with support and education around patient behavioral health concerns [17]. For people in rural areas, IBH clinicians may be the only mental health provider nearby [13]. There is robust evidence that IBH results in improved access and quality of care for behavioral health conditions [18–24] and improved biomedical outcomes for people with mental health concerns [25, 26].

Integrated behavioral health implementation

While the American Medical Association and other national health organizations advocate for IBH to become the standard of care [27], practices encounter significant barriers to implementing this critical service [28, 29]. These barriers include poor or unsupportive leadership; lack of time, space, and other resources; lack of training, role clarity, and scope of practice or staff buy-in; need to create new workflows and operating procedures; complex and separate financial and reimbursement systems; and need for attitude and practice changes on the part of both medical and behavioral health clinicians (BHCs) [30–33].

Case study reports [34–36] and initial implementation guides [32, 37, 38] have promoted initial understanding of potentially effective implementation strategies for IBH; for example, the use of learning collaboratives [39] and practice facilitation [40, 41] are common. The DIAMOND study in Minnesota was a large-scale effectiveness trial of IBH; however, in order to complete the study, the researchers found they needed to enlist several implementation strategies [42], such as addressing financial roadblocks through negotiated bundled payments and increasing buy-in with an advisory boards/workgroups. [42]. The Veterans’ Health Administration (VA) has an extensive network of IBH primary care clinics with some implementation research, but it is a self-contained system [36, 43, 44]. Inconsistent terminology makes it difficult to systematically categorize the strategies discussed in the literature [45], and most literature identifying implementation strategies has been specific to the Collaborative Care Model, which may have different implementation needs than other models of IBH [29, 46–48].

Objectives and aims

For IBH to be scaled across the nation for maximum impact, it is critical to understand not only if researcher-determined implementation strategies work, but what has already worked in an “implementation as usual” setting, which means an organization decided on its own to implement IBH and went about doing so without research-based external implementation support [49–51]. The goal of this study was to understand practice-driven IBH implementation strategies outside of research-funded implementation, and how these strategies vary across contexts.

Methods

Study design

Because practice-driven IBH implementation strategies are likely to vary depending on context, we needed to analyze more than one clinic or system and then compare them to get a sense of how homogeneous or heterogeneous their implementation strategies were. Therefore, we used a matrixed multiple case study design (MMCS; see Table 1) and engaged with a stratified, purposive sample of five primary care clinics across three health systems in two Midwestern states. MMCS requires nine steps, which are described in Table 1 [52]. MMCS allows for within- and across-site analysis to balance depth and breadth, facilitating generalizability within the context of qualitative data. This study used a simultaneous, convergent mixed methods approach [53]. We used qualitative and quantitative data collected simultaneously so that the two types of data validated each other, or where they didn’t, we analyzed that discrepancy. The study was approved by the Washington University in St. Louis IRB (#202,203,144). Clinic recruitment

Eligible clinics were primary care clinics with at least one licensed behavioral health professional in two Midwestern states. We recruited clinics through the Washington University in St. Louis Implementation Science Center for Cancer Control network and web searches for clinics involved in “healthcare home” initiatives or the equivalent (which typically include behavioral health) [54]. Recruitment ran July 2022-April 2024, with site visits September 2022-March 2024. We centered health equity for this project by prioritizing clinics that served Black and/or rural populations. Compensation included a $2500 clinic “impact fee” as recognition of their community partner status, and to cover costs of staff time in engaging in the study [55]. Individual participants were provided with $25 gift cards for completing questionnaires and $50 for formal interviews/shadowing.

Qualitative data collection

The qualitative method Rapid Ethnographic Assessment (REA) was used to determine how the various implementation strategies were operationalized in each clinic. REA allows for quick and deep understanding within a specific context [56, 57]. It includes interviews with key personnel, observations, informal interviews, and document review. Typically, REA is best utilized with highly specific questions, when the researchers have some level of affinity with the participants, and when there is a defined scope. Triangulating ethnographic qualitative data with quantitative data can facilitate validation of results [58, 59]. The first-author and co-PI, GB, has been an IBH clinician which supported her rapid, deep engagement with clinic personnel on this topic; other researchers on the team have also had either clinical or social work roles in healthcare settings.

Data collection activities included: (1) developing a semi-structured interview guide for the key informant interviews (see Appendix A), (2) developing an observation/interview plan cooperatively with each site, (3) conducting formal and informal interviews with 3–12 personnel per clinic while observing clinic processes at least one day per clinic. Two researchers attended each site visit. The research team debriefed after each site visit and separate formal interview.

Quantitative data collection

We used three surveys compiled as one battery. All clinic staff were sent a Qualtrics or Redcap link at the end of the site visit (institution policies required a transition from Qualtrics to Redcap part-way through the study).

Practice Integration Profile 2.0

The Practice Integration Profile 2.0 (PIP2) [60] is an 18-item validated measure of integrated behavioral health fidelity with five subscales. Example questions include “we screen adults for at least one lifestyle behavior concern” and “we provide behavioral (nonpharmacologic) care for patients with behavioral health needs.” Scores are percentages. There is no recommended categorization structure, so for ease of comparison in analysis we based our categorization of high, moderate, and low on where questions in the scale seem to define them the categorizations used by Kim et al. in their example MMCS paper [61].

Provider REport of sustainment scale

The Provider REport of Sustainment Scale (PRESS) [62] is a brief, 3-item validated measure of providers’ report of use of an evidence-based practice on a Likert scale 0–4. Similarly to PIP 2.0 categorization, there is no specific recommended categorization structure, so for ease of comparison in analysis we based our categorization of high, moderate, and low on where questions in the scale seem to define them (e.g., a 2 is “to a moderate extent” while a 3 is “to a great extent,” so we placed the cutoff for moderate and high at 3).

Data analysis

Guiding conceptual frameworks

The study was guided by three conceptual frameworks: the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) [63], a determinant framework focused on healthcare systems; the Experts Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [64, 65]; and the IBH Cross-Model Framework (CMF; Fig. 1) [66], a validated framework of IBH that encompasses five principles and nine structures necessary for a strong IBH program and which can be applied across multiple models of care. The interview guide and codebook were developed with these frameworks in mind.Fig. 1Cross-Model Framework of IBH [66]

Cross-Model Framework of IBH [66]

Rapid ethnographic assessment (REA)

All observations and interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed. Documents (e.g., manuals, reports) were labeled and cataloged. Transcripts and artifact descriptions were coded and analyzed using Dedoose (current version 9.2.22). We performed iterative data analysis and early findings resulted in minor modifications to the interview guide and protocol. Each transcript or artifact was blind double-coded by one PI and a graduate research assistant and then they consulted to resolve discrepancies. The other PI further reviewed the data in consultation with our REA methodologist.

We developed a codebook of all ERIC implementation strategies, the PRISM constructs, and CMF constructs. We primarily utilized deductive content analysis utilizing the guiding frameworks [67] but allowed space for novel codes to emerge. Coding involved reading through transcripts looking for quotes where participants discussed one or more of the three constructs. Excerpts were intentionally made large so as to include overlapping constructs. For example, a researcher would identify a sentence where an implementation strategy was discussed; they would review the surrounding area for contextual PRISM and IBH CMF constructs being discussed related to that implementation strategy and code everything relevant into that excerpt. Similarly, if a contextual factor or IBH process was identified, they would search for related implementation strategies within the paragraph or surrounding discussions. This meant that excerpts often included more than one implementation strategy, IBH process, and determinant. Then using co-occurrence analysis (see Fig. 2 for example), the three frameworks were cross-walked with each other to identify overlapping mentions. Dedoose allows a click-through co-occurrence analysis so the excerpts were able to be reviewed and verified as accurate. Critically, during coding we developed a code “negative example” to identify times when an implementation strategy was utilized and it did not work or was unacceptable to the participant or colleagues. We screened out these examples when we completed the co-occurrence analysis to ensure that we only counted when implementation strategies worked (according to participants).Fig. 2Co-occurrence analysis

Co-occurrence analysis

Data summarization

As seen in Fig. 2, the co-occurrence analysis results in a numeric count of the number of times two constructs occur within the same excerpt. Based on the totals column, we took the top five IBH (CMF) constructs discussed by participants to examine in more detail. IBH constructs most discussed were hypothesized to be either the most important or the most challenging to implement, given the focus of the interviews on the implementation process. From these constructs we identified the most discussed implementation determinants (PRISM framework) and strategies (ERIC taxonomy). The links between these three frameworks therefore portray the implementation object, its barriers and facilitators, and relevant strategies.

Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM)

The IRLM [68] is an established method of identifying relevant implementation determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes using implementation frameworks, along with identifying the intervention being implemented, and organizing these constructs into inputs and outcomes. After identifying the most salient constructs within each framework, we developed an IRLM. We included an implementation theory, Normalization Process Theory (NPT), to further explain the findings in a more causal approach.

Results

Clinic characteristics

The five sites varied in their demographics, particularly regarding race and income. For Sites 1–3 we were able to obtain site-specific demographics, while for Sites 4–5 we were only able to obtain ZIP code-level demographics (see Table 2). Site 1, in Healthcare System 1 (HCS 1) in State 1, served a largely urban and suburban, predominantly Non-Hispanic white (72.9%) patient population with a large minority of Black/African American patients (20.5%). 73% of patients lived at or below the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) and 68% of patients had Medicaid or Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP). It was its own separate clinic but was situated within an organization that had primarily provided behavioral health services until its start in 2019. Sites 2 and 3, in State 2, in Healthcare System 2 (HCS 2), are in an urban setting; 95.4% of the healthcare system’s patients identified as Black/African American and 97% below the Federal Poverty Level (FPL), mostly covered by Medicaid, CHIP, or uninsured. HCS 2 was primarily a primary care health system but also had a contract whereby some of its BH clinicians provided services in the local school system; some provided services both in the schools and clinics. Sites 4 and 5 in State 2, combined as Healthcare System 3 (HCS 3), are in both urban and rural settings. We were unable to obtain clinic-specific demographics for them; however, participant reports at both sites stated their patient populations were predominantly low-income and diverse. In the clinics’ ZIP code areas, 87% (site 4) and 83% (site 5) of the population were White. There are several models of IBH, including the Collaborative Care Model (CoCM; [69]), Primary Care Behavioral Health (PCBH; [70]), Co-Located Care, and various blends. We included all models of care as possibilities, but all the recruited clinics used a PCBH approach, which is a generalist approach whereby the behavioral health clinician (BHC) treats all patients, receives warm handoffs, and provides brief interventions for any patient in the clinic for whom the PCP requests services [70]. Site 5 also had an affiliated psychologist who primarily performed testing services and treatment for autism and was not involved in the IBH program. Of note, Sites 2–5 (all the clinics in State 2) provided a state Medicaid service called Primary Care Health Home (PCHH), the state’s name for the Primary Care Medical Home (PCMH) model [22], which was pivotal in both the startup and functioning of their larger PCBH IBH programs. PCHH is a care management program that provides per member per month (PMPM) payments for enrolled patients, who must have two or more chronic health conditions (among other qualifying factors). The team includes nurse care managers, health navigators, and a BHC and they enroll 750 patients per team.

Organizational characteristics

Many of the most-discussed determinants fell into the PRISM Organizational Characteristics domain. These included Staffing and Incentives, Systems and Training, Shared Goals and Cooperation, and Management Support and Communication, with both positive and negative considerations (See Table 3).

Staffing and incentives

All clinics indicated they were short-staffed, and a wide variety of staff interviewees volunteered that they thought they needed to hire more BHCs. One participant stated: “We've also had a little bit of difficulty with just filling the last open position… everyone's really learned how easy it is to do virtual behavioral health services and start their own private practices… and so there isn't a strong calling to come work for a nonprofit.” (director, Site 1). All interviewees either volunteered or agreed with the statement that if they had additional BHCs, they could fill their time, though they were grateful for what they had. Some sites did not have a full-time BHC. At Sites 4 and 5, the BHCs were trainees from an external organization andwere in the clinic 3.5 days a week. When BHCs were present only 1–2 days per week, there was difficulty keeping to the model. One participant stated:“At one point while I was here, we did have child psych[ology], but it was like someone who worked at [local university] and then they would come over like once or twice a week. And that was a disaster because she was only here – like sometimes she was here, sometimes she wasn't. And then we'd schedule patients and then she wouldn't come and, oh no, it was a mess.” (NP, Site 3).

“At one point while I was here, we did have child psych[ology], but it was like someone who worked at [local university] and then they would come over like once or twice a week. And that was a disaster because she was only here – like sometimes she was here, sometimes she wasn't. And then we'd schedule patients and then she wouldn't come and, oh no, it was a mess.” (NP, Site 3).

Another issue with hiring is finding therapists who want to work in the IBH model and have experience with it: “It is rare for us to find someone who has a lot of experience in doing integrated care, and if they do, oftentimes they come from a model that wasn't a PCBH integrated care model… [we have] a really robust training plan for new hires and for our interns.” (external BHC organization director, Sites 4&5).

Clinics varied on how well they managed clinicians’ time so that they were able to see both new/urgent patients and follow-up/scheduled patients. In addition to PCBH, HCSs 2 and 3 (Sites 2–5) also had PCHH. At HCS 2, one of the PCBH clinicians was also partially paid through and had responsibilities related to PCHH. The HCS 3 clinics had their own independent PCHH teams which were separate from their IBH programs. This separateness was reinforced by the fact that at this system, the BHCs were contracted by a third party to the HCS 3 and were not employees of the clinics. They were paid through a national psychology internship program which allowed for the BHC services to be free for patients.

Systems and training

In interviews, participants often described tasks they complete related to IBH, patient care, and interprofessional communication. One participant described their daily task of a typical IBH “schedule scrub:”“I scrub the charts, so I see everybody on all the PCPs… schedule for the day and then it will have the reason they're coming in. So I'll make little notes to myself of this 11:00 AM patient… Usually, I will get a message for those patients anyways. So it's more for myself to prepare my day as much as possible in terms of tracking screeners or things like that… whenever anyone over 11 would come in for a med check or a well child check or just if it says anxiety or depression or it's an acute visit of that nature, they will get those screeners as well.” (BHC, Site 5)

Staff also provided their opinions on whether workflows and systems were well known and well-followed. A big theme of confusion at Sites 2–5 was understanding when to call staff of the PCHH program. The general BHCs, running a standard PCBH model, were easily understood by non-behavioral health staff. However, the parameters of engagement with the PCHH staff confused most clinic staff, even BHCs and PCPs: “She's like, what do they do? Which is totally fair. Sometimes I'm still like, what do you guys do? They do so many different things in my experience, and I'm not always backed by the PCPs to know exactly if they already got the PCHH BHC or whatnot. I think they tend to just come to me because they're like, oh, we're going to get her anyways.” (BHC, Site 5).

Shared goals and cooperation

Shared goals and cooperation were seen as critical to interprofessional communication and teamwork. When everyone had a clear sense of why they were doing what they were doing, things went smoothly. That reason tended to be to take care of their patients, both medically but also their whole person, such as making sure they had warm coats, food, knew when to renew their health insurance, got seen by someone at the clinic even if it meant someone stepping out of their defined role, etc. There were also efforts to make the workplace a warm and welcoming place to be. Problems tended to arise when goals were unclear and cooperation was not facilitated by leadership.

Management support and communication

Management being present in the clinic on a regular basis, troubleshooting and supporting staff helped clinics run smoothly and ensured that BHCs were working to fidelity of the model. Staff consistently discussed the need for training involving their tasks and to clarify staff roles. A participant at Site 2 discussed the lack of communication from management on multiple topics as a key reason why they were choosing to leave the clinic.

Several participants also discussed how frequent communication with management allowed for problem solving at the organizational policy level: “We're not doing this just to change it. It's not just for fun. This is why, and this is how it's benefiting you. This is how it's benefiting the patients, and our whole job is reducing barriers and creating better access to care. Helping to get that buy-in from people to sustain the change ongoing.” (director, Site 1).

Clinician characteristics

Clinician characteristics are not an explicit construct in the current PRISM framework but can be seen as a sub-set of Organizational characteristics at the staff level. Many participants described the importance of clinician’s characteristics – both medical and behavioral health – to the fidelity and sustainment of an IBH program. Some PCPs specialize in certain populations, such as those with autism or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), which contribute to them being more enthusiastic about having BHCs. Clinicians often played a critical role as clinical champions—some advocated for the model explicitly while others it was more their personalities and clinical approach that fit well with the structure of IBH in their clinic. This characteristic dovetailed with the implementation strategy “ Promote adaptability” (below) such that clinicians who promoted adaptability and responded to management attempts to do so appeared more successful and content in the PCBH model.

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics were minimally discussed compared to organizational and clinician characteristics. Patients’ social determinants of health contributed to health conditions and their ability to engage in the healthcare system. Mental health and substance use were the topics most discussed. PCPs varied significantly in their comfort level with different health conditions (e.g., substance use, depression/anxiety, autism) and valued the presence of BHCs to either take on or augment treatment. The knowledge and personal beliefs/cultures of patients as well as competing demands with healthcare also were discussed.

External environment

BHCs filled a gap where there were long waitlists for long-term therapy clinics. They played a critical role in supporting clinic staff in keeping patients safe, and facilitating their treatment. One participant stated:“I came from the [city] clinic, and we don't have integrated BHC there. There's no BHC at all. It's just referrals to [local mental health clinic] where it's really hard to get patients care. And a lot of times with me working in peds with teenagers in crisis and stuff, we just have to send them to [hospital] or somewhere else where they can get that immediate access. But it's hard to fill that gap in between. If we had somebody there, like we have our BHCs here, maybe we could avoid situations like that.” (PCP, Site 5).

Implementation strategies using the ERIC framework

Implementation strategies most frequently discussed by participants included (see Table 4): (1) Create new clinical teams, (2) Revise professional roles, (3) Promote adaptability, (4) Facilitate relay of data to clinical providers, and (5) Purposely reexamine the implementation of IBH. Other strategies frequently discussed included: Mandate change, Provide ongoing consultation, Identify and prepare champions, Facilitation, Change physical structures and equipment, and the broader implementation strategy of Utilize financial strategies.

Create new clinical teams

To create new clinical teams, the broader category of Utilize financial strategies was described as a necessary earlier implementation strategy for IBH startup at all clinics. Site 1 identified a state grant in 2019 that allowed them to formally start their integrated clinic. HCS 2 stated they onboarded their first BHCs after being awarded the PCHH program by the state and expanded their BHCs into seeing patients outside of that as well. HCS 3 similarly started with PCHH and expanded. Site 5 reported they obtained a grant approximately 20 years ago where they had a BHC, but when the grant ended, the BHC had to leave. However, that primed them to be ready when the local behavioral health organization began talking with their health system about IBH, and they were the first clinic to sign on. One participant described their startup:“With the support of Dr. [redacted] and Dr. [redacted], they took it up to administration, they pitched it, they said, here's this program that we would like to try. At that time, it was a pilot, so it wasn't a guarantee that they would continue providing payment for our services beyond the three year pilot. So we got the approval from [HCS] and the support within the clinic and we launched, you had to hire all of your staff, you had to find all of your patients in the clinic. It was an adventure..” (population health director, Sites 4 & 5).

Revise professional roles

Revising roles was discussed frequently both in the context of the initial implementation, such as by educating providers on new workflows for mental health concerns, and ongoing, such as adaptations teams made during COVID-19 to how they provided patient care. All sites indicated they had gone through revisions, and while it was challenging, the successful clinics had strong leadership and clear communication.

Revisions were typically aimed at improving fidelity to the IBH model, and often followed by the hiring or on-boarding of a new director or when a BHC left:“When I took over… we shifted from having a completely open schedule and only doing warm handoffs as they came up to having the expectation to provide clinical services and to manage referrals internally… supporting using expert practices for mental health rather than just substance use, so doing a screening for depression, providing that brief intervention, and then referring only if needed.” (director, Site 1)

“When I took over… we shifted from having a completely open schedule and only doing warm handoffs as they came up to having the expectation to provide clinical services and to manage referrals internally… supporting using expert practices for mental health rather than just substance use, so doing a screening for depression, providing that brief intervention, and then referring only if needed.” (director, Site 1)

Some revisions were made more out of necessity: Site 4 had moved across town the previous year, and their PCHH team had to make some modifications. One participant stated “We developed a rotation where each person has one onsite clinic day each week and they do that rotation through. So it's covered and it works very well. The model is working amazing where we've pitched it to other PCHH programs and how we practice and they've started to pilot it.” (director, Site 4).

Promote adaptability

Being flexible in work roles and expectations was also critical for BHCs and all clinic staff to be able to be responsive to patient needs and organizational changes. Staff discussed the need for being flexible when communicating with BHCs:“We reach out to them by email or we reach out to them if they're in the office today, we have one in office, so we can actually just ask her questions or do we need to put this individual on the schedule. We're very lucky here because we can call upstairs, we can Skype, we can do whatever we need to at least get their little names in the book, get 'em on to where we can fit 'em in somewhere.” (receptionist, Site 5)

“We reach out to them by email or we reach out to them if they're in the office today, we have one in office, so we can actually just ask her questions or do we need to put this individual on the schedule. We're very lucky here because we can call upstairs, we can Skype, we can do whatever we need to at least get their little names in the book, get 'em on to where we can fit 'em in somewhere.” (receptionist, Site 5)

Most BHCs had periods of time when they would see pre-scheduled patients and periods of time when they were open for consults or warm hand-offs of patients by providers in the moment. Additionally, one participant pointed out that they promoted adaptability at their organizational level:“We've been able to change processes over time with my help as the leader or with input from the behavioral health clinicians… to get rid of our 10 minute late rule, to stay open during our lunch hour, to ensure that if someone shows up, regardless of when they got there that day, they should be seen because maybe that was the only thing they were able to do today. Let's not turn them away.” (director, Site 1)

“We've been able to change processes over time with my help as the leader or with input from the behavioral health clinicians… to get rid of our 10 minute late rule, to stay open during our lunch hour, to ensure that if someone shows up, regardless of when they got there that day, they should be seen because maybe that was the only thing they were able to do today. Let's not turn them away.” (director, Site 1)

Promoting flexibility had to be met with flexibility by staff to be successful. When BHCs were not able to be flexible or engaged in these ways, that was when clinics tended to struggle with sustainability. The BHC at Site 2 who was retiring indicated that she did not like the need for flexibility demanded by the model, which was consistent with HCS 2 director’s observation that when she started shifting the HCS from a co-located model to a PCBH model, many clinicians left, not wanting that type of practice.

Facilitate relay of data to clinical providers

Relaying data to clinical providers went both ways. Mainly, PCPs and medical staff focused on needing to get screening information to BHCs, such as if a patient screened positive on the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) depression scale:“[Participant 1] We usually have them fill out a questionnaire, the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. So if we notice that their answers on that are, or they score really high on that, sometimes we will just go get, if [BHC is] available, we'll go get her before the doctor goes in and just let her review that or tell her if somebody had a traumatic event happen, we'll let her know ahead of time so that she can make room with her schedule. All clinics reported having a protocol for screening patients using these standardized forms and what to do when patients scored higher than a certain cutoff, or if they indicated a safety risk.

Systematic patient and outcome tracking was on all the directors’ radars and initiatives were in place to build those systems and infrastructure. However, at the time of the research team’s site visits, all the clinics were in the early stages of having tracking systems in place. While directors at all of the sites talked about tracking, staff and BHCs did not express that this was a regular part of their work or that they used a rigorous “measurement-based care” approach beyond the screening instruments that the clinics used generally. However, one participant stated,“The BHCs keep a lot of data for us… they document every single encounter and we collect information about the primary diagnosis, the [Current Procedural Terminology] CPT code, what referrals were made from that, whether or not the visit was charged, who referred… we have a person that is right now dedicated to building an entire database for us to start collecting so we can merge all the information from all of our clinics into one space.” (director at the contracted external organization at Sites 4–5).

Purposely reexamine the implementation

One of the most critical strategies that the sites utilized for sustainability was purposely reexamining their IBH programs on a regular basis. This primarily occurred as problems or concerns came up. At this time management was responsive and would make policy changes or adapt to the needs of patients or staff in a continuous improvement approach. For example, scheduling procedures were being evaluated at multiple sites. One participant indicated they had recently started having their nurses schedule patients with a BHC if no one was available at the time of the current visit:“Now our nurses are scheduling for them to prevent that barrier… leaving without an appointment and then not being able to get ahold of them… And oh, the year goes by, we see them for the next wellness visit. Oh, it's the same issue.” (PCP, Site 3).

Mechanisms of change

To take the results one step further, we can examine the potential mechanisms of change that result in high implementation adoption, fidelity, and sustainment of IBH in primary care clinics. Carl May’s Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [71] states the mechanisms of implementation include coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring. In the case of IBH implementation in primary care, it is critical that a clinic’s staff have a coherent understanding of what IBH is and how to use it – and most importantly, why they are using it (to provide better patient care). These are reflected in the strong focus on “shared goals and cooperation” and the nature of each “organizational health and culture.” Positive scores on these determinants are achieved through appropriate staffing and defining clinical teams, systems and training, and management communication about the how and why of IBH, which eventually results in integration into organizational culture. The clinic then regularly engages in it, as a collective, doing the work that supports engagement with the IBH team such as performing PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) screenings, because it is meaningful to them (cognitive participation and collective action).

The importance of training staff is a critical piece to successful IBH outcomes. Training that emphasizes behavioral health services for existing and new staff addresses concerns around turnover and loss of institutional knowledge of IBH and supports the diffusion of the innovation across the organization. Our results build on the idea of identifying a champion for IBH. Additionally, for IBH to be successful and sustainable, the entire organization (e.g., leadership and staff) must have buy-in, which can be simply a practical sense that having BHCs around improves patient flow and patient care is sufficient. This study’s findings demonstrated that office staff are critical in diverting patients to BHCs at appropriate times. This conclusion is bolstered by the observations at Sites 4 and 5 whereby there was confusion amongst staff not part of the PCHH program about when and who to refer to it and when to call them for certain patients; due to this confusion, while the program itself ran quite smoothly and successfully, there was less integration with the immediate day-to-day operations of the clinic as the PCBH BHCs. Because the PCBH message was simple, “when someone is having or may be having a behavioral health concern, involve the BHC,” understanding was clear and integration was tight.

The IBH approach requires specialized training for staff and practitioners due to the unscheduled, brief, and problem-focused care [69]. Therefore, it is critical for clinics to communicate these factors when hiring new staff and practitioners.

The last mechanism in NPT, reflexive monitoring, came through in the strategies of purposefully reexamining the implementation, both formally and informally, and revising professional roles over time; changes required adaptability, and when paired with the message of “this will improve patient care” (if believable) from management, generally this adaptability was how clinics were able to sustain their IBH structures.

Discussion

In this study, there were overlapping and unique barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies that influenced implementation adoption, fidelity, and sustainability of IBH into community-based primary care clinics. Using a MMCS, we found remarkable consistency in the key influencing factors and using NPT, we were able to discern the mechanisms likely present (or not) that contributed to implementation success (or not).

Internal facilitators (typically behavioral health directors), were critical in communicating the benefits and limits of IBH to all staff, particularly with the medical staff. The directors’ ability to train BHCs well was also important in the success of those BHCs in a role that most behavioral health providers do not experience in graduate education. The ability for staff, both behavioral and medical, to be adaptable and have clearly defined roles were crucial. Having clear communication from management was helpful in adjusting care. Staff members in consistent collaboration had better outcomes of patient care.

IBH would be challenging without start-up funding from the states in which these clinics were located. Successful clinics initiated and developed their IBH programming. However, all the clinics required initial support to assure IBH's success with management. Recent funding opportunities at the Federal level from Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration and the Centers for Medicaid and Medicare Services provide this opportunity to many clinics, if their states have applied for it.

A recent study of Federally Qualified Health Centers [72] receiving funding to implement PCBH highlights similar implementation strategies that lead to successful outcomes based on Proctor et al.’s framework [73]. These strategies included: identifying the physical space for the BHC, establishing a scheduling system, identifying a champion with experience leading change, providing training that emphasizes behavioral health interventions, developing IBH training procedures for new staff, and identifying local IBH billing codes, policies, and procedures. While all of these strategies were discussed in our study as well, our study extends these findings by demonstrating more emphasis on strategies for sustainment in addition to implementation adoption success.

Limitations

While the study aimed to recruit clinics serving diverse populations, our limited geographical scope may reduce generalizability in other parts of the country. Additionally, due to the sample size of five clinics and our focus on successful clinics, we did not reach saturation of all possible implementation strategies in IBH and their success rates. We also only studies clinics with PCBH and PCHH models; the same strategies and contextual barriers may not apply to other IBH models such as the Collaborative Care Model. Our study design did not include evaluations of reach or effectiveness and we did not seek out patient perspectives. Our analytic approach did not focus on patient or organizational perspectives on the innovation (e.g., acceptability, appropriateness), and our use of the IBH CMF (which focuses on optimal aspects of IBH to ensure implementation and sustainability) in coding in some ways usurped the Implementation and Sustainability Infrastructure aspect of the PRISM framework such that we did not code both. We only focused the current paper on the CMF components that were the most discussed as they seemed the most relevant to implementation research, so other CMF components and their implementation strategies and determinants are not listed here. Finally, our primarily qualitative data sources meant that we were limited in our ability to quantify contextual factors, implementation strategy usage, and identify cutoffs for when they had reached “critical mass.” However, the remarkable consistency across sites in all three guiding frameworks (determinants, implementation strategies, and IBH constructs) indicates strong validity of the results on which future studies can build.

Future directions

Future studies could use surveys with larger sample sizes to develop a broader and more comprehensive list of strategies and their links to outcomes. These studies can also examine at what point a contextual factor shifts from a barrier to a facilitator, and at what level an implementation strategy is “enough.”

The implementation strategy bundle elucidated here, as well as the mechanisms, should be tested and validated. Scaling up IBH through use of an easy-to-follow, evidence-based guide is the next step in this work.

Conclusion

Our innovative use of rapid ethnographic analysis in a matrixed multiple case study with healthcare clinics that had implemented IBH generated novel information about practical strategies clinics can use to implement and sustain IBH. This study identified contextual determinants that can impact implementation of specific components of IBH and implementation strategies that can counteract these barriers. The findings can translate to clinics nationwide based upon the similarities of both strengths and challenges of each clinic. Implementation of IBH has the potential to lower healthcare costs and create a holistic approach to better health outcomes Successful IBH programs can provide evidence that impact how government funding policies make Medicaid and insurance companies compensate clinics who are implementing IBH.
