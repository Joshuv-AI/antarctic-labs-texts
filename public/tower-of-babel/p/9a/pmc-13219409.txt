Prevalence and Risk Factors for Cytomegalovirus Retinitis Among People Living with HIV in Sub-Saharan Africa in the Antiretroviral Therapy Era: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Mohamed Farah Ismail, Intisar Khalafalla, Zakaria Omar Sheck, Fathi Abdi Farah, Abdikarim Ahmed Warsame, Ibrahim Abdirahman Mohamed, Amina Mohamed Mire, Bahja Ahmed Mumin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytomegalovirus retinitis has remained an alarming and potentially vision-threatening opportunistic disease in the population living with HIV, especially in cases of extreme immunosuppression. Although the introduction of antiretroviral therapy has led to a drastic decline in the number of cytomegalovirus retinitis cases reported in most parts of the world, the current epidemiology of cytomegalovirus retinitis in sub-Saharan Africa has seemed unusual in early reports, unexpectedly portraying low disease prevalence rates despite the high number of HIV cases in the concerned region. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of cytomegalovirus retinitis among people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa in the antiretroviral therapy era, and to identify associated risk factors. The search of databases included Medline using the search engine PubMed, Google Scholar, and the African Journals Online database named AJOL. For inclusion, studies must have included an eye examination to verify the presence of cytomegalovirus retinitis. The data was combined using the logit transformation model. Heterogeneity was tested using the I-squared test and the Cochran Q test. A total of ten studies involving 1,931 participants from nine sub-Saharan Africa countries were found eligible. The proportion of cytomegalovirus retinitis varied from 0.34% to 15.8%. Pooled prevalence was found to be 3% (95% CI: 1.6–5.4%) with low to moderate inter-study variation (I² = 17.8%). Substantial variation in the proportion was found in different sub-Saharan Africa regions; it was found to be less than 1% in West Africa, 3–4% in Southern Africa, 7% in East Africa and 6% in Central Africa. Across all studies, the strongest and most consistent risk factors for cytomegalovirus retinitis were profound immunosuppression, particularly CD4 < 50 cells/µL. Antiretroviral therapy–naïve status, poor adherence, and presence of advanced AIDS-defining illnesses were additional contextual risk markers. Cytomegalovirus retinitis is an under-recognized but important source of preventable blindness among people living with HIV. Prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa differed from that in Asian early antiretroviral therapy era cohorts but is relevant in settings where late HIV presentation occurs. Introduction

Cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR) stands out as the most frequent form of ocular opportunistic infections in patients with advanced HIV/AIDS. It can be characterized by the absence of the retinal parenchyma due to necrotizing retinitis,” leading to retinal detachment and incurable blindness1,2. Before the widely successful antiretroviral therapy in the mid-1990s, CMVR was prevalent in 20% to 30% of AIDS patients in developed countries, and “CMV infections of the eye contributed to most cases of blindness due to HIV infection.” CMVR incidence was lowered by greater than 75% in developed nations after the widespread use of antiretroviral therapy in the mid-1990s, translating to “CMVR becoming from then on an unusual occurrence in the course of immune reconstitution in most patients2,3”.

Despite these factors, the epidemiology of CMVR in resource-poor environments, especially in SSA, has always presented differently from that in developed Western countries. In fact, initial SSA reports from the pre-ART and early-ART era reported unexpectedly low incidences of CMVR in the face of high HIV prevalence4. For example, in 1994, in Malawi, CMVR was detected in merely one out of 99 hospitalized AIDS patients (1%)5; other SSA reports in the 1990 s presented similar prevalence rates (0–2%) in West African cohorts4. The most likely explanation at that time related to the vast number of African HIV-infected patients dying from other opportunistic events such as TB, thereby preventing the onset of CMVR due to advanced immune suppression, in addition to possibly less developed ophthalmological care in SSA6, resulting in underreporting. However, following the increased ART rollouts in SSA in the early 2000 s, there was growing concern about CMVR potentially becoming an unidentified cause of blindness; in fact, due to expanded ART rollout in SSA, survivor function could lead to increased numbers of CMVR in the absence of preventive strategies, since ART effectively prolonged the lifespan of HIV-infected patients with low CD4 counts7.

Over the preceding two decades, there have been numerous observational studies in diverse African nations investigating the prevalence of CMVR in the era of ART. These studies included some that portrayed CMVR as remaining extremely rare in Africa (prevalence ranging from 0.34% in Nigeria and Ghana8,9 or greater in other settings (prevalence ranging from 6 to 8% in Eastern and Central Africa). One systematic review conducted in 2013, including 18 studies from Africa, reported a pooled prevalence of CMVR of 2.2% (95% CI 1.3–3.1%), which was substantially lower than estimates reported in Asia and Latin America2. It included many studies from pre- or early ART eras, although it was not SSA-focused or centered around risk factor assessment. It was since followed by further accumulating evidence from the current era of ART when widespread viral suppression might reduce the incidence of CMVR further or equivalently, when advanced HIV presentations with severely low CD4 counts continue to pose challenges in SSA.

Rationale

There is a need for an up-to-date synthesis of the prevalence of CMVR and its risk factors in SSA because of its relevance to the planning of eye care services in PLHIV. An understanding of the current burden of CMVR disease in SSA can help in the development of strategies related to ophthalmological screening, such as whether retinal examination needs to be undertaken in all patients with low CD4 counts, in addition to allocation of resources related to the treatment of CMVR using intravitreal injections of ganciclovir or oral valganciclovir in HIV programs in SSA. Patient characteristics associated with CMVR can help in risk factor analysis.

Notably, the main identified predictor of CMVR in the existing literature remains profound immunosuppression (CD4 < 50 cells/µL)1,10. It appears unclear whether secondary factors such as treatment nonadherence or certain co-infections or population characteristics increase the risk of CMVR in the African population. There have been some African studies suggesting that patients who acquire CMVR tend to have TB or not be on antiretroviral therapy at the moment of diagnosis, but these have not been quantitatively synthesized9,11.

Objectives

The aim of our review was twofold1: to calculate the pooled prevalence of CMV retinitis in people living with HIV in SSA post-ART; and2 to explore and pool risk factors or associations with CMVR found in the literature. By centering our analysis since 2000 when ART treatment was becoming more widely accessible in SSA, our aim is to offer current data in SSA regarding whether CMVR in Africa represents an underappreciated issue or whether it truly represents a rare occurrence or whether the presumed “neglected disease” of CMV retinitis in the HIV population in SSA was being effectively alleviated by ART.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

We sought peer-reviewed literature (or academic thesis) estimates of the prevalence of CMV retinitis in HIV-infected individuals in sub-Saharan Africa. The observational studies eligible included cross-sectional studies, prospective or retrospective cohorts, or case series with reporting of the number of HIV-infected patients in whom CMVR was sought. They could include adults or children infected with HIV, with or without antiretroviral treatment, provided there was reporting of the proportion with CMV retinitis. There had to be a diagnostic eye examination (usually dilated fundus examination by an ophthalmologist) to verify the occurrence of CMVR. There was no date or language restriction, but all studies had to be published since 2000 when the availability of antiretroviral treatment in SSA expanded in SSA. Case reports and trials had to be excluded in order to eliminate biases related to selection. Also excluded would be papers focused entirely on illnesses or deaths in specified subsets of HIV-infected (e.g., those presenting with symptoms in the eye or those in which diagnoses were made post-mortem), rather than in the larger population in order to prevent such biases.

Clinical trials were excluded because they often involve selected populations and intervention protocols that do not reflect the natural prevalence of CMVR in routine clinical settings. Inclusion of such studies could therefore introduce selection bias and distort prevalence estimates.

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted to identify studies reporting the prevalence of cytomegalovirus retinitis among people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed (via MEDLINE), Google Scholar, African Journals Online (AJOL), and institutional repositories, including university thesis databases and the World Health Organization Global Health Library.

The search covered studies published from January 2000 to September 2025, corresponding to the period of expanded availability of antiretroviral therapy in sub-Saharan Africa. A combination of controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) and free-text terms was used. The search strategy included terms related to HIV infection (“HIV” OR “AIDS”), cytomegalovirus retinitis (“cytomegalovirus retinitis” OR “CMV retinitis”), and geographic identifiers (“Africa,” “sub-Saharan Africa,” and individual country names).

The search strategy was intentionally broad to maximize sensitivity and ensure comprehensive identification of relevant studies. Therefore, screening was performed to exclude studies not directly related to cytomegalovirus retinitis in people living with HIV, in accordance with predefined eligibility criteria.

Grey literature was also explored through Google Scholar, institutional repositories, and manual searches of conference abstracts and academic theses to minimize publication bias. In addition, the reference lists of all included studies were screened to identify further eligible articles. Where necessary, corresponding authors were contacted to obtain missing or clarifying data.

The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Study selection and data extraction

After removal of duplicate records, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Studies that did not report on cytomegalovirus retinitis or did not include people living with HIV were excluded at this stage. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved and assessed against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Discrepancies between reviewers during title/abstract screening or full-text assessment were first resolved through discussion and consensus. Where consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer (corresponding author, MFI) acted as an adjudicator. A standardized and predefined protocol for study selection was followed throughout the process to ensure consistency. The same adjudicator was involved in all cases of unresolved disagreement, thereby minimizing variability and enhancing the reliability of the selection process.

Data were extracted using a standardized data extraction form. The following information was collected from each included study: first author, year of publication, country, study design, study setting (e.g., hospital-based or outpatient), sample size of people living with HIV, patient characteristics, antiretroviral therapy (ART) status, CD4 cell count distribution, number of cytomegalovirus retinitis cases, diagnostic methods, and any reported risk factor analyses (e.g., odds ratios).

Where necessary, prevalence estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated from raw data. Subgroup data, including CD4-based analyses, were extracted when available. For studies reporting data from multiple countries, only data relevant to sub-Saharan Africa were included. In cases of overlapping study populations, the study with the most complete data or the largest sample size was included to avoid duplication.

Data on reported risk factors for CMVR (including CD4 count, ART status, co-infections, and clinical characteristics) were extracted qualitatively from included studies where available. Due to heterogeneity in reporting and lack of comparable effect measures, a quantitative meta-analysis of risk factors was not performed.

In addition to prevalence data, information on reported risk factors for cytomegalovirus retinitis (including CD4 count, antiretroviral therapy status, co-infections, and clinical characteristics) was extracted where available. Due to heterogeneity in reporting and lack of comparable effect measures (e.g., odds ratios), risk factors were synthesized qualitatively rather than through quantitative meta-analysis.”

One included study involved a pediatric population. Due to the limited number of pediatric studies, separate subgroup analysis was not feasible. Therefore, pediatric data were pooled with adult data; however, this may introduce heterogeneity, and findings should be interpreted with caution.

Study registration and reporting guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (Registration number: CRD420261345870). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. Data synthesis and analysis

The proportion of participants with cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR) was calculated for each study as the number of CMVR cases divided by the total number of examined HIV-positive individuals. To stabilize variances for studies with very low or zero event rates, pooled prevalence was estimated using a logit transformation under a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method).

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, heterogeneity, respectively.

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimate, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed, with specific assessment of the influence of the Ugandan meningitis cohort (Molleti et al., 2025).

A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding non-peer-reviewed studies to evaluate their influence on the pooled prevalence estimate.

Publication bias was assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Effect sizes in the forest plot are presented on the logit-transformed scale to stabilize variance for proportions close to zero; negative values reflect low prevalence estimates and are not clinically negative values.”

Subgroup analyses were performed by African sub-region (West, East, Central, and Southern Africa) using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting. This approach was used to explore geographical variation in prevalence estimates.

Subgroup analyses were performed using a random-effects model, with pooled estimates calculated using inverse-variance weighting to account for differences in study size and precision.

All statistical analyses were performed using JASP (version 0.16).

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The search produced 1,247 records, of which 68 underwent detailed evaluation after the initial screening (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). In all, ten studies from nine countries included in the meta-analysis passed the criteria of fulfillable inclusion. Data collection spanned about 1,931 HIV-infected persons who underwent an examination of the eyes related to CMVR. Data was published from 2011 to 2025, which generally included data from the era of antiretroviral treatment following 2005. In terms of geographical distribution, there included studies Were West Africa (Ghana8, Nigeria9,), East Africa (Uganda12, Rwanda13, Tanzania14, and Ethiopia15, Central Africa (Sudan16, included under SSA) and Southern Africa (South Africa3,17 and Malawi10. Each included trial had sample sizes ranging from 50 to 303 cases (median sample size of approximately 100 participants). In four cases, there was hospital-based analysis concerning AIDS patients reported from diverse manifestations related to the eyes, but all studies included all HIV-infected patients without any symptom concerning first intention related to the eyes. Patients generally demonstrated advanced HIV disease, with mean or median CD4 counts generally under 100 cells per microliter in all cases, with most not-treated or newer in antiretroviral treatment.

Diagnostic methods

CMVR was identified by typical fundoscopic findings (including retinal hemorrhages and necrotizing lesions) in one or both eyes, in accordance with published definitions of cytomegalovirus retinitis based on characteristic retinal features2,4. In some studies, indirect ophthalmoscopy was performed in all participants with low CD4 counts (< 100 or < 50 cells/µL). Only one study confirmed CMV using polymerase chain reaction of ocular fluid, reported in a South African case series.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies.

Using the JBI appraisal tool, most studies had medium quality. Similarly, most studies had issues relating to the sampling process, including the absence of truly random sampling—e.g., sampling from an eye clinic, where there could be an overrepresentation of people due to biases in symptom prevalence. Three studies focused on general HIV screening regardless of any symptoms in the eyes. The loss to follow-up was shown to not apply in these analyzes due to the point prevalence analysis applied. Not one of these studies was excluded from analysis due to quality.

Pooled prevalence of cmv retinitis

Across the ten included studies, 99 cases of CMV retinitis were identified among 1,931 HIV-infected individuals. The pooled prevalence of CMVR in sub-Saharan Africa was 3.2% (95% CI: 1.6%–5.4%) under the random-effects logit model.

Between-study heterogeneity was low (I² = 17.8%, Q = 9.22, p = 0.416), indicating that most of the observed variation was attributable to sampling error rather than true differences between study populations.

Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that exclusion of the Ugandan meningitis cohort reduced the pooled prevalence to 2.4% (95% CI: 1.3%–4.0%), confirming that the overall estimate was not driven by a single outlier study. Exclusion of non-peer-reviewed studies in sensitivity analysis did not significantly alter the pooled prevalence estimate. Additionally, exclusion of studies classified as high risk of bias (e.g., Mussa et al., 2016) did not materially change the pooled estimate, indicating that the findings are robust to variations in study quality.

Risk factors and subgroup findings

Risk factors were synthesized narratively across studies In all the studies, the identified pivotal risk factor was the extent of immunodeficiency in respect to CMV retinitis (CMVR). Indeed, in all published studies, CMVR was found to appear in profoundly low CD4 counts. For example, from Malawi, all five reported cases of CMVR have come from patients whose mean CD4 counts were < 50 cells/µL; mean CD4 ≈ 15 cells/µL10. Adeiza & Habib (2019)9 from Nigeria found that all cases of CMVR had mean CD4 < 25 cells/µL compared to mean CD4 ≈ 300 cells/µL in non-CMVR patients; all five cases of CMVR advanced WHO stage 4 disease9. There are studies where all cases of CMVR failed to occur above specified thresholds of CD4 counts: Shi et al. (2011)11 in China found 0% cases of retinitis in patients who had CD4 ≥ 200, but < 2% in those having 100 < CD4<200 cells/µL; 17% CMVR in those who had < 50 cells/µL11. In corresponding studies from other African countries, there was uniform data indicating that patients who generally progressed to CMVR included those whose CD4 counts remained < 50/µL or in some cases < 100/µL. Indeed, most reports documented examination of the retinas only in cases whose cumulative immune function remained above specified CD4 thresholds9,10.

ART status: CMVR was observed more frequently among individuals who were not receiving ART or had recently initiated treatment. In the Nigerian series, all patients with CMVR remained virologically unsuppressed under ART care; two patients remained ART naive, and in addition, there was one treatment defaulter. All these patients presented with advanced AIDS disease9. In other series reported from South Africa, most CMVR patients had not started treatment despite low CD4 counts owing to late attendance at healthcare facilities17. Although increased ART availability means that some cases of retinitis can present as immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome after the onset of treatment due to immune recovery uveitis, such cases are beyond the scope of all these studies.

This subgroup analysis Subgroup analysis by African region demonstrated important geographical variation in CMV retinitis prevalence. West African studies from Ghana and Nigeria showed the lowest pooled prevalence, below 1%, suggesting either earlier HIV diagnosis, better immune recovery, or possible under-detection. In contrast, East Africa exhibited the highest pooled prevalence at approximately 6.5%, driven largely by data from Uganda, Ethiopia, and Tanzania, where late HIV presentation and profound immunosuppression remain common. Central Africa, represented by Sudan, showed a similarly elevated prevalence of 6.0%. Southern Africa demonstrated an intermediate burden of approximately 4.0%, reflecting wider ART access but persistent late presenters. These findings indicate that CMV retinitis burden in sub-Saharan Africa is unevenly distributed, with the greatest risk concentrated in settings with delayed HIV diagnosis and limited access to ophthalmic screening.

Publication bias was explored using visualization from a funnel plot (Fig. 4). and Egger’s test for publication bias. In Fig. 4, the funnel plot of the ten studies (logit prevalence vs. standard error) is shown. It can be seen that the plot was generally symmetric; that is, larger standard errors (smaller studies) were spread out both above and below the pooled estimate, without any obvious lack of studies in the bottom-right or bottom-left corners suggestive of missing data due to publication bias against negatively or positively biased estimates, respectively. Egger’s test was not significant (p = 0.32), suggesting absence of any significant publication bias. In practical terms, there was no evidence of any systematic under-reporting of low prevalence data. In any case, because there are few studies, these data must be treated with due caution. It is always possible that there has been an underrepresentation of settings reporting zero cases of CMVR in order to appear less mundane, at least in settings where at least one such report (Ghana) was found [3]. In any case, there was no apparent indication of significant publication bias in these data; the data synthesized above most likely are representative of documented CMVR in PLHIV in sub-Saharan Africa in the age of ART.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides updated evidence on the burden of cytomegalovirus retinitis among people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa in the antiretroviral therapy era. The pooled prevalence of approximately 3% indicates that cytomegalovirus retinitis remains a clinically significant and under-recognized opportunistic infection, affecting nearly one in thirty individuals with advanced immunosuppression. Although this burden is lower than that reported in pre-antiretroviral therapy cohorts in Asia and Latin America, it remains clinically important given the severe and often irreversible visual consequences of delayed diagnosis. The burden was not uniformly distributed across regions, with higher prevalence observed in East and Central Africa compared to West Africa. Across all included studies, profound immunosuppression (CD4 < 50 cells/µL) emerged as the most consistent and strongest risk factor, while ART-naïve status and poor adherence were additional contributing factors. These findings suggest that, despite ART scale-up, cytomegalovirus retinitis continues to represent a meaningful cause of preventable blindness in sub-Saharan Africa and warrants greater attention in HIV care program.

The collective data regarding prevalence of about 3% translates to approximately 1 in every 33 HIV-infected adults in SSA with or at risk of having CMVR18.

Comparison with previous studies: The combined prevalence of 3.0% is in keeping with but slightly above the 2.2% (95% CI 1.3–3.1%) reported in the previous review by Ford et al. (2013)2. These small discrepancies could represent the addition of newer studies reporting greater prevalences in individual locations (e.g., Sudan 6%, Uganda 8%), differences in methodology, or the fact that these studies are of sub-Saharan African data collected in the ART era rather than in older or North African data, in which CMVR was reported at close to zero4. Subsequently, one study have reported the prevalence of CMVR in Africa as generally being well under 5% but “distinctly lower than in Asia,…10–20% in most cohorts”19. Some early ART-era hospital-based series from India and other Asian settings reported CMVR prevalence as high as 15–20% among late-stage HIV patients2, whereas in Africa it has remained at or under 8.5%4. These data are supportive of these large geographical differences, suggesting that in the ART era, African series do not reflect the greater CMVR experienced in Asia.

The relatively lower prevalence observed in sub-Saharan Africa compared to Asian cohorts may be explained by several factors. Factors postulated to explain these disparities include competing mortality from opportunistic infections and health system limitations in sub-Saharan Africa2,4 first, competing mortality from other opportunistic infections such as tuberculosis and cryptococcal meningitis may limit survival to stages where CMVR develops. Second, underdiagnosis due to limited access to ophthalmic screening services may lead to underestimation of the true burden. Third, differences in study populations, including hospital-based versus screening cohorts, and variability in CD4 thresholds for examination, may contribute to heterogeneity in reported prevalence.

Additional factors such as late presentation to care and competing opportunistic infections may further contribute to the observed lower prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa, where patients frequently have concurrent TB or cryptococcal meningitis and succumb to these two illnesses _prior to development of CMVR4,20. In addition, the absence of comprehensive eye care evaluations in HIV treatment in SSA can result in underreporting of less-severe cases or missed diagnoses when patients present after having died from other diseases. CMVR has appropriately been considered “a neglected disease of the AIDS pandemic” in resource-scar settings, insofar as there has appeared to be incommensurate prioritization vis-à-vis actual potential. In conclusion, our analysis emphasizes that because, although prevalence in SSA does not appear to reach the peaks demonstrated in pre-ART eras in developed nations, in fact, non-trivial disease rates persist such that prevalence at 3% in PLHIV in SSA translates to thousands of active cases continent-wide, most of whom go blind without treatment20.

Risk factor implications

The results from this review emphasize that CMV retinitis in sub-Saharan Africa is closely related to the extent of immunosuppression, with a CD4 count less than 50 cells/µL as the most prominent and consistent predictor among all reviewed studies2,4,9,10. This strengthens the current recommendation from WHO and emphasizes the priority for retinal screening to be given to PLHIV patients with advanced immunodeficiency, regardless of the presence or absence of ocular symptoms21.

These findings have important clinical and public health implications. The persistence of cytomegalovirus retinitis in the antiretroviral therapy era may reflect gaps in early HIV diagnosis, timely treatment initiation, and immune recovery2,4. The high proportion of asymptomatic cases further emphasizes that symptom-based screening is insufficient, and targeted retinal examination should be integrated into HIV care for high-risk individuals10,14,21,22.

Based on the available evidence, routine retinal screening should be strongly considered for people living with HIV who have CD4 counts below 50 cells/µL, regardless of symptoms10,14,21. In addition, strengthening antiretroviral therapy programs to ensure early initiation and adherence remains critical to prevent severe immunosuppression2,4. Health systems in sub-Saharan Africa should also improve access to diagnostic ophthalmic services and ensure availability of anti-cytomegalovirus therapies such as valganciclovir to reduce preventable blindness4,21.

Notably, the most important clinical observation from these studies was that a significant number of affected individuals were asymptomatic at presentation10,14,22. This underscores the key point that relying on symptoms or patient-reported visual complaints alone is grossly inadequate and will miss the majority of cases10,21. Routine fundus examination for PLHIV with CD4 < 50 cells/µL represents a straightforward, high-yield strategy to detect CMVR early and prevent irreversible visual loss10,14,21.

Overall, these findings suggest that CMVR in SSA continues to be a disease of advanced uncontrolled HIV infection2,4. The most effective long-term strategies to reduce the risk of CMVR will continue to be strengthening early diagnosis of HIV infection, timely initiation of ART, and immune recovery2,4,21. However, in the short run, targeted screening among individuals with severe immunosuppression is critical for improved visual outcomes10,14,21.

Regional variations:

Subgroup analysis by African region demonstrated variation in CMV retinitis prevalence across sub-Saharan Africa. West African studies (Ghana and Nigeria) showed the lowest pooled prevalence, below 1%, whereas East and Central African studies demonstrated higher prevalence estimates (approximately 6–7%). Southern Africa showed intermediate prevalence levels.

These regional subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on a limited number of studies and were not powered for formal comparative analysis. Therefore, these analyses are considered exploratory.

The observed differences may reflect variations in study populations, healthcare access, timing of HIV diagnosis, or availability of ophthalmic screening services; however, these interpretations remain speculative and cannot establish causality.

Also, seroprevalence of CMVs and virulent factors can lead to variations—although there was little data, seroprevalence was likely high in adults in most of Africa, suggesting widespread exposure2,4. Also, there might have been inadequate power in the analysis concerning interregional variation; there was likely helpful information in other under-investigated regions, such as Central Africa. It was important to note that Sudan with 6% prevalence was representative of East African data and appeared suggestive of late presentation.

Strengths and weaknesses of the review:

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis in sub-Saharan Africa targeting the literature up to 2025, including risk factors. In fact, the search was comprehensive and included gray literature (one of the included data sets was from Sudanese Master of Philosophy thesis).

To deal with the zero-cell problem, the data was transformed using logit. Although there are strengths, there are some weaknesses too. In fact, the sample size was small at about 1,200 in ten studies; hence, low power and large prediction intervals. Indeed, the pooled prevalence estimate needs to be treated with care and viewed in context as it may not generalize to all locations.

There could be potential selection bias in these primary studies: some included symptomatic cases or patients referred to the ophthalmology department potentially leading to increased prevalence compared to HIV patients in the general population. Under-diagnosis in the current care setting could cause underestimation. The analysis is limited because of variance in ART inclusion in these studies—some cohorts included preemptive screening in ART-naive patients, whereas other cohorts included both treated and untreated patients. It was not possible to stratify these analyses based on ART because there was no individual data. Heterogeneity may result from diagnostic workup using retinal photographs.

The definitions of risk factors differed in the studies, and so a qualitative synthesis was required rather than any form of meta-analysis based on odds ratios. However, the fact that all studies found low CD4 count to be the important risk factor confirms the robustness of the result.

The geographical distribution was uneven: there were few or no eligible studies in large countries like Ethiopia and the DR Congo; these countries likely have undocumented cases. This highlights the under-investigation of CMVR in SSA.

The inclusion of one pediatric study may influence prevalence estimates, as CMVR epidemiology differs between children and adults. However, due to limited pediatric data, stratified analysis was not possible.

Conclusion

In the context of expanding antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage in sub-Saharan Africa, cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR) remains an important cause of preventable visual impairment among people living with HIV, particularly those with advanced immunosuppression. The estimated pooled prevalence of approximately 3% indicates a clinically meaningful burden, with prevalence ranging from 0% to 5–8% in high-risk populations. Although lower than that reported in pre-ART eras and in other regions, CMVR in sub-Saharan Africa is likely underrecognized and underdiagnosed, often presenting at advanced stages of disease21.

Profound immunosuppression, particularly CD4 counts < 50 cells/µL, remains the most critical prognostic factor, underscoring the need for targeted retinal screening in high-risk individuals. Early detection through routine dilated fundus examination—either by ophthalmologists or trained HIV care providers—combined with timely treatment can significantly reduce the risk of irreversible blindness19.

Strengthening ART programs to ensure early diagnosis, prompt initiation, and sustained adherence remains essential to prevent severe immunosuppression and reduce CMVR incidence. In addition, improving access to diagnostic ophthalmic services and ensuring the availability of effective anti-cytomegalovirus therapies, such as valganciclovir, are critical priorities for health systems in sub-Saharan Africa.

Policy efforts should focus on integrating CMVR screening and treatment into national HIV care programs, recognizing CMVR as a preventable cause of blindness in advanced HIV disease. Although ART has substantially reduced the burden of CMVR, it has not eliminated the disease. A significant proportion of individuals—particularly those presenting late or lost to follow-up—remain at risk. Coordinated strategies for early detection, treatment, and prevention are therefore essential to address this neglected complication of HIV in the ART era.
