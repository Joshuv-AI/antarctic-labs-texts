Exploring Thymus vulgaris Extract as a Phytotherapeutic Agent: A Multifaceted Approach to tackle Avian Colibacillosis and Drug Resistance
Authors: Sarah Saci, El-hafid Nabti, Hillal Sebbane, Hakima Ait Issad, Fehmi Boufahja, Luisa De Martino, Francesca Paola Nocera, Hamdi Bendif, Assia Derguini, Naoufel Ben Hamadi, Anis A. Chaudhary, Ahmed Rebai, Cristina Cruz, Takfarinas Idres, Karim Houali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Avian colibacillosis remains a major threat to poultry production and food security, whereas its antibiotic-based control accelerates antimicrobial resistance. This study investigated the antibacterial potential of the hydroethanolic extract of Thymus vulgaris, alone and in combination with antibiotics, against avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC). The phytochemical composition of the extract was analyzed by HPLC, while its antibacterial activity was assessed using agar diffusion and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays. Antibiotic–extract interactions were evaluated by the checkerboard method, and mechanisms of action were explored through assays targeting membrane permeability, proton pump inhibition, catalase inhibition, oxidative stress, and biofilm formation. Its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic activities were also evaluated. HPLC analysis revealed 16 phenolic compounds, including rosmarinic, ferulic, and salicylic acids, as well as flavonoids such as quercetin and luteolin, with an extraction yield of 15% ± 0.55. The extract displayed significant antibacterial activity (MIC: 5.46–10.93 mg/ml, p < 0.05). In synergy tests, the extract enhanced ampicillin efficacy 4–8-fold and showed additive effects with ciprofloxacin and tetracycline (2–4-fold). Mechanistic assays demonstrated disruption of bacterial membranes (32.66% electrolyte leakage, p < 0.05), inhibition of H⁺-ATPase proton pumps (p < 0.05), catalase activity reduction by 79.2% (p < 0.05), >50% decrease in cell hydrophobicity, and inhibition of biofilm formation (49.07–72.47%), alongside eradication of mature biofilms (41.89–64.67%) (p < 0.05). Beyond antimicrobial effects, the extract exhibited notable antioxidant capacity. In DPPH radical scavenging, the extract showed an IC₅₀ of 9.76 ± 0.228 μg/ml (p < 0.05), while in the TAC assay, it reached 467.25 ± 1.889 μg/ml (p < 0.05), and it reduced ampicillin-induced lipid peroxidation by 42.85% (p < 0.05). Anti-inflammatory testing revealed inhibition of protein denaturation (4.95–52.48% at 15–2000 µg/ml, p < 0.05), although weaker than aspirin. Hemolysis assays confirmed the extract was non-hemolytic at concentrations up to 11 mg/ml (p < 0.05). In conclusion, Thymus vulgaris extract demonstrated safe, multi-target bioactivity, supporting its potential as a promising alternative to combat avian colibacillosis and antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Global chicken meat production continues to rise to meet increasing demand, reaching 139.68 million tonnes in 2023 (Dong et al., 2024). The United States, China, Brazil, and the European Union are the main producers (Saraiva et al., 2022). In Algeria, the poultry industry has expanded considerably since the 2000s, covering around 60% of national consumption, estimated at 25 kg/year per capita. Despite its socio-economic importance, this sector faces major health challenges, notably avian colibacillosis (Joseph et al., 2023).

Avian colibacillosis is one of the most common and economically significant bacterial diseases in poultry worldwide. It is caused by APEC, extraintestinal pathogenic strains capable of inducing localized or systemic infections in birds. The disease often manifests as respiratory tract infections, pericarditis, perihepatitis, salpingitis, and coligranulomatosis, and may progress to septicemia in severe cases (Kathayat et al., 2021; Saci et al., 2025). Colibacillosis primarily affects broilers and laying hens, leading to high morbidity and mortality, reduced performance, and increased carcass condemnation at slaughter (Watts et Wigley, 2024). The infection is usually favored by predisposing factors such as poor housing conditions, stress, viral or mycoplasmal co-infections, and immunosuppression (Kabir, 2010). Because of its multifactorial nature and major economic burden, avian colibacillosis represents a critical challenge for the poultry industry, requiring integrated strategies for prevention, control, and treatment (Kabir, 2010).

In many regions, including Africa, the European Union, and the USA, between 50% and 80% of antibiotics are administered to animals (Xu et al., 2022). In poultry production, antibiotics are used in different ways: therapeutic antibiotics are given at full doses for a short period to treat infections; subtherapeutic antibiotics are administered at lower doses, often over longer durations, to reduce morbidity and prevent disease occurrence; and antimicrobial growth promoters (AGPs), also a subtherapeutic application, are incorporated into feed at low concentrations to enhance growth, feed efficiency, and yield (Abreu et al., 2023; Enshaie et al., 2025). However, the extensive and prolonged use of subtherapeutic antibiotics and AGPs is strongly associated with the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, a phenomenon with serious implications for human, animal, and environmental health (Abreu et al., 2023; Salam et al., 2023).

Faced with this alarming situation, there is an urgent need to identify sustainable and effective alternatives to antibiotics, both to combat avian colibacillosis and to limit the spread of antimicrobial resistance (Abreu et al., 2023). Several promising approaches have been investigated, including the use of vaccines, probiotics, antimicrobial organic acids, and medicinal plants with antibacterial properties (Kathayat et al., 2021). Among these, medicinal plants such as Thymus vulgaris (common thyme) appear particularly promising. This aromatic plant, native to southern Europe, belongs to the Lamiaceae family and is characterized by its small, aromatic leaves and violet to pink flowers (Galovičová et al., 2021). For centuries, thyme has been valued in traditional medicine for its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects (Halat et al., 2022), properties now supported by experimental research in poultry.

Several in vivo studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with thyme essential oil or powder reduces the colonization of pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella in broilers (Dardouri et al., 2025; Salinas et Barrios, 2024). Beyond its antimicrobial properties, thyme also displays anti-inflammatory and immunostimulatory effects, as evidenced by Awad et Hassan (2017), who demonstrated in vivo that dietary thyme supplementation alleviates intestinal inflammation and enhances immune responses in broiler chickens Moreover, its growth-promoting effect has been clearly proven in several studies, highlighting thyme’s potential as a natural alternative to conventional growth promoters in poultry farming (Ashour et al., 2024; Hassan et al., 2024) .

This study aims to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the hydroethanolic extract of Thymus vulgaris, both alone and in combination with antibiotics commonly used in poultry production, against avian pathogenic E. coli isolates. Additionally, it seeks to elucidate the extract’s mechanisms of action, including its effects on bacterial membrane integrity, enzymatic activity, oxidative stress, and biofilm formation. The study also investigates its anti-inflammatory properties, which may play an important role in controlling bacterial infections. Ultimately, this research aims to contribute to the development of sustainable therapeutic alternatives for avian health and to address the growing issue of antimicrobial resistance, a major public health concern in Algeria and worldwide.

MATERIAL and methods

Plant collection

Thymus vulgaris leaves were collected during the flowering period in 2022 in Maatkas, Tizi-Ouzou (36° 34′ 17" N; 4° 18′ 35" E). Botanical identification was conducted by Dr. Hocine Abbaci from the University of Bejaia (Algeria). A sample was deposited in Mouloud Mammeri University's (UMMTO) herbarium under the reference number 2023/UMMTO/59. After cleaning and drying the leaves in the dark, they were carefully ground with an electric grinder and stored for later use.

Preparation of the extract

The extract was prepared according to the protocol described by Luca et al (2023). In brief, 10 g of plant material was macerated in 100 mL of solvent (a 70:30 v/v mixture of ethanol and water) for 24 h while being agitated and protected from light. After 24 h of continuous agitation, the extract was separated from the marc using a strainer, and the resulting liquid was filtered through Wattman paper. The organic solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator at 45°C/180 mbar, and the resulting aqueous residue was lyophilized to produce a dry extract. The dried extract was stored in airtight bottles and refrigerated until used. The yield percentage of each extract was calculated using the equation:Yield(%)=(weightofthelyophilizate/weightofthedriedplantpowder)×100

HPLC analysis

The chromatographic analysis of the extracts was performed using an AGILENT 1100 series HPLC (High-performance liquid chromatography) coupled with a UV-Visible diode array detector (DAD G1315B) at different wavelengths. Five microliters of the extract (100 mg/ml) dissolved in methanol were injected onto a reverse-phase C18 column (250 * 4.6 mm) with a porosity of 5 μm. The mobile phase consisted of two eluents: acidified water (0.2% acetic acid, pH 3.1) and acetonitrile. The separation was carried out at room temperature with a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, using a linear elution gradient over 30 min starting with 95% H2O and ending with 100% acetonitrile. The various compounds were identified by comparing their peak retention time (tR) to that of their respective standards.

Agar diffusion test. The antibacterial activity of the extract was evaluated using a disk diffusion test, following Ghaly et al. (2023) protocol. Mueller Hinton agar plates were swabbed with a bacterial suspension of 108 CFU/mL. 6 mm diameter Whatman filter paper disks, impregnated with 10 μl of the hydroethanolic extract at the saturating concentration, were placed on the agar. Positive controls were antibiotic disks, and negative controls were water-soaked disks. After incubating at 37°C for 24 h the inhibition zones were measured. The antibacterial activity was evaluated according to Tafroji et al. (2022) classification, where zones > 20 mm were considered very strong, 15–20 mm strong, 10–14 mm moderate, 7–9 mm weak, and <7 mm indicated no antibacterial activity.

Determination of MIC of the extract. The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of a hydroethanolic plant extract was determined as described by Manandhar et al. (2019), using a method based on serial dilution, following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Initially, a stock solution of the extract was prepared, and serial dilutions were made ranging from 700 mg/ml to 0.49 mg/ml using Mueller-Hinton broth. Bacterial suspensions containing 106 CFU/ml were inoculated into each well, followed by incubation for 18 h at 37°C. Resazurin solution (0.015%) was then added to each well as an indicator of bacterial growth, and the plates were further incubated for 3 h (Elshikh et al., 2016).

Determination of antibiotic MICs. Antibiotic minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined following CLSI guidelines (2020). Serial dilutions of the antibiotic were prepared with 25 µl in a microplate. To each well, 5 µl of a bacterial suspension (107 CFU/mL) was added, followed by 70 µl of Mueller-Hinton broth (Oxoid) to reach a final volume of 100 µl. Control wells for sterility and growth were also included. Following an 18-h incubation at 37°C, 30 µl of a 0.015% resazurin (Sigma) solution was added to each well to indicate bacterial growth. The plates were further incubated at 37°C for 3 h (Elshikh et al., 2016).

Evaluation of the efficacy of antibiotic-plant extract combinations (as antibiotic-plant combination therapy). The study evaluated the effectiveness of combinations using a checkerboard synergy test method described by Sagar et al. (2020). Extract and antibiotic concentrations were serially diluted and added to wells in a 96-well microplate along with a bacterial suspension. After incubation, a resazurin solution was added to indicate bacterial growth. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was determined based on the lowest concentration of antibiotics and plant extracts that showed no change in the color of resazurin. The FICI values were calculated for each agent using the following formula:FICI=ΣFIC=FIC(antibiotic)+FIC(plantextract)

Where: FIC (antibiotic) = Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the antibiotic in combination / MIC of the antibiotic alone

FIC (extract) = MIC of the extract in combination / MIC of the extract alone

FICI values categorized synergy (FICI < 0.5), partial synergy (0.5-0.75), additive effect (0.76-1), indifference (1-4), and antagonism (>4).

Study of the modes of action of the thyme extract by various methods

Action on membrane permeability. The crystal violet assay was applied to study membrane permeability alteration according to the protocol described by Da Cruz et al. (2021). Briefly, E coli 29 suspensions were prepared in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI). The cells were harvested after centrifugation (3500 g, 10 min, 4°C), washed, and resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4). The bacterial cell suspension in buffer was treated with the extract at MIC concentration and with ampicillin, followed by incubation (18 h, 37°C). Similarly, control samples were prepared in the same manner without treatment. Another centrifugation (4500 g, 10 min) was performed to collect the cells, followed by resuspension in PBS containing 10 µg/mL crystal violet. The solution was then incubated for 10 min at 37°C, followed by centrifugation (4500 × g, 15 min), and the OD590 of the supernatant was measured. The OD value of the crystal violet solution initially used in the assay was taken and considered to be 100%. The percentage of crystal violet (CV) uptake by the tested samples was calculated using the following formula:(2)%CVuptake=100−[ODvalueofthesample/ODvalueofthecrystalvioletsolution]×100

Measurement of relative conductivity. The permeability of the bacterial membrane is expressed in terms of relative electrical conductivity according to the method described by Sadiq et al. (2017). A culture of the E. coli strain was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min, then washed with 5% glucose until the electrical conductivity was close to that of 5% glucose, indicating isotonic bacteria. The electrical conductivity was measured using an electrical conductometer. The extract at the MIC concentration was added to 5% glucose; the electrical conductivity of the mixture was measured and recorded as L1. Next, the extract at the MIC concentration was added to the isotonic bacteria. After thorough mixing, the samples were incubated at 37°C for 10 h; the conductivity was measured every 2 h and recorded as L2. The conductivity of the bacteria in 5% glucose treated in boiling water for 5 min was used as a control, recorded as L0. The permeability of the cell membrane was calculated using the following formula:Relativeelectricalconductivity(%)=100×(L2−L1)/L0

Evaluation of the effect of the extract on H+-mediated proton pumps. The method described by Kom et al. (2021). involved an 18-h culture of E. coli strain in broth, which was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min. The resulting pellet was washed with 50 mM KCl and then resuspended in 50 mL of 50 mM KCl. The suspension was then stored at 4°C for 18 h for glucose deprivation, and the pH was adjusted to 6,4. The extract dissolved in distilled water was added to this solution to obtain concentrations equivalent to the MIC of the extract. After a 10 min pre-incubation at 37°C, acidification of the medium was induced by adding a 20% glucose solution. Glucose was rapidly metabolized, resulting in the release of protons into the medium. Over a period of 2 h, the pH of the medium was measured every 20 min. The recorded pH values were used to plot pH variation curves over time. Inhibition of medium acidification in the presence of the extract was attributed to an inhibitory effect on the functioning of ATPase/H+-mediated proton pumps by the extract.

Effect of the extract on catalase activity in E. coli. The evaluation of the effect of the extract on catalase activity was conducted according to the method of Swant et al. (2023). Fresh colonies of E. coli 29 were cultured overnight in BHI. Additionally, fresh colonies were cultured in a mixture of 2 mL of the extract at MIC concetration and 2 mL of BHI. After incubation, 800 µL of each test sample with an Optical Density (OD) of 0.2 were transferred into test tubes. Then, 400 µL of 2% Triton X- and 800 µL of 10% (v/v) H2O2 were added to each tube. Following thorough mixing, the tubes were incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Once the reaction ceased and the foam height remained constant for 5 min, it was measured in centimeters using a ruler. The percentage change in foam height for each sample was calculated using the formula:(4)%change=[(heightoffoam(exposedcells)−heightoffoam(unexposedcontrol))/heightoffoam(unexposedcontrol)]×100

Evaluation of membrane oxidative stress induced by the extract. The level of lipid peroxidation was assessed by the concentration of malondialdehyde using thiobarbituric acid as described by Swant et al. (2023). The cultures of E. coli were treated with the extract, ampicillin or left untreated. After 24 h of incubation, 1 mL of the respective test samples, 1 mL of thiobarbituric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Gillingham, UK), and 1 mL of trichloroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Gillingham, UK) were added into screw-cap test tubes. Two controls were prepared, one with 1 mL of water instead of the test sample, and another with 1 mL of water instead of TBA. After thoroughly mixing the contents, the tubes were placed in a water bath at 100°C for 10 min. After cooling, the tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was measured at 535 nm against the first control, which contained only TBA and no test sample. Additionally, the obtained values were subtracted from the second control. The final value measures the amount of malondialdehyde. The concentration of malondialdehyde was calculated based on the millimolar absorbance coefficient equal (E0) to 156 L/cm mmol using the equation below:Malondialdehydeconcentration(mM)={Absorbanceofthesample(exposedcells)−Blank}/{E0(156)}

Evaluation of the effect of the extract on the hydrophobicity of E. coli. This protocol aims to evaluate the effect of a plant extract on the surface hydrophobicity of E. coli using the BATH (bacterial adherence to hydrocarbon) test as described by Sedláková et al. (2011) with modifications. To do this, an 18-h bacterial suspension of the E. coli strains is mixed with xylene (hydrocarbon), then homogenized by vortex for 120 s and equilibrated for 15 min. The absorbance at 400 nm of the lower aqueous phase is measured after treatment. The same protocol is repeated after a pre-treatment step of the bacterial suspension with the plant extract at MIC concentration to be tested in order to evaluate its effect on the surface hydrophobic characteristic of E. coli strains, a key factor in its adhesion. Hydrophobicity was expressed as the percentage of the applied cell suspension absorbance that had been excluded from the aqueous phase. The percentage expression of bacterial affinity for the organic phase (the degree of hydrophobicity) was calculated as follows:Hydrophobicity(%)=(1−A/A0)×100(%)where:

A0: Absorbance of the bacterial suspension alone before mixing with xylene

A water: Absorbance of the aqueous phase after homogenization with xylene

Test of the antibiofilm activity

Biofilm formation assay. Biofilm formation by APEC isolates was assessed using a crystal violet assay on 96-well microplates, following the methodology outlined by Jardak et al. (2017). with some modifications. In each well of the microplate, 200 µl of strains E15, E29, and E30, diluted to 106 in BHI enriched with 2% glucose, were seeded. A control well containing 200 μl of BHI with 2% glucose was also prepared. After 48 h of incubation at 37°C, the contents of the wells were aspirated, and the wells were washed with physiological water. The formed biofilm was fixed by heating the microplate at 60°C for one hour. Subsequently, 150 μl of 1% crystal violet solution was added to each well and incubated for 15 min. After another round of washing with distilled water, 150 μl of 96% ethanol was added to each well, followed by a 15-min incubation to dissolve the biofilm. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a microplate reader. The biofilm formation capacity was determined by comparing the optical density (OD) of the isolates to that of the control and the cut-off value (ODc), where ODc equals the mean OD of the negative control plus 3 times the standard deviation (SD) of the negative control. Isolates were classified into four groups: no biofilm formation (OD ≤ ODc), low biofilm formation (ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc), moderate biofilm formation (2 × ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc), and strong biofilm formation (OD > 4 × ODc).

Effect of thyme extract on biofilm adhesion and preformed biofilm. The ability of the extract to inhibit biofilm formation and eradicate preformed biofilms was assessed following the methodology described by Ben Abdallah et al. (2023). For the inhibition test, 100 μl of the extract at the MIC were added to the wells of a microplate containing E15, E29, and E30 strains, diluted to 106 CFU/ml in BHIB enriched with 2% glucose. A control well without the extract was also prepared. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, the formed biofilm was assessed, and the inhibition percentage was calculated using the formula:%Inhibition=100−(OD630sample/OD630control)×100.

For the eradication test, E15, E29, and E30 strains were incubated for 48 h at 37°C to allow biofilm formation. Subsequently, the extract at the MIC was added and incubated for an additional 24 h. After labeling, washing, and measuring the absorbance at 630 nm, the biofilm evaluation was conducted using the same formula as previously described.

Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential of thyme extract

TAC assay. The method employed, as detailed by Prieto et al. (1999), relies on the reduction of molybdate ion Mo (VI) to molybdenum Mo (V) by plant antioxidants. This conversion results in the development of a blue-green coloration due to the formation of a phosphate/Mo (V) complex under acidic conditions. To conduct the test, 0.1 ml of either plant extract or a standard solution (ascorbic acid) at concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 μg/ml is mixed with 1 ml of a reagent solution containing 0.6 mM sulfuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, and 4 mM ammonium molybdate. The mixture is vortexed, then incubated at 95°C for 90 min. After cooling, the absorbance is measured at 695 nm. The total antioxidant activity is determined by calculating the IC50 from the resulting linear regression curve.

DPPH assay. The antiradical potential of the extracts was assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical, following the methodology outlined by Santos et al. (2010). DPPH, initially a stable purple radical, undergoes a color change to yellow upon reduction by the extract. In this assay, 1.5 mL of the extract or ascorbic acid (used as a positive control) at concentrations ranging from 1 to 10 μg/ml is mixed with 100 μL of a methanolic solution containing DPPH (0.8 mM). The mixture is then kept in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Following incubation, the absorbance is measured at 517 nm, with each concentration tested in triplicate. The scavenging activity is computed using the formula: scavenging activity (%) = [(Ac – As)/ Ac] × 100, where Ac represents the absorbance of the control and As denotes the absorbance of the sample.

Lipid peroxidation assay. The level of lipid peroxidation was assessed by the concentration of malondialdehyde using thiobarbituric acid as described by Swant et al. (2023). Cultures of E. coli were treated with plant extract, ampicillin, or the combination of extract and ampicillin. Untreated samples were used as controls. After 24 h of incubation, 1 mL of the respective test samples, 1 mL of thiobarbituric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Gillingham, UK), and 1 mL of trichloroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Gillingham, UK) were added into screw-cap test tubes. Two controls were prepared, one with 1 mL of water instead of the test sample, and another with 1 mL of water instead of TBA. After thoroughly mixing the contents, the tubes were placed in a water bath at 100°C for 10 min. After cooling, the tubes were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was measured at 535 nm against the first control, which contained only TBA and no test sample. Additionally, the obtained values were subtracted from the second control. The final value measures the amount of malondialdehyde. The concentration of malondialdehyde was calculated based on the millimolar absorbance coefficient equal (E0) to 156 L/cm mmol using the equation below:Malondialdehydeconcentration(mM)={Absorbanceofthesample(exposedcells)−Blank}/{E0(156)}

Inhibition of BSA denaturation. The anti-inflammatory activity of the extract was studied by using the inhibition of albumin denaturation technique as described by Elkolli et al. (2023) with some modifications. 500 uL of 1% bovine serum albumin was added to different concentrations of plant extract. This mixture was kept at room temperature for 37 °C for 20 min, followed by heating at 80 °C for 20 min. The resulting solution was cooled down to room temperature, and absorbance was recorded at 660 nm. Asprin was taken as a positive control. The experiment was carried out in triplicate, and the percent inhibition of protein denaturation was calculated using the following equation:

%Inhibition = Abs control − Abs test/Abs control × 100 where Abs control = absorbance of the control; Abs test = absorbance of the test sample.

Hemolytic effect of the extract

The cytotoxicity of thyme extract was evaluated by studying its membranolytic effect on red blood cells, following the protocol described by Haddouchi et al. (2016). with some modifications. Briefly, the thyme extract at various concentrations (ranging from 1 to 6 mg/ml) was mixed with erythrocyte suspension at 5%. After incubation for 1 h at 37°C with agitation, the tubes were centrifuged at 700 g for 5 min at 4°C. Simultaneously, saponin was employed as a positive control for hemolysis, at equivalent concentrations to those used for the extract. A negative control containing PBS instead of the extract was tested under identical experimental conditions on the same day. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm, and the percentage of hemolysis was calculated using the formula:Hemolysis(%)=[(Asample−Anegativecontrol)/Apositivecontrol]x100

Where: A sample: absorbance of the extract or saponin A negative control: absorption of the negative control without extract or saponin A positive control is the absorbance of saponin at the concentration demonstrating maximum hemolysis.

The blood used comes from healthy poultry. The experimental protocol was approved by the ethics committee of our university (N°Ethi/UMMTO/26-MAR-2024).

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted utilizing Student's t-test and ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc tests, employing SPSS software version 25. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Yield and HPLC analysis

The extraction yield of Thymus vulgaris by maceration resulted in a yield of 15%±0,55. The obtained biomolecules can be classified into several distinct categories. Firstly, phenolic acids include resorcylic acid, salicylic acid, ferulic acid, rosmarinic acid, m-anisic acid, cinnamic acid, dihydroxycinnamic acid, and synapic acid (Balde, 2018). Secondly, flavonoids, such as spiracoside, are subdivided into flavanols, like catechin, epicatechin, and quercetin, and flavones, such as apigenin and luteolin. Lastly, other compounds include resorcinol (Balde, 2018).

Discussion

Medicinal plants have evolved complex biochemical pathways to produce a remarkable diversity of secondary metabolites that serve critical ecological functions in plant defense mechanisms against pathogens, herbivores, and environmental stressors (Al-Khayri et al., 2023). These specialized compounds, which include phenolics, terpenoids, and alkaloids, have attracted significant attention in pharmaceutical research due to their structural complexity, biological activity, and relatively low toxicity compared to synthetic drugs (Yeshi et al., 2022). Our comprehensive phytochemical characterization using HPLC identified 16 distinct bioactive compounds in the thyme extract, with the profile showing both consistencies and notable variations when compared to previous studies conducted in different geographical regions (Aguilar and Hernández-Brenes, 2015; Rtibi et al., 2019; Soliman et al., 2021). The absence of certain expected flavonoids (naringin, hyperoside, kaempferol) and phenolic acids (quinic acid, gallic acid, syringic acid) that have been routinely reported in other studies (Soliman et al., 2021), coupled with the unexpected presence of resorcinol, epicatechin, sinapic acid, and salicylic acid (Rtibi et al., 2019), underscores the remarkable phytochemical plasticity of thyme in response to environmental factors. These observed chemotypic variations can be attributed to a complex interplay of ecological and methodological factors including climatic conditions, soil composition, altitude, harvest time (which affects plant developmental stage and metabolic activity), as well as differences in extraction protocols and analytical techniques employed (Rathore et al., 2023).

The rich repertoire of phenolic compounds identified in our extract, particularly the flavonoids and phenolic acids, are of considerable pharmacological importance due to their well-documented biological activities (Nouir et al., 2023). These compounds exhibit a multitarget mode of action against microbial pathogens, which helps explain the extract's broad-spectrum efficacy against all tested bacterial strains in our study. Our findings align with previous reports demonstrating thyme's potent inhibitory activity against E. coli (Gedikoğlu et al., 2019), though it's noteworthy that some studies have reported even greater antimicrobial potency (Al-Bayati, 2008), possibly due to differences in phytochemical composition as discussed earlier. At the molecular level, the antimicrobial action of polyphenols involves two primary mechanistic pathways: direct interference with fundamental bacterial processes such as protein synthesis and cell wall biosynthesis, and innovative strategies that circumvent conventional antibiotic resistance mechanisms (Vaou et al., 2022). In parallel, the concerningly high MIC values we observed for conventional antibiotics reflect the substantial resistance developed by E. coli strains, a direct consequence of the intense selective pressure exerted by the widespread and often indiscriminate use of these drugs in intensive poultry production systems (Kousar et al., 2021). In fact, similar resistance trends were documented by Aberkane et al (2023) and Adwan et al (2024) in poultry-associated E. coli, confirming the widespread dissemination of resistance in avian pathogens. This resistance emerges through two principal evolutionary pathways: spontaneous genetic mutations that confer survival advantages under antibiotic pressure, and horizontal gene transfer events that facilitate rapid dissemination of resistance determinants among bacterial populations (Abreu et al., 2023).

In this context of escalating antimicrobial resistance, plant-derived compounds offer a promising alternative approach, particularly for use in agricultural settings where resistance development poses serious challenges (Abreu et al., 2023). An even more innovative strategy involves the judicious combination of plant extracts with conventional antibiotics, which can yield synergistic effects through multiple pharmacological mechanisms (Cheesman et al., 2017). The phytochemical complexity of plant extracts enables them to interact favorably with antibiotics by simultaneously targeting multiple bacterial systems, enhancing membrane permeability, inhibiting resistance enzymes, and potentially improving the pharmacokinetic profiles of co-administered drugs (Atta et al., 2023). This combinatorial approach capitalizes on the broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties of plant extracts while potentially restoring the clinical utility of antibiotics that have been compromised by resistance (Vaou et al., 2022). From a practical perspective, this strategy offers several compelling advantages over traditional drug development pathways, including significantly reduced development costs and timelines, enhanced efficacy against resistant strains through multi-target action, improved pharmacokinetic profiles, and the potential for reduced antibiotic residues in food products due to lower effective doses (Atta et al., 2023; Parent et al., 2020).

A particularly compelling example from our study involves the observed synergy between thyme extract and ampicillin, a β-lactam antibiotic whose clinical utility has been severely eroded by the emergence and spread of resistance mechanisms, especially in poultry production environments (Roth et al., 2019). Our results showing synergy between thyme extract and ampicillin are in agreement with Hossain et al. (2020), who demonstrated that phenolic compounds enhanced the activity of ampicillin against resistant E. coli.

Our data suggest that thyme extract can potentiate ampicillin activity through a multifaceted mechanism of action that operates at several levels: at the molecular level, phenolic compounds appear to interact with multiple bacterial targets, thereby enhancing the action of the antibiotic (Santos et al., 2021; Wagner and Ulrich-Merzenich, 2009); in terms of resistance modulation, polyphenols can inhibit both β-lactamase enzymes and efflux pump activity (Waditzer and Bucar, 2021); and clinically, the combination shows potential for reduced adverse effects at lower effective doses (Wagner and Ulrich-Merzenich, 2009). This multi-pronged synergistic interaction makes the development of concurrent resistance substantially more difficult for bacterial populations, as they would need to simultaneously evolve multiple resistance mechanisms to survive the combined assault (Saoudi et al., 2024; Ayoub, 2020; Da Cruz et al., 2023).

According to our results, the antimicrobial activity of thyme extract appears to primarily target bacterial membrane integrity through the combined action of its diverse phenolic constituents (Maliehe et al., 2023). Similar results were reported by Abdelhamed et al. (2023), who demonstrated that thyme alters membrane permeability and disrupts ionic balance. These observations are consistent with our findings of electrolyte leakage and membrane destabilization.

The partially lipophilic phenolic acids can passively diffuse across bacterial membranes, leading to intracellular acidification and structural disruption of membrane proteins, while also increasing permeability through chelation of divalent cations that are crucial for membrane stability (Lobiuc et al., 2023). Concurrently, the flavonoid components interact with membrane lipids through both hydrophobic interactions with the non-polar core and hydrogen bonding with polar head groups (Lobiuc et al., 2023; Krulwich et al., 2011). These complementary mechanisms collectively result in measurable membrane dysfunction, as evidenced by several experimental indicators: increased uptake of crystal violet dye, which normally has poor membrane penetration (Da Cruz et al., 2023); elevated electrolyte leakage detectable through changes in medium conductivity (Maliehe et al., 2023); and disruption of osmotic balance and membrane fluidity as demonstrated by biophysical assays (Ayoub, 2020).

Beyond these membrane effects, thyme extract significantly disrupts bacterial pH homeostasis through potent inhibition of H+/ATPase proton pumps (Kabała and Janicka, 2023; Saci et al., 2024b). Comparable results were obtained by Kom et al. (2021), who demonstrated that plant phenolics compromise proton motive force in Gram-negative bacteria, reinforcing our mechanistic interpretation. This inhibition occurs through two distinct but complementary mechanisms: direct binding and interference with pump proteins, and indirect alteration of membrane properties that affect pump function. The consequences of this inhibition are physiologically profound, as it compromises several essential bacterial functions including ATP synthesis through disruption of proton motive force, nutrient transport systems that rely on proton gradients, and cellular osmoregulation mechanisms (Kabała and Janicka, 2023; Saci et al., 2024).

The extract's impact on bacterial oxidative defense systems is particularly noteworthy from a therapeutic perspective. Through what appears to be competitive inhibition of catalase enzymes (Reiner, 2010), thyme extract prevents the detoxification of hydrogen peroxide, leading to dangerous intracellular accumulation of reactive oxygen species that cause oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids (Li et al., 2021; Spooner and Yilmaz, 2011). Interestingly, while inducing this internal oxidative stress, the extract paradoxically protects bacterial membranes from peroxidative damage (Mourenza et al., 2020), demonstrating a remarkable selectivity in its mechanism of action that targets pathogenic bacteria while potentially sparing host tissues, a significant advantage over many conventional antimicrobials that often generate ROS affecting both pathogens and host cells indiscriminately. Notably, our finding that thyme extract simultaneously protects bacterial membranes from peroxidative damage aligns with Abdel-Aziem et al. (2024), who demonstrated the membrane-protective effect of thyme.

Our investigation of thyme extract's antibiofilm activity showed that the extract significantly inhibited biofilm formation, eradicated mature biofilms, and reduced cell surface hydrophobicity, which impairs the initial attachment phase crucial for biofilm establishment. Our results regarding the impairment of initial adhesion are in strong agreement with the work of Anokwah et al. (2023) similarly demonstrated an inhibition of bacterial adhesion ranging from 49% to 77% for E. coli. Furthermore, our observation that the extract disrupts established biofilms aligns with and extends the findings of Bessalah et al. (2023), who reported that phenolic compounds degraded more than 50% of the biofilm matrix integrity of E. coli. Collectively, this suggests thyme extract employs a multi-target strategy, which is supported by other studies proposing additional mechanisms such as quorum sensing disruption and degradation of the extracellular polymeric matrix (Sharma et al., 2023; Sivaranjani et al., 2022; Balducci et al., 2023).Moreover, the strain-to-strain variability in biofilm-forming capacity observed in this study is in agreement with the reports of Al-Bayati. (2022) and Sivaranjani et al. (2022). This heterogeneity underscores the challenge of targeting biofilms with narrow-spectrum agents. Therefore, our finding that thyme extract maintained efficacy across these diverse strains highlights its significant advantage as a broad-spectrum antibiofilm agent capable of addressing the varied virulence strategies employed by APEC in field conditions.

Overall, these properties are particularly valuable in the context of avian pathogenic E. coli infections, where biofilm formation on both biological surfaces and farm equipment contributes significantly to bacterial persistence and recurrent infections in poultry operations (Sivaranjani et al., 2022; Kaleva et al., 2023).

From a therapeutic perspective in poultry health management, thyme extract offers multiple beneficial activities against avian colibacillosis, acting through both direct antimicrobial effects and host-modulating properties. The antioxidant capacity observed in our study is consistent with Kruk et al. (2022), who highlighted metal ion chelation as a key mechanism, and Zhao et al. (2022), who reported upregulation of host antioxidant defenses by polyphenols. Likewise, the anti-inflammatory properties we detected are supported by Rathod et al. (2023) and Vilas-Boas et al. (2021), who described NF-κB inhibition and Nrf2 pathway activation. Overall, our findings align with these studies and suggest that thyme extract can simultaneously mitigate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses induced by E. coli infections in poultry.

Furthermore, initial safety assessments are promising, showing no significant hemolytic activity at concentrations up to 11 mg/mL (Haddouchi et al., 2016), which is consistent with the observations of Rafique et al. (2023), who also reported the extract’s safety in similar experimental conditions. Nevertheless, comprehensive toxicological evaluations, including potential hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and effects of chronic exposure, remain essential before considering widespread therapeutic application (Krawczyk et al., 2023; Campbell et al., 2023).

Conclusion

The antibacterial properties of thyme extract, combined with its low toxicity at potential therapeutic doses, make it a promising candidate for combating colibacillosis and improving the health and productivity of poultry rearing. Combining this plant extract with existing antibiotic treatments could provide an effective and sustainable strategy for preserving poultry health while limiting the use of antimicrobials. Thyme extract thus appears to be an innovative and promising solution to the health and environmental challenges facing the poultry industry today. However, in vivo tests will be needed to assess the extract's efficacy on infected animal models as well as its potential effects on poultry growth and productivity.
