Balloon Venoplasty in Collateral-Assisted Venous Remodeling to Facilitate Left Ventricular Lead Delivery in Complex Anatomy
Authors: Jamal Yusuf, Ankur Gautam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimal left ventricular (LV) lead placement is pivotal for the success of cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator; however, tortuous, small, or sharply angulated coronary venous anatomy often frustrate standard delivery.Case SummaryA 60-year-old woman with ischemic cardiomyopathy (ejection fraction: 23%) and NYHA functional class III symptoms was referred for cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator implantation. A markedly narrowed, hairpin lateral vein prevented antegrade LV lead advancement. By exploiting a collateral from the middle cardiac vein into the lateral vein, we created a retrograde “through-and-through” rail that slightly straightened the bend; thereafter, multiple sequential balloon venoplasty of the collateral and target vein enlarged the lumen and enabled antegrade delivery of a quadripolar LV lead. Postimplant QRS duration narrowed from 160 to 120 milliseconds and symptoms improved to NYHA functional class II at 1 month.DiscussionCollateral-assisted retrograde wiring paired with multiple balloon venoplasty can safely remodel coronary sinus branches, transforming impassable anatomy into a navigable route for LV lead placement.Take-Home MessageWhen hostile venous anatomy blocks antegrade LV lead delivery, opportunistic collateral wiring plus balloon venoplasty offers a safe, effective bailout by straightening bends and enlarging the vessel lumen.

Background

Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) lowers mortality and heart failure hospitalization in appropriately selected patients.1,2 Clinical benefit hinges on positioning the left ventricular (LV) lead in a lateral or posterolateral vein of the coronary sinus (CS) to maximize mechanical synchrony.3 Unfavorable venous anatomy complicates up to 10% of CRT implants and contributes to nonresponse or procedural abandonment.4 We report a case in which a combination of retrograde collateral wiring along with multiple balloon venoplasty of the collateral and target vein enabled successful cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) implantation despite a small-caliber, sharply angulated lateral vein.Take-Home Messages•A structured, anatomy-guided approach with timely selection of bailout strategies is essential for overcoming complex venous challenges during CRT procedures.•Balloon venoplasty is a safe and effective method to enlarge small or tortuous coronary veins, making it a key component of the CRT bailout toolkit.

History of Presentation

A 60-year-old woman presented with progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class III) and fatigue. Blood pressure was 110/70 mm Hg, and heart rate was 78 beats/min. Jugular venous pressure was elevated, and the cardiac apex was displaced down and out; there were no murmurs, and the lung fields were clear.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus for 10 years and known coronary artery disease, with prior percutaneous coronary intervention with drug-eluting stents to the left anterior descending artery 8 years ago. She had been on guideline-directed medical therapy for ischemic cardiomyopathy for the past 1 year, including aspirin, high-intensity statins, carvedilol, sacubitril/valsartan, spironolactone, torsemide, and dapagliflozin. There was no history of hypertension, and her family history was unremarkable.

Investigation

A resting 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with complete left bundle branch block and a QRS duration of 160 milliseconds. Echocardiography revealed a dilated left ventricle (end diastolic volume: 338 mL; end systolic volume: 260 mL; end diastolic diameter: 65 mm) with an ejection fraction of 23% and an interventricular septal thickness of 0.7 cm. Laboratory tests showed elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, with normal electrolyte levels; baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate was 77 mL/min/1.73 m2. Coronary angiography revealed 100% in-stent restenosis of the left anterior descending and noncritical lesions in the left circumflex and right coronary arteries. A subsequent thallium viability scan showed nonviable myocardium in the left anterior descending territory, with the rest of the myocardium being viable.

Management

After multidisciplinary discussion, CRT-D implantation was pursued. Left bundle branch area pacing was not chosen because the patient had a markedly enlarged left ventricle with severe septal thinning (0.8 cm) and a nonviable septal territory on perfusion imaging, making reliable left bundle capture unlikely. Coronary angiography in the levophase revealed a hairpin latera vein of small caliber (Figure 1A). Axillary vein puncture granted venous access. The CS was engaged with a 5-F Judkins Right diagnostic catheter (Medtronic), and a 10-F CS guiding sheath (CPS Aim SL; Abbott) was advanced into the CS. Selective CS angiography revealed 2 upward-oriented branches of the middle cardiac vein (MCV) approaching the lateral surface of the heart—a distal branch and a proximal branch (Figure 1B). Anticipating potential challenges such as small vessel caliber, the distal MCV branch was first wired. A subselector (CPS Aim SL inner catheter; Abbott) was used to support lead delivery; however, advancement of the LV lead still remained unsuccessful due to the persistently small caliber of the branch and a constrictive band at its upward turn. Thereafter, sequential balloon dilatations were performed using noncompliant coronary balloons (Solarice; Medtronic), with sizes 2.0 × 12 mm followed by 2.5 × 12 mm at 10 atm (Figures 1C and 1D). This led to partial improvement in vessel caliber but remained inadequate for CS lead advancement. Subsequently, attention was directed to the proximal MCV branch, which underwent multiple balloon dilatations using the same balloon sizes and inflation pressures. Although both branches demonstrated modest luminal gain postvenoplasty, anatomic constraints—specifically vessel angulation and small caliber—remained prohibitive for LV lead delivery via these routes. A subselector (Figure 1E) was again used after venoplasty, but lead advancement continued to be unsuccessful, necessitating an alternative strategy.

A breakthrough occurred when the guidewire successfully traversed a collateral connecting the MCV branch to the distal lateral vein, reentering the CS and forming a retrograde “through-and-through” rail. This maneuver was crucial in straightening the venous course and reducing the acute angulation at the lateral vein origin. Externalization of the wire partially straightened the acute angulation at the lateral vein origin; thereafter, multiple balloon dilatations of the collateral (Figure 2B) were performed retrogradely using a 2.0- × 12-mm noncompliant balloon, enabling successful antegrade wiring from the CS (Figure 2C). However, advancement of the LV lead still remained unsuccessful due to the persistently small caliber of the lateral vein and a residual constrictive band at its ostium. To overcome this, multiple balloon venoplasty was performed at both the lateral vein ostium and along the lateral vein course using a 2.5- × 15-mm noncompliant coronary balloon at 10 atm (Figure 2D). These additional dilatations relieved the residual tortuosity, increased vessel diameter, and ultimately created adequate luminal gain for successful antegrade advancement of the CS lead.

A quadripolar LV lead (Quartet 1458Q; Abbott) was then smoothly advanced and secured in the distal lateral vein (Figure 2E). The right atrial lead (Tendril STS 2088 TC, 52 cm; Abbott) and right ventricular lead (Tendril STS 2088TC, 58 cm, St; Abbott) were implanted in standard fashion. Final lead positions are depicted in Figure 2F. Final lead parameters were satisfactory: LV threshold: 1.5 mV at 0.5 milliseconds; right ventricle: R-wave of 8 mV, and threshold of 1.2 mV at 0.5 milliseconds; and right atrium: P-wave of 2.2 mV, and threshold of 0.9 mV at 0.4 milliseconds. All impedances were within normal limits. The patient received 60 mL of contrast; total procedure time was 285 minutes with 175 minutes of fluoroscopy.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After implantation, the QRS duration narrowed to 120 milliseconds. Figures 3A and 3B depict the preprocedural and postprocedural electrocardiograms, respectively. Postprocedure, renal function remained stable, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 69 mL/min/1.73 m2, without evidence of acute kidney injury. The patient was discharged on day 6. Discussion

Successful CRT-D implantation depends on optimal LV lead positioning, typically in the lateral or posterolateral CS branches, which are most effective for electrical resynchronization. However, tortuous, sharply angulated, or narrow-caliber venous anatomy can make standard lead delivery technically challenging or impossible.3 In such situations, a flexible, stepwise approach incorporating interventional strategies becomes essential.

In our case, multiple anatomic hurdles complicated the procedure. The first was the acutely angulated origin and narrow caliber of the lateral vein, which hindered antegrade wire navigation and lead placement. Second, there were no suitable alternative lateral veins. Third, although the MCV is generally considered suboptimal for LV lead placement, a branch with lateral trajectory was attempted. Balloon venoplasty was performed on both distal and proximal MCV branches to improve vessel compliance, but both remained too narrow and tortuous for lead passage. A fourth challenge arose even after successful creation of a retrograde “through-and-through” rail via a collateral from the MCV to the distal lateral vein. Despite partial straightening, the residual band at the lateral vein ostium and its narrow caliber continued to hinder lead advancement.

A turning point was the identification of this MCV–lateral vein collateral. After navigating it with a 0.014-in guidewire, we performed multiple retrograde sequential balloon venoplasties, which improved luminal caliber and vessel compliance. This helped straighten the acute lateral vein angulation and enabled successful antegrade wiring. Subsequent venoplasty of both the lateral vein ostium and its course further relieved tortuosity and allowed stable lead delivery.

Balloon venoplasty plays a crucial role in overcoming venous challenges during CRT-D. By increasing the luminal diameter and reducing resistance, balloon dilatation helps to navigate focal stenoses and bends that impede wire or lead passage. It is a safe, reproducible adjunctive technique with increasing utility in difficult CRT cases.5 In this case, venoplasty was not supplementary but essential—performed at the MCV branches, the lateral vein, and the intervening collateral. Targeted use of noncompliant coronary balloons provided the necessary mechanical remodeling for lead advancement. This case demonstrates how venoplasty can transform a near-abandoned CRT-D attempt into a successful outcome, underscoring its role as a key bailout option for hostile venous anatomy.

In our case, balloon venoplasty was undertaken using a balloon-to-vein diameter ratio slightly higher than that typically applied in coronary arteries. This was considered safe given the structural differences between the venous and arterial systems because coronary veins are more compliant, distensible, and elastic, allowing them to tolerate larger balloon sizes with negligible risk of rupture.6 Sequential venoplasty was performed safely without any procedural complications. Complex wiring, although theoretically associated with small venous perforations, dissections, or local hematoma, carries minimal clinical risk in the low-pressure coronary venous system because such events are rare and typically seal spontaneously. Additional procedural considerations include radiation exposure and contrast use. Prolonged procedures inherently increase fluoroscopy exposure, and minimizing cine use, optimizing collimation, and relying on stored reference images are important strategies to reduce dose.7 In this case, fluoroscopy time was understandably prolonged (175 minutes) due to procedural complexity, and contrast use was kept judicious (total contrast volume: 60 mL), given the heightened risk of acute kidney injury in such patients. Thus, although complex wiring and venoplasty add some risk, they remain relatively safe compared with coronary artery interventions. With careful technique, attention to radiation safety, and judicious use of contrast, these advanced strategies can be performed effectively in complex CRT cases.

Alongside venoplasty, other adjunctive techniques can aid LV lead delivery:•Buddy wire technique: placement of a second guidewire alongside the primary wire offers added support, helps straighten tortuous venous segments, and facilitates delivery of the LV lead.8•Use of subselectors: these inner catheters with enhanced torque control and reach can help navigate sharply angulated or difficult branches within the CS.9•Snaring techniques: in select cases, a guidewire passed into collateral or epicardial branches can be snared from the right atrium via jugular or femoral access to establish a stable rail for lead advancement.10•Hybrid or surgical epicardial lead placement: reserved for situations where transvenous approaches fail, this method remains a reliable alternative for LV pacing.11

In our case, if venoplasty had failed, snaring would have been the next step. However, when narrow caliber or fixed narrowing is the main obstacle, snaring without prior venoplasty is unlikely to succeed. Hence, bailout techniques—and the order in which they are applied—should be selected based on the specific anatomy encountered. An anatomy-driven, adaptive approach allows selection of the most effective technique at the appropriate stage.

This case illustrates the critical role of imaging interpretation, procedural flexibility, and borrowing principles from interventional cardiology. The opportunistic “through-and-through” rail technique was decisive. Finally, prolonged fluoroscopy and procedure time are expected in such complex cases, reinforcing the need for operator experience and patient counseling.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that complex and hostile coronary venous anatomy need not preclude successful CRT. A systematic, stepwise approach—beginning with selective venography and using sequential bailout strategies—enabled successful CRT-D implantation despite initially prohibitive anatomy. This underscores the vital role of venoplasty and the importance of anatomic insight and procedural flexibility in overcoming technical challenges during CRT.
