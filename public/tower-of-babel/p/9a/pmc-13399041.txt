A Novel AP2/ERF Transcription Factor Controls Trichome Initiation and Fruit Development in Cucumber
Authors: Haifan Wen, Hui Du, Siqi Wang, Yiqun Weng, Juan Liu, Huanle He, Run Cai, Jian Pan, Junsong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrichomes, the hair‐like outgrowths of the plant epidermis, are critical for defence, reproduction and development. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms governing their initiation remain elusive. Here, we identified CsGL2, an AP2/ERF transcription factor, as a key integrator controlling trichome initiation and fruit development in cucumber. Genetic and molecular studies indicate that CsGL2 activates CsGL1/Mict, subsequently triggering the morphogenesis of multicellular trichomes. Beyond epidermal fate, CsGL2 upregulates the tubercle regulator Tu together with the cytokinin rate‐limiting enzyme gene IPT1, thereby initiating fruit tubercles through cytokinin‐linked pathways. Notably, we found that ovule‐specific CsGL2 activates the parthenocarpy gene CsNPF1, and CsGL2 overexpression markedly increases the frequency of seedless fruit set. Thus, our findings establish a switch‐based mechanism for progressive cell‐fate specification in multicellular epidermal organs. This reveals how a single transcription factor integrates transcriptional and hormonal pathways to coordinate distinct developmental programmes.Keywords: AP2/ERF, cucumber, parthenocarpy, trichome, tubercle

Introduction

Plant trichomes are specialized epidermal outgrowths that serve diverse protective and adaptive functions, ranging from deterring herbivores to secreting valuable metabolites (Ishida et al. 2008). In Arabidopsis thaliana , single‐cell (unicellular) nonglandular trichomes serve as a well‐established model for studying cell morphogenesis (Hülskamp 2004; Schwab et al. 2000; Szymanski et al. 1999; Yanagisawa et al. 2015), where the MYB–bHLH–WD40 activator complex involving GLABRA1 (GL1), GLABRA3 (GL3)/EGL3 and TTG1 specifies trichome cell fate (Grebe 2012), and downstream factors such as GL2 subsequently promote differentiation (Cristina et al. 1996; Pesch et al. 2015; Szymanski et al. 1998). However, approximately 30% of vascular tissues bear multicellular trichomes, often with glandular secretory structures, and no single genetic model fully captures their developmental complexity (Glas et al. 2012). Recent studies in horticultural plants like tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum ) and cucumber ( Cucumis sativus L.) have revealed that multicellular trichomes develop through mechanisms distinct from those in Arabidopsis, suggesting an evolutionary divergence in their regulatory pathways (Wang et al. 2016; Wu, Bian, Hu, et al. 2024; Wu, Bian, Huang, et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2023; Yang, Li, et al. 2011; Zhao et al. 2015).

In cucumber, fruit spines—a specialized type of multicellular, nonglandular trichome—are not only an important agronomic trait affecting fruit appearance and market value but also an excellent system for studying epidermal cell fate determination (Chen et al. 2014; Xue et al. 2019). Through the analysis of numerous mutants, a genetic framework for multicellular trichome development has been established. The Trichome‐less (Tril) locus, corresponding to CsGL3, encodes a HD‐ZIP IV transcription factor that mirrors tomato Wo in function (Cui et al. 2016; Pan et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016). Loss‐of‐function csgl3 mutants are completely glabrous and even a subtle promoter mutation in CsGL3 causes a drastic few‐spines phenotype (Zhang et al. 2016). In parallel, another cucumber gene, Micro‐trichome (CsGL1/Mict), was identified as a HD‐ZIP I class homeodomain protein required for trichome morphogenesis (Li et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2015). The discovery of CsGL1 was unexpected, as HD‐ZIP I genes had not previously been implicated in trichome development in any plant. Beyond epidermal development, the characteristic warty trait of cucumber fruit distinguishes it from other Cucurbitaceae. However, the molecular link between trichome initiation genes and tubercle development remains unclear. Notably, the Tuberculate (Tu) locus controls tubercle formation via a cytokinin‐related pathway and is genetically downstream of CsGL3 and CsGL1 (Yang et al. 2014). However, the molecular link connecting the trichome initiation machinery to downstream fruit‐specific developmental programmes, such as tubercle formation, has remained elusive.

In addition to these core regulators, multiple transcription factors and signalling components—including MYB regulators (CsMYB6 and CsTRY), the WD40 protein CsTTG1, and the HMG‐domain protein CsHMG9/FS2—fine‐tune various aspects of trichome initiation, spine density and secondary metabolism (Chen et al. 2016; Du et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2026; Yang et al. 2018). Hormonal pathways are integrated through the auxin transporter NS, the jasmonate synthesis‐related lipoxygenase CsLOX, and receptor‐like kinases such as CsTs and CsEMS1, while the C2H2 protein Tu and B3 factor CsRAV8 specifically regulate tubercle formation and glandular trichome integrity, respectively (Dong et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2018; Lv et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024). Despite this wealth of information, the key factor that directly integrates signals from the trichome initiation pathway to coordinate downstream processes such as tubercle formation and parthenocarpy has not been identified.

The AP2/ERF transcription factor family is unique to plants and is characterized by a conserved AP2/ERF DNA‐binding domain. These transcription factors play key roles in regulating plant developmental processes as well as responses to biotic and environmental stresses (Saleh and Pagés 2003). Moreover, they serve as central regulators of secondary metabolism, controlling the biosynthesis of terpenoids, phenolic compounds and alkaloids across diverse plant species (Chen et al. 2026). Emerging evidence highlights their diverse functions in the development of specialized epidermal structures and associated metabolic pathways. In tomato, LEAFLESS (LFS), an AP2/ERF‐family transcription factor regulates glandular trichome development and specialized metabolism (Capua and Eshed 2017; Wu et al. 2023). In the wild tomato relative Solanum pennellii , the LFS ortholog directly binds to the promoters of acylsugar and flavonoid biosynthetic genes in trichome tip cells, highlighting its tissue‐specific regulatory role in specialized metabolism (Mandal et al. 2025). In Citrus, the AP2/ERF‐family transcription factor DORNROSCHEN‐LIKE (DRNL) directly binds to a conserved GCC box in the promoter of LMI1 and activates its expression, thereby initiating oil gland development and regulating leaf margin serration (Wang, Ren, et al. 2024). Similarly, in grape, VviERF003 responds to ethylene signalling and activates VviGT14 expression to promote glycosylated monoterpenoid synthesis, contributing to fruit aroma quality (Wang, Wei, et al. 2024), while in pepper, CaERF102 and CaERF111 directly activate capsaicinoid biosynthetic genes and interact with CaMYC2 to regulate pungency (Song et al. 2025). Beyond positive regulation, AP2/ERF factors can also act as repressors; for example, FhAP2 in Freesia hybrida interacts with MYB activators to negatively regulate floral pigment and scent biosynthesis (Zhang et al. 2026). In cucumber, recent studies have shown that CsNPF1 (also known as CsTOE3), an AP2/ERF transcription factor gene originally cloned in a quantitative‐trait screen for natural parthenocarpy also influences fruit spine initiation, hinting at a broader role for this family in coordinating reproductive and epidermal traits (Guo et al. 2023; Nie et al. 2025). These findings suggest that AP2/ERF factors may serve as versatile regulators integrating multiple developmental programmes in plants.

In this study, we employed map‐based cloning and mutant analysis to demonstrate that the glabrous phenotype in csgl2 mutants is caused by loss of an AP2/ERF transcription factor, CsGL2. Genetic and molecular analyses reveal that CsGL2 functions as a key regulator within the trichome developmental hierarchy, acting downstream of CsGL3 and directly activating CsGL1. Importantly, CsGL2 not only promotes multicellular trichome (spine) initiation but also links epidermal development to fruit morphogenesis by regulating Tu and cytokinin biosynthesis genes in the ovary. This dual function controls tubercle (wart) formation and enables high levels of parthenocarpic fruit set. Our findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the co‐occurrence of trichome, tubercle, and parthenocarpy in cucumber, illustrating how a single transcription factor can integrate organ‐specific developmental programmes with broader hormone‐mediated pathways of agronomic significance.

Results

Loss‐of‐Function of CsGL2 Causes a Glabrous Phenotype

In our previous study, we developed a Tnt1 retrotransposon insertion mutant library using the high‐spine‐density North China‐type cucumber line 9930 (Huang et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2018). Through phenotypic screening of the library, we identified a glabrous mutant, TN47, which exhibited a near‐total absence of trichomes across the entire plant, with the exception of sparse trichomes on male floral receptacles. (Figure 1A; Figure S1A). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirmed that the trichome‐less epidermis of TN47 closely resembles the fully glabrous csgl3 mutant but differs significantly from the csgl1 mutant, which retains numerous malformed micro‐trichomes (Figure 1B; Figure S1B). Genetic analysis demonstrated that the glabrous phenotype of the TN47 is controlled by a single recessive locus (Figure S1C).

Owing to its similarity to the previously reported glabrous mutant NCG‐042 which was controlled by the gl‐2 locus (Wang et al. 2020; Yang, Miao, et al. 2011), we crossed TN47 with NCG‐042. The F1 plants of TN47 and NCG‐042 had the same glabrous phenotype as with the parents (Figure S2A), so we designated this mutant as csgl2. Through genome resequencing of TN47, we developed three Tnt1 insertion markers—TN2G14, TN2G15, and TN2G16—flanking the CsGL2 locus on chromosome 2 (Figure 1C). Using recombinant lines from an F2 population generated by crossing TN47 with wild‐type plants, we fine‐mapped the CsGL2 locus to a genomic interval between TN2G14 and TN2G16, with perfect co‐segregation observed for the TN2G15 marker (Figure 1C). Sequencing analysis revealed that Csa2G349090 contains a Tnt1 retrotransposon insertion (TN2G15) within its exon region (Figure 1D). Moreover, we identified two additional mutants, ccmc‐14 and 22TN48, that display glabrous phenotypes indistinguishable from TN47 (Figure 1E). Sequencing confirmed that these mutants also carry Tnt1 retrotransposon insertions in Csa2G349090 (Figure 1F). Additionally, the glabrous mutant NCG‐042 was previously mapped to a gl‐2 locus on cucumber chromosome 2 between SSR10522 and SSR13275 (Figure S2B) (Yang, Miao, et al. 2011), we compared this interval with our mapping results and found that the CsGL2 candidate resides within the same region. To further connect our candidate to the reported gl‐2 locus, we resequenced NCG‐042 and identified an ~20‐kb deletion spanning the CsGL2 genomic region (Figure S2C). These observations confirm that our CsGL2 corresponds to the previously identified gl‐2 locus; therefore, we have adopted the name CsGL2 to maintain consistency with prior reports.

To validate the role of CsGL2 in regulating trichome initiation and fruit development in cucumber, we generated the 35S:CsGL2‐GFP construct and transformed it into wild‐type cucumber 9930. Three independent CsGL2 overexpression lines (oxCsGL2#1–3) were obtained, all exhibiting significantly higher CsGL2 transcript and protein levels compared to the wild type (Figure 1G,H). Phenotypic analysis showed that the oxCsGL2 lines exhibited a significant increase in fruit spine density, whereas they had no significant effect on leaf spine density. (Figure 1I–K; Figure S3A,B). Interestingly, fruits from the oxCsGL2 line displayed altered fruit morphology, characterized by a larger diameter and shorter fruit neck (Figure 1L–N). These findings strongly support Csa2G349090 as the candidate gene underlying the csgl2 locus, which acts as a positive regulator of trichome differentiation and associated fruit developmental traits.

Phylogeny and Expression Patterns of CsGL2

To investigate the function of the CsGL2 candidate gene, we identified CsGL2 homologous proteins from major species in Cucurbitaceae, Solanaceae, Brassicaceae, and Poaceae using BLAST and constructed a phylogenetic tree with these protein sequences (Figure 2A; Figure S4A). The analysis revealed that CsGL2 encodes an AP2/ERF transcription factor most closely related to tomato LFS, which regulates the transition between glandular and nonglandular trichomes (Capua and Eshed 2017; Wu et al. 2023). In contrast, its Arabidopsis ortholog ESR1 does not influence trichome development but promotes shoot regeneration (Iwase et al. 2016).

To determine the tissue‐specific expression pattern of CsGL2, we performed RT‐qPCR on various tissues of the wild‐type cucumber 9930. CsGL2 transcript levels peaked during early female floral bud development (stages 8–2 to 8–4) and in the ovary epidermis at anthesis, with moderate expression in leaves and isolated trichome cells (Figure 2B). RNA in situ hybridization in young shoot apices and early‐stage female floral buds (stage 8–4) revealed enrichment of CsGL2 mRNA in shoot apex organ primordia and in the early female floral buds, suggesting a role in the initial stages of trichome formation, as well as strong signals in developing ovules (Figure 2C,D). Subcellular localization assays in both stable cucumber transformants and transiently expressed Nicotiana benthamiana systems confirmed the exclusive nuclear localization of the CsGL2‐GFP fusion protein (Figure 2E; Figure S4B).

To delineate the transcriptional consequences of CsGL2 loss, we integrated RNA‐seq with targeted expression analysis and verified the expression levels of genes by RT‐qPCR (Figure 2F; Figure S5A,B). Among trichome regulators, CsGL1 was markedly reduced whereas CsGL3 was elevated in csgl2, indicating that CsGL2 positively drives CsGL1 and may feed back on CsGL3. Consistent with its inhibitory role in trichome formation, NS was upregulated in the mutant (Xie et al. 2018). As expected for a genetic downstream component of CsGL1, Tu was significantly downregulated. Additional spine‐associated factors (MYB36 and MYB6) and the cuticle/wax regulator CsWIN1 also decreased, aligning with the defective epidermal differentiation in csgl2 (Hao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2020). By contrast, genes linked to spine density (FS2 and WOX3) and soft‐spine identity (Ts), showed no significant change (Du et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2024). KEGG enrichment of the RNA‐seq data revealed a prominent accumulation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cutin/suberin and wax biosynthesis and plant hormone signal transduction (Figure 2G). Our previous work on CsGL1 showed that trichome development is tightly coupled to fatty‐acid metabolism and cuticular wax formation (Pan, Zhang, et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2015); the strong enrichment of DEGs in this pathway therefore suggests that CsGL2 likely impacts these processes via the CsGL1 axis. In parallel, the broad shifts in hormone‐related genes—most notably those involved in cytokinin biosynthesis and catabolism—indicate that CsGL2 may also operate through hormonal signalling, with cytokinin as a major conduit. Collectively, these data support a working model in which CsGL2 acts through CsGL1 and other hormone‐related targets, which has guided our subsequent experiments.

CsGL2 Functions Downstream of CsGL3 to Regulate CsGL1

In cucumber, CsGL3 and CsGL1 are known regulators of trichome initiation and morphogenesis, respectively. Our findings now reveal CsGL2 as a key component of this pathway, functioning in coordination with CsGL3 and CsGL1 (Li et al. 2015; Pan et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2015). To clarify these relationships, we created F2 populations through crosses between csgl2 mutants and both csgl3 and csgl1 mutants, then self‐pollinated the F1 hybrids. Genetic analysis showed that CsGL3 displays recessive epistasis over CsGL2, evidenced by a 9:3:4 F2 segregation ratio (WT:csgl2:csgl3; χ 2 = 0.1904; Figure 3A). Conversely, CsGL2 and CsGL1 followed independent assortment, conforming to a 9:3:3:1 ratio (WT:csgl2:csgl1:csgl2/csgl1; χ 2 = 0.3569; Figure 3B). Intriguingly, while csgl2 mutants preserved trichomes exclusively on male floral receptacles and csgl1 mutants maintained them on hypocotyls, the double mutants completely lacked trichomes (Figure 3B).

RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that neither CsGL3 nor CsGL2 transcript levels were affected in csgl1 mutants (Figure 3C). In csgl2 mutants, however, CsGL1 expression was significantly reduced, while CsGL3 expression was elevated. Furthermore, both CsGL2 and CsGL1 transcripts were absent in csgl3 mutants, consistent with the RNA‐seq results. These results demonstrate that CsGL3 acts upstream of CsGL2, which subsequently regulates CsGL1. Additionally, CsGL2 may exert a feedback regulatory effect on CsGL3, consistent with previous reports that CsGL3 functions through a dosage‐dependent mechanism (Du et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2016).

Based on the above genetic and expression analyses, we wondered whether CsGL2 might activate CsGL1 expression. Accordingly, we conducted yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays using progressively truncated CsGL1 promoter fragments (2 kb, 1 kb, 0.5 kb, 0.2 kb and 0.1 kb) (Figure 3D, Figure S6A). The results showed that CsGL2 specifically binds to the 0.1 kb P1 region of the CsGL1 promoter. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) with recombinant MBP‐CsGL2 protein further confirmed direct interaction with the P1 region (Figure 3E). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)‐qPCR in oxCsGL2 lines (oxCsGL2‐GFP) verified in vivo occupancy of CsGL2 on the P1 region of the CsGL1 promoter (Figure 3F). Supporting these findings, dual‐luciferase (LUC) assays in tobacco also demonstrated that CsGL2 significantly activated CsGL1 promoter‐driven LUC expression, consistent with elevated CsGL1 transcript levels in oxCsGL2 lines (Figure 3G,H). These results collectively support CsGL2 as a direct activator of CsGL1, initiating trichome differentiation. In contrast, yeast one‐hybrid assays demonstrated that CsGL3 fails to bind the CsGL2 promoter (Figure S6B). EMSA with recombinant MBP‐CsGL3 protein further confirmed that it does not directly interact with the L1‐box (~50 bp) of the CsGL2 promoter (Figure S6C). These results indicate that CsGL3 regulates CsGL2 through an indirect mechanism, but CsGL2 directly activates CsGL1 expression, thereby establishing a transcriptional activation cascade of CsGL3–CsGL2–CsGL1.

CsGL2 Directly Regulates Tu and IPT1 to Influence Fruit Tubercle Development

Previous studies demonstrated that Tu regulates tubercle development through the cytokinin pathway and acts genetically downstream of CsGL3 and CsGL1 (Yang et al. 2014). Interestingly, fruits of the csgl2 mutant display a smooth, tubercle‐free phenotype. In contrast, oxCsGL2 plants frequently develop ectopic tubercles beneath newly formed fruit spines (Figure 4A,B), indicating that CsGL2 may also activate genes involved in tubercle development to regulate the formation of epidermal outgrowths. IPT1 encodes an ATP/ADP isopentenyltransferase, which catalyses the key step in iP/tZ‐type cytokinin biosynthesis (Miyawaki et al. 2006). The differential expression of IPT1 in the csgl2 mutant suggests that IPT1 may provide the active cytokinins necessary for tubercle development (Figure 2G).

Y1H assays showed that CsGL2 binds directly to the 0.1 kb P1 regions of both Tu and IPT1 promoters (Figure 4C; Figure S7A,B). EMSA with purified MBP‐CsGL2 protein and biotin‐labelled P1 probes provided independent confirmation of these interactions (Figure 4D,E). ChIP‐qPCR of oxCsGL2‐GFP plants showed significant in vivo enrichment of CsGL2 at the P1 regions of both Tu and IPT1 promoters (Figure 4F), further supporting their direct regulatory relationship. Dual‐LUC assays in tobacco revealed that CsGL2 significantly activated both Tu and IPT1 promoter‐driven reporter genes (Figure 4G), which correlated with the upregulation of their transcript levels in oxCsGL2 plants and downregulated in csgl3 and csgl2 mutants (Figure 4H, Figure S8). These results indicate that CsGL2 directly promotes Tu and IPT1 transcription, thereby contributing to tubercle formation through cytokinin‐mediated epidermal patterning.

To investigate the role of CsGL2 in cytokinin‐mediated tubercle formation, we analyzed expression patterns of key cytokinin metabolic genes and quantified cytokinin levels in young fruits. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that CsGL2 positively regulates cytokinin biosynthesis, as evidenced by decreased expression of CHL1 and CHL2 in csgl2 mutants and their significant upregulation in oxCsGL2 plants (Figure 4I). Conversely, three cytokinin degradation genes (CKX1, CKX3 and CKX6) exhibited an inverse expression pattern (Figure 4I).

Consistent with these transcriptional patterns, quantitative analysis of endogenous cytokinin levels showed reduced levels of four cytokinin compounds (DHZ7G, cZ, BAP, and BAP9G) in csgl2 mutants and their significant accumulation in oxCsGL2 plants (Figure 4J, Figure S9). Notably, two additional cytokinin derivatives (cZROG and pT9G) were undetectable in both WT and csgl2 but specifically accumulated in oxCsGL2 lines (Figure 4J, Figure S9). However, exogenous cytokinin treatments (6‐Benzylaminopurine) during early fruit development (stages 8–2 to 8–4) did not induce detect a significant change in tubercle number or morphology in the csgl2 mutant (Figure S10). These results collectively demonstrate that CsGL2 promotes cytokinin accumulation by enhancing biosynthesis and repressing degradation, thereby reinforcing Tu‐mediated epidermal outgrowth during tubercle formation.

Trichome Differentiation Genes Are Essential for Parthenocarpy in Cucumber

Given that CsGL2 promotes tubercle formation through cytokinin‐related pathways and that overexpression lines exhibit marked changes in fruit morphology (Figure 1L–N), we were intrigued by the functional role of CsGL2 expression in ovules, especially in relation to parthenocarpy (Figure 2D). In recent research, the AP2/ERF transcription factor CsNPF1 regulates both fruit spine/trichome initiation and parthenocarpy (Guo et al. 2023; Nie et al. 2025). These two independent reports characterizing distinct functions of CsNPF1/CsTOE3 imply a coordinated regulatory link between fruit spine differentiation and parthenocarpy. To further investigate the relationship between fruit spine/trichome differentiation and parthenocarpy, we quantified fruit set in the csgl3, csgl2, and cstoe3 mutants and oxCsGL2 lines, as well as in wild‐type (WT) plants, with forchlorfenuron (CPPU) treatment serving as a positive control. Phenotypic analysis revealed that oxCsGL2 plants exhibited significantly enhanced parthenocarpy, producing 5–6 fruits per plant—comparable to wild‐type (WT) plants treated with the exogenous cytokinin CPPU and substantially higher than the WT average of approximately two fruits (Figure 5A,B). Field trials conducted in 2023 and 2024 under optimal nutrient conditions consistently demonstrated this high parthenocarpic capacity in oxCsGL2 plants (Figure S11). Conversely, the parthenocarpy rate was significantly lower in glabrous mutants (csgl2, csgl3 and cstoe3) (Figure 5B), confirming that CsGL2 and CsGL3, like CsNPF1, contribute to parthenocarpy regulation in cucumber.

Given that CsNPF1 regulates both fruit spine/trichome formation and parthenocarpy, we examined CsNPF1 transcript levels in glabrous mutants and oxCsGL2 plants by RT–qPCR. CsNPF1 expression was markedly reduced in both csgl3 and csgl2 glabrous mutants, whereas it was significantly upregulated in oxCsGL2 plants (Figure 5C), implying that CsGL2 might transcriptionally activate CsNPF1 to promote parthenocarpy. To test this, we conducted Y1H assays with serially truncated CsNPF1 promoter fragments (2 kb, 1 kb, 0.5 kb, 0.2 kb and 0.1 kb) (Figure 5D, Figure S12). The experiments identified specific binding of CsGL2 to the 0.1 kb P1 region of the CsNPF1 promoter. This interaction was confirmed in vitro by EMSA and in vivo by ChIP–qPCR (Figure 5E,G), which showed significant enrichment of the P1 region of the CsNPF1 promoter. Additionally, CsGL2 elevated CsNPF1 promoter activity by more than three‐fold in the Dual‐LUC assay (Figure 5F). These findings provide evidence that CsGL2 directly activates CsNPF1 expression to participate in parthenocarpic fruit set.

To further probe the functional relationship between fruit spine/trichome differentiation and parthenocarpy, we generated a β‐estradiol‐inducible csgl3‐CsGL3ER line (Figure S13A). Upon application of 20 μM β‐estradiol to shoot apices, the expression level of CsGL3 was significantly increased (Figure S13B). Moreover, shoot apex‐specific CsGL3 reactivation produced mosaic vines containing both spined (induced) and glabrous (uninduced) ovaries on the same stem (Figure 5H). Quantitative analysis demonstrated complete abortion of all glabrous ovaries (both untreated and estradiol‐uninduced), while > 85% of spined ovaries developed into seedless fruits (Figure 5I). These findings offer direct evidence that CsGL3—and consequently CsGL2—coordinates trichome initiation with parthenocarpic fruit development.

Discussion

A Multicellular Trichome Initiation Mechanism Distinct From Unicellular Model

Our findings define a cucumber‐specific regulatory module for multicellular trichome initiation, in which CsGL3 (HD‐ZIP IV), CsGL2 (AP2/ERF) and CsGL1 (HD‐ZIP I) act in a defined hierarchy. Loss‐of‐function mutations in any of these genes abolish trichome formation. Notably, although csgl2 fruits appear macroscopically glabrous, SEM reveals irregular epidermal protrusions that do not progress into mature multicellular trichomes (Figure S1B). This observation suggests that CsGL2 is required for productive trichome initiation/early outgrowth rather than the earliest competence step, consistent with the completely smooth epidermis of csgl3. This regulatory logic diverges fundamentally from the classic MYB–bHLH–WD40 pathway described in Arabidopsis (Grebe 2012), underscoring the evolutionary innovation underlying multicellular trichome development in cucumber. Our findings underscore the central and unique role of CsGL2 in orchestrating multicellular trichome initiation. Within this module, CsGL2 functions as a pivotal relay: it responds to the upstream regulator CsGL3 and directly activates CsGL1, thereby coupling initiation cues to differentiation (Figure 6). This logic diverges from the MYB–bHLH–WD40 framework established in Arabidopsis, highlighting evolutionary innovation in multicellular trichome control.

Proposed regulatory model of CsGL3, CsGL2 and their downstream targets (CsGL1, Tu, IPT1 and CsNPF1) in trichome initiation, tubercle formation and parthenocarpic fruit development in cucumber.

At the transcriptional level, CsGL2 directly binds CsGL1 promoter and activates its expression, placing CsGL2 upstream of CsGL1 in the regulatory cascade. However, classical epistasis between csgl2 and csgl1 conforms to independent assortment rather than a strict linear order. To reconcile these observations, we propose that CsGL2 acts as an upstream activator of CsGL1 specifically in the trichome lineage, while CsGL1 also integrates inputs independent of CsGL2 in other tissues, such as the hypocotyl. Furthermore, the initiation module (CsGL3/CsGL2) and the morphogenesis module (CsGL1) interact non‐linearly at the whole‐organism level.

Dose‐Dependent Control of Epidermal Cell Fate During Multicellular Trichome Initiation

Our genetic and transgenic analyses demonstrate that the fate and density of multicellular trichomes in cucumber are likely controlled by a finely tuned dosage‐dependent mechanism involving both CsGL3 and CsGL2. While the absence of CsGL2 leads to a smooth phenotype with neither trichomes nor tubercles, overexpression results in excessive and often mis‐patterned trichomes. Notably, the upstream regulator CsGL3 shows the same quantitative control of epidermal cell fate: an 812‐bp promoter substitution in the fs1 allele lowers CsGL3 expression and sharply reduces spine number, while csgl3 null alleles eliminate trichomes entirely (Zhang et al. 2016). These observations indicate that precise expression levels of both CsGL3 and CsGL2 are required to ensure normal patterning and morphogenesis of multicellular trichomes. Interestingly, in this study, we found that expression of CsGL3 is significantly upregulated in the csgl2 mutant background, suggesting that CsGL2 may negatively regulate CsGL3 through a feedback mechanism. Rather than acting as simple binary switches, CsGL3 and CsGL2 together establish a quantitative regulatory module, in which graded gene activity translates into a spectrum of epidermal cell fates and trichome morphologies. This dose‐dependent logic likely provides developmental plasticity and robustness, allowing cucumber plants to modulate epidermal features in response to both genetic and environmental cues. These observations suggest that spine density and epidermal patterning are dosage‐sensitive traits of the CsGL3–CsGL2 module. In breeding, weak alleles or cis‐regulatory edits that fine‐tune expression levels may enable quantitative optimization of epidermal traits while avoiding complete glabrousness or excessive/mis‐patterned spines.

Spatial Control of CsGL2 Establishes a Cytokinin Switch for Tubercle Formation and Parthenocarpy

In this study, CsGL2 functions as a spatially regulated, cytokinin‐responsive transcriptional switch whose expression domain dictates distinct developmental outputs. In the fruit epidermis, CsGL2 promotes tubercle formation by directly activating Tu and IPT1 and by shifting cytokinin homeostasis towards accumulation: biosynthetic genes (CHL1 and CHL2) are upregulated, degradation genes (CKX1, CKX3 and CKX6) are repressed, and metabolite profiling shows higher levels of multiple cytokinin derivatives, with cZROG and pT9G uniquely accumulating in oxCsGL2. Notably, cZROG and pT9G are cytokinin derivatives that are commonly considered conjugated or modified forms of cytokinins, which may participate in cytokinin storage, transport, or buffering of active hormone pools. Their specific accumulation in oxCsGL2 plants suggests that CsGL2 not only elevates cytokinin production (via IPT1) but also rewires downstream cytokinin metabolism/conjugation, potentially contributing to the establishment of local cytokinin microenvironments that favour epidermal outgrowth. However, the direct contribution of these two derivatives to tubercle initiation remains unclear and will require targeted functional and biochemical studies. These molecular changes coincide with frequent ectopic tubercles beneath newly formed fruit spines in oxCsGL2, whereas csgl2 fruits are smooth, consistent with prior reports that Tu promotes tubercle formation through cytokinin signalling (Wang et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2014). Together, the binding (Y1H/EMSA/ChIP), reporter (dual‐LUC), transcript, and metabolite data indicate that CsGL2 activity in spine cells is both necessary and sufficient to drive localized cytokinin signalling and tubercle outgrowth, thereby linking trichome initiation with pericarp morphogenesis. In addition, oxCsGL2 fruits also exhibit a larger diameter and shorter fruit neck. Given that CsGL2 directly activates IPT1 and shifts cytokinin homeostasis towards accumulation in developing fruits, these shape changes may reflect broader cytokinin‐dependent remodelling of organ growth. This likely occurs by altering the spatial pattern and duration of cell division and expansion along the radial and longitudinal axes. Cytokinin is known to promote cell proliferation in developing organs, so local differences in its production or responsiveness could influence both fruit diameter and neck elongation.

Parthenocarpy in cucumber is generally regarded as a quantitative trait governed by the interaction of multiple genes and environmental factors (Gou et al. 2022; Lietzow et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2016). Synthetic cytokinin analogue, like CPPU, is widely used in agricultural production as an effective inducer of parthenocarpic fruit set in cucumber, melon and other horticultural crops (Curry and Greene 1993; Hayata et al. 2000). In our study, we found that CsGL2 transcripts are specifically enriched in ovules. Combined with its role in regulating cytokinin accumulation, as well as the empirical observation that glabrous mutants rarely exhibit parthenocarpy, we were able to demonstrate a direct link between trichome regulatory genes and parthenocarpic fruit set. Interestingly, we found that the npf1 mutant exhibits a spineless fruit phenotype, although this phenotype was not mentioned in the article (Nie et al. 2025). We further discovered that CsTOE3 and CsNPF1 are, in fact, the same gene (Figure S14), providing the most direct evidence for a tight genetic linkage between trichome development and parthenocarpy. A β‐estradiol–inducible CsGL3 system further demonstrates spatial necessity: mosaic plants bearing both spined and glabrous ovaries set seedless fruit only at spined nodes. Collectively, these results establish CsGL2 as a tissue‐specific integrator that converts epidermal differentiation cues into context‐dependent cytokinin outputs—activating the Tu and IPT1 module to drive tubercle outgrowth in the fruit epidermis, and the CsNPF1 pathway to enable parthenocarpy in ovules—thereby providing a mechanistic basis for the coordinated emergence of these agronomic traits and a handle for trait decoupling through targeted modulation of CsGL2 expression.

Pleiotropic Roles of Trichome Regulatory Genes in Plant Development and Metabolism

Recent studies, including our findings, demonstrate that key regulators of trichome development frequently exhibit pleiotropic roles beyond epidermal morphogenesis. In tomato, HD‐ZIP IV transcription factors control both multicellular trichome initiation and anther trichome development, facilitating self‐pollination and fruit set (Wu, Bian, Hu, et al. 2024; Wu, Bian, Huang, et al. 2024). Similarly, cucumber CsGL1 coordinates trichome formation, cuticle composition, and secondary metabolism, with csgl1 mutants displaying altered wax, flavonoids, and lipids (Pan, Zhang, et al. 2021). In an AP2/ERF transcription factor CsDRNL directly activates CsLMI1 via a conserved GCC‐box, establishing a regulatory module critical for both leaf shape and oil gland morphogenesis (Wu, Bian, Hu, et al. 2024; Wu, Bian, Huang, et al. 2024). In our study, the cucumber CsGL2 protein is essential for initiating multicellular spine formation. Notably, the CsGL2‐responsive promoter regions of its downstream targets do not share a uniformly conserved canonical GCC‐box, suggesting that CsGL2 may recognize a GCC‐box–like but highly degenerate binding signature in a context‐dependent manner. Additionally, CsGL2 regulates tubercle development through cytokinin‐mediated pathways and influences parthenocarpic fruit set. This functional integration likely represents a key adaptive innovation in plant evolution, positioning trichome regulators as central hubs linking developmental processes, physiological functions, and evolutionary fitness. Our findings not only advance the understanding of the genetic and hormonal control of complex organogenesis in crops, but also provide a molecular foundation for breeding strategies aimed at improving fruit quality and yield. The elucidation of this regulatory pathway highlights the evolutionary plasticity of transcriptional networks in plants and opens new avenues for crop improvement through rational manipulation of epidermal and reproductive traits.

Experimental Section

Plant Materials

The cucumber line 9930, a North China‐type cucumber with high‐density fruit spines, was used to develop a Tnt1 retrotransposon insertion mutant library (Zhang et al. 2018). The 9930 served as the wild‐type (WT) line and is also used for genetic transformation in this study. By screening the Tnt1 insertion mutant library, we identified three fruit spine mutants: TN47 (also named csgl2), ccmc‐14 and 22TN48. To determine the inheritance pattern of csgl2 and map its locus, we crossed the TN47 mutant with the WT plants to generate F2 mapping populations. The glabrous mutants csgl1 and csgl3/tril (Cui et al. 2016; Li et al. 2015; Pan et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2015) were separately crossed with csgl2, and the resulting plants were self‐pollinated to generate F2 populations for analysing the epistatic interactions between CsGL2 and CsGL1 or CsGL3. The csnpf1/cstoe3 mutant, generated by CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout, lacks obvious spine‐like structures (Figure S14) (Guo et al. 2023). Cucumber plants were cultivated in a greenhouse at Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Phenotypic Evaluation

Female flowers, male flowers, young leaves, petioles, and tendrils were collected from both csgl2 mutant and WT plants for comparative phenotypic characterization. Young leaves from csgl2, csgl3, csgl1 and WT plants were fixed in formaldehyde/acetic acid/ethanol (FAA) at 4°C for 24 h for scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis. The SEM images were acquired following previously described methods (Du et al. 2020).

For oxCsGL2 lines and WT plants, fruits at 1 week post‐anthesis were collected to quantify spine and tubercle numbers and measure fruit diameter and fruit neck length. These fruits were also subjected to SEM analysis.

To assess parthenocarpic capacity, female flowers 1 day pre‐anthesis were bagged using grafting clamps to prevent pollination. For all female flowers at nodes 1–20, fruits exhibiting expansion 1 week post‐anthesis were recorded as parthenocarpic fruits.

Genetic Mapping

To map the mutated gene in the Tnt1 insertion mutant TN47 (csgl2), whole‐genome resequencing was conducted to identify potential Tnt1 insertion sites based on the transposon's sequence features. Within the previously mapped CsGL2 region on chromosome 2, three Tnt1 insertion markers (TN2G14, TN2G15 and TN2G16) were identified and employed as molecular markers for mapping candidate genes. These three markers were then used to screen for recombination events in an F2 population generated from a cross between TN47 and wild‐type 9930. The precise Tnt1 insertion site at the TN2G15 locus was verified through PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing using specifically designed primers. Furthermore, resequencing analysis was performed on two additional TNT mutants (ccmc‐14 and 22TN48) that exhibited similar glabrous phenotypes to csgl2 to determine their respective Tnt1 insertion sites, which were subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

To identify the causal lesion in the previously reported gl‐2 mutant NCG‐042, genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves and subjected to resequencing. Clean reads were mapped to the cucumber reference genome, and structural variants were called using Geneious software. The deletion spanning the CsGL2 region was validated by PCR, and the genomic interval was visualized using the Geneious genome browser.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), we identified one homologous protein with the highest similarity to CsGL2 from each of the following species: Cucurbitaceae ( Cucumis melo , Lagenaria siceraria , Benincasa hispida , Citrullus lanatus , Cucurbita moschata , Trichosanthes anguina , Luffa aegyptiaca , Siraitia grosvenorii, Momordica charantia , and Sechium edule ), Solanaceae ( Solanum melongena and Solanum lycopersicum ), Brassicaceae ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) and Poaceae ( Oryza sativa and Zea mays ), and the related homologous protein, which belongs to AP2 family members in cucumber, tomato and Arabidopsis. Multiple sequence alignment of these proteins was performed using MUltiple Sequence Comparison by Log‐Expectation (MUSCLE; http://www.linuxlinks.com/MUSCLE). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbour‐joining method in MEGA 11 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

RNA Extraction, Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Roots and cotyledons were collected from 7‐day‐old wild‐type (WT) cucumber seedlings. Stems, leaves, tendrils, fruits, calyxes, and male flowers were harvested from mature WT plants. Early female floral buds at developmental stages 7, 8–1, 8–2, 8–3 and 8–4 were collected from WT plants (Bai et al. 2004). Additionally, mesocarp, epicarp, petals, pistils, and trichomes from female flowers, along with stamens from male flowers at anthesis, were obtained from WT plants. For gene expression analysis, the apical bud of young plants from csgl3, csgl2, csgl1, CsNPF1, oxCsGL2 lines and WT plants were collected to examine transcript levels of CsGL2, CsGL1, CsNPF1, Tu, IPT1, two cytokinin biosynthesis genes (CHL1 and CHL2), and three cytokinin degradation genes (CKX1, CKX3 and CKX6). To analyze the expression of CsGL3 after β‐estradiol induction, apical buds were harvested from the OlexA‐46:CsGL3 (CsGL3ER) transgenic line after treatment to examine CsGL3 transcript levels.

Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). First‐strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA per sample using the PrimeScript FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa), followed by five‐fold dilution with nuclease‐free water. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) with ROX plus (TaKaRa) on a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System. The cucumber CsActin gene served as the internal control (Pan, Wen, et al. 2021). Six biological replicates were analysed for each sample under identical conditions. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). All primers used for qRT‐PCR are listed in Table S1.

RNA‐Seq and Data Analysis

The plant samples were collected from the ovaries of female flowers on the day before flowering of WT (9930) plants and csgl2 mutants. Fifteen plants were randomly selected per treatment, with every five combined to form one RNA‐seq sample, yielding three biological replicates. RNA quality was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. RNA‐seq was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Biomarker Technologies. DEGs were analyzed by DEGseq; the criteria were false discovery rate < 0.01 and fold change > 2. DEGs are shown in Table S2.

RNA In Situ Hybridization

Young shoot apexes and early female floral bud (stages 8–4) from 6‐week‐old WT cucumber plants were fixed in FAA fixative (3.7% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid, 50% ethanol). We amplified a 200‐bp gene‐specific fragment of CsGL2 using specific primers (sequences in Table S1) and generated both sense and antisense digoxigenin (DIG)‐labelled RNA probes using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche, 11175025910). Tissue sectioning, hybridization, and detection were performed as described (Pan, Wen, et al. 2021). The probe sequences are listed in Table S1.

Subcellular Localization

The coding sequence of CsGL2 (without stop codon) was fused to GFP in the pHB vector under the CaMV 35S promoter (pHB‐35S:CsGL2‐GFP). The construct was transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 and infiltrated into the abaxial side of 5‐week‐old Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 2 days of dark incubation, GFP fluorescence was observed using a confocal microscope. To examine CsGL2‐GFP fusion protein expression patterns, fruit spines from oxCsGL2 lines (35S:CsGL2‐GFP) were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. Control samples consisted of cucumber plants transformed with the empty vector (35S:GFP). The primers used for constructing the vector are listed in Table S1.

Cucumber Transformation

The full‐length CsGL3 coding sequence (CDS) was cloned into the XVE modified inducible pERA vector OlexA‐46:CsGL3 construct using XhoI and SpeI sites. The full‐length CsGL2 coding sequence (CDS) was cloned into the pWMV vector using XbaI and BamHI site to generate the pWMV‐35S:CsGL2‐GFP overexpression vector. These constructs were individually transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. For plant transformation, wild‐type cucumber (cv. 9930) was used as the recipient for the pWMV‐35S:CsGL2‐GFP, while the csgl3 mutant served as the recipient for the OlexA‐46:CsGL3 inducible construct. All cucumber transformations were performed following established protocols (Xin et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2014). The primers used for constructing the vector are listed in Table S1.

β‐estradiol induction for the OlexA/XVE system. For induction of the OlexA‐46:CsGL3 (CsGL3ER) line, β‐estradiol stock solution was prepared in DMSO and diluted to 20 μM in water containing 0.015% (v/v) Silwet L‐77. Mock solution contained the same DMSO concentration and 0.015% Silwet L‐77 but lacked β‐estradiol. Induction was performed when plants reached the five‐node stage. The induction solution was applied to the shoot apices once per day for three consecutive days by topically delivering the solution directly onto the apical meristem (e.g., by pipetting a small droplet to fully wet the apex). After treatment, plants were allowed to resume growth and newly formed nodes were monitored; trichome restoration was typically scored at nodes 8–10 in successfully induced plants, and partial restoration frequently resulted in mosaic phenotypes along the same plants. Spine formation and fruit set were scored in tissues/ovaries of these nodes. This treatment formulation follows widely used estradiol‐inducible protocols.

Western Blotting

Total proteins were extracted from young leaves of lines (oxCsGL2) to detect CsGL2‐GFP fusion protein expression. As a positive control for antibody specificity and detection efficiency, we used protein extracts from N. benthamiana leaves transiently expressing the pHB‐35S:CsGL2‐GFP construct. Protein samples were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were then probed with anti‐GFP primary antibody (1:5000 dilution) followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody. Protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate.

Yeast One‐Hybrid (Y1H) Assays

To investigate the binding capacity of CsGL2 to the promoters of CsGL1, Tu, IPT1 and NPF1 (2 kb, 1 kb, 0.5 kb, 0.2 kb and 0.1 kb fragments) and CsGL3 to the CsGL2 promoter (similarly truncated fragments), we performed yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays by cloning the full‐length coding sequences of CsGL2 and CsGL3 into the pB42AD vector (EcoR I/Xho I) and inserting the various promoter fragments into the pLacZ reporter vector (EcoR I/Xho I). The yeast strain EGY48 was co‐transformed with either pB42AD‐CsGL2 or pB42AD‐CsGL3 together with the respective pLacZ‐promoter constructs, while empty pB42AD vector combined with pLacZ‐promoter constructs served as negative controls. Transformants were selected on SD/−Leu/‐Ura medium and protein‐DNA interactions were assessed by blue colony formation on medium containing X‐Gal, with pB42AD‐CsERF31 and D‐ERE:LacZ used as positive controls (Pan et al. 2018). The primers used for constructing these vectors are listed in Table S1.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assays (EMSA)

To validate CsGL2 binding to the P1 region (0.1 kb fragment) of CsGL1, Tu, IPT1, and NPF1 promoters, as well as whether CsGL3 binding to the L1‐box region of CsGL2, we performed EMSA as follows: The full‐length CsGL2 and CsGL3 coding sequences were cloned into pMAL‐p5X vector to generate MBP‐CsGL2 and MBP‐CsGL3 recombinant proteins and expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3). Bacterial cultures were induced with 0.6 mM IPTG at 16°C overnight, and the MBP‐CsGL2 and MBP‐CsGL3 recombinant proteins were then purified using MBP resin (NEB, catalogue number E8021V) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Biotin‐labelled probes containing the P1 region and L1‐box region (synthesized by Sangon Biotech) were prepared using the Biotin 3′ End DNA labeling kit. EMSA was performed using the Light Shift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers and the oligonucleotide probes used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Coupled With Quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR)

To validate in vivo binding of CsGL2 to the P1 region (0.1 kb fragment) in CsGL1, Tu, IPT1 and NPF1 promoters, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) using leaves from 35S:CsGL2‐GFP transgenic cucumber plants, with 35S:GFP empty vector lines (wild type phenotype) as negative controls. Chromatin was crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, sonicated to 200–500 bp fragments, and immunoprecipitated with anti‐GFP antibody. Beads‐only and non‐specific IgG served as additional controls. Enrichment of the P1 region was quantified by qPCR using specific primers, with parallel analysis of the non‐target P2 region from the same promoters. The ΔΔCt method normalized to Input and CsActin reference gene was used for data analysis. All experiments included three biological replicates for the ChIP assay and three technical replicates for the qPCR assay. The primers used for ChIP‐qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Dual‐LUC Assays

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of CsGL2 on the promoters of CsGL1, Tu, IPT1 and CsNPF1, we performed a Dual‐LUC assay as follows: The 2 kb promoter regions of CsGL1, Tu, IPT1 and CsNPF1 were individually amplified and cloned into the pGREENII0800‐LUC reporter vector. The full‐length coding sequence of CsGL2 was inserted into the pHB vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter to generate the effector construct (pHB‐35S:CsGL2). The reporter (pGREENII0800‐promoter:LUC) and effector (pHB‐35S:CsGL2) constructs were separately transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (with helper plasmid pSoup19 for reporters). Bacterial cultures carrying reporter and effector were mixed at a 1:2 (v/v) ratio and co‐infiltrated into leaves of 5‐week‐old Nicotiana benthamiana plants. After 48 h of incubation, infiltrated leaf discs were harvested for luminescence detection using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, China). The firefly luciferase (LUC) signal from each promoter‐reporter was normalized to the Renilla luciferase (REN) signal to account for variation in infection efficiency.

Detection of Endogenous Cytokinins

To quantify endogenous levels of cytokinins (DHZ7G, cZ, BAP, BAP9G, cZROG and pT9G), fresh shoot apices of csgl2, WT (9930), and the oxCsGL2 line were frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground, and extracted using methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, V/V/V) containing internal standards. After centrifugation, the supernatant was evaporated and re‐dissolved into 80% methanol, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane. Samples were analyzed on a UPLC‐ESI‐MS/MS system (ExionLC AD coupled with QTRAP 6500+). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm), using a gradient of 0.04% acetic acid in water and acetonitrile. Cytokinins were detected under scheduled MRM mode with positive/negative ESI, using Analyst 1.6.3 and MultiQuant 3.0.3 for data processing (Cai et al. 2014).

Chemical Treatment

CPPU was applied at a concentration of 20 mg/L, which was sprayed onto the ovaries of the csgl2 mutant at the early female floral bud stages (Stage 8–2 to 8–4) and young shoot apex. Identical treatment with water was used as a control. The CPPU‐treated fruits and control fruits were collected on the day of anthesis. All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates, each consisting of three fruits.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Statistical significance was determined using one‐way ANOVA (for multiple comparisons) or Student's t‐test (for t pairwise comparisons) in GraphPad Prism v8.2.1. Significant differences (p < 0.05) are indicated by lowercase letters (e.g., a, b and c) for ANOVA results.
