Molecularly defined auditory neuron subtypes show different vulnerabilities to noise- and age-related synaptopathy in mice
Authors: Joy A Franco, Taylor G Copeland, Ryan D Merrow, Lisa V Goodrich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuronal subtype-specific synaptopathy is a hallmark of many forms of neurodegeneration. We examined the cellular basis for synaptic vulnerability in the auditory system, where three subtypes of spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs)—Ia, Ib, and Ic—carry acoustic information from the cochlea to the brain. In response to noise and aging, a subset of synapses between inner hair cells and SGNs are lost, but it is unclear how this loss varies across SGN subtypes. Using genetic labeling, we showed that Ia SGNs have larger post-synaptic densities (PSDs) than Ib and Ic SGNs and are the most resilient subtype. Ia PSD volumes increase with age and are unchanged after noise exposure. By contrast, average Ib/Ic PSD volumes do not change with age but decrease with noise. Genetic reprogramming of Ib/Ic neurons to a Ia-like identity provides significant protection against noise-induced synaptopathy, linking identity to resilience and providing an entry point for therapeutics.Subject terms: Cochlea, Peripheral nervous system

Introduction

Excitatory, glutamatergic synapses mediate the rapid transmission and faithful encoding of information between cells. However, excessive glutamate release can cause excitotoxic damage to the post-synaptic neuron and hence synaptopathy, often resulting in synapse loss1. Synaptic dysfunction and loss precede cell death in a range of neurodegenerative disorders2. Despite the ubiquitous presence of excitatory synapses, some synapses are vulnerable and others are resilient, exemplified by the selective vulnerability of certain regions of the brain in Alzheimer’s Disease3. What makes some synapses better able to withstand excitotoxic insults remains unknown.

Type I spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) of the auditory system provide a tractable opportunity for uncovering the relationship between a neuron’s genetic identity and the vulnerability of its synapses. Sound detection begins in the cochlea, where inner hair cells (IHCs) respond to sound via mechanically sensitive ion channels. IHCs then release glutamate from presynaptic ribbon structures onto the postsynaptic terminals of Type I SGNs, which fire action potentials that travel from the cochlea to the brain. Although uniformly glutamatergic, individual IHC-SGN synapses exhibit a range of morphologies and properties that correlate with anatomical and physiological differences among the SGN subtypes4. Type I SGN subtypes exhibit differences in their spontaneous firing rates (SR) and the minimum sound intensity required for action potential generation5, with low SR fibers innervating the modiolus-facing (modiolar) region of the IHC and high SR fibers innervating the opposing pillar side. Type I SGNs were recently divided into molecularly distinct Ia, Ib, and Ic SGNs that differ in expression of many synaptic and calcium signaling proteins6–8. Based on the position of their terminals on the IHC and their firing patterns in vitro, Ia SGNs correspond best to high SR SGNs, whereas Ic SGNs are likely low SR SGNs9 (Fig. 1a). In contrast to the complexity of synaptic connectivity in the central nervous system (CNS), Type I SGNs receive inputs from only one or two IHCs and each IHC signals to 20 or fewer Type I SGNs10, making it straightforward to quantify and assess synaptic properties comprehensively. Additionally, the efficacy of signaling through IHC-SGN synapses can be assessed by recording auditory brainstem responses (ABRs), since the amplitude of the first coordinated neuronal response correlates with the number of cochlear synapses11. Excessively intense stimuli lead to loss of some IHC-SGN synapses and hence reduced ABRs, likely due to glutamate excitotoxicity12,13. Understanding why some IHC-SGN synapses are lost while others persist following acoustic trauma offers a valuable chance to characterize mechanisms of selective vulnerability among neurons that are otherwise very similar to each other.

Although cochlear synaptopathy does not affect all SGNs equally, it remains unclear which SGN subtypes are most resilient and which are most vulnerable. Noise exposure and aging can both lead to cochlear synaptopathy that has physiological relevance to hearing loss in humans. In humans and mice, early life exposure to excessive noise is a strong predictor of age-related hearing loss and is correlated with a permanent loss of synapses and eventual SGN death. In humans, this pathology likely underlies “hidden hearing loss” where auditory thresholds are maintained but patients report difficulties hearing in noisy environments14–17. SGN synaptic heterogeneity is thought to endow the cochlea with a broad dynamic range such that high SR fibers encode low intensity auditory information and low SR fibers are recruited at louder sound intensities, when high SR fibers are saturated. Loss of low SR SGNs would in theory reduce the dynamic range of the cochlea, making it more difficult to encode sounds in noisy auditory environments. However, definitive evidence that low SR SGNs are most impacted by synaptopathy is lacking. Being able to genetically access the most vulnerable IHC-SGN synapses would provide a handle for directly probing the contributions of synaptic heterogeneity to hidden hearing loss.

Physiological and anatomical approaches used to study cochlear synaptic resilience have faced several technical limitations. In vivo recordings of the auditory nerve in noise exposed guinea pigs showed a reduction in the prevalence of low SR SGNs18, which likely correspond to Ic SGNs9. However, no firm conclusions could be made about the identity of the affected neurons, as it was unclear whether the change in firing properties was due to a loss of specific synapses or because of noise-induced alterations in the properties of the remaining synapses and/or the post-synaptic neuron. Additionally, a loss of low SR fibers was not observed in noise exposed mice19, despite the fact that anatomical studies suggested Ic SGN vulnerability, as synapses on the modiolar side (where Ib and Ic fibers are positioned) were preferentially lost. Indeed, genetically labeled Ic SGNs experience significant synapse loss following acoustic trauma but the resilience of Ia’s was not directly tested nor was the effect of age20. Low SR SGNs, presumably Ic neurons, also seem to be lost from the aged cochlea, suggested both by in vivo recordings in aged gerbils21 and the fact that the surviving synapses in older animals primarily reside on the pillar side of the IHC22, suggesting they belong to Ia SGNs. In situ hybridization for Ia and Ic SGN markers similarly showed that Ia SGNs survive into old age whereas Ic SGNs are lost6. Since SGN loss can be exacerbated by early life exposure to noise14, the most parsimonious interpretation is that aging Ic SGNs lose their synapses and then die. However, the pattern of synapse loss along the pillar-modiolar axis is not consistent across the multiple strains of mice examined23, so it is also possible that different mechanisms are involved. Additionally, synaptic position is not an entirely reliable indicator of Type I SGN subtypes, as positions can shift during repair24 and high SR fibers have been identified on all sides of IHCs in cats25,26. On average, high SR fibers associate with smaller ribbons, which are on the pillar side of IHCs in mice27, but ribbon size is not a definitive indicator of an individual SGN’s identity. It is also possible that SGN synapses are more vulnerable due to their position on the modiolar side of the IHC, where efferent axons are more abundant. In fact, efferent innervation can induce compensatory changes in SGN output following damage, highlighting the potential for physiological changes that might be mistakenly interpreted as changes in SGN subtype identity. Thus, the question of whether selective vulnerability is due to molecular differences among SGN subtypes remains unanswered.

Here, we directly evaluated subtype vulnerability by using genetic approaches to label Ia, Ib, and Ic SGNs. Our data confirm that Ic subtypes are the most vulnerable to both age-related and noise-induced synaptopathy. Furthermore, a genetic manipulation that shifts Ic SGNs towards Ia identities without changing the position of their synapses resulted in enhanced resilience to noise exposure. Together, these findings link genetic identity to resilience, offering a fruitful platform for identifying relevant molecular programs and developing targeted therapeutics in the future.

Results

Type Ia SGNs are most resilient to age-related synaptopathy

To track synapse loss from molecularly defined SGN subtypes, Ib and Ic SGNs were genetically labeled using Netring1Cre/+;Rosa26Ai14+ mice (Ntng1Cre;Ai14). Consistent with the enrichment of Netring1 (Ntng1) in adult Ib and Ic SGNs6, tdTomato (tdT) is expressed in the vast majority of Ib and Ic SGNs and not in Ia SGNs in Ntng1Cre;Ai14 mice28. Cytosolic tdT fills the Ib and Ic SGN peripheral processes and their terminals, which were mostly on the modiolar side of the IHC (Fig. 1b), though this boundary was not strictly observed. Since this approach does not allow us to distinguish Ib’s from Ic’s, we refer to these SGNs collectively as “Ib/Ic.” IHC-SGN synapses were identified with antibodies for CtBP2, which detects Ribeye in the pre-synaptic ribbons, and Homer1, which marks the post-synaptic densities (PSDs) in the SGN terminals (Fig. 1b–d). Each PSD could be reliably scored as belonging to Ib/Ic (tdT+, Fig. 1b–d orange arrows) or Ia (tdT-) SGN subtypes by assessing the degree of overlap between the PSD and tdT. Obvious CALB2 + , tdT- afferent terminals were also visible, consistent with tdT- terminals belonging to Ia SGNs (Fig. 1c–d, green arrows). TdT was also visible in efferents (Fig. 1b–d, white arrows) and glial cells (Fig. 1, yellow arrows). However, these structures are spatially and morphologically distinct from PSD containing terminals and therefore did not confound analysis.

Visualization of synapses in cochleae from young (9–11 weeks) and old (83–85 weeks) Ntng1Cre;Ai14 animals confirmed age-related synaptopathy. For all studies, animals of both sexes were included. For each animal, a high-magnification, 100 µm wide field of view was acquired at the 32 kHz region, which included on average 11.4 + /−0.4 IHCs in young animals (N = 5) and 7.4 + /−0.6 IHCs in old animals (N = 7). We focused on the 32 kHz region as noise-induced synapse loss is maximal in the base20, and sufficient numbers of IHCs remain in the aged animals to enable analysis. The number of ribbons and PSDs was qualitatively reduced in older animals compared to young animals (Fig. 1c, d). In addition, fewer Ib/Ic terminals were apparent on and under IHCs in the old animals (Fig. 1d’). Analysis of SGN density in sections through the mid-base region of the cochlea revealed no significant effect of age, as assessed by staining for the pan-neuronal marker HuD (Supplementary Fig. 1c, young=4.72 + /−0.55 SGNs/µm2 [x10−3], N = 3, old=3.66 + /−0.87 SGNs/µm2 [×10−3], N = 3, mean + /−standard deviation, SD, p = 0.24, two-tailed unpaired t test). Likewise, co-labeling for tdT and HuD revealed no significant change in the density (Supplementary Fig. 1c, young=2.78 + /−0.55, N = 3, old=2.25 + /−0.57, N = 3, mean + /−SD, p = 0.65, two-tailed unpaired t test) or proportion of tdT+, i.e., Ib/Ic SGNs (Supplementary Fig. 1d, young=0.55 + /−0.07, old=0.59 + /−0.04, mean + /−SD, p = 0.32, two-tailed unpaired t test). Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed synapse loss is not secondary to SGN cell death.

Quantitative analysis revealed that most surviving synapses were associated with Ia SGNs. To quantify the proportion of surviving synapses that belong to Ia (tdT-) vs. Ib/Ic (tdT+) SGNs, we adapted an existing strategy29 to identify synapses, defined as closely paired ribbon and PSD puncta, and to assign SGN subtype identity based on the overlap of the PSD punctum with tdT signal (see Methods, GitHub repository, and Supplementary Figs. 2, 3 for full details). To quantify the density of synapses in the 32 kHz region for each animal, a paired synaptic index (SI) was calculated by normalizing the total number of paired synapses to the number of hair cells analyzed (see Methods). Regardless of whether pre- or post-synaptic puncta were used to anchor the analysis, mean SIs were significantly lower in old animals in comparison to young animals (Fig. 1e, ribbon-based paired SI: young=18.1 + /−1.7, old=11.8 + /−2.4, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test; PSD-based paired SI: young=17.3 + /−1.9, old=11.3 + /−2.2, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test). Among the surviving synapses, the proportion belonging to Ib/Ic SGNs was significantly reduced in old animals (Fig. 1f, young=0.45 + /−0.07; old=0.28 + /−0.11, mean + /−SD, p = 0.010, two-tailed unpaired t test). Similar results were obtained by manual scoring and using a custom Python-based automated classification algorithm that assigns molecular identity based on tdT intensity within the reconstructed PSD surface (Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, these data show that Type Ia SGNs are the most resilient to age and that enhanced loss of Ib/Ic synapses precedes SGN cell death.

Ib/Ic SGN synapses are disproportionally lost following noise exposure

To investigate whether Ib/Ic SGNs are also more vulnerable to noise-induced synaptopathy, we exposed Ntng1Cre;Ai14 mice (8–9 weeks of age) to moderately intense sound stimuli that cause permanently reduced auditory responses independent of hair cell damage (2-h exposure to 8–16 kHz octave band noise at 100 decibels sound pressure level, dB SPL, Fig. 2a). Responses to sound were evaluated by measuring auditory brainstem responses (ABRs), which reflect the collective activity of neurons upon presentation of stimuli of different frequencies and intensities30. The ABR threshold is the lowest intensity at which the peak in the first waveform (wave-I) is detected; the amplitude of this response reflects the summed SGN activity at that intensity. Increasing the sound intensity of the stimulus results in larger wave-I amplitudes as more SGNs are activated. Extremely intense stimuli can permanently elevate ABR thresholds, whereas more moderate stimuli cause elevations in ABR thresholds one day after exposure that return close to baseline after one week and are hence “temporary” threshold shifts. Wave-I amplitudes, however, do not fully recover because synapses are permanently lost15, predominantly in higher frequency regions of the cochlea. Thus, this Temporary Threshold Shift (TTS) paradigm is a reliable way to investigate physiologically relevant synaptopathy.

Since genetic background influences resilience to noise exposure31, we first confirmed that Ntng1Cre;Ai14 mice hear normally at baseline and reliably experience TTS after sound exposure (Fig. 2b, c). Noise exposed mice (N = 7) were compared to sham exposed controls (N = 7), which were handled identically except that no sound was played after they were placed in the chamber (Fig. 2a). ABR thresholds in the 32 kHz region were significantly elevated in noise exposed mice relative to controls one day after exposure and were statistically indistinguishable between the two groups at one week post-exposure (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Consistent with the presence of synaptopathy, wave-I amplitudes to pure tone stimuli of increasing sound intensity resulted in significantly different growth curves between noise and sham exposed mice (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 5b, p < 0.001, nonlinear fit of nonlinear regression Extra sum-of-squares F Test32).

We found that Type Ib/Ic synapses account for the vast majority of the total synapse loss in noise exposed Ntng1Cre;Ai14 animals. Qualitatively, ribbons and PSDs were sparser in cochleae from noise exposed (Fig. 2e) vs. sham exposed animals (Fig. 2d). Notably, the density of Type Ib/Ic terminals appeared relatively similar between the groups (Fig. 2d’ and 2e’), consistent with the persistence of SGN peripheral processes close to the IHC in electron microscopy (EM) reconstructions33. Quantification in the 32 kHz region using both ribbon- and PSD-based approaches confirmed a significantly reduced SI in noise exposed mice (Fig. 2f, ribbon-based paired SI: sham=16.4 + /−2.2, noise=6.7 + /−1.9, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test; PSD-based paired SI: sham=14.4 + /−2.7, noise=6.8 + /−2.1, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test). Ib/Ic synapses account for most of this loss, as the proportion of Ib/Ic (tdT+) synapses was significantly reduced in noise exposed mice relative to sham controls (Fig. 2g, sham=0.50 + /−0.07, noise=0.29 + /−0.09, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test). A small proportion of synapses in each group were excluded as they could not be definitively scored as being tdT+ (Supplementary Fig. 6a, sham=0.09 + /−0.03, noise=0.07 + /−0.03, mean + /−SD, p = 0.44, two-tailed unpaired t test). Assigning tdT+ or tdT- status to these synapses did not alter the conclusions of the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c), further supported by an independent assessment based on tdT intensity (Supplementary Fig. 4). When placed in the context of a model IHC that starts with 10 synapses, this analysis predicts that four out of five Type Ib/Ic SGNs lose their synapses, whereas only one out of five Ia’s appears to be affected (Fig. 2h). Thus, Ia SGNs are more resilient than Ib/Ic SGNs to both noise-induced and age-related synaptopathy.

Age and noise have different effects on PSD volumes

The identification of genetically defined subsets of vulnerable synapses offers an opportunity to systematically compare properties that might relate to their vulnerability. Ribbon and PSD volumes differ along the pillar-modiolar axis and might therefore relate to SGN identity29. However, since volume gradients are often subtle and vary depending on species34 and age22, subtype-specific differences in synaptic morphologies may be overlooked if position along the pillar-modiolar axis is used as a proxy for subtype identity. Additionally, it is unclear if noise exposure and age have similar effects on synaptic properties. To address this knowledge gap, we calculated the volume of the same PSD puncta that were reconstructed in Imaris and assigned subtype identities using SynAnalyzer.

We first compared the distribution of Ia vs. Ib/Ic synaptic PSD volumes (i.e., those belonging to a paired synapse) in baseline conditions. To do this, we normalized all Imaris-calculated volumes for old and noise exposed animals to the median volume in the relevant control dataset (i.e., young and sham exposed, respectively). This approach enables a straightforward assessment of distributions, where any normalized volume greater than one is above the median and any volume less than one is below the median. We found that in young animals, 61% of Type Ia PSD volumes were greater than one compared to only 51% of Type Ib/Ic PSDs (Fig. 3a). Similarly, in the sham dataset, 59% of Ia PSDs were above the median volume compared to 46% of Ib/Ic’s (Fig. 3b). Indeed, normalized PSD volumes were significantly greater for Ia vs. Ib/Ic SGNs at baseline (Fig. 3a, b, young: Ia=1.16 + /−0.56, Ib/Ic=1.04 + /−0.49, p = 0.004, sham: Ia=1.11 + /−0.71, Ib/Ic=0.98 + /−0.59, p < 0.001, median + /−SD, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction used for all comparisons).

This trend is reversed in the case of noise exposure (b), where Ia distributions do not significantly differ, but the Ib/Ic distribution is significantly shifted towards smaller volumes. PSD volumes changed with both age and noise, but these changes occurred in different SGN populations. With age, Ia PSD volumes became even larger, shifting to a higher normalized value in old vs. young animals (Fig. 3a, young=1.16 + /−0.56, old=1.33 + /−0.80, median + /−SD, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p < 0.001) and growing from 61% above the median in young animals to 69% in old animals. However, a similar shift was not observed in the Ib/Ic’s (Fig. 3a, young=1.04 + /−0.49, old=0.93 + /−0.73, median + /−SD, p > 0.99, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction). These trends were reversed in sham vs. noise exposed animals. Type Ia distributions did not change significantly (Fig. 3b, sham: Ia=1.11 + /−0.71, noise=1.21 + /−0.59, median + /−SD, p > 0.99, Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s correction), with 59% vs. 64% of PSDs above the median in sham vs. noise exposed animals. However, the Ib/Ic PSD volume distribution shifted toward smaller normalized volumes (Fig. 3b, sham=0.98 + /−0.59, noise=0.80 + /−0.48, median + /−SD, p < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction), shrinking from 46% of PSD volumes above the median in sham animals to 37% in noise exposed ones. Consistent with the decrease in Ib/Ic volumes, Ia PSDs remained significantly greater than Ib/Ic volumes following noise exposure despite the lack of any change in Ia PSD volumes (Fig. 3b, noise Ia=1.21 + /−0.59, noise Ib/Ic=0.80 + /−0.48, median + /−SD, p < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction).

Qualitative analysis of summed intensity projection thumbnails further confirmed that Ia and Ib/Ic PSDs undergo subtle but different changes in morphology both with age (Fig. 3c) and following noise exposure (Fig. 3d). Morphological changes were particularly evident in puncta from within 10% of the 75th percentile for each group, i.e., those that are most different quantitatively (Fig. 3a, b, upper dashed lines in violin plots). Altogether, these data suggest that SGN subtype identity can inform PSD properties and how they change with use. Moreover, age and noise exposure have different effects on the PSDs. Ia synapses may become larger with age, whereas in the case of noise exposure, Ib/Ic PSDs both start smaller and become smaller still.

More unpaired PSDs are present in Type Ib/Ic SGNs with age and noise exposure

Throughout the analysis of age-related and noise-induced synaptopathy, we frequently observed CtBP2+ and Homer1+ puncta lacking a closely apposed partner, consistent with previous studies of the effect of noise exposure11,35. Thus, synapse loss corresponds to loss of either or both the ribbon and the PSD. This raises the possibility that unpaired PSDs may also be biased to Ib/Ic SGNs. We therefore assessed the tdT status and hence identity of all unpaired PSDs in our dataset.

Consistent with prior reports, both unpaired ribbons and unpaired PSDs were identified in the complete set of synaptic reconstructions (Fig. 4a). We focused our analysis on unpaired PSDs, since the close spatial overlap in tdT and PSD signal within the complete 3D reconstruction allowed us to assign SGN identity confidently. PSDs were scored as unpaired if there was no signal in the ribbon channel (Fig. 4b, see Fig. 3c, d for comparison). An “Unpaired PSD Index” was calculated by normalizing the number of all unpaired PSDs or only Ib/Ic unpaired PSDs to the number of IHCs in the image (Fig. 4c). In young animals, unpaired PSDs were rarely observed (young=0.98 + /−0.53, N = 5) and of those, fewer than 10% belonged to Ib/Ic SGNs (proportion of Ib/Ic unpaired synapses: young=0.07 + /−0.06). More unpaired PSDs were present in old animals (old=1.61 + /−0.52, N = 7) but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.07, two-tailed unpaired t test), likely due to the small number of unpaired PSDs overall. However, the proportion of unpaired PSDs belonging to Ib/Ic SGNs increased significantly with age (old=0.24 + /−0.15, mean + /−SD, p = 0.02, two-tailed unpaired t test). Noise exposure had an even greater effect on the frequency of unpaired PSDs when assessed at 1 week post-exposure (sham=0.77 + /−0.34, N = 7 vs. noise=2.17 + /−0.99, N = 7, mean + /−SD; p = 0.009, two-tailed unpaired t test) and nearly doubled the proportion belonging to Ib/Ic SGNs (sham=0.19 + /−0.24 vs. noise=0.37 + /−0.09, mean + /−SD), though this increase did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.11, two-tailed unpaired t test).

Together these data suggest that with age, every other IHC gains an unpaired PSD, and of these, 60% belong to Ib/Ic’s. A similar trend occurs with noise where every IHC gains an unpaired PSD, and about half of those belong to Ib/Ic’s. Taken together, these data support the idea that Ib/Ic SGNs are more likely to lose their synapses, sometimes disappearing altogether and in other cases losing their apposition to a ribbon.

Complementary patterns of resilience and vulnerability in Type Ia vs. Ic SGNs

Given the clear differences in vulnerability revealed by analysis of Ntng1Cre;Ai14 animals, we sought to make a more direct comparison between the most (Ic) and least (Ia) vulnerable neurons. We therefore repeated the TTS-inducing noise exposure paradigm using tamoxifen-inducible Cre lines that drive tdT expression in mostly Ia (Calb2CreERT2) or just Ic SGNs (Lypd1CreERT2) (Fig. 5a). Calb2CreERT2 primarily labels Ia SGNs, as they have the highest levels of Calb2 expression, but Ib SGNs can also be labeled, albeit infrequently9. TdT+ fibers in the Calb2CreERT2;Ai14 mice frequently overlapped with Calb2 and were primarily on the pillar side of the IHCs (Fig. 5c), but as with prior reports, tdT+ fibers were occasionally on the modiolar side9. By contrast, tdT+ fibers in Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14 mice did not overlap with Calb2 and were mostly on the modiolar side, consistent with a Ic identity. In both lines, a subset of PSD puncta were localized within tdT+ terminals and apposed by ribbons (Fig. 5c–e).

Both lines exhibited TTS at 32 kHz, with thresholds returning close to baseline one week after exposure, with minor differences in the extent of recovery (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 5c). Noise exposure led to significant synapse loss in the 32 kHz region in both reporter lines (Fig. 5f, PSD-based SI, Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14: sham=19.3 + /−1.3, N = 7, noise=8.6 + /−0.9, N = 5, p < 0.001; Calb2CreERT2;Ai14: sham=18.8 + /−0.8, N = 7, noise=8.4 + /−1.0, N = 8, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test). The proportion of putative Ic synapses in Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14 was significantly decreased in noise exposed mice relative to sham controls (Fig. 5g, tdT+ synapse proportion: sham=0.35 + /−0.04, noise=0.15 + /−0.04, mean + /−SD, p < 0.001, two-tailed unpaired t test). By contrast, in noise exposed Calb2CreERT2;Ai14 mice, the mean proportion of putative Ia synapses modestly increased, but this change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5g, tdT+ synapse proportion: sham=0.16 + /−0.09, noise=0.28 + /−0.13, mean + /−SD, p = 0.06, two-tailed unpaired t test). Thus, these experiments directly demonstrate the resilience of Type Ia SGNs to noise-induced synaptopathy. Similar results were obtained when synapses with uncertain tdT status were included (Supplementary Fig. 6) and by automated analysis of tdT+ intensities (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Analysis of unpaired PSDs reproduced what was observed in Ntng1Cre;Ai14 mice. In both reporter lines, the unpaired PSD index was significantly greater in noise exposed animals relative to sham controls (Fig. 5h, Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14: sham=1.3 + /−0.1, noise=3.5 + /−1.2, mean + /−SD, p = 0.02, unpaired t test; Calb2CreERT2;Ai14: sham=1.4 + /−0.3, noise=3.4 + /−0.7, median + /−SD, p = 0.002, Two-tailed Mann–Whitney). This increase largely reflects the addition of Type Ic unpaired PSDs, since the proportion of putative Ic unpaired PSDs is significantly higher in noise exposed Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14 animals relative to sham controls (Fig. 5i, tdT+ Unpaired PSD proportion sham=0.08 + /−0.07, noise=0.20 + /−0.04, mean + /−SD, p = 0.007, two-tailed unpaired t test). The proportion of putative Ia unpaired PSDs did not change in noise exposed Calb2CreERT2;Ai14 animals (Fig. 5i, tdT+ Unpaired PSD proportion sham=0.18 + /−0.15, noise=0.20 + /−0.10, mean + /−SD, p = 0.69, two-tailed unpaired t test). Consistent with what was observed for Ntng1Cre;Ai14, these experiments confirm that Ic SGNs are more likely to lose synapses and hence retain unpaired PSDs than Ia SGNs following noise exposure (Fig. 5j).

Shifting Type Ib/Ic SGNs towards a Ia identity protects synapses from noise

The relative resilience of Ia synapses to noise exposure suggests that intrinsic molecular differences may be responsible. Alternatively, there may be something about the nature of the synapses in the pillar region of the IHC that makes them less susceptible to glutamate-induced excitotoxic damage36. For example, lateral olivocochlear efferent neurons provide more innervation to afferents positioned on the modiolar side than on the pillar side and de-efferentation leads to changes in ribbon and glutamate receptor patch sizes37.

To distinguish among these possibilities, we altered the proportions of Type I SGN subtypes by conditionally deleting Runx1 from SGNs using Bhlhe22Cre (aka bhlhb5Cre) mice, which mediates recombination in SGNs but not in other cell types in the cochlea38. Runx1 encodes a transcription factor that promotes Ib and Ic SGN fates39 and its conditional deletion from SGNs increases the proportion of Ia SGNs at the expense of Ib and Ic SGNs, as assessed by single cell RNA-sequencing39 (Fig. 6a). Importantly, this deletion does not affect SGN wiring, as Runx1 conditional knock-out (CKO, Bhlhe22Cre/+;Runx1flox/flox) and control animals (CON, Bhlhe22+/+;Runx1flox/flox) have similar densities of SGNs that innervate the entirety of the IHC basolateral surface39. However, gradients of ribbon and PSD size do differ in CKO animals relative to CONs, such that terminals on the modiolar side of the IHC take on Ia-like properties39, offering an opportunity to assess their resilience to noise.

Analysis of synapse loss in noise exposed Runx1 CKO and CON animals demonstrated that molecular differences among SGN subtypes impact vulnerability. Mice of both genotypes were more sensitive to auditory damage than the other mouse lines we characterized and experienced permanent threshold shifts even with a 97.5 dB SPL stimulus, likely due to background differences (Fig. 6b). However, there was no significant difference between genotypes in either sham or noise exposed animals at 32 kHz (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 5). As IHC-localized ribbons are rarely found unpaired from a PSD, they are an excellent proxy for synaptic index35,40. We therefore streamlined the analysis by quantifying only pre-synaptic ribbons. We found that ribbon density was significantly diminished following noise exposure in both genotypes (Fig. 6d, mean ribbon index, CON: sham=18.2 + /−0.6, N = 9, noise=8.5 + /−2.2, N = 10, p < 0.001, CKO: sham=16.3 + /−2.2, N = 7, noise=11.7 + /3.0, N = 9, p < 0.001, mean + /−SD, Two-way ANOVA with FDR correction). However, the severity of synapse loss was significantly greater in CON animals relative to CKO (Fig. 6d, p= 0.001, Two-way ANOVA with FDR correction). A similar difference was observed by quantifying either Ribbon- or PSD-based SIs for a subset of preparations that were also stained for Homer1 (Fig. 6d, Ribbon-based SI, CON noise=8.2 + /−1.9, N = 8, CKO noise=11.8 + /−3.4, N = 5, mean + /−SD, p = 0.003; PSD-based SI, CON noise=8.5 + -1.8, CKO noise=11.3 + /−3.4, mean + /−SD, p = 0.005, Two-way ANOVA with FDR correction used for both comparisons).

Analysis of unpaired synaptic markers further supported the conclusion that the shift towards a Ia identity made synapses more resilient. The mean unpaired ribbon index slightly increased with noise exposure for both genotypes, but this increase was only significant for CON mice (Fig. 6e, Unpaired Ribbon Index CON: sham=0.07 + /−0.18, noise=0.86 + /−0.45, p = 0.006, CKO: sham=0.00 + /−0.82, noise=0.18 + /−0.66, median + /−SD, p = 0.10, Two-way ANOVA with FDR correction used for both comparisons). However, no significant difference was found here between CON and CKO noise exposed animals, again due to the challenges of studying rare events (p = 0.10, Two-way ANOVA with FDR correction). The protective effect of conditional Runx1 deletion was more pronounced in the unpaired PSD index, where noise exposed CKO mice had significantly fewer unpaired PSDs than their CON littermates (Fig. 6e, CON noise=3.1 + /−1.5, CKO noise=1.4 + /−1.0, p = 0.008). Of note, at baseline, CKO mice have slightly fewer synapses than their CON littermates (Fig. 6d) and the same number of unpaired PSDs. Hence, noise exposure causes CON mice to lose about 53% of their synapses and gain a significant number of unpaired PSDs (Fig. 6f). By contrast, only about 33% of synapses are lost in the CKO mice, which acquire essentially no new unpaired PSDs. These findings strongly support the conclusion that gene expression differences between Ia and Ic SGNs are responsible for selective vulnerability to noise exposure.

Discussion

Though it has long been hypothesized that low threshold, high SR SGNs are more resilient than high threshold, low SR SGNs, definitive proof was missing due to the lack of specific molecular markers for the Type I SGN subtypes. Here, we used genetic approaches to demonstrate that Type Ia SGNs, which match the anatomical properties of low threshold, high SR SGNs, show a resilience to noise-induced and age-related synaptopathy that is not present in Ic SGNs, which match the anatomical properties of high threshold, low SR SGNs. Across three different Cre reporter lines that experienced the same degree of noise-induced synapse loss, Ib/Ic synapses were lost and Ia synapses were largely preserved. This was true regardless of whether Ia or Ic SGNs expressed tdT. Additionally, an independent study demonstrated Ic vulnerability using the same Lypd1CreERT2 driver on the C57BL/6J background, which experienced greater threshold shifts20. Ia resilience reflects, at least in part, intrinsic molecular differences, as we also observed greater protection from cochlear synaptopathy, resulting in more synapses and fewer unpaired PSDs, when SGN identities and synaptic properties were more Ia-like. This fits with the discovery that noise exposure activates an ATF3/ATF4 cellular stress response pathway in Ia’s and not in Ib’s or Ic’s41, although it is unclear what the consequence of this activation might be. We also observed enhanced loss of Ib/Ic synapses in old mice and this preceded any evidence of SGN cell body loss. Although definitive proof is still needed, our data suggest that Ib’s are also more resilient than Ic’s, since more tdTomato+ synapses survived in Ntng1Cre;Ai14 animals, where both Ib and Ic terminals are labeled, than in Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14 animals, where only Ic terminals are labeled. Additionally, Ib’s are more likely to survive with age than Ic SGNs6. An important area for future investigation is identifying what makes Ic’s vulnerable and Ia’s resilient. Together, these findings suggest that age-related and noise-induced cochlear synaptopathy could be mitigated by specifically inducing Ia-like protective mechanisms in Ic SGNs.

Despite the well-established anatomical and physiological heterogeneity of IHC-SGN synapses, it has been challenging to uncover which molecular differences might influence their resilience4,36,42. In fact, although the gradients of ribbon and PSD volumes map onto the same axis as the Ia, Ib, and Ic terminals, the volumes are widely distributed and overlap considerably. Further, the degree of heterogeneity varies across genetic backgrounds and can change depending on how the pillar-modiolar axis is defined43. Synaptic features also change after depletion of olivocochlear efferent innervation, highlighting the dynamic nature of the IHC-SGN synapse37. However, at least some of this heterogeneity appears to be intrinsically determined, as ribbon volume correlates with SGN identity6 and mean ribbon and PSD volumes shift predictably with SGN identity39 and when subtype-appropriate programs of gene expression are altered44. Consistent with these findings, we found that PSD volumes are largest in Ia SGNs and that Ia PSDs are more resilient, though it is unclear whether the increased volumes play any role in this resilience.

Aging and noise affected PSD properties in different ways, raising the possibility that there are different molecular mechanisms for resilience and vulnerability. In the case of aging, the mean Ia PSD volume increased. By contrast, with noise, the mean Ib/Ic volume decreased, whereas Ia PSD volumes were unchanged. The noise-induced decrease in PSD volumes could be caused either by loss of larger Ib/Ic PSDs or by a uniform shrinking of Ib/Ic PSDs that results in the complete loss of the smallest synapses. Such decreases in volume could reflect heightened activity in the same pathways that normally maintain pools of glutamate receptors (AMPARs) in an activity-dependent manner45. Similarly, Type Ic SGN PSDs could shrink as glutamate receptors are endocytosed and go unreplaced. In support of this idea, AMPAR cycling begins within minutes of excessive noise exposure46, possibly as a synapse-protective response40. Hence, Type Ic SGNs might experience more receptor endocytosis under excitotoxic conditions, such that the damage becomes too severe to repair. In parallel, Ia SGNs may be more efficient in rebuilding damaged synapses. There is strong evidence for some degree of synapse repair35. For instance, EM reconstructions indicate that three out of five terminals lose their entire post-synaptic density at 24 h post-exposure, but one out of the three that are lost will be rebuilt by one week post-exposure33. Low level repair may also explain the counterintuitive finding that Ia SGNs were more likely to be associated with unpaired PSDs both before and after exposure. Although more Ib/Ic terminals had unpaired PSDs with age and noise exposure, most of the unpaired PSDs were still associated with Ia SGNs. Perhaps Ia SGNs are more likely to retain a PSD and hence eventually repair the synapse, as others have observed a partial recovery of unpaired GluA3 puncta that occurs by two weeks post-noise exposure35. Of note, unpaired PSDs were rare in all conditions, with ~1 per IHC at baseline and 2-4 per IHC with age or noise exposure. Although this is a large fold change, the absolute numbers are small, making it challenging to detect a consistent increase in the proportion that belong to Ib/Ic SGNs.

The age-related increase in Ia SGN PSD volumes may reflect a different type of protective response. Surviving SGN terminals in aged animals appear larger22 and, given our findings, most likely belong to Ia SGNs. Since our data revealed essentially no loss of Ia synapses with age, the increase in mean volume is likely due to an increase in the size of the PSD in surviving terminals, rather than a loss of small PSDs. Such a compensatory response occurs widely in the nervous system, where neurons frequently scale their PSDs in response to changes in pre-synaptic activity45. A possible role for similar scaling in the cochlea is supported by the fact that AMPAR patches are larger in mice that cannot release glutamate35. Age-related changes in auditory function are not limited to SGNs and multiple sources of damage could result in age-related reductions in glutamate release from IHCs including loss of OHCs. Thus, the observed increase in Type Ia PSD volumes could be compensation for decreased glutamate release at the ribbon synapse that stems from age-related changes in cochlear function. Why such a mechanism is not evident in Ic synapses is unclear.

Another key contributor to heterogeneity in IHC-SGN synapse resilience may be subtype-specific differences in calcium buffering properties. The current model of noise-induced synaptopathy is that excessive glutamate leads to calcium-induced excitotoxicity that overloads post-synaptic calcium buffering capacity12,13, perhaps pushing some synapses past the point where they can be repaired. Differences in the axon caliber and mitochondrial content of pillar vs. modiolar terminals support the idea that Type Ib/Ic SGNs are more vulnerable to calcium dysregulation33,47,48. Mitochondria act as calcium sinks during synaptic transmission49–51, so the presence of more mitochondria could enhance calcium buffering capacity. In support of this idea, the majority of remaining terminals in aged mice were pillar-innervating and had larger mitochondrial volumes22, consistent with a Ia identity. More work is needed to determine how different subtypes handle calcium, how the number of mitochondria influences this response, and whether there are also subtype-specific differences in mitochondrial biology that cause functional differences in calcium buffering capacity and/or ATP production.

The identification of intrinsically different vulnerabilities among SGN subtypes provides a valuable entry point for therapeutic studies. The loss of Ic and perhaps also Ib synapses leads to clear differences in auditory responses that fit well with what we know about subtype-related differences in physiology. Low SR SGNs resist saturation at higher sound intensities and could be important for sound encoding in loud environments52. Although cochlear synaptopathy also affects humans53, there is not yet a proven human perceptual correlate. This may be a limitation of diagnostic power, as patients with normal ABR thresholds can experience hidden hearing loss, i.e., poor hearing performance despite normal abilities to detect sound17,54. This has been hypothesized to be due to a loss of Type Ib and/or Ic SGNs and our data in mice supports this. In these experiments, despite preservation of ABR thresholds, 32 kHz wave-I amplitudes at 70 dB SPL (the sound intensity of loud conversations) were significantly reduced by 30% and correlated with a 42% reduction in Type Ib/Ic synapses. What this degree of loss means for hearing function in humans, including those with cochlear implants, remains to be determined. Our analysis of Runx1 CKO mice confirmed that synaptic vulnerability can be tuned towards resilience by altering neuronal identity. This raises the possibility that certain genes or drugs might be able to reproduce Ia-related resilience, preferably without altering the Ib/Ic properties needed for achieving a wide dynamic range. Future research should address whether conferring such resilience to Ib/Ic SGNs can indeed preserve auditory function in the face of noise exposure and aging. Additionally, it will be interesting to see whether molecular differences in the vulnerability or resilience of SGN synapses are relevant to other forms of synaptopathy, including Alzheimer’s Disease, as well as how neuroimmune responses and efferent activity contribute32,55.

Our conclusions are supported by a multi-pronged genetic approach, but some limitations should be noted. First, our analysis was restricted to the 32 kHz region of the cochlea. This region was chosen based on foundational literature that establishes it as the region of greatest synaptopathy in the mouse cochlea15,17. However, the patterns of subtype specific loss may differ in other regions, especially where the proportions of subtypes are known to differ. We were also not sufficiently powered to make any conclusions about the effect of sex, though both sexes were included in all experiments. Another limitation is that we only tracked terminals that were filled with tdT in each reporter line and thus could only infer what happened to unlabeled terminals. This especially limited our ability to make strong conclusions about Ib synapses. Additionally, the reduced Ib/Ic synapse density could be due in part to downregulation of the Rosa26 locus or altered trafficking of tdT between the soma and terminal. For instance, if insufficient tdT was trafficked to the repaired terminal it could appear tdT-. With these potential confounds in mind, we scored terminals based on signal distribution rather than absolute intensity (see Methods for details of all scoring methods). This approach appears to be robust, as an automated classification of subtype synapse proportions reproduced results from manual scoring. However, it remains possible that some terminals may have been misidentified because of the nature of the tdT signal. Additionally, although the proportion of HuD+ and tdT+ SGN somata in Ntng1Cre;Ai14 animals did not decrease with age, the complex morphology of the cochlea combined with slight variations in mounting and sectioning angles makes it technically difficult to robustly locate the somata of the SGNs that innervate the 32 kHz region in sections across animals. Thus, while our data strongly suggest that there is a substantial degree of age-related synapse loss that cannot be explained entirely by the loss of SGNs, it is possible that some of the synapses are missing because the SGN itself is gone, not because of synaptopathy per se.

Methods

Mice

Animal work was conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Harvard Medical School (protocol number IS00000067-9). Mice were housed in the Harvard Center for Comparative Medicine in groups of up to five animals, maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle, between 20 and 26˚C, and relative humidity of 30-70%. As there is not evidence for significant effects of sex on synaptopathy at the 32 kHz region11,56, all experiments used both male and female mice. Food and water were available ad libitum. The following previously published mouse lines were used: Netring1Cre (MGI: 6740547)57, Rosa26LSL-tdTomato (Jax: 007914)58, Calb2CreERT2 (MGI: 4880758), Lypd1CreERT2 (MGI: 8248018), Bhlhe22Cre (MGI:4440745)59, Runx1flox (MGI:4358510). Netring1Cre; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato (Ntng1Cre;Ai14) breeders and experimental mice were maintained on a mixed CBA/J (Jax: 000656) and C57BL/6 J background (Jax: 000664). Ntng1Cre;Ai14 experimental mice for aging studies were homozygous for Cdh23753G>A mutation. Ntng1Cre;Ai14 experimental mice for exposure studies were heterozygous for this allele (Cdh23753G/753A). Calb2CreERT2 and Lypd1CreERT2 mice were on a CBA/CaJ background (Jax: 000654) and were maintained as homozygous for the respective CreERT2 alleles with Cdh23753G/753G allele status. Calb2CreERT2;Ai14 and Lypd1CreERT2;Ai14 animals were Cdh23753G/753A. All experimental animals were heterozygous for the respective Cre and reporter alleles. CreERT2 activity in these mice was induced with a single intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen (0.1 mg/g body weight) between P26-P31. Tamoxifen (Sigma Aldrich T5648-1G) was dissolved in corn oil to a final concentration of 20 mg/mL. Bhlhe22+/+;Runx1fl/fl (CON) or Bhlhe22Cre/+;Runx1fl/fl (CKO) were on a mixed CBA/J and C57BL/6 J background with Cdh23753A/753A allele status.

Noise exposure

Noise exposure was conducted in a custom plexiglass trapezoidal box located inside a tabletop sound-attenuated chamber. Acoustic stimuli were delivered from a speaker at the top of the chamber. To ensure even sound exposure, animals were placed in individual wire mesh boxes on a mesh platform located in the center of the chamber. Sound pressure levels were measured with a 1⁄4” free-field microphone (PCB 378C01) which was calibrated prior to each exposure session (Larson-Davis CAL200). Stimuli consisted of an 8–16 kHz octave-band noise for 120 min. Up to 4 animals were exposed at a time with the light on inside of the box. Sham exposed animals were placed in the wire mesh boxes within the plexiglass chamber for the same duration as noise exposed animals. Because noise/sham exposures were administered across multiple days, special care was taken to mix animals of different genotypes and sex for each exposure when possible. Sound intensity was recorded for the duration of the exposure to ensure constant noise level.

Cochlear function testing

Mice were anesthetized with an initial intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Meloxicam (1 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously for analgesia prior to assessments. Animals were placed on a 37 °C heating pad (ATC1000, World Precision Instruments) and the anesthetic plane was maintained with additional ketamine (30–40 mg/kg) injections as needed. Note that Ntng1Cre;Ai14 mice exhibit a heightened sensitivity to anesthesia such that additional ketamine administration often leads to death. Therefore, in these experiments, if the anesthetic plane was lost during recording the session was terminated and the mouse was placed on a heating pad for immediate recovery.

ABRs were measured using a custom acoustic system (Eaton-Peabody Laboratories, EPL, Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary, MEEI) in an electrically shielded and sound attenuating chamber. ABRs were recorded from three subcutaneous needle electrodes: a recording electrode caudal to the pinna, a reference electrode at the vertex, and a ground electrode by the tail. ABR stimuli were typically presented from 20 to 80 dB SPL in 5 dB steps at frequencies specified in each ABR graph. Each stimulus was presented as a 5 ms tone-pip at a rate of 31/s, with a 0.5 ms rise-fall time and alternating polarities. Responses were amplified 10,000x, filtered with a 0.3–3 kHz passband (P511, Grass), and averaged 512 times. Recordings with peak-to-peak amplitudes exceeding 15 μV were rejected as artifacts. ABR thresholds and amplitudes were analyzed using ABR Peak Analysis software (v1.9.0; EPL MEEI Engineering Core). ABR threshold was defined as the lowest stimulus level at which wave-I could be identified at a consistent latency by qualitative visual inspection. If animals showed no response at 80 dB SPL, a threshold of 85 dB was assigned. Experimenters were blind to genotype and condition throughout the ABR recording and threshold analysis process.

Immunohistochemistry

All samples were blinded throughout processing, imaging, and quantification. Unblinding occurred after all images were analyzed. To minimize the potential for batch-to-batch variability, tissue for each experiment was collected and processed in one to three batches using the same aliquot of antibodies for all batches. Within three days after the final ABR, animals were transcardially perfused and cochleae post-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight fixation at 4˚C. Tissues were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual PFA and then decalcified while shaking in 120 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at room temperature for three days. After decalcification, tissues were rinsed three times with PBS, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4˚C

After cryopreservation, cochleae were stored at −80 °C until further processing. Tubes containing the cochleae were thawed in room temperature water. Thawed cochlea were rinsed three times in PBS to remove sucrose, and micro-dissected in PBS. Primary antibodies were applied in 1% serum and 0.3% PBST and incubated shaking overnight at 37 °C. The following antibodies were used: goat anti-Calb2 (1:500, Swant, cat. no. CG1, no clonal information or lot number provided by manufacturer), mouse anti-CTBP2 (1:500, BD Transduction Laboratories, cat. no. 612044, clone 16, lot no. 3324608), guinea pig anti-Homer1 (1:500, SynapticSystems, cat. no. 160004, polyclonal antibody, no lot number provided by manufacturer), and rabbit anti-dsRed (1:500, Takara, cat. no. 632496, polyclonal antibody, lot no. 2210019). Samples were incubated with secondary antibodies (all raised in donkey against either goat – 405 nm conjugate, 1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 705-475-003, polyclonal antibody, lot no. 161971; mouse IgG1- 1:1000, 488 nm conjugate, Invitrogen, cat. No. A21202, polyclonal antibody, lot no. 1796361; rabbit – 1:1000, 568 nm conjugate, Invitrogen, cat. no. A10042, polyclonal antibody, lot no. 2433862; or guinea pig – 1:500, 647 nm conjugate, Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 706-605-148, polyclonal antibody, lot no. 149191) in 1% serum and 0.3% PBST for 2 h at 37 °C, with the solution replaced by a fresh identical secondary antibody solution at 1 h. Rinses after primary and secondary antibody were done using 1% PBST for 10 min at room temperature.

Image acquisition

All images were acquired on the same Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope. Standardized imaging procedures for each sample type were developed during pilot experiments and utilized for all samples. Laser and detector settings were minimally adjusted to avoid pixel saturation and background generation. Imaging setting optimization was based on a previously published method29 to fully capture synaptic puncta morphology without overestimating puncta size.

Whole mount synapse imaging

Confocal z-stacks of cochlear turns were first imaged at low-magnification (10x objective, NA 0.30, 8 bit images 1024×1024 pixels, 0.8 pixels per µm, 5.34 µm steps), for tonotopic mapping using the Measure Line ImageJ plugin from MEEI (https://meeeplfiles.partners.org/Measure_line.class), which translates cochlear position into frequency according to the published map for the mouse60,61. On rare occasions, cochlear turns that did not contain the 32 kHz region were lost during immunolabelling. In these cases, a surrogate micrograph that contained a cochlear turn of a similar length and always from the same region was used to aid in mapping. The approximated 32 kHz region was then imaged using a 63x oil-immersion objective (NA 1.4). The field of view was rotated so that the inner hair cells were aligned horizontally across the field of view during image acquisition. For each animal a 16-bit z-stack was taken of the inner hair cells with dimensions of 2048 × 624 pixels per image, 16.2 pixels per µm, and 0.34 µm z-steps. Image boundaries were set to ensure that all synaptic puncta were well within the z-limits.

Cochlear section imaging

Slides were first manually inspected at low magnification to identify the ganglion region most likely to correspond to the 32 kHz region (i.e., expected to be between the basal-most and mid-regions of the ganglion). Z-stack images were taken of this region of interest using a 40x objective (NA 1.4, 2048×2048 pixels, 12.8 pixels per µm, 0.34 µm z-steps). For all sections, 16 z-slices were acquired beginning just above the interface of the tissue and the glass.

Synapse analysis

Throughout the reconstruction process, reconstructed surfaces were examined to ensure they faithfully reproduced the underlying fluorescent puncta. Following surface generation, all surfaces in the file were inspected to confirm whether they were closely associated with CALB2 (i.e., the IHC boundaries). Surfaces that did not meet this criterion were deleted prior to statistics file generation. Surfaces were also inspected to identify any puncta that had been merged into a single surface by the software or cases where the surface was artificially elongated due to spherical aberrations. In these cases, Imaris’ built-in tools were used to refine the surface and ensure it closely matched the raw fluorescence intensity signal as much as possible given the limitations of all thresholding methods. Following surface refinement, surface statistics (including xyz position in μm, volumes in µm3, and mean tdT intensity within the surface) were exported as Excel files using the built-in Imaris export feature.

SynAnalyzer overview

We developed source code for a semi-automated image analysis pipeline to quantify the density of paired and unpaired synaptic markers, their association with tdTomato (tdT) signal, and other properties of interest in images of cochlear wholemounts stained for synaptic markers. This code relies on a user-provided spreadsheet that contains the xyz coordinates for every region of interest in the micrograph, which were automatically generated by Imaris from synaptic marker surface reconstructions. Each surface reconstruction encapsulates the fluorescence signal created by a synaptic marker punctum. Imaris calculated the center of this reconstruction, its volume, and the mean fluorescence intensity within this surface for each channel in the micrograph. With this information (exported from Imaris as an Excel spreadsheet) and the original micrograph, the code generates a set of arrays that show a summed intensity projection thumbnail of the 2 × 2 µm square area centered at the appropriate xyz coordinate for each reconstruction.

The image analysis pipeline is broken down into several steps. First, the spreadsheets generated in Imaris are converted into DataFrames so that the Python package Pandas can be used to compute additional values of interest, such as the control median normalized volume (details for each computation are provided below). In this step, key image metadata is added to each entry within the DataFrame so that a single surface ID can always be traced back to its original image and to the originating fluorescence channel (i.e., whether the surface reconstruction was generated from the ribbon or PSD immunolabelling). These DataFrames are saved as new CSV files that can be read by ImageJ.

The second step is running an ImageJ macro that identifies all micrographs and CSV files in the batch directory specified by the user (see User Guide for more details). It then opens an image-CSV file pair and generates a set of thumbnail images at each location in the CSV file. To do this, the original z-stack undergoes three-dimensional cropping where a 2 × 2 µm bounding box is placed with the xyz coordinate in question at the center of the box and a total of five image slices are included with the center slice corresponding to the z-coordinate. From this cropped z-stack, a summed intensity projection is generated, and then rolling background subtraction with a 50-pixel rolling ball radius is applied. These thumbnails are then compiled into ribbon, PSD, or terminal arrays, which are saved in the batch directory. These arrays are then compiled into an image stack by the macro so that the user can easily flip channels from each single fluorescence channel or the merged (see examples at GitHub repository in DemoKeyOutputs folder). The macro walks the user through manual scoring of each thumbnail for its “synaptic” and/or “terminal” status (i.e., whether or not the PSD signal overlaps closely with tdT signal), which is inputted by the user into the CSV file.

Once all image-CSV file pairs have been analyzed, an additional Python notebook is used to compute normalized values and complete the analysis. First, the data from all CSV files is compiled into a master “XYZSummary” DataFrame. This DataFrame has one entry for every reconstruction analyzed along with all relevant metadata, such as the originating image, animal ID, experimental condition/group, etc. Within this same notebook, two new values are calculated for every surface. An “image maximum normalized tdT intensity” is calculated using the mean tdT intensity within a synaptic PSD, as calculated by Imaris. The code takes the brightest value in the corresponding image and normalizes the mean tdT intensity of all surfaces to this value and saves the information in a new column in the DataFrame. Additionally, a “control median normalized volume” is calculated by finding the median volume for all images in the control group and then dividing all volumes in all images, including those from experimental animals, by this value. This new value is saved as a new column in the DataFrame which is exported as a CSV file called “XYZSummary.” Then, the notebook summarizes the data for each image (e.g., PSD-based Paired Synapse Index, tdT+ Synapse Proportion, etc.) and saves this information in a new CSV file called “BatchMaster.” These final two CSV files can be opened in Excel for viewing outside of the pipeline and data can be imported into Prism for statistical analysis.

Synapse array scoring procedure

Following array generation, users evaluated all thumbnails to determine whether the surface was generated from a synaptic marker that was paired or unpaired (i.e., ribbons were evaluated for the presence of an apposed PSD; PSDs were evaluated for the presence of an apposed ribbon). Paired pre- and post- synaptic puncta are known to be very closely localized even when detected with the techniques here. Therefore, thumbnails were only scored as being paired if there was very close association between the puncta.

Terminal array scoring procedure

Users reviewed and scored terminal arrays to assess whether a PSD punctum was closely associated with tdT signal. Due to variability in the orientation of synaptic structures relative to the z-axis and the size of the terminals containing cytosolic tdT, we assessed the relative intensity and spatial orientation of tdT signal relative to the PSD punctum by eye, while blinded to condition, and assigned a tdT+ or tdT- score to each synapse. To assist in this process, we made linear adjustments in the displayed brightness/contrast. To avoid introducing bias to judgements for a subset of images, these brightness/contrast adjustments were made for the entire array of images for a given cochlea. In many cases, thumbnails were completely void of tdT+ pixels and these PSDs were scored as “negative.” For the vast majority of thumbnails with tdT+ pixels, the signal was clearly overlapping with or directly adjacent to the PSD and these were scored as “positive.” Cases in which a clear gap existed between the PSD and terminal were scored as “negative.” For synapses from noise exposure experiments ~10% of thumbnails were unable to be scored using these criteria and were thus categorized as “uncertain.” Inclusion or exclusion of these synapses had no impact on the outcome (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Cochlear section image analysis

All image measurements were made in ImageJ using standard software tools (i.e., no custom macros or plugins were used). Maximum projection images were generated from raw confocal z-stacks. HuD+ cells were identified, outlined by hand using the “Freehand” selection tool, and then the resulting region of interest (ROI) was added to the ROI Manager. Only those cells whose perimeters were fully within the field of view were included. Once all HuD+ cells were traced, the total number of ROIs was recorded, and the ROIs were saved. To calculate the area of the entire region containing SGNs, a new ROI was created from all HuD+ ROIs using the “Combine” command. The area of the resulting convex hull polygon was calculated using the Measure function. The HuD + SGN density was calculated as the total number of HuD+ ROIs divided by the overall area of the region. The original set of HuD+ ROIs were then applied to the tdT channel and the number of tdT+;HuD+ cells counted. This value was manually recorded in an Excel spreadsheet for each image. The density of tdT+;HuD+ SGNs was calculated as the total number of tdT+;HuD+ ROIs divided by the overall area of the region. The Type Ib/Ic SGN proportion was calculated as the total number of tdT+;HuD+ SGNs divided by the number of HuD+ SGNs.

Statistics

For all display items, measurements shown are taken from distinct samples (i.e., each individual data point is a distinct biological replicate). No samples were measured repeatedly for the same property (e.g., PSD-based synaptic index was measured once for each sample). Prism GraphPad version 10.6.1 was used for all statistical analysis. All datasets were tested for normality prior to the selection of an appropriate statistical test (e.g., unpaired t tests for normally distributed data and Mann-Whitney or Kruskall–Wallis tests for non-normal data). Two-tailed tests were used for all analyses with corrections for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Welch’s correction for unequal standard deviations was used for all t tests. The type of statistical test used is indicated in the text and in the figure legends. For analysis of wave-I amplitudes, nonlinear regression (NLR) analysis was performed as described in Frank, Sitko, Romero, Goodrich 202532. Briefly, a second-order polynomial equation was fit to the wave-I amplitudes at 32 kHz for all sound intensities tested using least-squares regression. Fits were compared using the Extra sum-of-squares F test which determines whether one fit sufficiently describes both curves (p > 0.05) or if each dataset is best described by significantly different curves (p < 0.05). The full details of the parameters used for each statistical test, including the Prism output, are provided in the Source Data file.
