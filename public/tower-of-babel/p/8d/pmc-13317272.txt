Prediabetes and diabetes exacerbated cardiac systolic function in obese patients at the subclinical stage: insights from global longitudinal strain and myocardial work
Authors: Kai Lu, Guang-an Li, Yan-jiao Wang, Jun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis investigation aims to estimate the diagnostic value of global longitudinal strain (GLS) and myocardial work (MW) indices for the identification of subclinical left ventricular (LV) systolic impairment (SLVSI) in individuals with simple obesity, obesity and prediabetes, and obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsThis investigation included 302 participants: 225 obese patients and 77 healthy individuals. LV GLS and MW-derived values, namely, the global constructive work (GCW), global work index (GWI), global wasted work (GWW), and global work efficiency (GWE), were analyzed. All individuals with obesity were classified into three groups: simple obesity, individuals with obesity and prediabetes, and individuals with obesity and T2DM. Comparisons of the LV GLS and MW were conducted among the four groups. The independent factors that affected the SLVSI in individuals with obesity were assessed using a logistic regression model. We conducted an ROC analysis to estimate the performance of MW in detecting SLVSI in individuals with obesity.ResultsSignificant differences were detected in the GWI, GCW, LV GLS, GWW, and GWE among normal controls, individuals with simple obesity, individuals with obesity and prediabetes, and individuals with obesity and T2DM (P < 0.05). The combination of the GCW, GWW, GWI, and GWE resulted in the best AUC (0.896) for the determination of SLVSI in individuals with obesity. After adjustment for confounders, prediabetes or T2DM was independently associated with an absolute LV GLS < 20% in obese individuals.ConclusionsIt is feasible to use LV GLS and MW-related parameters to determine SLVSI in individuals with obesity, individuals with obesity and prediabetes, and individuals with obesity and T2DM. Even in the preclinical stage prior to the onset of diabetes, prediabetes still has an incremental effect on SLVSI in obese patients.Keywords: Obese, Prediabetes, T2DM, GLS, MW

Introduction

Obesity is the most significant risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and is the most significant promoter of the disease [1, 2]. Connections between obesity and T2DM are well known and widely appreciated. Obesity and T2DM are major global epidemic metabolic disorders that influence several organs and are characterized by hyperglycemia, which contributes to cardiovascular disease, microangiopathy, renal failure, and stroke [3]. Prediabetes represents an intermediate stage in glucose dysregulation that occurs when the pancreas can no longer sustain hyperinsulinemia to maintain normoglycemia [4, 5], and it is associated with a transition between normal and diabetic glucose homeostasis [6]. Previous investigations have shown that obesity, prediabetes, and T2DM are linked to left ventricle (LV) dilation, hypertrophy, elevated wall stress, and heart failure [7]. Among these complications, LV systolic dysfunction is a main cause of morbidity and mortality, which highlights the importance of detecting subclinical LV systolic impairment (SLVSI) in individuals with simple obesity, obesity and prediabetes, and obesity with T2DM.

With the development of imaging methods, LV systolic function can now be determined via cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [8], echocardiography [9] and other methods. Of these modalities, cardiac MRI is expensive, and the detection time is long, and echocardiography is extensively utilized in the clinic because it is convenient. LV global longitudinal strain (GLS) and myocardial work (MW) obtained from speckle tracking echocardiography (STE) have been validated for the precise detection of SLVSI in individuals with various cardiovascular disorders (e.g., coronary heart disease [10], cardiomyopathy [11, 12], valvular disease [13, 14], hypertension [9, 15–17], and T2DM).

Our previous study revealed that SLVSI can be detected by the MW assessment of obese patients and that the impairment of MW parameters becomes more significant with worsening obesity [17]. However, whether prediabetes and T2DM further exacerbate SLVSI in obese patients remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate LV systolic function and to characterize subclinical myocardial impairment in patients with simple obesity, obesity complicated with prediabetes, and obesity complicated with T2DM using LV GLS and MW-related parameters. In addition, we further explored the incremental negative impact of glycemic abnormalities on myocardial function in obese patients.

Method

Study population

In all, 225 obese patients admitted to the Department of Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery, the Affiliated Changzhou Second People’s Hospital with Nanjing Medical University, were enrolled in this study. Patients with a history of arrhythmia (e.g., including tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, bundle branch block), congenital heart disease, myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, thyroid disease, or renal insufficiency were excluded. Patients with hypertension were also excluded. In addition, 77 healthy age- and sex-matched individuals were recruited as the normal control group. Patients were classified as obese if their BMI was greater than or equal to 30 kg/m². Individuals with obesity were stratified into the categories of (1) simple obesity with no hypertension, prediabetes, or T2DM; (2) obesity with prediabetes, defined by elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG, 5.6–6.9 mmol/L [100–125 mg/dL]) or increased glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c, 5.7–6.4% [39–47 mmol/mol]); and (3) obesity with T2DM, diagnosed per the American Diabetes Association [18].

We recorded height, weight, and blood pressure prior to echocardiography, after which BMI was calculated. Laboratory assessments were performed during hospitalization and included tests for HbA1c, total cholesterol (TCH), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), FPG, and serum creatinine (SCR) levels, after which the TG/HDL-C ratio was subsequently derived.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Qualified sonographers conducted standard transthoracic echocardiography on all participants using a GE Vivid E9 system (GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Norway), with ECG leads attached. We acquired 2D, color Doppler, and pulsed-wave Doppler data from three apical chamber views (2, 3 and 4) over three consecutive cycles at high frame rates (> 60 fps). Offline analysis was then conducted by an experienced echocardiographer (EchoPAC v204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Norway).

M-mode measurements included the LV diameter (LVDd), left atrial diameter (LAd), LV wall thickness, and mitral annular plane systolic excursion (MAPSE). We obtained the left atrial maximum volume (LAV) via 2D echocardiography using Simpson’s technique, and we assessed the LAV index (LAVI). The LVEF was determined according to Simpson’s biplane technique. Pulsed-wave Doppler measured peak early (E) and late (A) diastolic mitral velocities, and their ratio was subsequently calculated (E/A). Peak early (e′) and late (a′) diastolic annular velocities were assessed via TDI, and the E/e′ ratio was derived.

2D-STE analyses

Myocardial motion in an apical view over three consecutive cardiac cycles was tracked and analyzed using EchoPAC software (v204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Norway) to calculate the LV GLS for each participant [19].

MW parameters were derived from pressure‒strain loops (PSLs) by inputting blood pressure values into the software. The software automatically generated the following: Global Myocardial Work Index (GWI): the total work performed by the LV myocardium during systole, i.e., the area within the PSL; Global Constructive Work (GCW): myocardial work performed during shortening in systole and lengthening in isovolumic diastole; Global Wasted Work (GWW): myocardial work performed during lengthening in systole and shortening in isovolumic diastole; Myocardial Work Efficiency (GWE): GCW/(GCW + GWW)×100% [20, 21].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 (Chicago, USA) was used for all the analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to estimate data normality. For comparisons among normal controls, individuals with simple obesity, individuals with obesity and prediabetes, and individuals with obesity and T2DM, we utilized one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, whereas for abnormal variables, we utilized the Kruskal–Wallis test. Obese individuals with an absolute LV GLS < 20% were defined as abnormal, and those with absolute LV GLS ≥ 20% were defined as normal [22].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated in MedCalc (v19.6.4; Ostend, Belgium) to evaluate the diagnostic value of the GWI, GCW, GWW, GWE, and their combination for the prediction of LVSD. The sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) were measured, and differences in the AUC values were tested using the DeLong method.

Correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the potential risk factors for an absolute LV GLS < 20% in obese patients, and the enrolled parameters included age, sex, BMI, SBP, prediabetes status and T2DM status, TCH, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG/HDL-C, BUN and SCR.

Four forced-entry logistic regression models were used to determine the independent association of an absolute LV GLS < 20% with concomitant prediabetes or T2DM. Model 1 was a crude model, Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex, Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 covariates + BMI, and Model 4 was adjusted for Model 3 covariates + HDL-C and SCR.

Categorical variables are represented as counts/percentages, normally distributed data as means ± standard deviation (SD), and nonnormally distributed data as medians (interquartile range). P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Reproducibility and repeatability test

Interobserver and intraobserver variabilities for LV GLS and MW-related parameters were assessed via repeated assessments in 20 randomly selected individuals from both the obese and normal groups. For the interobserver assessment, the 2nd observer was blinded to the initial assessments.

Discussions

The key outcomes of this study were that SLVSI was observed in individuals with simple obesity, obesity and prediabetes, and obesity and T2DM according to the LV GLS and MW-related parameters. After adjustment for confounders, prediabetes or T2DM was independently associated with an absolute LV GLS < 20% in obese individuals.

Obesity is associated with coronary heart disease, heart failure, and peripheral vascular diseases [23]. Obesity is also believed to promote T2DM [1].

Previous data from the Copenhagen Heart Failure Risk Study indicated that HbA1c levels were significantly correlated with heart failure classification in elderly high-risk individuals [24]. Another STAAB cohort and the German subset of the EUROASPIRE IV study reported that diabetes was the only cardiovascular risk factor associated with an increased likelihood of receiving lifestyle modification recommendations among individuals enrolled in primary and secondary cardiovascular disease prevention lifestyle programs [25]. Further studies have demonstrated that a healthy overall lifestyle can significantly reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality in patients with T2DM [26, 27]. In addition, one study revealed that among patients with T2DM and no prior cardiovascular disease, a higher glycemic index (HGI) was associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular events; moreover, HbA1c was similarly associated with cardiovascular event risk in this population [28]. Therefore, the incremental cardiac impairment induced by glycemic abnormalities in obese patients warrants close attention.

Studies related to EACVI NORRE provide further clinical evidence for the application of the LV GLS and MW-related parameters in the evaluation of LV systolic function [29]. Orhan AL et al. determined the subclinical effects of isolated obesity via routine echocardiography and the tissue Doppler myocardial strain rate and reported that obesity was related to loss of subclinical systolic and diastolic function [30]. Aristizábal-Duque CH et al. reported that high BMI was linked to significantly decreased LV and right ventricular free wall longitudinal strain [31]. Haley JE et al. reported that adiposity and T2DM had deleterious effects on systolic cardiac function [32]. Huang J et al. studied MW and reported that SLVSI occurred in individuals with obesity and T2DM and was more severe in individuals with T2DM and hypertension [9, 15, 17].

However, additional subclinical studies focusing on the incremental impairment of LV systolic function caused by glycemic abnormalities in obese patients are lacking. Therefore, this study employed the LV GLS and MW-related parameters to evaluate LV systolic function in patients with simple obesity, obesity with prediabetes, and obesity with T2DM. The aim was to explore whether glycemic abnormalities cause additional impairment of LV systolic function in obese patients and whether such impairment can be detected at the subclinical stage.

The results revealed that the LV GLS, GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE were significantly different among the normal control group, simple obesity group, obesity with prediabetes group, and obesity with T2DM group (all P < 0.05). The absolute values of the LV GLS, GWI, GCW and GWE tended to gradually decrease in the following order: normal control group < simple obesity group < obesity with prediabetes group < obesity with T2DM group (Ptrend<0.001).

Increased sympathetic nervous system activity in obese patients [33] leads to overactivation of beta-adrenergic receptors on cardiac fibroblasts, which in turn causes pathological activation of the Gαq pathway in cardiomyocytes, resulting in impaired cardiac function [34]. As a chronic disease, T2DM causes chronic inflammation, which increases the number of inflammasomes in patients. An increase in the level of inflammasome nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 has been shown to induce diabetes-related cardiomyopathy [35], which causes SLVSI in individuals with T2DM. On the contrary, in both obese patients and obese patients with prediabetes and T2DM, an increase in free fatty acids in the body causes excessive fat deposition in myocardial cells [36]. This fat deposition causes lipotoxicity to myocardial cells, thus further impairing myocardial systolic function. Taken together, these findings suggest that T2DM results in additional impairment in the myocardial systolic function of obese patients, and that this impairment can be reflected by the LV GLS and MW-related parameters, even in individuals with prediabetes. Early warning signs may be detected in obese patients with elevated blood glucose levels, after which early intervention and treatment can prevent further myocardial systolic dysfunction.

ROC curve analysis revealed that the combination of the GCW, GWI, GWW, and GWE resulted in the best AUC (0.896) as well as good sensitivity (90.38%) and specificity (77.69%) for predicting SLVSI in obese individuals. It is feasible to use either the LV GLS or MW when individuals with obesity and prediabetes and those with obesity and T2DM are evaluated, but this does not mean that a single indicator must be used for evaluation. The LV GLS and its combination with MW may improve the accuracy of the evaluation. Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed that the LV GLS is significantly related to prediabetes and T2DM in obese patients. Therefore, the regular detection of abnormal changes in FPG and HbA1c would be meaningful for predicting SLVSI in obese patients.

Conclusion

It is feasible to use the LV GLS and MW-related parameters for predicting SLVSI in individuals with obesity, individuals with obesity and prediabetes, and individuals with obesity and T2DM. Even in the preclinical stage prior to the onset of diabetes, prediabetes still has an incremental effect on SLVSI in obese patients. The implementation of more stringent standards for blood glucose monitoring and control in obese patients appears to be beneficial for reducing the burden on their cardiac systolic function.
