Lifestyle strategies and mechanistic implications for slowing neurodegeneration
Authors: Jordan A. Gunning, Mario I. Hernandez, Behzad Gudarzi, Hana M. Roushdy, Yonas E. Geda, Susan B. Racette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is growing evidence that lifestyle habits influence neurodegenerative disease risk. Dietary strategies and exercise are among the most important lifestyle approaches to promote brain health. This review highlights the primary mechanisms by which three dietary approaches (metabolic switching, calorie restriction, high diet quality) and exercise may promote neuroprotection and inhibit neurodegeneration. Neuroprotective mechanisms induced by a healthful lifestyle include reduced inflammation, increased autophagy, neurogenesis, decreased neuronal cell death, and attenuation of pathological protein burden.

Introduction

Alzheimer disease (AD) and AD-related dementias (ADRD) affect more than 55 million people worldwide and their prevalence is increasing rapidly1. AD is the most common neurodegenerative disease and is characterized by cognitive impairment that can have devastating effects on physical function, social function, quality of life, and mortality. AD severity is a spectrum ranging from preclinical AD to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to severe dementia.

Pathologically, AD is defined by the presence of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles in the brain and neurodegeneration. Fluid AD biomarkers, measured in blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), can indicate amyloid burden, tau pathology, and neurodegeneration, while neuroimaging can reveal loss of brain volume and pathological changes as a broader indicator of neurodegeneration. β-secretase (BACE1) is the rate-limiting enzyme for Aβ production; BACE1 cleaves amyloid precursor protein (APP) at the β-site, which generates secreted APPβ (sAPPβ) and the 99 amino acid C-terminal fragment of APP (C99). This cleavage initiates the amyloidogenic pathway2. Subsequently, γ-secretase mediates the cleavage of C99, which releases Aβ2. Some data indicate that patients with AD have increased expression and activity of β-secretase and γ-secretase in the brain; however, the mechanisms that contribute to this effect are highly debated and the impact of confounding factors, such as neuronal loss, is not well understood3.

In contrast, when α-secretase (ADAM10) cleaves APP, the cleavage products are sAPPα and the 83 amino acid C-terminal fragment of APP (C83), which initiate the non-amyloidogenic pathway2. Therefore, α-secretase activity is essential for controlling Aβ production. Aβ deposition is tissue-specific: increased Aβ in brain tissue is indicative of increased pathological burden, whereas increased Aβ in the blood or CSF may reflect enhanced clearance of pathological proteins from the brain and reduced disease burden.

In addition to accumulation of pathological proteins in the brain, numerous other mechanisms contribute to the development and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, including elevated cytokines and activated immune cells in the brain (neuroinflammation), neuronal death, brain volume loss, reduced brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced synaptic plasticity, and dysregulated autophagy.

Currently, there is no cure for AD. The newest pharmacologic options are humanized IgG monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that reduce plaque in the brain and are classified as disease-modifying agents. Currently, the 2 FDA approved mAbs that are used clinically are lecanemab and donanemab, which target Aβ protofibrils and plaques, respectively4,5, and slow cognitive decline compared to placebo; however, they neither reverse nor arrest the progressive cognitive decline. Many patients are not eligible to receive these mAbs due to the risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with edema or hemorrhage (ARIA-E/H). Symptomatic treatment with acetylcholinesterase enzyme inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) and the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor inhibitor memantine are additional pharmacotherapies for AD6 that delay but do not arrest cognitive decline, but gastrointestinal distress is a common side effect that causes many patients to discontinue treatment.

With the alarming increase in AD incidence and prevalence, lifestyle approaches to prevent and slow cognitive decline are needed, whether used independently or added to pharmacological therapy. Lifestyle strategies may enhance the benefits of pharmacological therapies and are promising options for patients who are not appropriate candidates for or who elect not to receive pharmacological therapy. It is probable that certain lifestyle patterns have independent effects on metabolic pathways that are not targeted by drug therapies and may have synergistic effects on pathways that are the target of drug therapies.

It is well established that metabolic switching, high diet quality, and regular exercise have the potential to impact mechanisms that have roles in maintaining brain health (Fig. 1). Importantly, several large randomized clinical trials, including the POINTER and the FINGER trials, have shown that a multidomain lifestyle intervention improves cognition in older adults7,8. The POINTER and FINGER trials were large studies (n = 2111 and n = 1260, respectively) in older adults at risk for dementia; both studies utilized a multi-modal intervention that included physical activity, a healthy diet, social engagement, cognitive activity, and monitoring of cardiovascular disease risk factors7–9. It is critical to understand the neuroprotective mechanisms induced by different lifestyle interventions. Systemic inflammation and oxidative stress are associated with numerous chronic illnesses, including AD, and neuroinflammation is a major contributor to AD progression10. Activated astrocytes and microglia, increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and inflammasome activation in the brain contribute to neuroinflammation11. T cells also play a major role in AD11 but are not discussed in detail in this manuscript. Neuronal loss and synapse loss are critical to AD pathology, and there is a strong correlation between synapse loss and cognitive decline12. Exerkines, including BDNF, irisin, and β-hydroxybutyrate (β-OHB), are signaling molecules released in response to exercise that mediate physiological responses and may enhance brain health13,14. β-OHB is a ketone body that is also produced in response to prolonged fasting or a ketogenic diet and influences neuronal gene expression. β-OHB can induce expression of BDNF, which is reduced in many neurological conditions, including AD, Parkinson disease (PD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and traumatic brain injury15. Remarkably, several studies have demonstrated that lifestyle interventions can positively impact brain volume, neuronal damage, and BDNF concentrations. Because of the wide array of mechanisms contributing to neuroprotection and neurodegeneration, a multimodal lifestyle approach may be optimal to slow cognitive decline meaningfully.

Certain lifestyle choices are well-established to have beneficial effects for metabolic health, but the mechanisms by which they promote brain health are not fully understood. This review will focus on metabolic pathways through which specific dietary strategies and exercise may enhance brain health by promoting neuroprotection and slowing neurodegeneration16.

Dietary approaches

Metabolic switching: fasting and ketogenic diets

Intermittent fasting (IF)16 is characterized by switching between periods of normal eating and voluntary fasting; common IF methods include time-restricted eating (TRE, humans) or time-restricted feeding (TRF, preclinical models), alternate day fasting (ADF), and 5:2 fasting. TRE/TRF involves limiting the eating window to 4–10 hours per day to achieve prolonged fasting. ADF is characterized by alternating between a day of fasting (or significantly reducing calories) and a day of ad libitum intake. 5:2 fasting is defined by two nonconsecutive days of fasting and 5 days of normal eating per week. These IF protocols may or may not involve calorie restriction (CR). A ketogenic diet is very low in carbohydrates, high in fat, and moderate in protein. The goal of IF and ketogenic diets is to induce a metabolic switch in which the body shifts from utilizing glucose to fatty acids and ketones as the major fuel sources. Preclinical studies

Several studies demonstrate that IF reduces systemic inflammation and ameliorates neuroinflammation17. Interestingly, one study identified functional nuclear factor (NF)-κB binding elements in the BACE1 promoter, suggesting that neuroinflammation may be directly involved in initiation of the amyloidogenic pathway10. In a mouse study of ischemic stroke, IF attenuated ischemia-induced inflammasome activity in the brain, with reductions in NF-κB, NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3), NOD-like receptor protein 1 (NLRP1), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, as well as reduced concentrations of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-18, cleaved caspase-11 and cleaved caspase-118. Neuronal cell death was also reduced, as evidenced by a decrease in cleaved caspase-3 and an increase in B-cell lymphoma-extra-large (BcL-xL)18. In a mouse model of bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS), which induces chronic cerebral hypoperfusion and inflammation that contribute to cognitive impairment, a 16:8 TRF diet (i.e., 16 h fasting/8 h eating window) led to significantly lower NLRP3, absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), caspase-8, and precursor IL-1β in brain tissue at 30 days post-surgery compared to ad libitum-fed BCAS mice19. BCAS mice on the TRF diet also had significantly lower cleaved caspase-1, cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-11, and mature IL-1β at 30 days post BCAS, indicating reduced inflammasome activation and cell death in the cerebellum19.

In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (a mouse model of MS), a fasting mimicking diet ameliorated demyelination and clinical disease severity that was attributed to greater corticosterone levels and regulatory T cells and decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, TH1 and TH17 cells, and antigen-presenting cells20. Another study utilizing an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis mouse model found that ADF reduced inflammation, demyelination, and axonal damage in the spinal cord21. Fecal microbiome transplantation from these ADF mice to ad libitum-fed mice ameliorated the symptoms of encephalomyelitis, indicating that the impact of ADF on the brain is mediated through gut microbiome changes. Whole-brain irradiation induces significant neuroinflammation and can result in irradiation-induced cognitive dysfunction22. In mice treated with whole-brain irradiation, ADF significantly improved cognitive function, which was mediated through inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines22. This study also identified that ADF mice had significantly less CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus neuronal loss in the hippocampus22. Oxidative stress is a well-established mechanism of neurodegeneration and can amplify neuroinflammation. Mice undergoing ADF for 11 months had significantly lower oxidative stress as measured by lower glutathione disulfide, 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal, and nitrotyrosine containing proteins in brain tissue when compared to AL fed mice23. Additionally, ADF in rats significantly reduced oxidative stress, as indicated by decreased malondialdehyde levels after 1 and 7 days of ADF, and decreased nitric oxide after 1 and 15 days of ADF24.

BDNF is a neurotrophic factor that is linked to neuroplasticity, neuronal survival, and autophagy; BDNF is responsive to diet interventions and exercise. A study utilizing the 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) mouse model of PD reported that ADF reduced motor impairment and significantly attenuated MPTP-induced dopaminergic motor neuron loss and astroglia activation in the substantia nigra when compared to ad libitum-fed mice25. Another study utilizing a fasting mimicking diet, characterized by three days of fasting followed by four days of refeeding for three one-week cycles, found retained motor function and reduced loss of motor neurons in the substantia nigra in MPTP-treated mice26. Both studies reported significant increases in BDNF levels with ADF and a fasting mimicking diet in PD mice25,26. The ketogenic diet induces a metabolic switch from glucose to ketone utilization and has been demonstrated to improve cognition and longevity via several mechanisms, such as reduced inflammation27–29. A study reported that APP/PS1 mice on a ketogenic diet had higher blood concentrations of β-OHB and maintained greater levels of both early and late-phase long-term potentiation (LTP) as evidenced by induction of six pathways related to synaptic plasticity measured by RNAseq27. Further, mice fed a ketogenic diet had increased levels of phospho-ERK and phospho-CREB in the hippocampus when compared to control mice27. To confirm that β-OHB mediates the favorable changes, they incubated APP/PS1 mouse brain slices with β-OHB and found significantly increased late-phase LTP compared to brain slices incubated with vehicle27. This study also indicated that the ketogenic diet resulted in increased concentrations of BDNF in female mice and better cognitive performance27. Another study utilizing young and aged rats found that those fed a ketogenic diet performed better on cognitive tests, had significant increases in ketone body transporters MCT1 and MCT4, a reduction in the glucose transporter GLUT1, and increased prefrontal cortex expression of vestibular GABA transporter (VGAT), suggesting a possible mechanism by which cognitive function is enhanced28. In rats fed either a control TRF diet or a ketogenic TRF diet, cognitive function was improved when compared to ad libitum-fed rats; however, only rats fed a ketogenic TRF diet had a significant increase in β-OHB29. Interestingly, there was a significant difference in beta diversity between control TRF, ketogenic, and ad libitum-fed rats29. Whittaker et al. reported that 18:6 TRF in APP23 mice significantly increased β-OHB and total sleep duration, and restored circadian rhythms and activity patterns similar to those of wild type (WT) control mice30. This study also reported that TRF has effects on the brain transcriptome of APP23 mice, with differential expression of 86 AD-related genes and 100 neuroinflammation genes, including those with functional roles in autophagy, myelination, cytokine signaling, and the adaptive immune response30. Finally, this study revealed that TRF has a substantial impact on Aβ pathology, with TRF APP23 mice showing significantly reduced full-length APP, amyloid CTF, soluble and insoluble Aβ42 and Aβ40 in the brain, and significantly increased serum Aβ42 and serum Aβ42/4030. Using the APP-KI mouse model of AD, which exhibits a faster disease progression, they showed that TRF significantly reduced amyloid plaque count and area and significantly improved multiple aspects of cognitive performance30.

Dysregulated autophagy is observed in AD and PD31, and some data show that autophagy can inhibit Aβ plaque and Lewy body formation. Autophagy plays a vital role in clearing pathological proteins, including Aβ, and there are multiple mechanisms by which dietary restriction enhances autophagy in the brain that may be neuroprotective. Studies in non-AD models indicate that IF can suppress mTOR and enhance transcription factor EB (TFEB) expression32, increase PPARα33, and activate the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-Sirtuin 1-peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator (PGC)-1 axis, which can then activate several mediators, including Beclin1 (Becn1), cAMP response element binding protein (CREB), and Unc-51-like kinase-1 (ULK1), to promote autophagy. Studies in AD models not utilizing IF show that upregulation of autophagy by several mechanisms ameliorates Aβ burden34,35. A delicate balance of these mediators must be maintained; for example, overactivation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) can inhibit autophagy, but insufficient mTOR activity can impair synaptic function.

Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) is a histone deacetylase that appears to be involved in protecting against age-related neurodegeneration36. SIRT1 is reduced in blood and brain tissue of patients with AD; IF increases SIRT1 levels37. SIRT1 is also a major regulator of cholesterol metabolism, and defective SIRT1 in AD may contribute to increased Aβ aggregation37. In contrast, overnutrition downregulates SIRT138. Fasting for 24 hours significantly reduced expression of BACE1 and significantly increased AMPK, PGC-1α, and PGC-1β protein levels in eNOS-deficient mouse forebrains39. Overexpression of PGC-1α both in vitro and in vivo suppressed BACE1 transcription and protein levels, and this was found to be dependent on both SIRT1 and PPARγ activity39. Importantly, SIRT1, PPARγ and PGC-1α were all found to directly interact with the BACE1 promoter. Additionally, ADF in a MPTP mouse model of PD significantly increased phosphorylation of cAMP response element binding protein (CREB)25. Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) is a mitochondrial protein deacetylase. After both one week and one month of ADF, AppNL-G-F mice had significant elevation of SIRT3 levels in the hippocampus compared to AL-fed mice40. ADF WT mice also had better synaptic plasticity after one month than AL-fed WT mice, as measured by increased cFos immunoreactive neurons in the amygdala and hypothalamus, and increased GABAergic neurotransmission40. Superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) is an antioxidant with important roles in brain health and is a direct target of SIRT3. In Sod2 + /− mouse embryos, the upregulation of synaptic activity by picrotoxin was reduced, suggesting that SOD2 activity may be necessary for ADF to improve synaptic plasticity via SIRT340. Further, behavior tests showed that ADF reduced anxiety behaviors, and this effect was found to be dependent on SIRT3; the ADF-induced reduction in anxiety behaviors was not observed in SIRT3-deficient mice40.

Human studies

Human trials of TRE in older adults with cognitive impairment are limited. Compared to baseline values, APP expression was significantly reduced in humans who fasted from sunset to dawn for 30 days41. A study in middle-aged patients with MS undergoing intermittent CR, a form of ADF defined as ~25% of usual caloric intake on 2 non-consecutive days per week, reported decreased neuroinflammation and increased brain volume and cortical thickness compared to control patients consuming their normal diet42. Another larger study of intermittent CR in patients with MS reported a significant increase in regulatory T cells and improved Symbol Digit Modality Test scores after 12 weeks43. One observational study conducted in older adults with MCI found that those regularly practicing IF had significantly improved cognitive function, including increased Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores, after 36 months, whereas those who never practiced IF had significant declines in their MMSE and MoCA scores44. Participants regularly practicing IF also had lower levels of oxidative stress as evidenced by an increase in superoxide dismutase levels, DNA damage markers, and C-reactive protein44. A pilot study of prolonged nightly fasting (PNF), defined as a 14-hour nightly fast, in older adults with memory decline reported a significant improvement in cognitive function as measured by the Memory and Attention Phone Screener and a decreased severity of insomnia after 8 weeks45.

Considering these data, we hypothesize that high adherence to a rigorous fasting protocol may have the potential to slow neurodegeneration. Further research is needed to confirm these mechanisms in humans and the impact on AD pathology, and human studies are needed to assess if intermittent fasting can meaningfully slow cognitive decline in humans.

Calorie restriction

It is well-established that CR can increase healthspan in many species, including humans, and increases lifespan in some preclinical models46. CR also has been hypothesized to protect cognitive function. CR is defined as reducing daily caloric intake below maintenance levels without causing malnutrition. Preclinical studies

CR may promote brain health via increased β-OHB47 and BDNF48; however, the neuroprotective mechanisms of CR are not well understood49. Long-term 30% CR in wild-type mice resulted in enriched expression of genes associated with LTP, indicating that CR may promote LTP in aging. APP/PS1 mice undergoing 40% CR had reductions in Aβ plaque number and area and less astrocyte activation50. A study using a low-carb 30% CR diet in Tg2576 mice reported significantly less accumulation of Aβ plaque in the cortex and hippocampus and decreased Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 in neocortical samples that was identified to be mediated by upregulation of sAPPα and ADAM10 activity51. Interestingly, one study reported that 30% CR significantly mitigated hippocampal and cortical Aβ plaque burden in 15-month-old female Tg2576 mice, but not age-matched male counterparts52. Additionally, CR downregulated γ-secretase subunit transcript levels in female Tg2576 mice but not males52. In aged obese WT rats, 30% CR significantly reduced neuroinflammation and increased SIRT1 expression in the brain and abrogated memory deficits induced by aging and obesity53. It is well established that CR can reduce oxidative stress54,55. A study of 30% CR in rhesus monkeys reported that monkeys undergoing long term CR had significantly fewer 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8OHdG) positive cells in the corpus callosum, cingulum bundle, and oligodendrocytes, indicating a reduction in oxidative DNA damage56. Interestingly, another study of 40% CR in mice reported significantly higher post-prandial carnitine-related metabolites when compared to AL fed mice, possibly showing a reduction in oxidative stress57. Remarkably, long-term CR in APPswe/PS1delta9 mice preserved cerebral glucose metabolism and neuronal integrity58. They also report increased autophagy as evidenced by increases in LC3BII and p62, a significant reduction in Aβ plaques in the hippocampus, reduced numbers of Iba1+ microglia, and better cognitive performance58. In APP/PS1 mice, 6 weeks of 30% CR initiated at 30 weeks of age resulted in better learning and spatial abilities, and reduced Aβ40, Aβ42, and neuroinflammation59. Further, CR caused significant diversity in the composition and abundance of gut flora, highlighting another potential mechanism by which CR influences cognition59. One study reported that short-term 30% CR in male PS19 tau P301S mice ameliorated cognitive decrements observed in AL mice and significantly reduced in Iba1+ microglia proliferation in the hippocampus, but did not impact tau pathology or proinflammatory cytokine expression60.

In Tg2576 mice, 30% CR results in a significant upregulation of SIRT1 in the brain and a decrease in Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 peptides61. In addition, this study reported that viral expression of SIRT1 in primary Tg2576 neuron cultures and CHO-APPswe cells significantly reduced Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 content, and SIRT1 expression in CHO-APPswe cells significantly elevated α-secretase activity and sAPPα. Likewise, suppressing SIRT1 activity with dominant negative SIRT1 resulted in significant increases in Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 and reductions in α-secretase activity and sAPPα, confirming the importance of SIRT1 in the AD pathology61. A study utilizing the MPTP PD mouse model reported that 30% CR exerted neuroprotective effects, including reducing the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, through the upregulation of ghrelin and AMPK phosphorylation62. Specifically, CR upregulated ghrelin, and exogenous ghrelin administration had similar neuroprotective effects to CR that were abrogated in AMPK knockout mice. Additionally, studies conducted in non-neurodegenerative models indicate that CR can promote autophagy through numerous mechanisms including increased TFEB, ULK1, Becn1, and PPAR-α, and suppressed mTOR, which may further add to potential benefits of CR for brain health63.

Mediterranean, DASH, and MIND diets

High diet quality, specified as consuming a diet of mostly whole, unprocessed foods, is essential for maintaining metabolic and brain health. CR and IF are not appropriate for all populations; thus, additional dietary strategies to prevent and slow neurodegeneration are valuable. Notably, a high-quality diet is high in antioxidants, making it a superior choice for improving oxidative stress and synapse loss. The Mediterranean diet is characterized by high intake of olive oil, vegetables, low-glycemic fruits, nuts, moderate intake of lean protein and fish, and little to no consumption of ultra-processed foods, making it abundant in flavonoids, polyphenols, secoiridoids, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, and monounsaturated fatty acids. The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet focuses on reducing sodium and increasing potassium intake by promoting whole foods. The Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet is a dietary pattern specifically designed to promote brain health and combines elements of the DASH diet and the Mediterranean diet. Preclinical studies

Adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with a lower risk of neurodegenerative disease; however, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. WT mice fed a Mediterranean-style diet had increased exploratory behaviors, reduced anxiety behaviors, and preserved spatial learning and memory when compared to mice fed a typical American diet control64. Further, the Mediterranean diet resulted in significantly less Aβ1-42 in the hippocampus and cortex, and less peripheral TNF-α compared to control. The Mediterranean diet also protected against lipopolysaccharides (LPS)-induced TNF-α and IL-1β gene expression and BDNF loss when compared to control64.

The gut microbiota has a critical role in AD pathology and research indicates that probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics can improve disease pathology65. In contrast, many dairy foods contain gram negative bacteria that contain LPS; LPS in the gut can enter the bloodstream and may pose a risk for neuroinflammation and development of AD66. Success of diet interventions may partially depend on the impact on the gut microbiome. In APP/PS1 mice, researchers reported that a Mediterranean-ketogenic diet induced substantial shifts in the gut microbiome when compared to a Western-style diet as indicated by higher alpha-diversity and distinct beta-diversity clusters between the two diets67. Notably, there was a significant increase in the Lactobacillus population in mice fed the modified Mediterranean-ketogenic diet67. Additionally, a Mediterranean-ketogenic diet ameliorated behavioral changes and neuromuscular abilities, reduced inflammation in the gut and hippocampus, and rescued levels of metabolic precursors for neurotransmitters and neuroactive metabolites in AD mice67.

The source of fatty acids can have a protective or deleterious impact on the amyloid cascade; diet patterns favoring unsaturated fatty acids may reduce AD risk and progression68. In the Ts65dn mouse model of Down syndrome, daily extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) consumption improved spatial learning and memory, improved synaptic function as measured by an improvement in LTP, and significantly reduced concentrations of pro-inflammatory biomarkers in the cortex when compared to mice not consuming EVOO69. A different study of EVOO added to the diet of 3xTg-AD mice reported significant downregulation of IL-17E, IL-12p70, IL-23, MIP-2, and IL-28 in the cortex, corroborating that EVOO can reduce neuroinflammation70. Further, another study using EVOO in TgSwDI AD mice reported a significant decrease in protein carbonyl levels and a significant increase in SOD protein levels in the brain, showing a reduction in oxidative stress71. This study also showed reduced Aβ plaques, APP, sAPPβ, BACE1, tau, and p-Tau231, and an increase in sAPPα in mice receiving EVOO71.

Ligostroside, a secoiridoid found in olives, upregulated mRNA expression of SIRT1, CREB1, and ETC complex I and increased ATP production in SY5Y-APP695 cells; in vivo administration of ligostroside increased lifespan compared to control mice72. Oleocanthal, another olive secoiridoid, increased complex I respiration, ATP production, and Aβ1–40 production in vitro72. In the TgSwDI mouse model of AD, oleocanthal administration significantly decreased Aβ accumulation in the brain, reduced neuroinflammation as evidenced by reduced astrocyte activation and IL-1β levels and activated Aβ clearance pathways73. Interestingly, a study using the same AD mouse model reported that a long-term EVOO-rich diet, started before Aβ accumulation began, significantly reduces Aβ and tau accumulation in the brain through upregulation of Aβ clearance pathways and improvement of APP processing74. This study also showed that the long-term EVOO-rich diet resulted in preserved cognitive function when compared to mice on a short-term EVOO-rich diet or a control diet74. Hydroxytrosol is another bioactive compound found in olive oil; hydroxytrosol administration in APP/PS1 mice improved mitochondrial function, increased SOD2, attenuated protein carbonyl content and NF-κB levels, but did not impact Aβ accumulation75. Hydroxytrosol also reduced inflammation, improved short term memory and cerebral blood flow, and increased Bdnf mRNA expression in a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion mouse model of ischemic stroke76. Aged mice fed a diet containing highly purified olive secoiridoids had improved spatial working memory and increased ATP production compared to aged mice fed a control diet, indicating that olive polyphenols may ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction77. Further, another study found that 3xTg AD mice fed an EVOO-rich diet for 6 months had fewer cognitive and behavioral deficits when compared to control mice78. This was found to be due to reduced Aβ and tau pathology, increased synaptic integrity and autophagy markers ATG7 and ATG5, and decreased neuroinflammation. Supplementation with oleuropein aglycone, another polyphenol in olive oil, improved memory and attenuated Aβ plaque area,soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42, and astrocyte activation in the brains of TgCRND8 AD mice, an AD mouse model that overexpresses human APP, encodes the Swedish and Indiana mutations and develops substantial Aβ plaque buildup by 3 months of age79. Further, oleuropein aglycone supplementation enhanced autophagy as indicated by an increase in Becn1 and LC3II/LC3I ratio. Together, these data indicate that the bioactive components that are abundant in a Mediterranean diet have the potential to prevent and slow neurodegeneration.

Human studies

In a randomized controlled trial of cognitively normal adults with a family history of dementia, there was no difference in cognition or brain imaging outcomes after 3 years of following the MIND diet versus mild CR80. An analysis of the Framingham Offspring Cohort data found that greater adherence to the MIND diet was associated with reduced risk of dementia, slower biological aging as measured by DunedinPACE, and slower DunedinPACE was associated with decreased risks for dementia and mortality81. Causal and mechanistic human data are limited; however, it is possible that mechanisms induced by high diet quality may reduce neurodegenerative disease risk and improve brain health.

Exercise

Consistent exercise may reduce neurodegenerative disease risk and slow disease progression. Preclinical studies use aerobic exercise, whereas human studies include both aerobic and resistance training. In various mouse models of neurodegeneration (AD or PD), exercise attenuates neuronal cell death82–85 and Aβ aggregation or production82–84,86–89, though the underlying mechanisms are not fully elucidated. Importantly, exercise induces production of exerkines β-OHB, BDNF, PGC-1α and irisin13,14,90,91. Similar to the mechanisms induced by fasting, exercise also enhances autophagy and reduces inflammation. In addition to the well-established neuroprotective mechanisms of exercise, there are a multitude of other physiological effects that are likely to be valuable. Preclinical studies: aerobic exercise

In 2xTgAD mice, running wheel access resulted in a significant reduction in Aβ40 and Aβ42 and significant increases in sAPPα and BDNF in the hippocampus when compared to control sedentary mice86. Neural cells treated with BDNF produced significantly less Aβ40 and Aβ42, and this effect was ablated with the α-secretase inhibitor batimastat, indicating that BDNF produced by exercise may enhance α-secretase activity, thus reducing Aβ production86. Another study reported that treadmill exercise in NSE/APPsw mice significantly decreased ADAM10 expression via activation of SIRT192. Moreover, exercise decreases BACE1 in the hippocampus up to 24 hours after exercise93. Treatment of hippocampal or prefrontal cortex samples with BDNF resulted in a significant reduction in BACE1 activity, depicting a mechanism by which exercise may alter AD pathology94. Further, 8 weeks of regular treadmill exercise reduced BACE1 activity in the prefrontal cortex but not the hippocampus of WT mice95. These studies indicate that exercise may shift the APP processing pathway towards the nonamyloidogenic phenotype, thus resulting in reduced AD pathology and enhanced cognition.

Intriguingly, a treadmill exercise program with gradual increases in intensity in APP/PS1 mice altered the gut microbiome profile favorably, protected intestinal barrier function, attenuated Aβ aggregation in brain tissue and neuronal loss, reduced inflammation in the serum and brain tissue, and improved spatial memory ability when compared to sedentary APP/PS1 mice82. Further, several studies show that exercise improves synaptic function. Specifically, 6 months of wheel running in 3xTgAD mice resulted in significant increases in NMDA subunit proteins, indicating improved NMDA receptor function96. The authors also reported reductions in SIRT1 and the synaptic proteins synaptophysin and PSD95 in 3xTgAD mice; this alteration was significantly ameliorated with exercise96. A different study reported improved synaptic function, as measured by input/output relationships at the CA1-medial prefrontal synapse and LTP induction, in 3xTgAD mice with running wheel access for 7 months compared to sedentary 3xTgAD mice97. Another study utilizing AlCl3/D-gal-treated AD mice reported that aerobic exercise ameliorated neuronal loss in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, reduced Iba1+ microglia but increased numbers of Arg1+ reparative microglia, which are anti-inflammatory and may have neuroprotective roles, and induced Histone H3 lactylation in microglia85. Notably, this study reported that exercise improved learning and memory abilities and reduced neuronal loss in AD mice and in WT aged mice, indicating that exercise may not only slow neurodegeneration associated with AD, but also slow normal brain aging processes. In an MPTP mouse model of PD, aerobic exercise reduced neuroinflammation and PD disease pathology. Furthermore, serum from exercising mice significantly reduced inflammation and increased irisin expression in α-synuclein-treated microglia in vitro98. One study reported that chronic endurance exercise in NSE/htau23 mice, which overexpress Tau23 in the brain, significantly reduces neuroinflammation as evidenced by significant reductions in activated astrocytes and microglia and downregulation of proinflammatory cytokine expression99.

Exercise upregulates autophagy; dysfunctional autophagy contributes to AD pathology31. Treadmill training for 8 weeks in APP/PS1 mice led to increased neuronal survival and autophagic lysosomal pathway activity as measured by a higher number of autophagic vacuoles and upregulation of TFEB target genes Atg5 and Amp1lc3b83. Exercising mice also had increased expression of mitochondrial genes Prkaa2, Nrf2, and Tfam, fewer abnormal mitochondria as assessed by TEM analysis, and decreased Aβ levels when compared to sedentary controls83. It is well established that exercise activates β2-adrenergic receptors; in APP-PSEN1/PS1 mice, β2-adrenergic receptor activation was responsible for upregulating AMPK, downregulating mTOR signaling, and activating autophagy through VMA1 upregulation, thereby inhibiting Aβ production and ameliorating cognitive impairment87. Interestingly, β2 receptor inhibition with propranolol abrogated the exercise-induced improvements in autophagy, cognitive function, and Aβ levels in the hippocampus87. Another study reported that exercising APP/PS1 mice had decreased mTOR expression, increased Becn1 expression, and increased LC3-II/LC3-I, further confirming that exercise can enhance autophagy in AD brains88. Additionally, exercise increased AdipoR1 expression, AMPK phosphorylation, TFEB, and cathepsin D protein levels, indicating mechanisms by which exercise induces autophagy88. In APP/PSEN1 mice, long-term treadmill running significantly increased total and nuclear TFEB, reduced lysosomal area, and increased co-localization of lysosomes with Aβ when compared to sedentary APP/PSEN1 mice, suggesting that exercise can enhance lysosomal function to clear Aβ plaque100. Importantly, the studies cited here also report that exercise alleviates cognitive deficits, indicating that these molecular alterations induced by exercise translate to functional improvements.

Mitophagy is the degradation of defective mitochondria via autophagy; mitophagy deficits may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction observed in neurodegeneration. In APP/PS1 mice, exercise reduced the percentage of damaged mitochondria in the hippocampus, increased ATP, and increased ETC complex I and IV levels by normalizing mitophagy through PINK1/Parkin regulation89. However, exercise differentially affects mitophagy activity in WT mice than AD mice; the authors noted that exercise restored weak mitophagy activity and enhanced degradation of damaged mitochondria in APP/PS1 mice, whereas in WT mice, exercise appears to increase basal mitophagy89. Mitophagy alterations induced by exercise also ameliorate Aβ pathology and cognitive decrements. Exercising mice had reductions in plaque load, Aβ42, Aβ40, and improved learning and memory abilities, and this was abrogated by treatment with the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine89. Furthermore, the exercise-induced regulation of mitophagy was identified to be mediated through activation of the SIRT1-FOXO1/3 axis in the hippocampus89. A different study also utilizing APP/PS1 mice reported similar results; treadmill exercise reduced hippocampal Aβ area and mitochondrial Aβ levels, increased PGC-1a and TFAM, reduced damaged mitochondria, regulated PINK1/Parkin levels, and increased autophagy activity101.

Human studies: multicomponent exercise/aerobic and resistance exercise

Human studies investigating the impact of exercise on cognition in adults with AD are limited. Many exercise intervention studies utilize a multimodal exercise training program. Numerous clinical studies report that both aerobic and resistance exercise increase blood BDNF in humans13. A 3-month exercise intervention, comprised of aerobic, strength, and balance training, in adults with MCI significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and p-tau181, and improved the ratio of Αβ42/40 in serum102; however, this study did not assess changes in cognition. A randomized controlled trial in cognitively normal postmenopausal women showed that a single bout of high-intensity interval aerobic exercise alone or in combination with whole-body vibration resistance training significantly improved cognitive performance and increased BDNF and irisin levels when compared to pre-exercise values103. A study in cognitively normal older women found that a multimodal exercise intervention significantly improved cognitive performance and increased plasma BDNF104. In an observational study of middle and older-aged adults without cognitive impairment, high cardiorespiratory fitness was significantly associated with a reduced risk of becoming Aβ positive105. A meta-analysis of observational studies found that individuals with AD have significantly less lean mass, worse handgrip strength, lower limb muscle strength, and slower gait speed compared to those without AD106. In patients with PD, high levels of physical activity were significantly positively correlated with Aβ42 in the CSF and significantly negatively correlated with anxiety and depression scores107. Collectively, these data show that consistent high levels of physical activity can reduce neurodegenerative disease risk.

Conclusions and future directions

The evidence presented in this article strongly supports the premise that lifestyle habits can influence mechanisms that drive neurodegenerative disease risk and progression. Diet restriction, diet quality, and exercise all have the potential to improve cognitive function, alleviate Aβ burden, and alter numerous other molecular mechanisms that are beneficial to brain health.

There are many gaps in knowledge remaining. It is critical to understand how stringently one must follow intermittent fasting, calorie restriction, a Mediterranean diet, or another dietary pattern or exercise regimen and for how many years in order to elicit meaningful cognitive benefits. Moreover, it is essential to understand how interventions initiated in late life or after the onset of disease compare to those followed long-term or prior to the onset of disease, and how the timing may impact the magnitude of potential benefits. Future clinical studies should focus on how these lifestyle interventions can be implemented effectively, alone or in combination, to slow cognitive decline. Parallel, two-arm, randomized controlled trials assessing cognition and relevant biomarkers timepoints are critical to elucidate the cognitive and physiologic benefits of lifestyle interventions. It is also important to understand how lifestyle interventions may interact synergistically with traditional pharmacological therapies; it is probable that a combination could elicit the greatest benefits. Additionally, it is critical to identify metabolites, proteins, and biomarkers, particularly those that can be measured in serum or plasma, that are responsive to intervention and relevant to cognitive function in humans. Continued research is critical to distinguish lifestyle strategies that may be preventative and those that have the potential to be therapeutic.
