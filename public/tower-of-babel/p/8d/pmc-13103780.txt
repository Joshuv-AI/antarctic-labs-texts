Uniform TiN-Capped Co Buried Bit Lines for 4F2 DRAM Vertical Channel Transistors: Mitigating Agglomeration with Co Nitridation
Authors: Jiabao Sun, Tielu Liu, Jun Zhou, Baodong Han, Chao Tian, Hongbo Sun, Chao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, due to the high demand for high-density memory, the cell architecture of the vertical transistors has been continuously shrunk from 6F2 to a more compact 4F2. The bit lines (BLs) play an important role in fulfilling the functions of such scaled, vertically stacked arrays. Here, we report the formation of conformable Co-linear films covering the vertical structures with a high aspect ratio (AR), i.e., ∼8:1, using the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) technique. A detailed microscopic analysis was performed on the comfortable Co layers capped by TiN films by using a transmission electron microscope (TEM). The Co layers were damaged after the deposition of TiN layers, which resulted in the agglomeration of the Co layers. A nitridation process of Co layers was introduced to form a uniform and continuous Co layer. Our work paves a new way for forming well protected and highly uniform Co silicide-based buried BLs interconnecting the arrays of vertical transistors.

Introduction

Growing demand for high-density memory has driven continuous shrinkage of memory cells, from the conventional 6F2 architectures to the more compact 4F2 design, , achieving a ∼33% reduction in cell area. As device dimensions scale down, narrow metal conductors exhibit a marked increase in resistivity, − which increases signal delay and power consumption in microelectronic devices. At the nanoscale, defects such as grain boundaries, ,− surface roughness, − and interface imperfections , can dramatically elevate the resistivity of metallic interconnects, thereby degrading device performance. The downscaling of interconnect lines amplifies the impact of these defects, underscoring the need for optimized metallic bit lines (BLs) to enable robust interconnections between transistors. The more conductive Co and their silicide (e.g., self-aligned CoSi2) have emerged as promising alternatives to tungsten (W) and copper (Cu) metallization.

Over the past decades, Co lines have been widely employed in first-level metallization, serving as the narrow interconnects, including source/drain electrodes and bit-line connections, to form desired Ohmic contacts in field-effect transistors. ,− Some efforts have also focused on developing adhesion layers for copper interconnects. , The relatively low melting point of Co films enables defect healing via annealing. However, oxygen exposure during annealing leads to the oxidation of metal lines and increases sheet resistance (due to enhanced electron scattering at the surface) or even shifts the formation temperature of Co silicide. In situ capping layers, such as Ti or TiN, , are therefore required to protect Co films from oxygen and form smooth surfaces and interfaces for Co layers and its silicide.

Recently, alternative passivation approaches have been widely explored for MOL/BEOL interconnects. The nonhalide TiN deposition methods have been explored, including plasma-enhanced ALD (PE-ALD) using metal–organic precursors or hydrazine coreactants, which yield lower impurity content and low resistivity (<220 μΩ·cm). Metal nitride alternatives such as TaN or MoN x have also been investigated for their superior barrier properties and oxidation resistance in advanced DRAM/MOL applications. , Furthermore, single-layer amorphous alloys like Co–Ti have been proposed as effective barriers/liners that reduce overall line resistance by eliminating thick bilayer stacks. , In addition, emerging passivation approaches include Ru-based liners/capsoffering excellent thermal stabilityand atomically thin 2D materials such as graphene, which provide exceptional diffusion barrier performance at minimal thickness. − While these alternatives offer excellent intrinsic resistivity and barrier properties, their integration into extreme vertical 3D architectures often suffers from poor conformal step coverage (e.g., PVD TaN) or severe process incompatibility (e.g., high-temperature 2D material transfer). Therefore, chemical vapor deposition (CVD) or atomic layer deposition (ALD) of TiN remains the industry standard for conformal 3D integration.

The TiN capping layers, however, can compromise Co film uniformity and induce agglomeration of Co atoms. − The underlying mechanisms of such damage and the structural/topographical evolution of TiN-induced Co layer degradation need to be further explored. Here, we report TiN-capped Co layers deposited via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) in vertical channel transistors (VCTs) with a high aspect ratio (AR) (∼8:1). Detailed analyses of surface morphology and topology were performed by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The agglomeration of Co films after the TiN deposition was observed with its systematically elucidated origin. We further introduce an advanced Co-layer nitridation process that effectively suppresses the Co-Agglomeration. Our in situ NH3 plasma nitridation step is seamlessly integrated into standard TiCl4–ALD TiN flows without altering precursor chemistry or requiring additional deposition modules, addressing key reliability bottlenecks for scaled MOL/BEOL interconnects.

Materials and Methods

Arrays of silicon pillars were fabricated using self-aligned double patterning technology intended for the formation of VCT arrays. The detailed process flow has been reported in our previous work, , with the associated process flow shown in Figure . In this work, we focus on the formation of buried BLs connecting the silicon pillars. As shown in Figure a, deep trenches were formed via silicon etching, with sufficient depth to prevent bit-line shorts; the structure exhibits an aspect ratio of ∼8:1. The silicon arrays were fully encapsulated by SiO2 layers deposited by using atomic layer deposition (ALD), followed by chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) to planarize the structures, as illustrated in Figure b. SiO2 lines were then formed by dry etching to serve as insulating bit-line spacers and diffusion barriers (Figure c).

Proposed process flow with the fabrication of the silicon arrays and buried BLs.

(a–f) The schematic of the conformable formation of TiN-capped Co layers covering vertical silicon arrays using the CVD technique.

The buried BLs of the vertical channel transistor were constructed by creating bowl-like structures at the bottom of the deep trenches, designed to minimize resistance. The Siconi etch process was adopted to remove Si films to confine bowl-like profile at the bottom, as illustrated in Figure d. Immediately after the Siconi etch, the x-nanometer-thick Co films were deposited via Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) using the flowable precursor Dicobalt hexacarbonyl tert-butylacetylene (CCTBA), forming direct contact with each other (Figure e). TiN capping layers were deposited using the thermal ALD technique to prevent oxygen contamination during annealing (Figure f), which utilized titanium tetrachloride (TiCl4) and ammonia (NH3) as precursors at a temperature of 430 °C. Subsequent annealing was introduced at 630 °C in a high-purity N2 ambient for defect healing, serving as an improvement of the metallic interconnection between each array; this healed buried BLs ensures low contact resistance. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to characterize the structural and chemical properties of the TiN/Co/SiO2 film stack. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to measure the surface roughness of films deposited on blank wafers.

Results and Discussion

The high aspect ratio (∼8:1) of vertical channel transistors with nanoscale critical dimensions becomes an obstacle for forming uniform Co films. To ensure good conformality in such complex structures, the flowable precursor CCTBA was employed for the CVD deposition of Co films. Conformality of the Co layers is critical for the subsequent formation of high-quality interconnection using annealing, as Co acts as the primary diffusing species in CoSiX formation. , Postdeposition structural characterization using TEM combined with EDX (Figure ) reveals that the Co layer conformally and uniformly covers the SiO x /Si arrays with a step coverage of 78.1% on the sidewalls of silicon pillars. Notably, the bowl-shaped structures are fully covered by CVD-Co, exhibiting step coverages of ∼78.2% at the top, ∼83.8% on the sidewalls, and ∼98.62% at the bottom. In addition, a clear oxidized Co surface is observed after the postannealing healing, as shown in Figure b, which needs to be prevented by introducing a capping layer.

Given the relatively inert nature of TiN compared to Ti, , the t nm-thick TiN films were deposited as capping layers. To ensure high conformality, TiN deposition was performed via thermal ALD at ∼430 °C using TiCl4 as the titanium precursor and NH3 as the nitrogen source. However, after the TiN deposition process, the Co films exhibited severe damage and discontinuity, manifesting as clusters and agglomerations (Figure a). Element-resolved EDX measurements (Figure b) were performed to further characterize these defects and analyze the chemical compositions of the clusters. Despite complete TiN coverage (blue) over the Co layers, the continuity and homogeneity of the Co layers (purple) were disrupted, with Co atoms tending to aggregate, resulting in rough surfaces and interfaces.

To quantify the impact of morphological changes on the electrical performance of the buried bit lines (BLs), we employed the Fuchs–Sondheimer (FS) , and Mayadas–Shatzkes (MS) , models. The total resistivity (ρ) of the thin metallic Co lines, considering both surface and grain boundary scattering, can be expressed as ρ≈ρbulk(1+34k(1−p)+32R1−Rλd) where p is the phenomenological surface specularity parameter (FS model), k is the line width of metal lines, and R is the grain boundary reflection coefficient (MS model), and d is the grain size. According to the classical Fuchs-Sondheimer (FS) model, when the dimensions of the defects is smaller or near the mean free path of electrons, the electron scattering will be dramatically increased and hence increase the resistivity, even higher than that of the bulk counterpart. Such electron-surface scattering can be described by a phenomenological parameter, i.e., p, which is in the range of 0 and 1. For the system with p = 0, the momentum of the electrons will be randomized during the scattering and hence decrease the conductivity of metallic interconnections. Milosevic et al., reported that a broken surface and interface of Co can induce a zero parameter. Therefore, the Co clusters and agglomeration need to be suppressed and form smooth Co-based buried BLs. The grain boundary scattering described by the MS model also contributes to the resistivity of nanometric Co lines. For CVD-Co films, the reflection coefficient (R) at grain boundaries typically ranges from 0.25 to 0.45. The thermal budget during TiCl4-based deposition may also promote grain growth (increasing d), which further compensates for the interfacial scattering.

Such agglomeration may be attributed to two reasons. One is the recrystallization during the deposition of TiN layers above 400 °C, and the second is the reaction between the TiCl4 precursor and Co layers. To verify the first assumption, the Co films were directly deposited on two identical bare Si wafers using the same recipes as we used for the VCT structure. An annealing process was performed at 430 °C on one of them to imitate the thermal budget caused by the deposition of TiN capping. It gives a roughness of ±0.624 nm and a peak-to-valley (P–V) height of 7.562 ± 0.8 nm characterized using AFM, as shown in Figure c. Moreover, the Co clusters were not observed by using the TEM-EDX investigations shown in Figure a. In contrast, the TiN capping was deposited on the other Co/Si multilayers, whose large roughness (i.e., 4.521 ± 0.5 nm and a P–V height of 37.008 ± 3.7 nm) was determined using the AFM technique (Figure d). The TEM-EDX topology of the films is shown in Figure b. The rough surface was imaged, where the Co layers were damaged and formed hump features. It can be concluded that the cluster observed in Figure originates from the reaction between TiCl4 and metal films. A similar effect has been reported in the TiCl4-based CVD-Ti by Elers et al.

According to thermodynamic considerations and the NIST-JANAF thermochemical tables, TiCl4 acts as a potent halogenating agent toward transition metals at elevated temperatures. At the deposition temperature of 430 °C, the proposed reaction between TiCl4 and Co is as follows TiCl4(g)+Co(s)→TiCl2(g)+CoClx(g/s)

The calculated Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for this reaction at 430 °C is −16.4 kJ/mol, indicating that the reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous. The formation of volatile or semivolatile CoCl x species during the initial incubation period of TiN deposition induces “chemical etching” on the Co surface. , This erosion increases the surface energy and atom mobility of the Co layer, triggering the formation of island-like agglomeration. Additionally, the byproduct HCl generated during the reaction of TiCl4 with NH3 may further exacerbate the formation of CoCl x species, consistent with the corrosive mechanisms reported for other transition metals.

As we have mentioned above, the surface roughness and film homogeneity will have a great influence on electron transport. To form a smooth surface and interface, the surface of Co needs to be protected from the reaction with the TiCl4 precursor. In this work, the nitridation of the Co surface was introduced using an in situ NH3 plasma treatment at ∼430 °C. Nitridation of the Co surface has been widely used in microelectronic applications as diffusion layers and gate electrodes. The TEM results of the structure postnitridation are shown in Figure a. The continuous and uniform Co layers were observed. An elemental-resolved EDX characterization was performed, shown in Figure b. The agglomerations of Co layers are not observed, which indicates that the Co layers are well protected by the Co-nitride at the very top surface. It can be widely used in the future to form a uniform and continuous Co-layer with the TiN capping.

To understand the chemical nature of interface protection, the bonding states induced by the nitridation process must be considered. Although EDX mapping primarily identifies elemental distribution, the dramatic shift from an agglomerate (Figure b) to a uniform morphology (Figure b) provides indirect evidence of a change in chemical states. The NH3 plasma treatment transforms the metallic Co surface into a thin, chemically stable CoN x layer. According to previous spectroscopic studies by Mattson et al., the formation of Co–N bonds significantly reduces the surface energy and enhances the chemical resistance of the film. This nitridation state is crucial for the suppression of Cl-induced corrosion.

While direct quantitative mapping of subnanometer Cl distributions is challenging due to the detection limits of standard EDX, the interfacial integrity serves as a sensitive indicator of the chemical environment. In the untreated sample, TiCl4 precursors react with metallic Co to form volatile TiCl4 species that act as catalysts for atom migration and agglomeration. However, the CoN x layer acts as a passivation barrier that is thermodynamically resistant to Ti–Cl exchange reactions. Furthermore, the NH3 plasma environment facilitates the scavenging of residual chlorine into volatile HCl or NH4Cl byproducts, effectively depleting the halogen concentration at the growth front. The preservation of an atomically sharp interface in the nitridated sample (Figure b) supports the conclusion that Cl incorporation and the subsequent formation of Co–Cl bonding are effectively suppressed. However, it should be acknowledged that the identification of the CoN x phase in this work is a proposed mechanism inferred from the observed morphological stabilization and established literature. To definitively map the stoichiometric evolution and confirm the Co–N coordination, complementary techniques such as X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling and high-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy-Electron Energy Loss Spectroscopy (TEM-EELS) are recommended for future studies. Such analyses would provide subnanometer chemical resolution to further validate the nature of the passivated Co surface.

In the case of TiN capping without nitridation, the dramatic increase in RMS roughness from ±0.624 to ±4.521 nm signifies a transition in the surface scattering regime. By correlating these values with the electron mean free path of Co (λ ∼ 11.8 nm), the ratio of roughness to the mean free path increases from approximately 0.05 (nitridated) to 0.38 (untreated). Within the framework of the FS model, a ratio exceeding 0.3 signifies a regime in which interface-induced momentum randomization becomes severe. For the reported 10 nm thick bit lines, where the film thickness is comparable to λ, the transition from a smooth interface to a rough, agglomerated state causes the specularity p to drop toward zero. It indicates completely diffusive scattering. Specifically, utilizing the standard parameters for Co (ρbulk = 6.24 μΩ·cm, λ = 11.8 nm), we quantitatively estimate the total resistivity for a 10 nm nominal bit line using the combined FS and MS equation. For the nitridated Co surface, the remarkably low RMS roughness of σ = ±0.624 nm translates to a high specularity regime (p ≈ 0.4), yielding a theoretical resistivity of ∼13.5 μΩ·cm (assuming R ≈ 0.35). Notably, this roughness metric is highly competitive with state-of-the-art, highly scaled Cu and Ru interconnect liners reported in recent literature. ,, Conversely, the catastrophic agglomeration in the non-nitridated sample (σ = ±4.521 nm) drives p → 0 and reduces the effective conductive width k, forcing theoretical resistivity to spike well above 25 μΩ·cm.

Therefore, the nitridation process preserves the continuity of the x nm Co line. The role of CoN x in maintaining a smooth interface (p > 0) and preventing structural breaks is the dominant factor in stabilizing the line resistance. Although the hundreds of micro-Ohmic-resistivity of CoN x is larger than that of Co itself, it is still smaller than that of Co with a damaged surface. Moreover, only the bottom regions contact the Si pillars and function as interconnections. The plasma-induced CoN x layer is extremely thin such that it does not significantly increase the resistivity of the buried BLs. Based on the MS model, the thermal budget during TiCl4-based deposition may also promote grain growth (increasing d), which further compensates for interfacial scattering. These theoretical estimations suggest that the nitridation-induced morphological stability is the prerequisite for achieving the low-resistance regime required for 4F2 DRAM architectures, as structural continuity outweighs the minor increases in material resistivity. Looking forward, while the current morphological and theoretical models provide a robust framework, disentangling the exact contributions of electron–phonon interactions from electron-defect scattering remains a critical challenge. Future studies employing temperature-dependent resistivity measurements to extract the Temperature Coefficient of Resistance (TCR), alongside Hall mobility analysis, will be invaluable to definitively isolate these scattering mechanisms and fully quantify the transport physics in highly scaled Co and CoN x interconnects.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates the conformal deposition of Co films via CVD within high-AR (∼8:1) bowl-like structures, a critical step for fabricating buried BLs in 4F2 DRAM architectures. The results of TEM-EDX investigations reveal that the precursor TiCl4, employed for the deposition of CVD-TiN, induces severe agglomeration and discontinuity of the underlying Co films. It is attributed to the chemical reactions between the TiCl4 and Co atoms, proven by the experiments on blank wafers. The Co-nitridation process effectively suppresses such agglomeration, preserving the continuity and uniformity of Co layers even after the TiN deposition. This strategy addresses a key challenge in scaling VCTs, where metallic interconnect integrity directly impacts electron transport and device performance. Our findings provide a viable pathway for realizing high-quality, Co-based buried bit lines, enabling continued vertical scaling of 3D memory devices through improved metallization.
