Peritoneal inclusion cyst presenting as a rectocele after sacrohysteropexy: A case report
Authors: Kasra Akbari, Mansooreh Yaraghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peritoneal inclusion cysts (PICs) are mesothelial-lined cystic lesions that usually arise in reproductive-aged women after prior pelvic or abdominal surgery. Their diagnosis is often difficult, as they can mimic ovarian tumors or malignant pelvic masses. There are very few reports of PICs following pelvic reconstructive surgery. This report concerns the case of a 40-year-old nulliparous woman with a history of open sacrohysteropexy for stage IV pelvic organ prolapse who presented with progressive left lower quadrant pain and a vaginal bulge. Imaging revealed a large multilocular retroperitoneal cyst enveloping the left ovary and extending into the rectovaginal septum. Tumor markers were within normal limits. Due to the cyst's proximity to the bowel, ureter, and major vessels, and to avoid mesh erosion, a vaginal surgical approach was selected. Approximately 300 mL of serous fluid was drained, the cyst wall was excised under rectal guidance, and marsupialization was performed to the vaginal mucosa. Histopathology confirmed a benign mesothelial-lined cyst. At one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence evident on imaging. This is seemingly the first reported case of a retroperitoneal PIC following sacrohysteropexy. The case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by PICs and highlights the role of individualized, minimally invasive management. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PICs when evaluating postoperative pelvic or vaginal masses, particularly when imaging suggests a cystic lesion.

Introduction

Peritoneal inclusion cysts (PICs) are benign multilocular cystic lesions formed by fluid accumulation between intraperitoneal adhesions [1]. They are most frequently encountered in women of reproductive age, particularly in the third and fourth decades of life [2], often following prior pelvic or abdominal surgery, sometimes occurring months to even 20 years later. PICS are exceedingly rare, and very few cases have been documented in the literature since their first description in 1979 [3].

The diagnosis of PICs can be challenging, as they may mimic malignant or other cystic lesions [1], and their occurrence is particularly rare following gynecologic procedures such as sacrohysteropexy. Even more unusual is their clinical manifestation as a rectocele-like vaginal bulge, which can mislead both patients and clinicians. This uncommon presentation further complicates both diagnosis and management, as clinicians must balance the need to exclude malignancy with the uncertainty surrounding optimal treatment strategies [4].

This report presents the case of a premenopausal woman with a large retroperitoneal PIC after sacrohysteropexy, which uniquely presented as a rectocele. The diagnostic difficulties and therapeutic considerations associated with this rare case are reviewed. This report is written in accordance with the CARE guidelines [5].

Case Presentation

A 40-year-old Iranian Kurdish woman, who was nulliparous and a farmer, had been experiencing progressive left lower quadrant (LLQ) pain and vaginal bulge for four months. The pain was colicky, radiated to the back, and was rated 4/10 in severity. It was constant, non-positional, and unrelated to meals or defecation. She reported no gastrointestinal or urinary symptoms, spotting, or weight loss during this period.

Two years earlier, she had presented with stage IV pelvic organ prolapse (POP-Q) without any known risk factors. Considering the patient's desire for uterine preservation and future fertility, open sacrohysteropexy using knitted macroporous polypropylene monofilament mesh (Sacromesh) was performed. Her past medical, surgical, and family histories were otherwise unremarkable, and she was not on any medications.

On examination, her vital signs were stable (pulse rate 84/min; respiratory rate 14/min; blood pressure 110/80 mmHg; temperature 36.4 °C). She appeared well. Abdominal examination revealed mild distension and a non-tender palpable mass in the LLQ without guarding or rebound tenderness. Vaginal examination demonstrated no apical or anterior prolapse (POP-Q stage 0), but a reducible stage III posterior bulge (Fig. 1). Bimanual vaginal examination revealed a soft, mobile mass extending to the level of the umbilicus. The cyst was retroperitoneal, located over the major vessels, and situated between the left ureter and the sigmoid colon, with intestinal loops and mesh anteriorly. To minimize the risk of injury to adjacent organs and mesh complication, a vaginal approach was chosen as a minimally invasive alternative to abdominal surgery. Under spinal anesthesia, a vertical vaginal incision was made at the point of maximal bulging. During surgery, the entire cyst along with the extra epithelium of the posterior wall extending to the cul-de-sac was excised. This was done under rectal guidance with care to preserve the integrity of the mesh. Approximately 300 mL of serous fluid was drained. The posterior wall was closed and the cyst edges were marsupialized to the vaginal mucosa at cul-de-sac using interrupted 2–0 Vicryl sutures, promoting continuous drainage and reducing the risk of recurrence. The procedure was completed without intraoperative or postoperative complications. A small remnant measuring approximately 12 × 4 cm was seen posterior to the mesh on post-operation sonography.

Histopathological examination confirmed a benign mesothelial-lined cyst without atypia, consistent with a PIC.

The remnant of the cyst did not increase in size postoperatively and was not associated with any symptoms or clinical signs during follow-up. The patient experienced vaginal secretion and fluid drainage for approximately three months following the marsupialization procedure. At one-year follow-up, pelvic examination (POP-Q stage 0) and sonography showed no recurrence. The patient remained asymptomatic, reported a good quality of life, and experienced no adverse or unanticipated events.

Discussion

PICs are benign, fluid-filled lesions lined by cuboidal mesothelial cells. They typically occur in women of reproductive age and are recognized as non-ovarian causes of cystic pelvic masses [1]. Their pathogenesis is thought to involve peritoneal fluid accumulation within adhesions following pelvic or abdominal injury [6]. While PICs are usually confined to the pelvis, they may occasionally extend into the upper abdomen or retroperitoneal space [7]. The present patient, consistent with this profile, developed a large retroperitoneal cyst after open sacrohysteropexy.

The diagnosis of PIC is often challenging due to its rarity, the variety of presentations, and the broad spectrum of differential diagnoses, which include lymphangiomas [8], mesenteric cysts, pseudomyxoma peritonei, malignant mesothelioma, and adnexal pathologies [1,9,10]. Careful imaging and identification of normal ovaries can help exclude ovarian malignancy [11]. In the present case, CT and MRI were crucial in demonstrating the cystic nature of the lesion and its relationship to adjacent organs.

Importantly, this case highlights a particularly rare clinical presentation of PIC as a rectocele, a finding that can easily be mistaken for recurrent pelvic organ prolapse [12]. This diagnostic challenge highlights the importance of considering alternative etiologies when evaluating patients with a new or recurrent vaginal bulge after prolapse surgery.

Management of PICs is complex and depends on the lesion's size, location, recurrence risk, and proximity to vital structures [1]. In this patient, the cyst was retroperitoneal, closely associated with the bowel, major vessels, ureter, and surgical mesh. An abdominal approach carried a significant risk of visceral injury and mesh erosion; therefore, a minimally invasive vaginal approach was selected. Marsupialization of the cyst wall to the vaginal mucosa allowed continuous drainage, minimizing the risk of recurrence. The patient remained recurrence-free and asymptomatic at one-year follow-up, highlighting the effectiveness and safety of this tailored approach.

Only one case report of PIC after sacrocolpopexy could be found in the literature [13], and the present report is the first of PIC following sacrohysteropexy with the unique presentation of rectocele. This emphasizes the rarity of this complication and the importance of considering PIC in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with a vaginal mass after pelvic reconstructive surgery. This case also emphasizes the importance of thorough preoperative counseling for patients undergoing pelvic organ prolapse surgery. Clinicians should discuss the risk, however small, of PIC formation as a delayed complication.

In summary, this case adds to the very few reports of PIC after prolapse surgery and shows that management must be tailored to each patient. Clinicians should keep PICs in mind when evaluating postoperative pelvic or vaginal masses, especially cystic ones, to avoid misdiagnosis. More research is needed to identify risk factors and to guide prevention, early recognition, and treatment.
