Prognostic value of C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index in hospitalised patients with acute decompensated heart failure
Authors: Wei Wang, Rong Huang, Miaoxin Chen, Yu Wang, Jiaming Zhang, Xingyi Lai, Lei Hu, Lian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) is a composite marker integrating systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. Although CTI has been associated with cardiovascular risk in general and cardiometabolic populations, its prognostic significance in patients hospitalised with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) remains unclear.MethodsThis single-centre retrospective cohort study included 1,494 patients hospitalised with ADHF between January 2020 and June 2024. CTI was calculated as 0.412 × ln[CRP (mg/L)] + ln[TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2], and patients were stratified into tertiles. The endpoints were all-cause mortality, cardiovascular (CV) death, and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs). Associations between CTI and outcomes were assessed using multivariable Cox regression and restricted cubic spline analyses. Incremental prognostic performance beyond a comprehensive clinical model was evaluated using optimism-corrected Harrell’s C-index, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, and decision curve analysis.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 498 days, event rates increased progressively across CTI tertiles. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-standard deviation increase in CTI was independently associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 1.52, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.29–1.80), CV death (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.24–1.84), and MACCEs (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.26–1.59). Patients in the highest CTI tertile had the greatest risk across all endpoints (all P for trend < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analyses showed positive, approximately linear associations between CTI and all outcomes. Adding CTI to the clinical model yielded modest improvements in discrimination, with C-index increases from 0.665 to 0.672 for all-cause mortality, from 0.697 to 0.705 for CV death, and from 0.644 to 0.652 for MACCEs. Significant improvements in NRI and IDI were also observed for all outcomes.ConclusionsElevated CTI was independently associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, CV death, and MACCEs in patients hospitalised with ADHF. Addition of CTI to a comprehensive clinical model provided modest but statistically significant incremental prognostic value beyond established clinical predictors and related metabolic or inflammatory markers evaluated in this study. CTI may serve as a simple adjunctive marker for metabolic–inflammatory risk profiling and refined risk stratification in ADHF, pending external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06241-6.Keywords: C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index, Acute decompensated heart failure, Insulin resistance, Systemic inflammation

Introduction

Hospitalisation for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) is associated with substantial risks of early readmission and mortality. Despite advances in guideline-directed medical therapy, ADHF continues to impose a major clinical and economic burden worldwide [1–6]. Current guidelines emphasise early identification of high-risk patients and refined risk stratification to support clinical decision-making and healthcare resource allocation [1, 3]. However, prognostic models used in ADHF, which commonly incorporate demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, natriuretic peptides, and routine laboratory indices, still have suboptimal predictive performance [7]. This limitation has prompted interest in readily available biomarkers that may capture pathophysiological processes beyond haemodynamic congestion and neurohormonal activation.

Systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are important contributors to heart failure (HF) progression and adverse outcomes [8, 9]. C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase reactant induced by inflammatory signalling, is a widely available marker of systemic inflammation. Elevated CRP has been associated with incident HF and increased mortality in population-based cohorts and established HF populations [10–12]. Composite inflammatory indices may further improve risk assessment by integrating multiple aspects of the inflammatory response [13–15]. In parallel, insulin resistance (IR) is closely linked to cardiometabolic dysfunction in HF. IR may impair myocardial substrate utilisation, promote lipotoxicity and oxidative stress, and contribute to endothelial and microvascular dysfunction. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, derived from fasting triglycerides and glucose, is a practical surrogate marker of IR and has been associated with incident HF and adverse outcomes in patients with ADHF [16–18]. These findings suggest that both inflammatory and metabolic pathways are relevant to risk stratification in HF.

Inflammation and IR are biologically interconnected through shared mechanisms, including adipose tissue–derived cytokines, innate immune activation, and altered insulin signalling. During acute decompensation, this interaction may be amplified by haemodynamic stress, congestion, and end-organ dysfunction, resulting in a metabolic–inflammatory milieu that may not be fully captured by isolated biomarkers. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI), a weighted composite derived from CRP and the TyG index, has been proposed as a marker integrating systemic inflammation and IR [19]. Previous studies have reported associations between elevated CTI and major adverse cardiovascular events or mortality in general and high-risk cardiometabolic populations [19–22]. However, the prognostic significance of CTI in patients hospitalised with ADHF remains insufficiently defined.

Importantly, it remains unclear whether CTI provides prognostic information beyond its individual components, CRP and TyG, their simple combination, and established multivariable clinical models. Given the high prevalence of dysglycaemia and subclinical IR among patients with HF, an integrated metabolic–inflammatory marker may provide additional risk information across different glucose-metabolism profiles. Therefore, this study investigated the association between CTI and adverse clinical outcomes in patients hospitalised with ADHF. We further evaluated the incremental prognostic value of CTI compared with TyG, CRP, TyG + CRP, and a comprehensive clinical model, including analyses across glucose-metabolism subgroups.

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-centre retrospective cohort study included patients hospitalised with ADHF at the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital, Medical School of Nanjing University, between 1 January 2020 and 1 June 2024. The diagnosis of ADHF was established according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines. From an initial screening of 2,245 patients, 751 were excluded to minimise confounding from conditions associated with acute inflammatory activation or substantial metabolic disturbance. The exclusion criteria were concomitant acute coronary syndrome or recent major vascular intervention; advanced pulmonary disease, including cor pulmonale; haematological disorders, active malignancy, or chronic hepatitis; acute infectious or systemic inflammatory diseases; long-term glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy; missing key variables required for CTI calculation; and incomplete follow-up outcomes. The final analytical cohort comprised 1,494 patients (Fig. 1). The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee.

Patient selection flowchart

Data collection and definitions

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic, and admission medication data were retrieved from electronic medical records by trained physicians. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²). Peripheral venous blood specimens were obtained after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and processed in the central laboratory using standardised procedures. Laboratory parameters included haemoglobin, uric acid, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), creatinine, C-reactive protein (CRP), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), white blood cell count (WBC), sodium, potassium, albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST).

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, current use of antihypertensive medication, or a documented prior diagnosis. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or a self-reported history of diabetes. Normal glucose regulation (NGR) was defined as FPG < 100 mg/dL and HbA1c < 5.7%, without a history of DM. Prediabetes (Pre-DM) was defined as FPG 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%, without a prior diagnosis of DM. Hyperlipidaemia was defined as TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, TGs ≥ 150 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, ongoing lipid-lowering therapy, or a documented prior diagnosis.

Endpoints and follow‑up

The prespecified endpoints were all-cause mortality, CV death, and MACCEs. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause. CV death was defined as death resulting from fatal stroke, myocardial infarction, progressive HF, malignant ventricular arrhythmia, or other structural or functional cardiac causes. MACCEs were defined as a composite of non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, and unplanned hospitalisation for worsening HF. Following hospital discharge, clinical follow-up was conducted at 6-month intervals through structured telephone interviews or outpatient visits by trained physicians.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range) for skewed distributions. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Patients were stratified into tertiles according to cohort-specific CTI values: T1, CTI < 8.828; T2, 8.828 ≤ CTI < 9.514; and T3, CTI ≥ 9.514. Baseline characteristics across CTI tertiles were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the χ² test for categorical variables. Standardised mean differences were calculated to quantify between-group differences, and the maximum pairwise SMD across tertiles was reported. An SMD > 0.1 was considered indicative of a potentially meaningful difference.

Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets using the mice package in R under the missing-at-random assumption. The variables with the highest proportions of missing data were BNP and LVEF, with missing rates of 12.5% and 8.9%, respectively. Estimates from imputed datasets were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Time-to-event outcomes were initially assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared across CTI tertiles using the log-rank test.

Associations between CTI and clinical endpoints were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and visual inspection of scaled residual plots. CTI was modelled both as a continuous variable per 1-standard deviation increase and as a categorical variable based on tertiles, with T1 as the reference group. Potential nonlinear associations were assessed using restricted cubic splines with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the CTI distribution. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the median CTI value as the reference for spline analyses.

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and known prognostic importance in HF. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values < 5 considered acceptable. All covariates included in the fully adjusted model had VIF values below this threshold, with a maximum VIF of 2.34, indicating no substantial multicollinearity. Three hierarchical models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, heart rate, and blood pressure. Model 3 was further adjusted for ischaemic cardiomyopathy, atrial fibrillation, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, BNP, haemoglobin, albumin, creatinine, sodium, uric acid, HDL-C, HbA1c, LVEF, and admission medications, including statins, antiplatelet agents, aldosterone antagonists, β-blockers, diuretics, ACEI/ARB/ARNI, digoxin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and insulin.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. A 30-day landmark analysis was conducted to reduce the potential influence of early post-discharge events. Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were used for CV death, with non-CV death treated as a competing event. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, BMI, LVEF, hypertension, and glucose metabolism status, with interaction terms used to assess effect modification.

As secondary analyses, the incremental prognostic value of CTI was assessed by adding CTI to the fully adjusted clinical model and comparing nested models incorporating TyG, CRP, or TyG + CRP. Model performance was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, continuous net reclassification improvement, integrated discrimination improvement, 1-year calibration plots, Brier scores, and 1-year decision curve analysis. Time-dependent AUCs were estimated using the timeROC package with marginal inverse probability of censoring weighting. Internal validation and uncertainty estimation were performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected C-index, NRI, IDI, and decision curves, with 95% confidence intervals calculated from the bootstrap distribution.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics of the study population

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort stratified by CTI tertiles are shown in Table 1. The median follow-up duration was 498 days. The median age was 71 years, and 799 patients (53.48%) were male. Across increasing CTI tertiles, patients were slightly younger and had higher proportions of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and diabetes mellitus, whereas atrial fibrillation was less frequent. Higher CTI tertiles were also associated with higher levels of WBC, CRP, FPG, HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, uric acid, creatinine, and TyG, and with lower HDL-C, sodium, and albumin levels. The distribution of glucose metabolism status differed significantly across CTI tertiles, with a higher proportion of diabetes mellitus in the highest tertile. BMI, BNP, and LVEF did not differ significantly across tertiles, whereas LAD differed significantly. Regarding medications at admission, use of antiplatelet agents, SGLT2 inhibitors, and insulin was more frequent in higher CTI tertiles, whereas digoxin use was less frequent.

Predictive value of CTI for adverse outcomes

During follow-up, 281 patients (18.8%) died from any cause, 179 (12.0%) experienced CV death, and 590 (39.5%) developed MACCEs. Cumulative event rates increased across CTI tertiles. Kaplan–Meier analyses showed lower event-free survival with increasing CTI tertiles for all-cause mortality, CV death, and MACCEs (log-rank P < 0.001 for all comparisons; Fig. 2).

Kaplan–Meier curves for clinical outcomes according to CTI tertiles. Kaplan–Meier event-free survival curves for (A) all-cause mortality, (B) cardiovascular death, and (C) MACCEs according to CTI tertiles. P values were calculated using the log-rank test. Abbreviations: CTI, C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index; MACCEs, major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events

In Cox regression analyses, CTI was associated with all three endpoints (Table 2). When modelled as a continuous variable, each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.29–1.80; P < 0.001), CV death (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.24–1.84; P < 0.001), and MACCEs (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.26–1.59; P < 0.001) in the fully adjusted model. When CTI was analysed by tertiles, compared with T1, the fully adjusted HRs for T2 and T3 were 1.38 (95% CI 1.01–1.90; P = 0.048) and 1.86 (95% CI 1.34–2.59; P < 0.001) for all-cause mortality, 1.41 (95% CI 0.93–2.14; P = 0.105) and 2.17 (95% CI 1.45–3.24; P < 0.001) for CV death, and 1.46 (95% CI 1.17–1.82; P < 0.001) and 1.83 (95% CI 1.46–2.28; P < 0.001) for MACCEs, respectively. Tests for trend across CTI tertiles were significant for all endpoints (all P for trend < 0.001).

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

In Fine–Gray competing-risk models accounting for non-CV death as a competing event, the highest CTI tertile remained associated with CV death compared with the lowest tertile in the fully adjusted model (sHR 2.13, 95% CI 1.40–3.24; P < 0.001). The association for the middle tertile did not reach statistical significance (Table S4).

In the 30-day landmark analysis, CTI remained associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.17–1.68), CV death (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.17–1.82), and MACCEs (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50) per 1-SD increase (all P ≤ 0.001; Table S5).

Comparative analysis of CTI prognostic performance with TyG and CRP

Parallel analyses were performed for TyG and CRP. In the fully adjusted models, TyG as a continuous variable was associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.71), CV death (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.19–2.04), and MACCEs (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.23–1.54; all P ≤ 0.004). When TyG was analysed by tertiles, the highest tertile was associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality (HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.25–2.37), CV death (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.40–3.17), and MACCEs (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.18–1.85) compared with the lowest tertile. Detailed results for TyG are shown in Table S1.

Associations between CRP and clinical outcomes were attenuated after full adjustment, and several associations were no longer statistically significant. Detailed results for CRP are shown in Table S2. Compared with models incorporating TyG, CRP, or TyG + CRP, the CTI-augmented model showed more consistent incremental prognostic performance across endpoints.

Discussion

In this single-centre cohort of patients hospitalised with ADHF, elevated CTI values were independently associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, CV death, and MACCEs. Over a median follow-up of 498 days, 18.8% of patients died from any cause, 12.0% died from cardiovascular causes, and 39.5% experienced MACCEs, underscoring the high residual risk that persists after ADHF hospitalisation. After comprehensive adjustment for demographics, cardiometabolic comorbidities, echocardiographic parameters, BNP, renal function, and admission pharmacotherapy, each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with a 52% higher risk of all-cause mortality, a 51% higher risk of CV death, and a 42% higher risk of MACCEs. A clear dose-response relationship was observed across CTI tertiles, and RCS analyses supported an approximately linear association across the observed CTI range. Patients in higher CTI tertiles exhibited a greater burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities and were more frequently treated at admission with insulin, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, and antiplatelet agents. Despite this, event rates remained higher in this subgroup, suggesting that CTI may identify a residual metabolic–inflammatory risk profile not fully captured by conventional clinical variables and admission therapies. Nevertheless, given the observational design, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as lifestyle factors, treatment adherence, or post-discharge therapy modification, cannot be excluded, and causal inference requires confirmation in prospective studies.

Systemic inflammation and IR are increasingly recognised as important contributors to HF progression and adverse outcomes, particularly in patients with cardiometabolic comorbidities [24–27]. Elevated CRP and other inflammatory biomarkers have been associated with increased mortality and rehospitalisation in both acute and chronic HF, including ADHF [11, 12, 28]. Composite inflammatory indices, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, and aggregate index of systemic inflammation, have also shown prognostic value in HF and ADHF [8, 12, 13, 29–32]. In parallel, the TyG index has emerged as a simple and reproducible surrogate of IR and has been associated with incident HF, HF hospitalisation, major cardiovascular events, and mortality across general and high-risk cardiometabolic populations, including patients with ADHF [17, 33–36]. These observations support a pathophysiological framework in which low-grade inflammation and IR may interact to contribute to adverse myocardial remodelling, endothelial dysfunction, neurohormonal activation, and microvascular impairment in HF. However, when assessed in isolation, these markers may only partially reflect the complex metabolic–inflammatory milieu underlying HF progression [17, 24–27, 36–38].

The present findings extend this body of literature by showing that CTI, which integrates inflammatory and insulin-resistance components, was consistently associated with adverse outcomes in hospitalised patients with ADHF. Although TyG was also independently associated with adverse outcomes, CTI showed more consistent associations across modelling strategies and modestly improved incremental prognostic performance compared with TyG, CRP, or their simple combination. Notably, the highest CTI tertile was associated with more than a twofold higher risk of CV death compared with the lowest tertile, whereas the middle tertile showed a more modest and non-significant association. This pattern suggests that excess CV mortality may be concentrated among patients with markedly elevated CTI, although the overall exposure–response relationship appeared approximately linear in spline analyses. The prognostic association of CTI remained consistent across prespecified subgroups, including patients with and without DM, indicating that CTI may capture metabolic–inflammatory risk beyond conventional glucose-metabolism categories. By identifying patients with a higher metabolic–inflammatory risk profile within a clinically heterogeneous ADHF population, CTI may provide complementary information for risk stratification. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in independent cohorts before CTI can be used to inform clinical decision-making.

Several interrelated pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the observed associations between elevated CTI and adverse outcomes in ADHF. Systemic inflammation and IR are closely linked biological processes. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and acute-phase reactants can impair insulin receptor signalling and promote adipose tissue lipolysis, thereby increasing circulating free fatty acid flux. Excess lipid flux may further contribute to mitochondrial oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and activation of innate immune pathways [24–27, 39]. In ADHF, acute haemodynamic deterioration and neurohormonal activation may intensify these processes through tissue hypoperfusion, venous congestion, and end-organ injury [1, 24, 25, 40, 41]. At the myocardial level, IR may reduce cardiomyocyte glucose uptake and shift substrate utilisation towards less oxygen-efficient fatty-acid oxidation, whereas inflammatory mediators may contribute to contractile dysfunction, myocardial stiffness, and electrical instability [24–27, 39]. Systemically, endothelial activation, microvascular dysfunction, renal impairment, electrolyte imbalance, and heightened neurohormonal activity may further increase vulnerability to adverse events. Within this framework, an elevated CTI at admission may serve as a readily obtainable marker of heightened metabolic–inflammatory stress associated with subsequent fatal and non-fatal events. The persistence of significant associations in competing-risk models and 30-day landmark analyses suggests that CTI may reflect sustained biological vulnerability beyond transient haemodynamic instability during the index hospitalisation.

Beyond confirming independent associations, the present study demonstrates that CTI provides modest but statistically significant incremental prognostic information beyond related metabolic and inflammatory markers (TyG and CRP), their simple combination, and a comprehensive clinical risk model that included established heart failure prognostic variables such as BNP, LVEF, renal function, haemoglobin, albumin, sodium, comorbidities, and admission pharmacotherapy. Incorporation of CTI into the fully adjusted base model increased the optimism-corrected C-index from 0.665 to 0.672 for all-cause mortality, from 0.697 to 0.705 for CV death, and from 0.644 to 0.652 for MACCEs. Although the absolute gains in discrimination were modest, they were accompanied by improvements in continuous NRI, IDI, and time-dependent AUCs. Such increments are common when novel biomarkers are added to clinically enriched multivariable models, in which large increases in the C-statistic are uncommon. Accordingly, the observed improvements should be interpreted as complementary refinements in risk stratification rather than transformative enhancements in model performance. DCA at 1 year demonstrated a small increase in net clinical benefit for all-cause mortality and CV death after CTI addition, whereas net benefit for MACCEs was comparable to that of the base model. These results suggest potential clinical relevance for CTI as an adjunctive risk marker rather than as a stand-alone prediction tool. External validation in independent cohorts and prospective studies is essential to confirm reproducibility, generalisability, and the potential role of CTI in risk-guided evaluation.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that CTI may help identify ADHF patients with a higher metabolic–inflammatory risk profile. This information may be particularly relevant for refined risk assessment in patients with intermediate baseline risk, although the present study does not establish that CTI-guided management improves outcomes. The association between higher CTI tertiles and adverse outcomes, despite more frequent baseline use of cardiometabolic therapies, suggests a residual metabolic–inflammatory burden that warrants further investigation. Future prospective studies should determine whether patients with elevated CTI benefit from closer monitoring, more intensive optimisation of guideline-directed medical therapy, or interventions targeting metabolic–inflammatory pathways. In addition, serial CTI measurements during hospitalisation and after discharge may clarify whether dynamic changes in CTI provide prognostic information beyond a single baseline value.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-centre, retrospective observational design limits the generalisability of findings to broader populations and precludes definitive causal inference. Although extensive multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses, including competing-risk and landmark approaches, were performed, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely captured variables—such as socioeconomic factors, physical activity, nutritional status, detailed post-discharge therapy changes, medication adherence, or evolving comorbidities—cannot be fully excluded. Second, CTI and its components were assessed at a single time point during the index hospitalisation, precluding evaluation of temporal changes and post-discharge CTI trajectories in relation to recovery or recurrent decompensation. Third, detailed longitudinal data regarding pharmacotherapy, including up-titration, discontinuation, or long-term adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy, were not systematically captured beyond admission and may have influenced the observed associations between baseline CTI and long-term outcomes. Fourth, comparative analyses were limited to TyG, CRP, and their simple combination; other established or emerging inflammatory and metabolic indices were not evaluated, and these comparisons should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Fifth, the weighting coefficient for CRP in the CTI formula was originally derived from an oncology population rather than a heart failure cohort. Although the present study evaluated the prognostic relevance of this established CTI formula in ADHF, whether this weighting is optimal for patients with heart failure remains uncertain. Future studies should assess whether recalibrated or heart failure-specific CTI formulations provide superior prognostic performance. Sixth, although competing-risk analysis was performed for CV mortality, residual competing-risk issues may remain for non-fatal composite outcomes. Finally, external validation in an independent ADHF cohort was not performed, underscoring the need for prospective, multicentre studies across diverse populations and healthcare systems. Despite these limitations, the consistency of associations across multiple modelling strategies and sensitivity analyses supports further evaluation of CTI as a simple and readily obtainable marker of metabolic–inflammatory risk in hospitalised patients with ADHF.

Conclusions

Elevated CTI levels were independently associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, CV death, and MACCEs in patients hospitalised with ADHF, with broadly consistent associations across glucose-metabolism subgroups. Incorporation of CTI into a comprehensive clinical model yielded modest but statistically significant incremental prognostic value beyond established clinical predictors and related metabolic or inflammatory markers evaluated in this study. These findings suggest that CTI may serve as a simple adjunctive marker for metabolic–inflammatory risk profiling and refined risk stratification in ADHF. Further external validation is needed before routine clinical implementation can be recommended.
