Minimal Stiffness After Rotator Cuff Repair With Bioinductive Collagen Implants
Authors: Brandon D. Bushnell, Brendan T. Jarvis, Raymond C. Jarvis, Christopher P. Piller, Robert S. Baudier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Bioinductive collagen implants (BCIs) have been growing in popularity for use in rotator cuff repair (RCR) over the past several years, but recent literature has raised concerns about the implants contributing to postoperative stiffness. The purpose of this study was to investigate the incidence of stiffness over a decade of experience with the BCI.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted of all cases of RCR using a BCI performed between September 2014 and December 2023. The primary outcome measure was postoperative range of motion, with significant stiffness defined by parameters in the existing literature. The secondary outcome measure was any revision procedure for stiffness.Results:After application of inclusion and exclusion criteria to 522 cases of RCR, there were 432 cases (390 individual patients) available for outcome analysis with an average follow-up of 34.9 months (range, 6 months to 9.25 years). There were only 12 cases (2.8%) of significant postoperative stiffness. All of them required additional operative intervention for stiffness, and all but two patients had at least one risk factor for stiffness. Stiffness rates were 4 of 291 (1.4%) for full-thickness tears and 8 of 141 (5.7%) for partial-thickness tears (P = 0.0149).Conclusion:This study, the largest single cohort to date analyzing BCIs in RCR, found a low incidence of significant postoperative stiffness in cases associated with the use of the implant. Stiffness rates were markedly higher for repairs of partial-thickness tears. To further improve understanding of postoperative stiffness after RCR with BCI, better definitions and prospective comparative studies across larger groups are needed.Level of Evidence:Level IV, retrospective cohort with no comparison group.

Tendon healing in rotator cuff repair (RCR) remains a challenge, even as the orthopaedic community perfects surgical techniques and develops biological supplementation methods for improving healing rates. The past several years have seen increased clinical implementation of a non–load-bearing bioinductive collagen implant (BCI) as either a stand-alone method of RCR or as a supplement to conventional repair techniques.1-18 Several studies in the literature have shown promising clinical results with use of this technology for both partial-thickness and full-thickness tears of the rotator cuff. Although clinical experience has not shown the implant to cause the robust immune response and pseudoseptic reaction problems that characterized many prior structural “patch” options,19,20 some recent studies have raised legitimate concerns about the problem of postoperative stiffness.21-23 The purpose of this study was to review 9 years of clinical experience with the BCI and determine rates of stiffness as well as other complications. It was our hypothesis that the observed stiffness rates would be lower than those recently reported.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective cohort study reviewed and approved by the WCG IRB (Protocol 20226688). Records were obtained and reviewed for all patients who underwent RCR involving a REGENETEN bovine BCI (Smith + Nephew) in our practice from its first use in September 2014 through December 2023. Two surgeons at our institution used the implant during this time and participated in the conduction of the entire study. Cases coded and billed as arthroscopic RCR using Current Procedural Terminology Code 29827 were the primary focus. To maximize data capture, cases were also identified and cross-checked using a combination of hospital billing records, quality assurance records, hospital implant records, manufacturer sales records, operating room (OR) posting records from clinic, and handwritten OR schedule logs. Patient demographic data were recorded and then relevant patient-specific information was deidentified. Clinical notes and surgical reports were reviewed as necessary to determine relevant surgical information for purposes of inclusion and exclusion criteria, surgical indications, surgical details, and outcomes through clinical follow-up. Records of cases that met inclusion criteria were then reviewed for maximum clinical follow-up and relevant clinical data. Because of its retrospective nature, the study was unable to include cross-sectional imaging data for all patients (magnetic resonance imaging/MRI or CT/CT). Furthermore, several patients lacked formal patient-reported-outcome scores, so they were not included in this review.

Inclusion criteria were (1) patient age older than 18 years, (2) arthroscopic RCR, (3) use of a BCI, and (4) a minimum of 6-month clinical follow-up. Cases involving BCI use in other body parts (knee, elbow, hip, ankle, etc.) were not included, nor were cases involving total shoulder arthroplasty. Some cases were initially placed into the study cohort because they were coded in the system as arthroscopic RCR (CPT 29827), but were excluded based on our formal exclusion criteria (Table 1) which included (1) open or mini-open RCR, (2) concomitant labral repair, (3) concomitant os acromiale procedure, (4) concomitant use of a subacromial balloon spacer device, and (5) less than 6-month clinical follow-up.

The primary outcome for this study was postoperative range of motion (ROM). Precise parameters for significant stiffness have been inconsistently defined within the literature.24 For this study, reference parameters were drawn from recent publications focusing specifically on RCR with BCI and supported by other, older sources.5,6,22,24,25 “Significant stiffness” was defined clinically as a loss of passive ROM in at least two planes, with cutoffs of 120° of forward elevation, 30° of external rotation, and internal rotation to the buttock.22 ROM measurements were completed as part of the standard postoperative clinical evaluation, performed by the senior surgeon or a physician assistant using estimation of ROM. A goniometer is not routinely used in our practice because it has been shown to have no relationship with patient-reported outcomes after RCR.26 Patients with limited active ROM, but still with well-preserved passive ROM, were not classified as having postoperative stiffness. Preoperative passive ROM and postoperative contralateral passive ROM were not analyzed on all patients, but these factors were analyzed secondarily for those patients identified as having postoperative stiffness.

The secondary outcome measure was any revision procedure on the index shoulder with stiffness as the primary indication. This included arthroscopy with lysis of adhesions (LOA) as well as isolated manipulation under anesthesia (MUA). The third outcome measure was any failure—defined broadly as any additional surgery on the index shoulder either completed or offered; stiffness as defined above; radiographic, ultrasonography, CT, or MRI evidence of cuff repair failure and/or cuff arthropathy; or diagnosis of deep infection. Reasons for failure and/or indications for secondary surgery were recorded. Patients were still included for the secondary and tertiary outcome measures even if surgical intervention or failure occurred before 6 months of clinical follow-up, as the clinical decision to intervene for stiffness or failure is often made at the point of diagnosis.

For patients meeting the criteria for stiffness or undergoing revision surgery for stiffness, additional chart review was conducted to identify the presence of any conditions that have previously been described in the literature to predispose patients to postoperative stiffness, including bariatric surgery, diabetes, preoperative stiffness, reflux, tobacco abuse, revision surgery, thyroid disease, or workers’ compensation status.24,27-33

Surgical Technique and Clinical Follow-Up

Given the retrospective study design, exact surgical algorithms were not standardized for all patients. Surgeries were performed under general anesthesia and involved a preoperative interscalene block. Diagnostic arthroscopy was performed in all cases, with concomitant pathology addressed as indicated. Additional procedures consistently performed included débridement, chondroplasty, synovectomy, subacromial decompression, and subacromial bursectomy. Most cases involved distal clavicle excision and a long head biceps tendon procedure (either tenotomy or tenodesis).

Rotator cuff tears were characterized during diagnostic arthroscopy and then repaired based on tear pattern and surgeon preference. Full-thickness tears were repaired using conventional techniques and influenced by the characteristics of each particular tear. BCI was used as a supplement to primary repair in all cases of full-thickness tears. Partial-thickness tears were treated either by take-down and repair with subsequent supplemental BCI, transtendinous repair with subsequent supplemental BCI, or isolated bioinductive repair with the BCI.8,14 The BCI was inserted using manufacturer’s recommended technique as described previously in the literature.14

Patients were followed postoperatively according to the standard surgical follow-up interval routine of 1 to 4 days, 2 to 3 weeks, 6 to 8 weeks, 12 to 14 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year. Several patients had longer follow-up intervals because of requested re-evaluation, participation in other research studies, the occurrence of complications or continued/recurrent pain, or visits to the clinic for other problems or procedures.

Rehabilitation

Customary standardized rehabilitation programs were prescribed in each case based on surgical procedures performed, with very few exceptions—usually because of patient noncompliance. Patients with full-thickness tears were immobilized for 4 weeks in a sling, during which time they were allowed limited pendulum exercise and PROM only below 90°. Active ROM and full PROM began at 4 weeks and then progressed to strengthening at 12 weeks. Patients with partial-thickness tears followed the manufacturer’s recommended protocol for rehabilitation, including a discontinuation of the sling and rapid progression of passive and active ROM within 1 week after operation, with strengthening beginning at 6 to 8 weeks.13

Statistical Analysis

This study was descriptive only and had no comparison or control group for the primary, secondary, or tertiary outcome measurements. A subgroup analysis was performed for statistical significance on the relative stiffness rates for patients with full-thickness tears versus partial-thickness tears using a standard chi-squared technique, with P value set for significance below 0.05.

Results

A total of 522 cases (468 individual patients) were initially screened for analysis based on use of a BCI in the shoulder for arthroscopic RCR. The time frame for screening began with our initial use of the implant in September 2014 and ran through December 2023. There were 216 female and 252 male patients with an average age of 57.2 years (range, 17.7 to 82.6). The average length of follow-up was 30.2 months (range, 1 month to 9.25 years). There were 192 left shoulders and 330 right shoulders.

Ninety cases (78 patients) were excluded based on our exclusion criteria (Table 1), leaving 432 cases (390 individual patients) in the cohort for outcome analysis. There were 177 female and 255 male patients with an average age of 57.3 years (range, 30.3 to 82.6). The average length of follow-up was 34.9 months (range, 6 months to 9.25 years). There were 162 left shoulders and 270 right shoulders. There were 291 cases involving full-thickness tears and 141 cases involving partial-thickness tears. Of the partial-thickness tears, 113 (80%) underwent IBR, 21 (15%) underwent tear take-down and repair, and 7 (5%) underwent in situ transtendinous repair.

There were only 12 cases (2.8%) of significant postoperative stiffness (Table 2), with all of them offered surgical intervention (11 arthroscopic LOA, one MUA). Ten of the 12 patients proceeded with the recommended intervention (nine LOA, one MUA). Of the two patients who declined the recommended intervention (both were offered LOA), one was managed conservatively, and one pursued additional intervention elsewhere because of scheduling delays related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Of the 12 patients with stiffness, all but two patients had at least one risk factor for stiffness (bariatric surgery, diabetes, preoperative stiffness, reflux, revision surgery, tobacco abuse, thyroid disease, and/or workers’ compensation status). None of these patients had contralateral shoulder stiffness.

Stiffness rates were 4 of 291 (1.4%) for full-thickness tears and 8 of 141 (5.7%) for partial-thickness tears. This difference was statistically significant (P = 0.0149). Within the 12 cases of failure because of stiffness, 66% (8/12) were cases involving partial-thickness tears. There was no statistically significant difference in stiffness rates between the type of partial-thickness repair technique performed.

There were 78 cases of all-cause failure (18.1%) identified in our review. Within this group, specific causes of failure included trauma or noncompliance (34/7.9%), failure to heal (17/3.9%), stiffness (12/2.8%), infection (6/1.5%), hematoma (3/0.7%), progression of osteoarthritis (without stiffness) requiring surgery (3/0.7%), and painful bursitis (without stiffness) requiring surgery (2/0.5%).

Discussion

Shoulder stiffness remains one of the most common postoperative complications after arthroscopic RCR, and it can lead to poor quality of life and poor patient satisfaction.24,27,34 In the literature, however, the true incidence of shoulder stiffness after surgery is unknown because nomenclature and parameters remain inconsistent—and thus a true operational definition for stiffness is persistently elusive. This lack of consistency is demonstrated by a systematic literature review which revealed 16 distinct definitions of shoulder stiffness.24 These definitions include inconsistent planes of ROM, varying combination of ROM restrictions, different patient outcome measurement tools, and contrasting postoperative evaluation periods for when stiffness can be attributed to surgery.

This lack of consistency in definition severely limits a surgeon’s ability to assess how new and emerging technology may influence the incidence of stiffness after surgery. This ability is especially important as the focus of RCR research shifts away from mechanical repair toward biological support and enhancement, in which the goal of tissue augmentation is to improve the healing response. For example, several case series have been published on the BCI evaluated in this study and have consistently reported postoperative stiffness under 2%,7-11,14 with multiple recent comparative studies reporting no difference in postoperative stiffness between a BCI group and a control group.1-6 These results stand in considerable contrast to those from a recent, small cohort study by Yeazell et al22 in which 25% of BCI cases experienced “significant stiffness.” Comparison between studies is difficult because these studies all had different definitions of stiffness and different methods of measuring and reporting ROM. The definition used in this study was identical to the definition used by Yeazell et al, outlined as loss of passive ROM in at least two planes, with cutoffs of 120° of forward elevation, 30° of external rotation, and internal rotation to the buttock. This definition is clinically beneficial as it is concise, composed of routine clinical examination elements, and does not require a goniometer. Precise goniometer measurements are rarely used in day-to-day clinical practice, and they have been shown not to correlate with patient-reported outcomes.26 However, the current definition is also more aggressive than that used in other studies, in which stiffness cutoffs have been set as low as 90° of forward elevation and 10° of external rotation. Using Yeazell’s more aggressive definition of “stiffness” allows us to capture more patients that might otherwise be labeled in other studies as “not stiff”—making our results more sensitive to problems potentially created by the implant.

In addition to specific restriction measurements in ROM, the definition of postoperative stiffness typically includes a time component. The onset of postoperative stiffness typically peaks at 3 months after RCR and can be “transient” or “resistive,” based on the presence or absence of a response to nonsurgical treatment (respectively).24,27 However, stiffness occurring after a period of normal ROM may not be related to the primary surgery—a distinction that is rarely made in the literature.29 Patients in this study were required to have a minimum follow-up time of 6 months to allow for rehabilitation progression. Patients who presented before 6 months with delays in progression or limitations in ROM would be offered an escalation in care, including aggressive rehabilitation and/or surgical intervention. Six months is slightly longer that the “peak” incidence of postoperative stiffness; however, previous research has shown distinguishable remnants of the BCI to remain at 3 months after implantation,8,14,15,18 which could theoretically still cause or contribute to stiffness until full implant absorption by 6 months.4,15 In this study, therefore, a minimum follow-up time of 6 months was established as an acceptable time frame to fully evaluate stiffness over the expected lifespan of the BCI.

In addition to highlighting the need for a more consistent, well-accepted definition of postoperative stiffness, comparison between studies also raises specific concerns around confounding variables, such as postoperative rehabilitation and systemic medical comorbidities, both of which may influence the incidence of stiffness after surgery. Aligning with previously published rehabilitation recommendations, patients in the current study with partial thickness tears underwent an accelerated rehabilitation, with recommendations for patients to remove the sling within 1 week.13 Comparatively, Yeazell et al35 maintained patients in a sling for up to 7 weeks after surgery, and later agreed that this prolonged period of immobility could have contributed to the rates they observed. While controversy remains regarding the best rehabilitation protocol, the results of this study do seem to support the idea that a more aggressive time frame of sling discontinuation and ROM exercises can decrease the risk of stiffness in cases where the BCI is used. Future studies should directly analyze rehabilitation “aggressiveness” as an independent factor for stiffness and other outcome measures.

In addition to rehabilitation considerations, this study reported that nearly all patients presenting with postoperative stiffness also had at least one comorbidity that has been identified to predispose patients to suboptimal postoperative ROM. These include systemic risk factors such as diabetes, thyroid disease, gastroesophageal reflux, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid or inflammatory arthritis, and smoking, among others.24,27-33 These risk factors in theory could also be extended to include concomitant procedures, such as long-head biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, although previous studies have shown that these procedures do not have an impact on outcomes.7,10,22,35 Given the large number of comorbidities (Table 2) and concomitant biceps procedures performed across our patient population, one would expect the stiffness rate to be much higher if indeed the BCI was an independent risk factor for the development of postoperative stiffness. Instead, there were only two cases of stiffness out of the entire cohort (0.005%) in which no other predisposing conditions were identified.

One of the more striking findings of this study was the statistically significant difference in stiffness rates between cases involving a full-thickness rotator cuff tear versus cases with a partial-thickness tear. The partial-thickness cases had a stiffness rate over four times that of the full-thickness cases. Although an aggressive postoperative rehabilitation protocol was prescribed for the partial-thickness cases, the occurrence of stiffness was still observed at a higher rate. Previous studies about use of the BCI have likewise shown higher rates of stiffness in cases of partial-thickness tears compared with full-thickness tears.7-11,13,14,17 This appears to be a problem innate to partial-thickness tears in general, however, as several studies have also identified this issue even in cases without use of a BCI.27,36 Take-down and repair, transtendinous repairs, and even nonsurgical treatments have been shown to have stiffness rates higher than those for full-thickness tear repairs.29,30,36-39 Understanding of this phenomenon and further development of rehabilitation protocols is needed.

Although it was not the primary purpose of this study to evaluate the overall complication rate from RCR surgery with BCI, nor to do a subgroup analysis of other specific complications, there is some utility in comparing the overall complication data of this cohort to the literature. A previous publication by Felsch et al,34 was chosen as the primary comparator. This was a high-quality study evaluating a single-center, consecutive patient cohort from an on-site registry for RCR. This article seemed ideal for comparison because of the clearly defined complication classifications and severities, as well as the grouping of complications based on anatomy and/or etiology. The overall population and single-center cohort design likewise seemed analogous to that of this study. The specificity and transparency in complication reporting by Felsch et al, addressed a common limitation cited in systematic reviews, in which inconsistencies in complication reporting often make comparison impossible (similar to the previously discussed difficulties in defining stiffness). Similar complication rates were seen between this study and the results of Felsch et al—indicating an apparent minimal negative impact of the BCI across multiple complication categories. This study had several limitations. First of all, its retrospective nature limited the use of imaging studies and patient-reported outcome measures as part of the study design. Although imaging is clearly important in evaluating tendon healing, it is not necessarily relevant as a means of assessing stiffness. Outcome scores are likewise important for an overall assessment of clinical success and patient satisfaction, but multiple studies have generally shown good correlation between lower outcome scores and significant stiffness. As such, it was felt that “significant stiffness” (as defined above) and the need for additional procedures to treat stiffness can effectively serve as acceptable surrogates for more sophisticated outcome scoring systems in this case. Another limitation was the lack of formal measurements of ROM with a goniometer by a single, consistent examiner. As discussed above, postoperative shoulder stiffness lacks a universal definition across the literature, so the same methods and criteria used by a recent cohort study specific to BCI in RCR were also used herein.22,24,27 Third, this study did not control for tear size, chronicity, length of time to surgery, preoperative or contralateral shoulder stiffness, surgical technique, number of anchors, or exact details of rehabilitation. The retrospective nature of this review precluded any strict standardization of these obviously important factors. Although the study’s population represents a heterogeneous “mixed bag” of cases, it also represents a “real-life” practice experience applicable to many other practices around the world. Strengths of this study include its large size (432 patients), extended length of clinical follow-up (minimum of 6 months; average of almost 3 years; maximum of 9.25 years), and consistency in the technique established by most cases coming from the senior author.

Conclusion

Stiffness does not appear to be a notable complication in the common clinical use of a BCI for RCR. Cases involving use of the implant in repair of partial-thickness tears, however, do seem to be at higher risk of developing stiffness in comparison with repairs of full-thickness tears. Future research should seek to provide a consistent definition of stiffness and conduct comparative prospective study of factors that may contribute to the development of stiffness after the use of a BCI in RCR.
