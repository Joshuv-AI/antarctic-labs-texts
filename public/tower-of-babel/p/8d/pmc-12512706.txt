Complicated pharyngocutaneous fistula repair after head and neck cancer surgery: a case series of 7 patients
Authors: Weihua Zhou, Huajun Feng, Sai Liang, Shengen Xu, Zhuoping Liang, Yuanyuan Wang, Gang Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo retrospectively analyze the repair of seven cases of complicated pharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF) to provide a reference for the treatment of complicated PCF.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of seven patients with complicated PCF from February 2017 to December 2021, including six males and one female, with a history of neck radiotherapy in two cases. The range of PCF mucosal defects was 1.0 cm × 2.0–5.0 cm × 7.0 cm. A pedicled flap or free flap was chosen for repair depending on the site and size of the defect. A total of eight flaps were used to repair complicated PCF defects.ResultsOne of the seven patients healed after conservative treatment. Among the five patients who underwent pedicled flap repair, three had first-intention healing, one had second-intention healing, and one had tumor recurrence with unhealed PCF. Free flap repair was performed in one case, which had first-intention healing.ConclusionEarly, prompt, and appropriate treatment can prevent a PCF from progressing to complicated PCF. The repair of the complicated PCF needs to be individualized according to the patient’s local defect and the surgeon’s clinical experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03223-x.

Introduction

Pharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF), is a common serious complication after pharyngeal tumor surgery: its overall incidence is approximately 14.3–27.6%, but approximately 22.4–31.4% in patients underwent preoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy [1, 2]. Once PCF occurs, it seriously affects the quality of life of the patient, prolongs the patient’s hospital stay, delays the subsequent adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy, increases the economic burden, and even invades the common carotid artery, resulting in its rupture and fatal hemorrhage [3–5]. At present, the cause of PCF is still unclear. It may be related to tumor staging, tracheotomy, cervical lymph node dissection, early oral feeding, preoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, hypoproteinemia, anemia, diabetes, and other factors [6–8]. Treatment of PCF can be conservative of surgical. Once conservative treatment fails or the large blood vessels of the neck are exposed, surgical repair is the only treatment option [3, 9].

There is no unified, standard, optimal method for the surgical treatment of PCF, especially for the surgical treatment of complicated PCF. The patient’s general condition, PCF site, fistula size, and neck tissue need to be taken into account comprehensively [10], and no single repair method is suitable for all patients. The repair of complicated PCF is still a difficult issue that cannot be avoided by head and neck surgeons. Previous studies have mostly reported on repair methods, and there are few reports on the selection of the repair plan for complicated PCF. This article summarizes the repair of 7 patients with complicated PCF after head and neck cancer surgery in our department, hoping to provide a reference for the selection of flap repair strategies for patients with complicated PCF after head and neck cancer surgery.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of seven patients with complicated PCF who were admitted to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology/Head and Neck Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, from February 2017 to December 2021. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, and the approval number is [KY2023018]. Patients participating in the study have signed an informed consent form. The specific information of the clinical data of the patients is shown in Table 1. There were eight flaps (six pedicled flaps + two free flaps) for the repair of PCF. Among the seven patients, six were male and one was female, with an age of 53–78 years. Two patients received radiotherapy.

Seven patients developed PCF on postoperative days 3–17, with mucosal defects ranging from 1.0 cm × 2.0 cm to 5.0 cm × 7.0 cm. The time of PCF repair was from day 21 to 86 after the occurrence of the PCF. Except in case 7, who healed by conservative treatment (staged repair and local suturing), the PCF was not closed by conservative treatment.

Treatment methods

Conservative treatment (without transfer flap repair)

PCF usually occurs 1–10 days after surgery, and early detection of PCF is the key to early treatment. In the presence of neck skin congestion and swelling, pressure and pain, subcutaneous twisting sensation, impact sensation when swallowing or coughing, fluctuating sensation when breathing, and foul smell from the wound, the wound should be opened, the fistula opening should be fully exposed, necrotic tissues should be removed, and continuous dressing changes with adequate drainage should be performed. Anti-infective therapy with sensitive antibiotics and systemic supportive therapy should also be included. The need for surgical repair is assessed based on the growth of the granulation, the size of the fistula opening, and the extent of the surrounding skin and mucosal defects. In Case 7, PMMF was used to repair a postoperative defect in the tongue root cancer. Due to timely and adequate exposure and excision of necrotic tissue at the distal end of the flap, the flap survived well, the extent of necrosis was small, and the PCF healed after conservative treatment (Fig. 7E).

Flap transfer repair

Flap transfer repair is used in patients with PCF in whom the above conservative treatments have not been effective. Poorly supplied inactivated tissue and scar tissue were excised to create a fresh wound. Depending on the location and size of the PCF, the length and width of the flap were designed to be approximately 1 cm longer and wider than the defect in order to minimize the tension on the repair flap.

Pedicled flap included the supraclavicular artery island flap (SCAIF) and the PMMF. The SCAIF flap is pliable, incorporates a reliable tip, and does not require microsurgical access, which makes it a versatile and reliable flap option for use in reconstruction of head and neck defects such as cervical, pharyngeal wall, and stoma defects [11]. PMMF is a safe and versatile flap that is widely used for head and neck cancer reconstruction, but carries the risk of complicating oral or pharyngeal cutaneous fistula [12]. In case 1, a SCAIF was used to remove part of the epidermal tissue to form a folded flap and simultaneously repair the neck skin and pharyngeal cavity defects. Flap repair of the skin defect was on the distal side prone to ischemia (which could also be used as a viewing window to observe the flap blood supply) and pharyngeal cavity defect was repaired at the proximal end of the proximal vascular pedicle (Fig. 1B-E). In case 2, the patient underwent total laryngectomy and repair of the left PMMC metastasis at an outside hospital and was transferred to our hospital after the development of postoperative PCF. After the inflammation was controlled, primary repair of SCAIF was performed on the pharyngeal cavity. Two days after surgery, the SCAIF was partially necrotic distally. The patient underwent debridement and dressing change. Secondary repair was performed subsequently, i.e., suturing of SCAIF and pharyngeal mucosa to close the pharyngeal lumen, repair of the skin defect by grafted right PMMF, and successful repair of PCF (Fig. 2C-E). In case 3, ALT was performed to repair the pharyngeal cavity during resection of the primary tumor. On postoperative day 6, the patient developed necrosis secondary to PCF and the entire free flap was removed. After inflammation control, SCAIF was used to repair the pharyngeal lumen defect (Fig. 3D.E). In case 5, the mucosal defect of the pharyngeal cavity was repaired using a one-stage PMMF repair, and the skin defect of the neck was repaired using a two-stage localized bilobed flap(Fig. 5C-F). In case 6, the right PMMF was initially repaired intraoperatively, but examination revealed poor blood supply. Therefore, left PMMF repair was performed (Fig. 6C-E).

Free flap repair includes the radial free forearm flap (RFFF) and the anterolateral thigh flap (ALT). The RFFF was one of the first free tissue graft flaps described, with a thin, pliable flap suitable for reconstruction of fine structures, and a donor site distant from the head and neck resection site allowing simultaneous incision and resection [13]. The ALT is hidden in the donor area, which has little impact on the function and aesthetics of the donor area; it can carry different amounts of skin, muscle, fascia and nerves; the vascularity of the flap is long and thick in caliber, which makes it easy to anastomose; and it is easy to operate and has a high success rate [14]. In case 4, the general condition of the patient was fair. The patient did not receive radiotherapy before surgery. During PCF repair surgery, the right SCAIF was prepared, and the distal blood supply was poor. During the surgery, the right RFFF was used instead to repair the pharyngeal cavity defect, the ALT was used to repair the skin defect of the neck, and the SCAIF was made into a full-thickness skin slice to repair the skin defect on the right forearm (Fig. 4B-E). This case series has been reported in line with the Process Guideline [15].

Results

One of the seven patients healed after conservative treatment (Fig. 7F). One patient had tumor recurrence and an unhealed PCF. Six patients with complicated PCFs were repaired using six pedicled flaps and two free flaps. All six pedicled flaps survived, with three patients healing by first intention (Fig. 2F、Figs. 3F and 6F), one patient healing by second intention (Fig. 1F). Case1 had a SCAIF folded to repair a skin and pharyngeal cavity defect, and who had a recurrence of the PCF above the flap postoperatively, but with a small fistula opening, which healed after conservative treatment (Fig. 1F). Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 shows the treatment process and prognosis of seven patients with complicated PCF.

Tumor recurrence was observed in case 5. A stage I PMMF transfer was performed to repair the pharyngeal cavity. During the operation, a neoplasm of 3.0 cm × 3.0 cm at the base of the tongue in the contralateral pharyngeal cavity of the primary lesion was observed. Pathological examination suggested squamous cell carcinoma. Tumor recurrence was considered. The family members requested a stage II conservative cervical bilobular flap transfer to repair the neck skin defect, after which the PCF recurred. The patient and his family members gave up the treatment and asked for discharge, and he died 23 days after discharge.

One patient underwent free flap repair, achieved first-intention healing (Fig. 4), but the PCF relapsed 1 month after radiotherapy. The fistula orifice above the median flap of the neck was small, approximately 1.0 cm × 1.5 cm, and healed after 4 months of conservative treatment with oral feeding (Fig. 4F).

Typical case (case 2)

The patient was a 58-year-old male who had two partial laryngectomies and radiotherapy for laryngocarcinoma and had thyroid cartilage radionecrosis and hemorrhage after the second radiotherapy. He underwent total laryngectomy, partial hypopharyngeal resection, and left PMMF transfer repair in a local hospital (Fig. 2A). The patient presented with a PCF, hemoptysis in the tracheal tube, and a hematoma on the left anterior chest wall on the 3rd day after surgery (Fig. 2B). The local hospital made three surgical explorations, and symptoms such as PCF and hemoptysis were not controlled. On the 10th day after surgery, he was transferred to our hospital and admitted to our department for emergency chest wall exploration hemostasis + hematoma removal + pharyngeal fistula debridement. Intraoperatively, a 4.0 cm × 4.0 cm fistula opening was observed communicating with the pharyngeal cavity. After postoperative dressing change and systemic symptomatic support, the PCF did not progress further. After 24 days of conservative treatment of the PCF, the infection was well controlled, and the partial necrosis of the transferred left PMMF was removed.

Due to the poor general condition of the patient and the hard scar-like texture of the local neck tissue after multiple rounds of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the risk of postoperative recurrence of PCF was high, so a staged repair was Done. In the primary repair of the pharyngeal cavity, the mucosal defect of the PCF was approximately 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm, and the scar tissue surrounding the internal fistula orifice was cleaned up. An island flap of approximately 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm was designed in the right supraclavicular area (Fig. 2C). The skin and subcutaneous tissue were dissected. The flap was Lifted along the surface of the distal deltoid muscle of the flap to loosen the tissue around the pedicle of the flap, and the flap was transferred to the pharyngeal cavity with a small tension to repair the pharyngeal cavity defect. The skin fistula orifice was filled with gauze, and the skin defect was to be mended by secondary repair. Anti-infection and systemic symptomatic supportive treatment were given after surgery. On postoperative day 2, saliva exudation was observed on the right side above the flap. On postoperative day 4, the distal edge of the SCAIF was slightly necrotic, so the necrotic tissue was removed. On the 9th day after surgery, the right shoulder Donor valve area was sutured. On the 15th day after surgery, the patient had a good blood supply to the SCAIF and the original PMMF. There was good flap survival and controllable neck infection (Fig. 2D).

Because a mucosal fistula orifice of 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm and a skin defect of 11.0 cm × 5.0 cm were visible in the pharyngeal cavity, secondary repair was performed. The pharyngeal cavity was closed by suturing the SCAIF the tongue roots the mucosa of the pharyngeal cavity, and the entrance of the esophagus. A right PMMF (12.0 cm × 6.0 cm) was taken to repair the skin defect (Fig. 2E). The flap survived well after surgery, and there was no recurrence of PCF. One month later, the gastric tube was removed for oral feeding. One year later, the pharyngeal esophageal anastomosis was stenotic. The patient received dilation of pharyngoesophageal anastomotic strictures with oral feeding after surgery (Fig. 2F).

Discussion

The pathogenesis of PCF remains multifactorial, with potential contributors including surgical technique, neoadjuvant chemoradiation, anemia, hypoproteinemia, prophylactic tracheostomy, neck dissection, infection, tumor stage and extent, and systemic underlying diseases such as diabetes, liver diseases, and pulmonary infections [6, 16]. Notably, preoperative radiotherapy induces cervical tissue fibrosis, scar contracture, and microvascular endothelial damage leading to luminal stenosis/occlusion, constituting critical risk factors for PCF [9, 17]. Case 1 and 2 had Documented histories of cervical radiotherapy. Notably, Case 2 underwent two full-course radiotherapy regimens, which resulted in severe cervical tissue fibrosis with compromised vascularity, ultimately contributing to PCF formation. Case 6 developed PCF secondary to laryngotracheal flap necrosis caused by intraoperative injury to the superior laryngeal vascular pedicle. Case 3 exhibited total free flap necrosis from unresolved vascular crisis at postoperative day 6. Cases 4, 6, and 7 experienced PMMF distal ischemia-induced PCF.

The timely identification, appropriate management, and early intervention are critical for mitigating complications in the treatment of PCF during their initial stages. Early necrotic tissue clearance and drainage optimization minimize tissue loss and accelerate recovery [18]. Delayed recognition exacerbates infective necrosis, increasing therapeutic complexity [19]. Cases 2, 4, and 6 exemplify poor outcomes from delayed intervention, whereas Case 7 achieved healing through prompt conservative measures despite large fistula size.

Conservative treatment is the main treatment for PCF. The effective rate can be as high as 94%. In addition, proper early conservative treatment can reduce the probability that a PCF will develop into a complicated PCF and can promote the early healing of complicated PCF, such as in case 7. However, in complicated refractory PCF where conservative treatment, including pulling sutures together and localized arbitrary flap repair, is often unsuccessful, flap transfer repair is the only potentially effective treatment method [3, 9]. Surgical repair of flaps uses local pedicled flaps or pedicled myocutaneous flaps, such as PMMF, SCAIF [20], thoracic triangular flaps, submental flaps, etc., as well as free flaps such as the RFFF and the ALT [21, 22].

SCAIF is commonly used for intraoperative repair and reconstruction of head and neck tumors [23]. Compared to the deltopectoral flap (DP), the advantages of SCAIF include ease of acquisition, simple preparation, thinness, and flexibility, and it rarely causes pharyngeal stenosis due to muscle flap atrophy [11]. The SCAIF donor site can be directly sutured, with the scar concealed in the supraclavicular fold; it offers significant aesthetic benefits; it can carry sensory nerves, restoring protective sensation; it allows for a large range of motion, eliminating the need for post-operative positional fixation, and provides high patient comfort. The core advantage of DP is its large tissue volume and reliable blood supply, making it suitable for extensive head and neck defects or situations requiring thick tissue coverage to protect major vessels. However, its excessive bulk often requires secondary thinning, the donor site requires skin grafting, leaving noticeable chest wall scars, and it lacks sensory function [24, 25]. The SCAIF surgical technique involves partial epidermal excision to create a de-epithelialized folded flap for simultaneous pharyngeal lumen and cutaneous defect reconstruction [20], Or island flaps can be used to repair the pharyngeal cavity and then combined with other flaps to repair the skin. However, at present, there are few reports on the use of SCAIF for postoperative PCF repair, and the number of cases studied is small. Some foreign scholars have reported successful repair of PCF using a SCAIF in a few cases. In addition, these patients all received radiotherapy and chemotherapy before surgery [26–28]. In our series, three cases were managed with SCAIF reconstruction. Notably, two patients presented with histories of cervical radiotherapy and level II-IV neck dissection, with Case 2 having undergone two full courses of cervical radiation resulting in severe cervical tissue fibrosis. Nevertheless, all SCAIF-repaired fistulae achieved favorable outcomes. These findings suggest that previous neck dissection and radiotherapy exposure should not be considered absolute contraindications for SCAIF utilization in PCF management [11, 29]. For patients who have undergone level V neck dissection, the SCAIF is not considered. In cases involving level III-IV neck dissection, we meticulously dissect and preserve the transverse cervical vessels and external jugular vein during surgery unless there is tumor invasion, in which case they are resected. For patients with a history of level III-IV neck dissection, we perform angiography or ultrasound Doppler examination to confirm the presence of transverse cervical vessels before preparing the SCAIF. During flap preparing, the light transmission test can be used to assess perforator vessels-a simple and practical technique, as previously described in our published articles on the SCAIF, which detail the surgical steps [30]. Additionally, for any flap preparing procedure, we always have a backup plan in case of flap failure.

The PMMF is currently the most widely used and effective myocutaneous flap for the repair of PCF [29]. It has the advantages of simple preparation, reliable blood supply, providing enough tissue volume to fill the dead space, and a good variety of flap designs. However, this flap is relatively bloated, has poor peristalsis, and is prone to cause muscle atrophy after radiotherapy and chemotherapy, resulting in pharyngeal cavity stenosis and anastomotic scar stenosis that hinder swallowing. Therefore, in pharyngeal cavity repair, a thinner and simpler SCAIF is a better choice than the PMMF. However, for large PCF neck defects that need to eliminate dead space and cover and protect important blood vessels and other structures, a PMMF that can provide abundant tissue volume and has a guaranteed blood supply is superior to a SCAIF.

Contingency planning is essential in flap reconstruction surgery, particularly for two critical scenarios: (1) intraoperative discovery of suboptimal flap perfusion necessitating alternative flap selection, and (2) postoperative complications including flap necrosis, recurrent pharyngocutaneous fistula, or tumor recurrence requiring secondary reconstruction. Based on our clinical experience, for patients with poor general condition or poor neck tissue conditions following chemotherapy and radiotherapy, SCAIF is a suitable option due to its simple harvesting process, thinness, and flexibility. PMMF is retained as an alternative option due to its reliable PCF repair capability, sufficient tissue volume, and the need for advanced microsurgical anastomosis techniques for free flaps. For patients with good overall and neck conditions, free flaps can be selected. Simple and reliable pedicled flaps are reserved as a backup option to address situations such as failure of free flap reconstruction, recurrent fistulas, or tumor recurrence.

The repair of PCF advocates the simultaneous repair of pharyngeal cavity defects and skin defects [31, 32]. However, for patients with complicated PCF accompanied by compromised systemic status, prior radiotherapy, severe cervical fibrosis, impaired cervical tissue perfusion, large fistulas, advanced malignancies, multiple comorbidities, or high risks of fistula recurrence and flap necrosis, our experience is that staged repair may represent a viable option. Phase I focuses on pharyngeal reconstruction, with cutaneous defect repair (Phase II) deferred until successful pharyngeal repair or flap viability is confirmed. In Case 2, staged reconstruction was implemented due to a history of two prior radiotherapy courses, severe cervical scarring, and compromised tissue perfusion, all of which contributed to an elevated risk of recurrent PCF. PCF typically originate from the pharyngeal lumen, with saliva leakage through mucosal dehiscence accumulating within subcutaneous and soft tissues [32, 33]. Staged reconstruction provides an optimal observation window, enabling direct assessment of flap viability, tissue integration, and early detection of fistula recurrence [33]. This approach facilitates timely interventions at the initial onset of flap necrosis or fistula formation, thereby minimizing progressive tissue damage. In cases such as arteriovenous crisis where the flap is ischemic or bruised, the flap pedicle can be examined under local anesthesia, the tissue around the pedicle can be released, which can reduce the chance of flap necrosis and avoid the failure of skin defect repair due to recurrent infection of the PCF during simultaneous repair [33]. Secondary reconstruction of cutaneous defects should be deferred until either pharyngeal-side flap viability is confirmed or successful pharyngeal reconstruction is achieved, this may reduce the probability of both pharyngocutaneous fistula recurrence and flap necrosis. However, staged reconstruction carries inherent limitations, including necessitated reoperations, prolonged treatment duration, elevated costs, cervical skin contracture, inflammatory scar tissue proliferation, and enlarged defect dimensions—all of which compound the technical complexity of subsequent reconstructive interventions [34]. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive assessment of each patient’s specific condition in order to develop an appropriate flap repair plan.

There are also many risk factors leading to flap repair failure in flap repair surgery: (1) history of preoperative radiotherapy: radiotherapy causes extensive fibrosis and endothelial damage in neck tissues, leading to narrowing and occlusion of small blood vessels and impaired blood supply to the flap; at the same time, radiotherapy suppresses immune function and increases the risk of infection. (2) Poor systemic basic conditions: malnutrition, hypoproteinemia and anemia lead to decreased tissue healing ability. (3) Inadequate flap selection design and repair. Insufficient blood supply of pedicled flap: although the blood supply of PMMF is reliable, it is large in size and prone to atrophy after radiotherapy, leading to narrowing of the pharyngeal cavity [35]. In patients with severe scarring after radiotherapy, simultaneous repair of the pharyngeal cavity and skin in one stage may lead to necrosis of the flap due to insufficient blood supply. It should be “pharyngeal cavity first, then skin” to ensure smooth drainage. (4) Incomplete debridement or poor infection control. (5) Untimely postoperative monitoring and intervention of the flap.

This case series analysis constitutes a single-center retrospective review, summarizing our institutional experience in managing complicated PCF. However, this study has inherent limitations. The small sample size, involvement of multiple flap types, and absence of comparative studies render these findings descriptive and experience-based, lacking validation through large-scale statistical analysis in this specific population. Consequently, the conclusions may carry a degree of subjectivity and reflect regional characteristics. Furthermore, the therapeutic outcomes for complicated PCF are highly variable, influenced by factors such as individual patient tumor status, prior treatment regimens, surgical techniques employed, specific operative plans, and overall health status. The efficacy and success rates also differ among various types of flap reconstruction techniques, introducing multiple confounding variables. These factors may introduce bias into the experience-based summary. We also explored certain approaches for PCF, such as staged repair. Although this technique has achieved success in our patients and provided an additional possibility for clinical management, due to the lack of controlled studies and large-scale observational data, its feasibility does not necessarily equate to the optimal choice. Therefore, its broader applicability needs to be further validated through larger case series and comparative analyses. We hope that future opportunities will arise to integrate similar cases from multiple clinical centers. This would enable a systematic analysis to consolidate clinical experience in the repair of complicated PCF.

Conclusions

We retrospectively analyzed the clinical cases and treatments of 7 patients with complicated PCF. Early, prompt, and appropriate treatment can prevent PCF from progressing to the complicated PCF. For complicated PCF reconstruction, formulating a tailored flap strategy requires consideration of the patient’s overall condition, PCF dimensions, cervical tissue status, comorbidities, surgeon proficiency with flap techniques, and salvage options following repair failure.
