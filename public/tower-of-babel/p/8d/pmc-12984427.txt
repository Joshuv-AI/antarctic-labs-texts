High Accuracy Quantification of Aflatoxin B1 via a Compact Smart Gas Sensing System Assisted by Dual-Branch Convolutional Neural Network
Authors: Changyi Liu, Yu Guo, Qi Bao, Junqiao Li, Peipei Huang, Xiulan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycotoxin contamination of grains during storage and transportation represents a significant threat to global food security. Conventional detection methods exhibit limitations in terms of real-time monitoring. This study presents a compact smart gas sensing system for mycotoxins, facilitating non-destructive testing of corn infected with fungi by analyzing the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted during fungal growth. It also facilitates the precise quantitative detection of Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1). Additionally, a dual-branch convolutional neural network (DB-CNN) model has been developed to conduct an in-depth analysis of the temporal and spatial characteristics of VOCs signals. The system achieves 100% accuracy in identifying grains (corn, peanuts, wheat, and rice) infected with Fusarium graminearum and Aspergillus flavus by extracting the characteristic fingerprint spectra of fungal VOCs. In the quantitative analysis, the DB-CNN exhibits good performance (RMSE = 1.0292 μg/kg, R2 = 0.9994). In addition, the designed detection system supports wireless transmission and can be connected to a smartphone for data transfer, thereby facilitating data storage and remote monitoring. The entire detection process is completed within 4 min. This study provides an innovative technical foundation for dynamic real-time monitoring of fungal contamination in the food supply chain, contributing to early warning systems and quality control measures.

1. Introduction

Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is a secondary metabolite produced by specific fungi [1]. It is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization [2]. AFB1 is mainly produced by Aspergillus flavus. This fungus is widely present in the environment. It can easily contaminate various crops, including corn, peanuts, sorghum, and nuts. Contamination is especially likely under warm and humid storage conditions [2]. According to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), over 25% of the global food crops are contaminated by mycotoxins, causing economic losses of hundreds of billions of dollars each year [3]. Furthermore, AFB1 exhibits significant health risks, such as potent carcinogenicity and immunosuppressive effects [4], thereby posing a substantial threat to human health [5,6]. Therefore, the timely detection of AFB1 in food is the most critical step for reducing the occurrence of foodborne public health events, which highlights the importance of developing AFB1 detection methods.

Common methods for detecting mycotoxins (such as AFB1) include liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS) [7], high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [8], enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [9], and lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA) [10]. In addition, many researchers have designed compact systems for highly accurate detection of AFB1. Wang et al. developed a novel “liver lobule” microtissue biosensor based on 3D bio-printing to rapidly determine AFB1 [11]. Han et al. developed a surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy aptasensor for detecting AFB1 in coix seed [12]. Zheng et al. developed a sensor based on AFB1-responsive DNA hydrogel, achieving visual quantitative detection of AFB1 [13]. Wang et al. developed a chemiluminescence immunoassay based on nanobody-alkaline phosphatase fusion protein and immunomagnetic separation technology for the detection of AFB1 in agricultural products [14]. Lv et al. developed a competitive immunosensor based on in situ generated platinum nanoparticles to enhance the electrochemiluminescence signal of covalent organic polymers, achieving highly sensitive detection of AFB1 [15]. Li et al. developed an immunochromatographic test strip based on a dual-probe signal amplification system of nanobodies and the biotin-streptavidin system, achieving highly sensitive and rapid detection of AFB1 by using two gold nanoflower probes of different sizes [16].

Compared with the above-mentioned mycotoxin detection techniques, technology based on volatile organic compounds (VOCs) gas sensing can non-destructively capture and analyze characteristic VOCs during processes such as food spoilage and fungal contamination [17,18,19]. Thus, it enables non-destructive and rapid on-site detection of AFB1 in grains. Ma et al. proposed a new array of whole-cell biosensors capable of effectively distinguishing healthy peanuts from moldy peanuts by analyzing VOCs released from peanuts [20]. Liu et al. developed a paper-based colorimetric sensor array to capture volatile gases emitted by wheat with varying degrees of mold contamination, enabling non-destructive assessment of wheat quality [21]. Zhang et al. utilized an electronic nose (E-nose) to analyze VOCs produced during the moldy process of rice, facilitating early detection of mold contamination in rice [22]. Gu et al. utilized an extreme learning machine (ELM) to analyze collected gas information, achieving an accuracy of 92.86% in distinguishing three types of fungi [23]. Cervellieri et al. applied multivariate statistical analysis (MSA) and support vector machines (SVMs) to process E-nose collected gas data, enabling rapid assessment of flour quality [24]. Chen et al. employed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) on Aspergillus-specific VOC profiles, achieving 92.86% accuracy in the early detection of A. flavus-infected wheat [25]. In addition, different techniques have been proposed for the quantitative detection of fungi in grains. Jiarpinijnun et al. successfully achieved this by analyzing gas information collected via an E-nose using the partial least square (PLS) regression algorithm (R2 = 0.969) [26].

However, the current VOC-based detection systems are mainly applied to determine whether grains and foods are contaminated or to accurately detect the number of fungi, but there is a lack of relevant work on accurately detecting AFB1 through VOCs.

AFB1 itself is not a volatile compound, but the Aspergillus flavus strains that produce AFB1 release unique VOCs during their growth and metabolism. These VOCs can serve as “chemical fingerprints” to distinguish between toxigenic and non-toxigenic strains early and rapidly, thereby enabling indirect detection and early warning of AFB1 contamination risks [25,27]. Studies have shown that the VOC profiles released by toxigenic and non-toxigenic A. flavus strains are significantly different, and some compounds have been identified as specific markers related to the toxigenic trait. α-Cadinol, cis-muurola-3,5-diene, α-isocomene, and β-selinene are VOCs specific to toxigenic A. flavus strains [27]. Specifically, the emissions of β-chamigrene and α-corocalene are correlated with AFB1 production, providing direct evidence for predicting the toxin production potential by monitoring specific VOCs [27].

Based on the above considerations, this study develops a compact smart gas sensing system for AFB1 that integrates a dual-branch convolutional neural network (DB-CNN). The DB-CNN extracts “gas fingerprints” by co-analyzing the temporal dynamics and spatial features of VOCs data from fungal metabolism through parallel convolutional channels, demonstrating good performance in both classification and regression tasks. The detection system achieves two core functions: (1) it achieves 100% accuracy in distinguishing grains (corn, peanuts, wheat, and rice) infected by F. graminearum from corn infected by A. flavus; (2) it performs a quantitative analysis of AFB1 with high accuracy. Additionally, the designed detection system supports wireless transmission, enabling connection to a smartphone for transferring detection data, thus facilitating data storage and remote monitoring. This study provides an innovative technical solution for dynamic real-time monitoring of fungal contamination in the food supply chain, facilitating early warning and quality control.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Grain Sample Collection

Corn kernels were collected from agricultural production areas in the surrounding region (Wuxi, China). The corn variety is Zhengdan 958, which is widely cultivated in China. All corn samples were harvested during the 2024 growing season. Peanuts, wheat, and rice were purchased from a local supermarket in Wuxi. After manual screening to remove impurities and damaged kernels, the samples were stored under low-temperature, dry, and light-protected conditions to maintain their physicochemical properties.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the detection system and the robustness of the model, we purchased six independent batches of corn samples from Sun Valley Food Technology Co., Ltd. (Chuzhou, China) as an external validation set. These samples differed from the training samples cultivated under controlled laboratory conditions. Specifically, they differed in terms of harvest season (August to October), storage duration (2 to 18 months), and storage environment (ventilated room at normal temperature vs. sealed room at low temperature). Consequently, they strictly met the requirements of independence, authenticity, and scene representativeness for the external validation dataset.

2.2. Grain Samples Inoculated with F. graminearum and A. flavus

Under sterile conditions, spore suspensions of F. graminearum PH-1 and A. flavus 185860 (provided by the Collaborative Innovation Center of Jiangnan University) were prepared. The spore concentration was determined using a hemocytometer and adjusted to 105–106 CFU/mL. For each treatment group, 300 g of corn, peanut, wheat, and rice were separately taken. The grains were pretreated by soaking in 1% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, followed by rinsing with sterilized water 3 times, 1 min each time. Subsequently, the grain kernels were immersed in the F. graminearum and A. flavus spore suspension for 10 s and then placed in a sterile culture bottle. The grain kernels were then dried at room temperature and subsequently placed on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates for culturing. They were then stored in a humidified incubator (70% relative humidity, RH) at 28 °C to observe the process of fungal contamination. Grain kernels without fungus inoculation were used as the control group. Samples (5 g) were taken every 24 h and stored in a refrigerator. Twenty samples were collected at each time point.

2.3. Detection of AFB1 Content in Corn Samples Infected with A. flavus by Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

AFB1 levels in corn kernels stored for varying durations were determined using LC-MS/MS (type: ACQUITY Premier/Xevo TQ Absolute; manufacturer: Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). After the sample was crushed (90% passed through a 40-mesh sieve) and mixed, 5.00 g was weighed accurately. Then, 20 mL of acetonitrile-water-acetic acid (70:29:1) solvent mixture was added, vortexed and mixed for 1 min, shaken for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 7000 r/min for 10 min to separate the solid from the liquid. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane, and 20 μL of the pre-vortexed stable isotope mixture was pipetted into a 400 μL interpolation tube, followed by the addition of 180 μL of the sample. The sample filtrate was mixed and prepared for measurement.

2.4. Composition of the Gas Sensing System for Fungi

The data acquisition module of the detection system is displayed in Figure 1a. The system adopts an active air circulation design. After the gas enters the detection device through the air intake hole, it forms a directional airflow driven by the intake fan (5 V, 20 × 20 × 6 mm3; manufacturer: Sanyo Denki, Tokyo, Japan). The airflow first passes through a filter module filled with desiccant, which reduces the humidity of the gas through physical dehumidification to ensure environmental stability in the subsequent detection process. A temperature and humidity sensor (type: Sht30, temperature accuracy: ± 0.3 °C; humidity accuracy: ± 3% RH; manufacturer: Sensirion, Shenzhen, China) is integrated into the device to monitor gas temperature and humidity parameters in real time, providing environmental compensation data for the system. The pre-treated gas then flows through the gas sensor array for quantitative detection of the target gas. Based on the types of VOCs released during fungal growth [17,25,27,28] and our laboratory’s previous research [17], characteristic VOCs produced by common fungi in grains such as F. graminearum and A. flavus include, but are not limited to, ammonia (NH3), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), alcohols, and thiols. Therefore, the commercial metal oxide (MOX) sensor array is carefully selected to cover these target gases. Detailed specifications of the sensors are presented in Table 1. After detection, the gas is expelled from the system through the exhaust hole under the forced convection effect of the exhaust fan, completing air circulation. The dual-fan configuration enhances the gas replacement efficiency within the device.

The data processing and analysis module of the detection system is displayed in Figure 1b. The analog-to-digital conversion module (ADC, type: ADS1115; manufacturer: TI, Dallas, TX, USA) converts the collected analog signals into digital signals for further analysis. The ADS1115 is a 16-Bit ADC with four inputs which can convert an analog signal into 16-Bit binary number which is in proportion to the amplitude of detected analog signal. The GPS (Global Positioning System) sensor (type: LC76G; accuracy: 1.5 m CEP (Circular Error Probable); manufacturer: Waveshare, Shenzhen, China) enables real-time positioning of the detection equipment, making the detection system suitable for food storage and transportation. The Raspberry Pi Zero 2W (manufacturer: RASPBERRY PI, London, UK) is the main control board of the detection system. It achieves multiple functions, including system control, data stream scheduling, image analysis, and processing. In addition, data transmission and communication can be achieved by its powerful data processing capabilities and wireless transmission functions. Additionally, the detection system is equipped with an uninterrupted power supply (UPS, 5V, 7800 mAh; manufacturer: Waveshare, China). This ensures stable power delivery. The UPS supports functions such as charging and discharging, power level monitoring, and battery temperature status detection. The detection system can operate continuously for over 96 h (detection frequency: once per hour; the system remains in standby mode during the rest of the time), enabling mobility and long-term autonomous operation in complex environments.

The gas sensing system is displayed in Figure 1c. The system housing is designed based on 3D printing technology. The material selected is 9000R SLA (Stereolithography Appearance; manufacturer: JLC, Shenzhen, China) photosensitive resin, ensuring both the lightness and durability of the sensing system. The detection equipment adopts a miniaturized design concept, with the data acquisition module measuring 28 (L) × 20 (W) × 3 (H) cm3 and the data processing module measuring 15 (L) × 10 (W) × 6 (H) cm3. The compact layout and the lightweight materials have effectively enhanced the system integration, while also demonstrating good portability. This makes it highly applicable in food supply chain operations and mobile inspection scenarios. As shown in the inset of Figure 1c, the data processing module includes a power supply, a GPS sensor, a switch, a Raspberry Pi, and two ADC modules. The ADC1115 utilizes four inputs for gas sensors. Since there are six gas sensors utilized in the designed sensing system, two ADCs are utilized. Figure 1d displays the layout of the gas sensor array in the detection system, which is arranged vertically to ensure that each gas sensor has effective contact with the detected gas. As shown in the inset of Figure 1d, each gas sensor is connected with resistor RL. As the measured concentration of the detected gas changes, the corresponding voltage of RL will change. It is transmitted to ADC and is converted to a digital signal. Since the ADC is a 16-Bit ADC, a sixteen-bit binary number will be generated by the ADC, and then it is transmitted to the Raspberry Pi, which can convert it to a decimal number and then output.

2.5. Working Principle of the Gas Sensor

Equivalent circuit model of the gas sensor utilized in the gas sensing system is shown in Figure 2. When the Vc is applied, the heating resistor (RH) is heated by the VH to an optimum temperature for the gas sensor required to detect the target gas. The gas sensor is represented by a variable value resistor Rs. When the sensor comes into contact with the detected gas, a redox reaction occurs on the surface of the gas sensor’s semiconductor’s material, resulting in a change in RS. A load resistor (RL) is connected in series with the sensor resistor (RS). As the concentration of the detected gas increases, the redox reaction of the gas sensor improves, and the Rs decreases. Therefore, the corresponding voltage of the RL increases as the concentration of the detected gas increases. The corresponding voltage is output to a 16-Bit ADC, which can convert the analog voltage of RL into a sixteen-bit binary number in proportion to the analog voltage. The measured results are transmitted to the Raspberry Pi. Then, the binary number is converted into decimal number by the Raspberry Pi. The corresponding decimal number represents the digital output voltage, which is in proportion to the concentration of the detected gas. By utilizing the ADC, a much higher sensing resolution can be achieved. Before the experiment, the designed gas sensing system was compared with a commercial VOC gas detector, which demonstrated the high accuracy and stability of the designed sensing system.

2.6. Gas Chamber Optimization for the Gas Sensing System

The goal of optimizing the gas chamber design is to enable the gas sensor to have effective contact with the gas being detected. The gas chamber design should ensure uniform gas flow, exposing all sensors to the same gas concentration. Additionally, it should control the gas composition. It should maintain consistent composition during sensor exposure. This control must continue during rapid transitions from reference to target gas [29].

As displayed in Figure 3, in the gas chamber, each gas sensor is equipped with a separate small gas chamber. The gas chambers are interconnected via air holes, with the gas sensors fixed behind these holes. This design effectively increases the effective contact area between the sensors and the detected gas, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of the entire sensing system. Simultaneously, the individual gas chambers can effectively mitigate abrupt changes in gas composition.

2.7. Stability Design of the Gas Sensing System

To mitigate the adverse effects of gas humidity on gas sensor performance [30], a filter chamber is integrated into the front end of the gas sensing system. This chamber is filled with 4g of food-grade silica gel desiccant. Under the condition of 80% relative humidity, the water adsorption capacity is ≥40%. The adsorption capacity after continuous exposure for 100 min is ≥30%. The dehumidification process effectively removes moisture from the detected gas without altering its composition. Therefore, the adsorption effect of the desiccant on the target VOCs is minimal and does not affect the subsequent detection results. Meanwhile, to reduce measurement uncertainty and minimize background interference, algorithmic optimizations on the collected gas data are implemented. These optimizations specifically included removing outliers and subtracting the complex gas background baseline (response value of the gas sensor when it is stable) from the acquired signals. As shown in Figure 4, after subtracting the baseline, the data curve becomes more stable. The data curve after subtracting the baseline is used as the input data for model training.

As for the changes in temperature, since the MOX sensor requires preheating, the variations in the external environment temperature have little impact on the detection results.

For non-fungal VOCs, the algorithm identifies them as background information. Since we link the collected gas data with mycotoxins and the collection operation remains unchanged, the algorithm will extract the gas features related to mycotoxins. Meanwhile, multiple sensors can effectively collect gas information related to mycotoxins and reduce the influence of non-fungal VOCs.

To evaluate the long-term stability of the gas sensor, sensor response data were collected at five-day intervals, with each measurement lasting 3 min and yielding 180 data points. As displayed in Figure S1, the sensor exhibited minimal output signal drift over the 15-day continuous testing period, with a coefficient of variation below 0.06%, demonstrating that the designed system possesses excellent stability.

2.8. Design of DB-CNN Algorithm Model for Fungal Detection

The gas sensor array exhibits cross-sensitive response characteristics, meaning that a single sensor may respond to multiple gases, and multiple sensors can detect the same gas. Due to the varying sensitivity levels of different sensors, a single response curve represents a composite of information about the detected gas. Additionally, as the number of sensors increases, the dimensionality of the collected gas data rises, thereby increasing the complexity for traditional machine learning (ML) models to accurately analyze the effective information of the gas. In deep decoding based on convolutional computation, while important features that enhance classification performance can be extracted, redundant features that may impair accuracy are also present.

To solve the above problems, this study proposes a dual-branch convolutional neural network structure and adopts a heterogeneous feature fusion strategy. Its structure is displayed in Figure 5. This model addresses the need to extract both temporal and spatial features from sensor array time-series data. It innovatively uses two feature extraction pathways. Each pathway employs distinct nonlinear activation mechanisms. This design achieves multi-dimensional feature complementarity through differentiated convolution operations. The dual-branch channels are equipped with independent parameters, enabling the network to independently learn the temporal and spatial mapping of different features.

The model accepts two identical single-channel input tensors, defined as X1, X2 ∈ R18×20×1, X1 = X2. Each branch processes the input independently, generating heterogeneous feature representations through different convolutional kernels, activation functions, and pooling strategies. First Convolutional Layer: uses 32 convolutional kernels of size 3 × 3 (weight matrix: W1(1) ∈ R3×3×1×32, bias: b1(1) ∈ R32) for feature mapping, introducing nonlinearity via the ReLU activation function:C1(1) = ReLU(W1(1) * X1 + b1(1))(1) where * denotes the convolution operation and ReLU(z) = max(0, z). First Pooling Layer: employs 2 × 2 Max Pooling for downsampling, preserving the most salient features:P1(1)[i, j, k] = max(C1(1)[2i + p, 2j + q, k]), p = 0, q = 1(2)

Subsequent Layers: Repeat the above convolution and pooling process, increasing the number of kernels to 64 to further abstract higher-order features. The final output is flattened into a vector f1 ∈ R567.

Branch 2: Feature Extraction Based on Sigmoid and Average Pooling. This branch focuses on generating smooth, probabilistic feature responses. First Convolutional Layer: uses 32 convolutional kernels of size 3 × 3 (parameters: W2(1), b2(1)), compressing activations to the interval (0, 1) via the Sigmoid function:C2(1) = σ(W2(1) *X2 + b2(1))(3) where σ(z) = 1/(1 + EXP(-z)). First Pooling Layer: uses 2 × 2 Average Pooling for downsampling, preserving the overall distribution information of the feature maps:P2(1)[i, j, k] = 1/(4Σp=0Σq=0 C1(1)[2i + p, 2j + q, k])(4)

Subsequent Layers: Repeat the above process, with the final output flattened into a vector f2 ∈ R567. The feature vectors from the two branches are fused via a concatenation operation, forming a high-dimensional joint feature representation:(5)fmerge=[f1; f2]∈R1152

For classification tasks, the probability matrix of each label is output via the SoftMax activation function. The Adam optimizer is used to minimize multi-class cross-entropy loss, while tracking the model’s accuracy as a performance metric during training. For regression predictions, the output layer is designed as a linear regression unit that directly maps to the target concentration value. The Adam optimizer is employed for parameter update, with the initial learning rate set to 0.01. The loss function uses mean square error (MSE).

Through the two nonlinear functions (ReLU and Sigmoid), the model can simultaneously capture sparse salient features and smooth distributional features of the input, achieving feature diversity. Max Pooling enhances translation invariance, while Average Pooling helps preserve contextual background information. The parallel structure increases the model’s width rather than its depth, helping to enhance expressive power without excessive depth. Feature concatenation achieves an implicit ensemble effect, theoretically improving generalization performance.

2.9. Design of Mobile Phone APP for Remote Wireless Connection with the Gas Sensing System

The detection system employs Bluetooth/Wi-Fi dual-mode communication. It links the detection terminal with smart mobile devices. This enables real-time data synchronization and backup. The system effectively constructs distributed data management architecture. The Android client application (Figure 6) developed using the AppInventor visual programming platform includes the following core functionalities: (1) device control unit: manages the full lifecycle of the detection process (start, run, and terminate); (2) status monitoring unit: provides real-time updates on device battery levels, GPS coordinates, and environmental parameters; and (3) data visualization unit: dynamically displays the time-domain response curve of the gas sensor (with a sampling rate of 1 Hz) along with its characteristic amplitude spectrum analysis.

Additionally, the local storage module of the system is capable of caching detection records for over 30 days and supports timestamp-based traceability queries for historical data. Specifically, to address the risk of fungal contamination in stored grains, the system has implemented a hierarchical early warning mechanism based on the response thresholds of gas sensors: when the concentration of VOCs exceeds the predefined safety limit (the content of AFB1 exceeds 20 μg/kg), the detection terminal triggers an alarm signal, while the mobile application simultaneously pushes the early warning information, thereby forming a closed-loop monitoring system.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Acquisition and Processing of VOCs Data from Grain Samples Inoculated with F. graminearum and A. flavus

The process of gas data collection and processing is displayed in Figure 7. In the detection system, the gas acquisition module captures the detected gas and generates the corresponding response timing curve. To conform to the data input required by DB-CNN, the response matrix of the sensor array, which includes 60 sampling points in the time dimension and six sensors in the space dimension, is transformed into a three-dimensional tensor of size 18 × 20 × 1. Subsequently, the data are analyzed using DB-CNN, and the detection results are output. Finally, the detection system establishes a wireless connection with the smart mobile device via Wi-Fi, enabling real-time data synchronization and backup.

Each time the detection system is activated, the sensor array is preheated for 20 min (no preheating is required for subsequent tests) to establish a stable state. Purge all chambers and pipes connected to the sensor array with clean air for 3 min to eliminate adsorbed interfering substances. The 5 g of grain samples is placed in the detection chamber. The detection system samples data at a frequency of 1 Hz and collects gas from the corn sample 20 times per day (60 s each time). Subsequently, the gas chamber is purged with clean air to restore the sensor (2 min) to a stable state for subsequent tests. The entire testing process is completed within 4 min. The detection frequency is once every 6 min.

3.2. Detection of AFB1 Content in Inoculated A. flavus Corn Samples

A. flavus 185860 is a toxigenic strain that produces the toxic secondary metabolite AFB1 during fungal proliferation. As displayed in Figure 8, AFB1 content gradually increases over the course of cultivation. In the later stages of growth, rapid mold proliferation leads to a significant accumulation of toxins. By the 8th day, AFB1 levels exceed the Chinese national standard of 20 μg/kg, posing a serious threat to human and animal health. Therefore, early determination of contamination levels is crucial to mitigate potential risks before more severe consequences arise.

3.3. Calibration of the Gas Sensing System Using a Commercial VOC Gas Detector

In order to demonstrate the performance of the designed sensing system, it is compared with a commercial VOC gas detector (detection accuracy: ±5 ppm; instrument model: BTYQ-JIUP-100; manufacturer: YUZBA, Shaanxi, China). VOCs released by corn infected with A. flavus (1 to 9 days post-infection) were used as the detection gas. The measured results are shown in Figure 9. As can be seen, the measured results of the designed sensor are consistent with the measured results of the VOC detector. In the inset, the measured results of the commercial detector are plotted on the x-axis, while the corresponding measured results of the designed sensing system are plotted on the y-axis. The fitted curve exhibits strong linearity (R2 = 0.9876), confirming that the designed sensing system is capable of quantitative VOC detection. Therefore, the designed gas sensing system is utilized in the following experiments for the accurate detection of VOCs.

3.4. Classification Performance Analysis of the Gas Sensing System

To evaluate the classification performance of DB-CNN, gas information from eight different sources was collected, including air, ethanol (50 ppm), fresh corn (Fresh-C), corn infected with A. flavus (A. flavus-C, mild infection), corn infected with F. graminearum (F. graminearum-C, mild infection), rice infected with A. flavus (A. flavus-R, mild infection), wheat infected with A. flavus (A. flavus-W, mild infection), and peanut infected with A. flavus (A. flavus-P, mild infection). Utilizing the volatility of ethanol, 50 ppm ethanol gas was prepared at 25 °C by placing 1.2 μL of anhydrous ethanol solution and a standard gas detector into a 10 L capacity airtight test box. Gas information from each source was collected 20 times, resulting in a total of 160 gas samples.

To visually examine the distribution and characteristics of the data, principal component analysis (PCA) is employed to reduce the dimensionality of the data. As displayed in Figure 10a, the cumulative variance explained by PC1 and PC2 is 99.7%, with PC1 accounting for 97.8% and PC2 accounting for 1.9%. The gas characteristics of A. flavus-C and F. graminearum-C are more distinguishable in the two-dimensional space. Figure 10b displays the contribution of each principal component to the detected gas data. The first two principal components cumulatively account for 99.69% of the total variance, indicating that they adequately reflect the gas characteristics and can represent the overall information of the response curve. Additionally, Figure 10c displays the variable loads of six gas sensors in the PCA. All gas sensors exhibit a strong positive correlation with PC1. Regarding PC2, MQ-137 exhibits a significant positive correlation, whereas the remaining gas sensors display weak negative correlations. These results confirm that the gas sensor array of the detection system can effectively capture gas-related information and extract the corresponding “gas fingerprints” for different gases.

The dataset is randomly split into training and testing sets in a 4:1 ratio. The classification cross-entropy is used as the loss function, and accuracy is employed as the evaluation metric. To evaluate the performance and generalization ability of the model, 5-fold cross-validation was adopted. The validation results are shown in Figure S2. As displayed in Figure 10d, after 100 iterations, the loss curve of DB-CNN converges, with accuracy at 100% starting from the 7th iteration and the loss decreasing to 0.1954. To comprehensively evaluate the model’s classification performance, the classification confusion matrices for DB-CNN’s classification of eight gases are plotted (Figure 10e). In the test detection of DB-CNN, the classification accuracy for all eight gases reaches 100%.

The classification results of DB-CNN are compared with other algorithms. Table 2 shows the classification results for the eight gases, with accuracy, precision, F1-score, recall, and Cohen’s kappa serving as evaluation metrics. As can be seen, deep learning algorithms can autonomously extract features from gas data and perform deep mining of data information. CNN and long short-term memory (LSTM) show improved performance (CNN: Accuracy = 96.88%, precision = 97.50%, Recall = 96.88%, F1-score = 96.71%, Cohen’s kappa = 96.43%; LSTM: Accuracy = 97.50%, precision = 98.17%, Recall = 97.50%, F1-score = 97.40%, Cohen’s kappa = 97.15%). The DB-CNN proposed in this paper conducts deep mining of gas data in both temporal and spatial dimensions, thereby achieving deep extraction of gas features. As shown in the table, the designed system gets the best results (Table S1), with all evaluation metrics reaching 100%.

To verify the effectiveness of DB-CNN, we conduct an ablation analysis. Table 3 shows the impact of different convolutional branches on the classification performance of DB-CNN. DB-CNN achieves good classification performance with only one convolutional branch. Among them, Branch 1 consistently achieves the best classification results, demonstrating the effectiveness of the convolutional calculation method in processing and recognizing gas information. When two convolutional branches are used, the best overall performance is achieved. This indicates that the dual-branch structure significantly enhances classification performance.

To gain deeper insights into the operational mechanism of DB-CNN, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) is employed to visualize the network’s prediction process using heat maps. As displayed in Figure S3, the two branches have different focuses in capturing the characteristics of gases.

SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanation) values offer both global and local interpretability by quantifying the marginal contribution of each feature to the model output. This enables a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s behavior and the relative importance of input features. As displayed in Figure 11, the SHAP summary plot for all variables of F. graminearum-C generated by DB-CNN ranks features based on their correlation with the classification outcome. Each point in the plot corresponds to a data instance in the dataset. It is evident that the top three contributing features identified by DB-CNN are MQ-137, TGS2600, and MQ-136. Their horizontally dispersed distributions suggest a stronger influence on the model’s predictions.

To further investigate feature importance and their contributions to model outputs, SHAP force plots are generated based on the SHAP values. Force plots serve as effective tools for visualizing how individual features influence the final prediction for a specific instance. To assess the relative significance of different features in determining classification outcomes, representative observations from the dataset are selected, and corresponding force plots are produced. Figure 12a,b displays the SHAP force plots for two randomly selected samples: (a) sample A. flavus-C and (b) sample F. graminearum-C. For sample A. flavus-C, TGS2600 exerted the strongest positive influence, whereas MQ-137 had the most pronounced negative effect. In the case of sample F. graminearum-C, MQ-136 contributed the most positively, while TGS2602 had the strongest negative impact. The differential influence of features across sample types enables DB-CNN to achieve accurate classification.

Based on the analysis of the results above, the designed gas sensing system is capable of effectively identifying and classifying corn infected with different fungal species.

3.5. Gas Sensing System Quantitatively Detects AFB1 in Corn Samples Inoculated with A. flavus

During the proliferation of A. flavus, a toxic fungal secondary metabolite, AFB1, is generated. This toxin presents serious health risks, including strong carcinogenicity and immunosuppressive effects, thereby endangering human health. Hence, early assessment of contamination levels is of critical importance. In this study, a quantitative relationship model between the VOCs emitted by A. flavus-infected corn and the AFB1 content was established through correlation analysis.

To evaluate the regression prediction performance of DB-CNN, the collected gas data are randomly split into the training set and the testing set at a ratio of 4:1. MSE is employed as the loss function, while mean absolute error (MAE) and Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) serve as the evaluation metric. The prediction performance of DB-CNN is displayed in Figure 13a. The differences between the predicted and actual values are predominantly within the 95% limits of agreement (95% LoA, +1.55, −1.43). The mean difference is 0.06, indicating that the designed system achieves a high level of predictive accuracy. Meanwhile, the goodness of fit (R2 = 0.9994) is displayed in Figure S4. The error distribution is displayed in Figure 13b. For the low-concentration AFB1 range (0–10 μg/kg), the MAE is 0.26 μg/kg. The box plot shows an overall upward shift, indicating a systematic overestimation by the model in this range. In the medium-concentration range (10–50 μg/kg), the maximum absolute error reaches 3.86 μg/kg. However, 94% of predictions fall within a narrow band of 0.01–1.40 μg/kg, demonstrating tight clustering and low dispersion. Within the high-concentration range (50–200 μg/kg), the MAE is further reduced to 0.323 μg/kg, with no significant outliers observed. This confirms the model’s high robustness and excellent predictive accuracy within this high-concentration range.

Figure 13c displays the influence of different features on the model’s prediction of AFB1 content. All features exhibit a generally positive correlation with SHAP values. Notably, TGS2603 demonstrates the widest range of SHAP values, suggesting that higher values have a stronger positive impact on the prediction, while lower values have a stronger negative impact on the prediction.

Figure 14a displays the specific contribution of each feature during the model’s prediction of a single sample. The cumulative negative impact of all features outweighs the positive impact, resulting in a leftward shift in the predicted value. Notably, when AFB1 concentrations approached the regulatory limit of 20 μg/kg (GB 2761-2017) [31], TGS2603 and MQ-136 exhibited the most significant positive and negative influences on the prediction, respectively, highlighting their critical role in the model’s decision-making process. Figure 14b displays the Grad-CAM analysis of DB-CNN, demonstrating that Branch 1 is more responsive to temporal variations, whereas Branch 2 is more attuned to global feature patterns.

To demonstrate the performance advantages of DB-CNN over other algorithms, its regression metrics are systematically compared with those of multiple established models (Table 4). The results indicate that the designed system has a good performance in prediction accuracy, thereby confirming its effectiveness and potential for application in mycotoxin detection.

To evaluate the potential applicability of the designed detection system in addressing multi-fungal contamination in grains, we employed it to determine AFB1 levels in corn samples co-infected with multiple fungal species. We purchased some corn samples from different batches from Sun Valley Food Technology Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China), as an external validation set. The corresponding volatile gas detection data were used for the external validation of the DB-CNN model to objectively evaluate the model’s generalization ability and robustness in real complex scenarios.

4. Conclusions

In this study, a gas sensing system integrating a dual-branch convolutional neural network was developed for real-time monitoring of fungal contamination in grains. Through sensor array response and correlation analysis, the system identified VOCs associated with fungal metabolism and extracted the corresponding “gas fingerprints”. The system achieved highly accurate discrimination (accuracy = 100%) between grains (corn, wheat, peanuts, and rice) samples infected by F. graminearum and A. flavus. In the quantitative prediction of AFB1, the designed system demonstrated good regression performance (AFB1: RMSE = 1.0292 μg/kg, R2 = 0.9994). Through dual-branch architecture, the DB-CNN achieved complementary feature fusion by not only extracting temporal dynamic features but also extracting spatial probability features of VOC signals. The designed system is more adaptive to complex VOC data, and its performance advantages are verified by confusion matrix analysis and Bland–Altman analysis. The system features a fast detection speed (4 min/sample). It supports wireless data transmission via smartphone APP and enables real-time monitoring and hierarchical early warning of fungal contamination in the grain supply chain. The method can be extended to online analysis of other grains, such as wheat and rice. The integration of gas sensor arrays and DB-CNN provides an innovative solution for non-destructive, rapid, accurate identification and dynamic monitoring of fungal contamination in grains. Its high accuracy and portability make it an essential tool for ensuring food safety and reducing economic losses in grain storage and transportation.
