Acute hydrated nucleus pulposus extrusion in dogs with severe neurologic status: comparison between conservative and surgical treatment
Authors: Martin Di Tosto, Cristina Toni, Barbara Contiero, Francesca Cozzi, Katia Marioni-Henry, Marika Menchetti, Barbara Betti, Federica Tirrito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHydrated nucleus pulposus extrusion (HNPE) is associated with favorable outcome after conservative or surgical treatment. However, optimal management for dogs with severe neurologic deficits remains unclear.Hypothesis/ObjectivesCompare conservative vs surgical treatment in nonambulatory paretic or plegic dogs with HNPE, focusing on recovery of unassisted ambulation and voluntary urination, and identify prognostic factors.AnimalsEighty-six dogs with severe neurologic impairment and a presumptive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) diagnosis of HNPE.MethodsRetrospective multicenter study. Signalment, neurologic grade, neurolocalization, MRI findings, and treatment were recorded. Outcome was assessed at 1 week, 4 weeks, and 6 months. Satisfactory outcome was defined as recovery of voluntary urination and independent ambulation. Recovery time was defined as days to regain unassisted ambulation.ResultsSixty dogs were treated conservatively and 26 surgically. Hydrated nucleus pulposus extrusion was cervical in 71/86 (83%) dogs and thoracolumbar in 15/86 (17%). At presentation, 62/86 (72%) dogs were nonambulatory paretic, 22/86 (26%) were plegic with intact nociception, and 2/86 (2%) were plegic without nociception. At 1 week, neurologic status was better in the conservative group than in the surgical group, whereas no differences were observed at later follow-up. Satisfactory outcome was achieved in 85% of conservatively treated and 100% of surgically treated dogs. Median recovery time was 7 days with conservative treatment and 9 days with surgery. Non-chondrodystrophic breed, MRI intramedullary T2-weighted hyperintensity, and physical therapy were associated with longer recovery.Conclusions and clinical importanceConservative treatment represents a valid option for nonambulatory dogs with HNPE. Signalment and MRI features should guide treatment selection.

Introduction

Compressive hydrated nucleus pulposus extrusion (HNPE) is a subtype of intervertebral disc herniation (IVDH) in dogs characterized by the acute extrusion of hydrated or partially degenerated nucleus pulposus, resulting in spinal cord compression (SCC).1,2 Although this condition also has been described as acute compressive HNPE to emphasize its compressive nature, it is more commonly referred to by the abbreviated term HNPE.2 This subtype predominantly affects the cervical spinal cord and is responsible for severe neurologic deficits such as nonambulatory tetraparesis or tetraplegia, but it occurs less commonly in the thoracolumbar region.1 Hydrated nucleus pulposus extrusion predominantly affects older dogs, with a mean age of 9 years.3 There is no particular breed predisposition; both small and large breeds are susceptible, as well as chondrodystrophic and non-chondrodystrophic dogs.4 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for diagnosing HNPE, allowing accurate differentiation from other types of disc herniation and assessment of SCC and parenchymal changes.5 Although treatment decisions often are based on clinical severity, MRI can provide additional prognostic information, such as the presence of T2-weighted (T2-W) intramedullary hyperintensity, which has been associated with neurologic severity and outcome in dogs with spinal cord injury.5 Follow-up MRI studies in dogs treated conservatively for compressive HNPE have demonstrated the disappearance of the original compression and the initially extruded material, indicating that conservative treatment can effectively resolve the physical manifestations of this condition.6 Although surgery often is recommended in severe cases to rapidly relieve SCC, recent studies have shown that conservative treatment including corticosteroids or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) along with strict rest can achieve similarly favorable outcomes, making it a valid and potentially equivalent therapeutic option.3,7 However, treatment strategies for HNPE remain under debate, particularly concerning the efficacy of conservative vs surgical interventions in cases with severe neurologic impairment. Previous studies have included a broad spectrum of neurologic severities, ranging from patients that remained ambulatory with spinal pain to those that were nonambulatory or paralyzed.2,3,7 Including subjects with variable degrees of neurologic severity may influence the statistical analysis and outcome data. It therefore is crucial to clarify whether conservative treatment remains a viable option for dogs that are exclusively nonambulatory or paralyzed. Our objective was to compare the outcomes of conservatively vs surgically treated dogs with acute compressive HNPE presenting with severe neurologic deficits, specifically focusing on nonambulatory or paralyzed dogs. We hypothesized that although surgical treatment might offer rapid improvement in neurologic function, conservative treatment may yield comparable rates of long-term recovery.3,7 Our study intended to provide a more clear understanding and evidence-based recommendations for managing severe cases of HNPE, ensuring optimal care and informed decision-making.

Materials and methods

A retrospective multicenter study was conducted (April 2013 to January 2022) including client-owned dogs with an MRI presumptive diagnosis of compressive HNPE from 5 veterinary centers (Italy and the United Kingdom). Because of the retrospective design, ethical committee approval was not required. Both conservative and surgical approaches are recognized standards of care.3,4,6,7 Written informed owner consent for study inclusion and research use was obtained. Inclusion criteria comprised neurologic examination, neurolocalization consistent with spinal cord segments (C1-C5, C6-T2, T3-L3, and L4-S3), severe neurologic dysfunction (nonambulatory paresis or plegia), and presumptive HNPE MRI diagnosis. Exclusion criteria were clinically relevant comorbidities, mild neurologic signs, non-spinal deficits, or insufficient follow-up. Collected data included age, sex, breed classification (chondrodystrophic, non-chondrodystrophic, or mixed), weight, duration of clinical signs (<24, 24-48, and > 48 h), and neurolocalization.8,9 Neurologic severity at presentation was graded 3 (nonambulatory tetraparesis or paraparesis with intact nociception), 4 (tetraplegia or paraplegia with intact nociception), and 5 (tetraplegia or paraplegia without nociception).10 Magnetic resonance imaging (0.4, 1.5, or 3 Tesla) was performed. Diagnosis of HNPE was based on established criteria.11 Extruded disc material was classified as homogeneous (uniform T2-W hyperintensity, indicating hydrated nucleus pulposus) or heterogeneous (iso-hypointense components within T2-W hyperintensity, indicating degeneration).5 Presence of intramedullary T2-W hyperintensity was recorded. The SCC ratio was calculated.9,12 Treatment decisions were made jointly by owners and clinicians. Conservative management involved corticosteroids or NSAIDs, analgesia, strict confinement, and physical therapy. Surgical treatment consisted of ventral slot, hemilaminectomy, or mini-hemilaminectomy based on HNPE location. Surgeon experience was categorized (European College of Veterinary Neurology [ECVN] diplomate or neurosurgeon with ≥ 8 years’ experience). Neurologic assessments were performed at 3-7 and 21-31 days using a 1-5 grading system.10 Grade 0 was assigned to normal neurologic function without pain.12 A 6-month follow-up was included when available. Outcome was defined as satisfactory if voluntary urination and independent ambulation were regained; otherwise, it was considered unsatisfactory, including recurrence or euthanasia.12 Hospitalization and recovery time, defined as days to satisfactory outcome, were recorded.12

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc and SAS software. Group (conservative vs surgical) comparison was performed using Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney test, or z-test. Changes in outcome within groups were evaluated using Friedman test and Spearman correlation. Recovery time was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing to evaluate influencing factors. The median recovery time and 95% CI were estimated. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

A total of 86 dogs were included in the study; 60 (70%) received conservative treatment and 26 (30%) underwent surgical treatment (Table 1). No differences were recorded between groups regarding demographic data (Table 1). There were 26 (30%) female and 60 (70%) male dogs. The mean age of the subjects was 9.7 ± 2.7 years and mean body weight was 15 ± 12 kg (Table 1). Twenty-three (27%) dogs were chondrodystrophic breeds, mixed breeds accounted for 25 (29%), and non-chondrodystrophic breeds comprised 38 (44%). Neurolocalization was cervical in 71 (83%) patients and thoracolumbar in 15 (17%) dogs (Table 1). At presentation, 62 (72%) dogs had a neurologic grade of 3, 22 (26%) had a grade of 4, and 2 (2%) had a grade of 5, with a median neurologic grade of 3 in both groups. The distribution of neurologic grades at admission did not differ significantly between the conservative and surgical groups (Table 1). The mean degree of SCC ratio was 28 ± 12% and the MRI intensity of extruded disc material was heterogeneous in 27 (31%) dogs and homogeneous in 59 (69%; Table 1). T2-weighted intramedullary hyperintensity, compared to the gray matter of the spinal cord, was found in 45 (52%) of 86 patients (Table 1). Sixty-three (73%) dogs underwent physical therapy after diagnosis. At 1 week posttreatment, conservatively treated dogs exhibited a median neurologic grade of 2, whereas surgically treated patients had a median grade of 3 (P = .01). However, after 1 and 6 months, all dogs showed improvement, regardless of the type of treatment (P = .50 at 1 month; P = .99 at 6 months; Table 1). A satisfactory final outcome was achieved in all surgically treated dogs. Regarding neurologic status, conservatively managed dogs presenting with severe deficits were significantly more likely to retain a higher neurologic grade at 1 (P = .002) and 4 weeks (P = .02) compared with those with lower initial grades. Notably, dogs with grades 4 and 5 exhibited slower neurologic improvement than those with grade 3 at 1 week, and this difference persisted at 1 month in dogs presented with grade 5 (Supplementary Table S1). In contrast, when surgery was chosen, improvement was more rapid for dogs with high neurologic grade (grade 4) than for cases with low neurologic grade (grade 3) at 4 weeks (P < .001) and 6 months (P < .001; Supplementary Table S2). In the conservative group, cases with heterogeneous extruded discs had worse outcomes at 1 (P = .04) and 6 months (P = .01) compared with dogs with homogeneous material (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, non-chondrodystrophic breeds (P = .03) and those with thoracolumbar herniations (P < .001) in the conservative group were more likely to present with severe neurologic status 6 months after diagnosis (Supplementary Table S1). In both treatment groups (conservative and surgical), the presence of T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity was associated with worse neurologic status at 1 week (P = .001 in the conservative medical group; P = .03 in the surgical group) and at 4 weeks (P = .02 in the conservative medical group; P = .05 in the surgical group), compared with dogs without T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The neurologic grade also remained higher at 6 months in the conservative group (P = .04; Supplementary Table S1). Initiating a physical therapy program had a negative impact on outcome within a week, leading to higher neurologic grades in both treatment types (P = .02 for the conservative group; P = .02 for the surgical group; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Data on final outcome were available for 47 dogs treated conservatively and 24 dogs treated surgically. Final outcome data were not available for 15 cases, including 13 treated conservatively and 2 treated surgically, because of incomplete long-term follow-up. The final outcome was considered satisfactory in 40 of 47 (85%) of conservatively treated and 24 of 24 (100%) of surgically treated cases (P = .02). Unsatisfactory outcome (7/47, 15%) was observed exclusively in the conservative group and included persistent inability to ambulate independently beyond 30 days (4/7, 57%), euthanasia because of lack of neurologic recovery (2/7, 29%) and clinical recurrence after tapering medication (1/7, 14%). The duration of hospitalization was similar between the 2 therapeutic modalities (P = .50), with a mean of 3.6 ± 2.6 days. The median recovery time was 7 days (95% CI, 4-14 days) for the conservative group and 9 days (95% CI, 4-20 days) for the surgical group (P = .45). Factors associated with a longer recovery time, regardless of therapeutic approaches (conservative or surgical), included breed type, presence of T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity, and initiation of physical therapy. Specifically, non-chondrodystrophic dogs had a median recovery time of 15 days (95% CI, 3-30 days), compared with 7 days in chondrodystrophic dogs (95% CI, 4-13 days) and 6 days in mixed breed dogs (95% CI, 3-10 days), respectively (P = .04; Figure 2). The presence of T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity was also associated with delayed recovery: dogs with T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity had a median recovery time of 15 days (95% CI, 10-25 days) vs 4 days (95% CI, 3-7 days) in those without this finding (P < .001; Figure 3). Furthermore, dogs that underwent physical therapy had a median recovery time of 10 days (95% CI, 5-21 days) compared with 4 days (95% CI, 3-7 days) for those that did not (P = .003). Additionally, each additional year of age increased the recovery time by 14% (P = .01). Recovery time was not significantly influenced by sex, body weight, onset of clinical signs, neurologic localization, intensity of extruded material, SCC ratio, type of treatment, or surgeon experience.

A post-hoc power analysis was performed. Based on the observed change in clinical assessment scores in the medically treated group (mean 3.3 ± 0.5 at admission vs 2.5 ± 0.7 after 3-7 days), a minimum sample size of 13 subjects was required to achieve statistical power of 80% with a type I error rate of 0.05, using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples. Therefore, the actual sample size included in the study (60 medically treated and 26 surgically treated patients) provided sufficient statistical power for the analyses performed.

Discussion

Our multicenter retrospective study compared the outcomes of dogs with MRI and surgical diagnoses of HNPE affected by severe grades of spinal cord injury, ranging from grade 3 to 5, that were managed either medically or surgically. Both conservative and surgical treatments of HNPE led to favorable outcomes, consistent with the veterinary literature.3,7 However, debate persists regarding the most appropriate treatment option, as previous studies included subjects with HNPE presenting with variable degrees of neurologic deficits, ranging from isolated spinal pain to complete paralysis.3,7 The main finding of our study was that both groups improved over time and no significant difference in neurologic outcome was detected between the 2 groups at 1 and 6 months posttreatment. Regarding conservative treatment, dogs presenting with higher neurologic grades at admission (grades 4 and 5) tended to maintain higher neurologic grade for up to 1 month posttreatment compared with dogs presenting with grade 3. However, surgical intervention was associated with more rapid neurologic improvement in dogs admitted with high neurologic grades at 1 and 6 months follow-up. These findings suggest that surgical intervention should be considered in patients with more severe neurologic impairment, because their recovery appears slower with conservative treatment alone. In addition, our study indicated that conservatively treated non-chondrodystrophic breeds had significantly higher neurologic scores at the long-term follow-up compared with chondrodystrophic and mixed breeds, with the majority of the heavier dogs being primarily non-chondrodystrophic. A possible explanation is that smaller and lighter dogs are easier to manage using a conservative approach. To the best of our knowledge, no other studies have evaluated the correlation between these variables and outcome. Further research involving a larger number of cases is needed to confirm these findings. Our results indicated that dogs conservatively treated for thoracolumbar HNPE showed less favorable long-term outcomes than dogs with cervical HNPE. This finding supports recent research suggesting that thoracolumbar HNPE may benefit from surgical decompression, because it is more frequently painful, often involves lateralized extrusion, and is associated with more severe SCC compared with cervical HNPE.13 In the surgical group, recovery was slightly slower than in the conservative group during the first postoperative week, which may be a consequence of postoperative inflammation or edema. Another prognostic factor was the MRI appearance of the extruded material. Dogs with heterogeneous T2-W signal (likely indicating partially degenerated nucleus pulposus) had worse outcomes at 1 and 6 months, when treated conservatively. These findings align with previous studies suggesting that partially degenerated extruded disc material, particularly when heterogeneous on MRI, may contribute to persistent SCC and secondary inflammatory changes if not surgically removed, potentially leading to delayed or incomplete recovery.3,8,14,15 Moreover, the presence of T2-W intramedullary hyperintensity—suggestive of spinal cord edema, gliosis, or malacia4,14,16— was strongly associated with worse neurologic outcome in both treatment groups and longer recovery time compared with dogs without MRI intramedullary signal changes. These results confirm prior observations that intramedullary hyperintensity is predictive of slower or incomplete recovery.16,17,18 Notably, dogs with heterogeneous disc material, intramedullary T2-W hyperintensity, or both were more likely to experience delayed or incomplete recovery. Although both features were associated with worse outcomes across treatment groups, these effects were especially pronounced in dogs treated conservatively. These findings suggest that the presence of such MRI features should prompt careful consideration before opting for conservative treatment. Unexpectedly, dogs that underwent physical therapy improved more slowly and had longer recovery times compared with dogs that did not undergo rehabilitation. This finding contrasts with a recent retrospective study on acute myelopathies, which found no effect of exercise restriction on outcome.19 Notably, only 7 HNPE cases were included in that study, the majority of which were assigned to the rest rather than the exercise group.19 Our results may be partially explained by selection bias, because more severely affected dogs might have been prioritized for early physical therapy. This factor also raises the question of whether early mobilization before dissolution of the hydrated extruded nucleus pulposus potentially could exacerbate SCC. Additionally, early intensive physical therapy may interfere with annulus fibrosus healing and increase the risk of further nucleus pulposus extrusion. This aspect warrants further prospective investigation. Age was another factor that influenced recovery time: each additional year increased recovery time by 14%, a finding consistent with previous studies suggesting decreased quality of life in older dogs treated conservatively for cervical disc disease.20 This observation may be related to age-associated changes in spinal cord plasticity and regenerative capacity rather than concurrent comorbidities, which were excluded from our study. Notably, this factor has not been extensively addressed in the previous literature on HNPE.3,7,18 Duration of hospitalization and recovery time were approximately 1 week and comparable between treatment groups, which is in agreement with previous studies.3,21 Alongside the previous literature, outcome in our study was also satisfactory, and recovery was complete in the majority of dogs regardless of the treatment category.3,7 However, in our study, cases with unsatisfactory final outcomes were found only in the conservative group. Specifically, 2 dogs were euthanized because of lack of neurologic improvement, emphasizing the importance of careful case selection and clear communication with owners regarding potential complications and prognosis when opting for conservative treatment.

Limitations of our study included its retrospective design and a small sample size, particularly in the thoracolumbar HNPE group. Also, treatment allocation was based on owner preference rather than standardized clinical criteria, which may have led to selection bias. Interobserver variability in neurologic grading and MRI interpretation across multiple centers was also a possible limitation, despite using a standardized protocol. In addition, the study population mainly consisted of dogs with neurologic grade 3 at presentation. Therefore, results in dogs with higher neurologic grades, especially grade 5, which included only 2 cases, should be interpreted with caution and warrant further investigation in larger studies. Another limitation was the lack of a comparative analysis between conservative treatment regimens (eg, corticosteroids vs NSAIDs). Given the multicenter and retrospective design, conservative treatment protocols were not standardized, with variability in drug type, dosage, duration, and timing of administration. This heterogeneity precluded a sufficiently robust statistical comparison between specific pharmacological treatments and limits the interpretation of their individual effects on outcome. Additionally, although the 6-month outcome was assessed when available, some follow-up data were missing or based on owners’ reports. A key limitation of our study is the inclusion of dogs with HNPE affecting different regions of the spinal cord (cervical and thoracolumbar), resulting in a heterogeneous study population, which may have influenced the treatment response. These limitations are consistent with prior systematic reviews of the IVDH literature, which emphasize the scarcity of controlled studies and the risk of bias inherent in retrospective analyses.22

In conclusion, both conservative and surgical approaches can lead to favorable outcomes in dogs with compressive HNPE and severe neurologic deficits. However, surgical decompression may be considered a preferred option, particularly for dogs presenting with severe neurologic deficits, non-chondrodystrophic breeds, or thoracolumbar HNPE. Additionally, opting for surgery may be more advantageous even for patients with a heterogeneous appearance of extruded disc material on T2-W MRI sequences. The role of early physical therapy should be further investigated, because its current early use may delay neurologic improvement. Prospective studies using standardized treatment and rehabilitation protocols are needed to further refine HNPE management in dogs.
