Seroprevalence of dengue virus infection and associated risk factors: a community-based cross-sectional survey in Burkina Faso
Authors: Siaka Debe, Guilllaume Sylvestre Sanou, Isidore Tiandiogo Traore, Harouna Sore, Rene Kinda, Wendyam Gérard Nonkani, Nicolas Ouedraogo, Henri Sansoa Some, Mafama Siribié, Tarama Wendlamita Casimir, Sonia Marie Rouamba, Hermann Jonas Tapsoba, Mireille Ouédraogo, Aristide Ouattara, Salif Sombié, Adama Ganou, Faridatou Tiendrébeogo, Kassoum Yira, Winnie Batango, Moussa Wandaogo Guelbéogo, Léon G Blaise Savadogo, Adama Gansane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSince 2017, dengue fever has been endemic in Burkina Faso. However, epidemiological data to guide effective dengue control policies remain limited in the country. To support decision-making with scientific evidence on the burden of dengue, this study aimed at estimating the seroprevalence of dengue infection in the population and to identify its potential risk factors.MethodsA nationwide, community-based cross-sectional survey including both urban and rural areas was conducted in May 2022 in Burkina Faso. A two-stage stratified cluster sampling design was used to select resident participants aged ≥5 years. Five millilitres of blood samples was obtained from each participant. Seroprevalence estimates were based on an IgG rapid test (sensitivity 95%, specificity 82%) and were not adjusted for misclassification. Data analysis accounted for the complex survey design including clustering, stratification and sampling weights. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with dengue seropositivity.ResultsOverall seroprevalence was 39% (95% CI 37% to 41%) and was consistently higher in urban areas (50%) versus rural areas (24%). Seroprevalence varied significantly by geographic region, with the highest values observed in Centre, Centre-North and North. In multivariable analysis, urban residence was significantly associated with seropositivity (OR 2.35, 95% CI 1.81 to 3.05). Increasing age also showed a strong association, with the highest odds observed among participants aged ≥55 years (OR 10.32, 95% CI 7.42 to 14.35). Individuals with at least a primary education had higher odds of seropositivity (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.55). In contrast, high socioeconomic status (SES) was associated with lower odds of seropositivity (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.71).ConclusionsThe observed seroprevalence suggests moderate transmission; however, interpretation for vaccination policy should be cautious given assay limitations and cross-sectional design. Findings indicate that urban residence, older age, lower SES and high education level are associated with higher dengue seroprevalence.Keywords: Epidemiology, Risk Assessment, Disease Vectors

Introduction

Dengue fever is a tropical disease caused by four antigenically distinct dengue virus (DENV) serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), infection with which does not confer cross-protective immunity.1 The virus is transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti, a mosquito highly adapted to urban environments, and secondarily by A. albopictus.1 In the absence of specific antiviral treatment, dengue prevention relies mainly on vector control measures including larvae breeding destruction, insecticide use, sanitation, mosquito bednets use, etc.

Over recent decades, dengue incidence has increased substantially worldwide, although the true burden remains underestimated due to under-reporting and misclassification. Current estimates suggest approximately 390 million dengue infections occur annually, with nearly 4 billion people at risk globally.1,3

Despite growing recognition of dengue as an emerging public health threat in Africa, its epidemiology remains insufficiently characterised in many countries, including Burkina Faso.2 4 The country reported epidemics in 1925, 2016 and 2017, the latter resulting in over 15 000 suspected cases and multiple deaths.5,7 Current knowledge largely derives from outbreak investigations and facility-based surveillance, approaches that tend to capture symptomatic infections while overlooking the substantial proportion of asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic cases.8,13 As a result, the true population-level burden and extent of silent transmission remain uncertain.

Nationally representative sero-epidemiological data are particularly scarce in West Africa, limiting accurate assessment of cumulative exposure and population immunity. This evidence gap constrains the ability of health authorities to anticipate epidemic risk, prioritise high-transmission areas and allocate resources effectively. Furthermore, existing studies are disproportionately conducted in urban settings, reinforcing the traditional view of dengue as primarily an urban disease while leaving rural transmission dynamics poorly understood. Given rapid environmental change, vector adaptation and increasing human mobility, this assumption may no longer hold.

In addition, the role of sociodemographic and household-level factors in shaping dengue risk has not been adequately explored in African contexts. Without such evidence, prevention strategies often remain generalised rather than targeted towards the most vulnerable populations.

Establishing baseline immunity is also becoming increasingly important as dengue vaccines gain global attention; however, the absence of reliable seroprevalence data complicates informed policy decisions regarding potential vaccine introduction. Indeed, the WHO recommends dengue vaccination in populations with high transmission intensity (seroprevalence ≥60%),14 underscoring the need for population-based data for assessing the country eligibility. Such data remain scarce in Burkina Faso and most African countries.

Addressing these gaps is critical for moving from reactive outbreak response towards proactive dengue prevention and control.

Therefore, this study aimed to provide nationally representative estimates of DENV seroprevalence and to identify key demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with dengue infection. Findings are expected to inform national dengue control strategies, including the potential implementation of targeted vaccination programmes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a national, community-based cross-sectional survey to estimate the seroprevalence of dengue infection and to identify associated risk factors in the general population of Burkina Faso.

Setting

The study was conducted in all the 13 health regions of Burkina Faso ensuring nationwide geographic coverage. These regions represent two geographical divisions with different climatic, socioeconomic and cultural characteristics. Both urban and rural areas were included to capture heterogeneity in dengue transmission related to population density, environmental conditions and access to infrastructure. Burkina Faso has a tropical climate with a long dry season from November to May and a short rainy season from June to October.

Selection of participants

The sampling plan combined stratification and clustering. Stratification was based on the country’s 13 regions. Cluster sampling was based on the sampling frame available from the 2019 general population and housing census (GPHC 2019; population: 20 505 155). This census list includes 1749 and 3970 enumeration areas (EAs) in rural and urban areas, respectively. EAs are geographically delimited administrative census units defined by the national statistical authorities for census and survey purposes, each comprising approximately 200–250 households. All random selection processes used Excel software.

Sample was selected at two levels: EA at the first level and households at the second level. Within each region, two enumeration areas were selected, one in rural area and one in urban area, using a simple random sampling from the GPHC 2019 list. For this purpose, random numbers were generated for each EA per stratum (rural/urban) and then sorted in ascending order. The first EA in each stratum was selected. Within each selected EA, a household census was performed for the study purpose in order to have a complete and updated households list. Then, 62 households were computer-based selected through systematic random sampling in each EA using the complete household list. To achieve this, the sample fraction was set by dividing the number of households in each EA per 62. A random number between one and the sample fraction was generated to select the first household. Finally, the sample fraction was applied to select the next households up to 62 households. Within each selected household, all eligible household members were invited to participate. The target population included individuals aged 5 years and above. Inclusion criteria were a permanent residence of at least 6 months prior to the survey, obtaining written informed consent and agreement to comply with the study requirements. Individuals with medical conditions contraindicating blood collection or any illness requiring urgent medical care were excluded from the study. The sampling weights were calculated using the following formula: weight=1/(probability of selection × response probability).

Outcomes measurement

The primary outcome was DENV seropositivity, defined as the presence of anti-dengue IgG antibodies ascertained by Dengue IgG Rapid Tests. Explanatory variables included demographic, environmental and socioeconomic factors: climatic zones (humid, intermediate and dry zones), area of residence (urban and rural), age, sex (male and female), level of education (none, primary, secondary and tertiary), marital status (single, married, not applicable) and socioeconomic status (SES) (poor, middle and rich status).

Data collection

Trained field workers administered standardised, pre-tested questionnaires through face-to-face interviews. Data were collected electronically using the KoboCollect platform installed on electronic tablets. The household questionnaire captured information on household and socioeconomic characteristics: housing features (wall, ceiling and floor type); main sources of water, fuel and energy; household assets (livestock including cattle, donkeys, horses, goats, fowl, pigs, sheep and household goods such as radios, televisions, telephones, refrigerators, bicycles, motorcycles, tricycles, carts, cars, trucks and canoes). The individual questionnaire captured demographic data, including age, sex, marital status and education level. Clinical assessment included medical history, current signs and symptoms, and axillary body temperature measurement. A total of 5 mL of venous blood was collected using a syringe from each participant under aseptic conditions. For young children, finger-prick samples were used.

Laboratory methods

Serum samples were tested for anti-DENV IgG antibodies using the CTK Onsite Dengue IgG Rapid Test (R0065C) following the manufacturer’s instructions. This test is developed by CTK Biotech, Inc. (USA) and has sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 82%.15 16 To perform the test, 5 µL of blood was applied to the sample well of the test cassette, followed by one drop of buffer added to the buffer well. Test results were read after 20–25 min and interpreted as IgG-positive or IgG-negative on a valid test.

Quality control procedures

All laboratory analyses were performed by trained laboratory personnel following standard operating procedures. Each test was considered valid only when the control line was visible; tests with invalid control results were repeated using a new test cassette. Test kits were stored and used in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, including temperature and expiry date monitoring.

Bias mitigation

Selection biases were minimised by applying a random selection approach and predefined eligibility criteria. Only individuals meeting all inclusion criteria and no exclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. Observation biases were reduced through prospective data collection, using standardised electronic questionnaires and applying standard assessment approaches to all participants. All study team was trained on the protocol, study procedures and data collection tools. All statistical analyses accounted for complex survey design to minimise errors in estimations.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using standard formulas for prevalence estimation in complex survey design, assuming an expected dengue seroprevalence of 50% in households, a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 5%, a design effect of 2 and an anticipated non-response rate of 20%. Considering 5.4 residents per household (GPHC 2019 data), this was expected to yield a final sample size of approximately 5255 participants nationwide, allowing precise national and regional estimates.

Statistical analysis

The dataset was first compiled in an Excel spreadsheet and then imported into Stata/SE (V.17; StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis. Missing values related to Rapid Diagnosis Test (RDT) results were excluded from the analysis. Before analysis, the survey data design was set up including clustering, stratification and sampling weights. Principal component analysis was used to classify SES based on possession of goods and house features. Descriptive statistics summarised demographic and socioeconomic characteristics in frequency and mean for categorical and numerical variables respectively. Observed prevalences were provided with 95% CIs. Sensitivity analysis was performed with the Rogan-Gladen estimator using the manufacturer-reported sensitivity and specificity to provide corrected prevalences.17 In univariable analysis, associations between explanatory factors and DENV seropositivity were evaluated using the χ2 test adjusted on design effect. Backward multivariate logistic regression model was used to assess independent associations with dengue seropositivity. Variables with p<0.20 in univariable analysis were considered for multivariable models. A parsimonious model was then developed, retaining only variables that remained statistically significant. Results were presented with adjusted ORs and 95% CIs. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. After regression, multicollinearity was checked ensuring moderate correlation.

Patient and public involvement

Study participants or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Participants flow

As depicted in figure 1, a total of 6006 residents from 1601 households were recruited across the 13 regions, including 3001 individuals from rural areas and 3005 from urban areas. Of these, 481 individuals were excluded due to refusal to participate (380), blood collection failure (60), severe illness at the time of survey (25) and missing individual-level data (16). The final analytical sample therefore included 5525 participants, with an almost equal distribution between rural (2751 participants) and urban (2774 participants) areas.

Population characteristics

A total of 5525 participants were included in the analysis (table 1). After weighting, just over half of participants resided in urban areas. Females accounted for the majority of the study population. The population was predominantly young, with a mean age of 27 years (95% CI 25 to 29) and children aged 5–14 years represented the largest age group. More participants had no formal education, while only a few had attained tertiary education. The distribution of marital status was balanced between single and married individuals. The findings indicated that more participants belonged to the lowest socioeconomic group.

Overall dengue seroprevalence

Considerable geographical variation was observed across regions, with the highest seroprevalence in the Centre region followed by regions Centre-North, North and Centre-West (table 2). The lowest seroprevalence was recorded in the East region (table 2). Nationally, seroprevalence in urban settings was significantly higher compared to rural settings (table 3), and across almost all regions, similar patterns were observed (table 2).

Dengue seroprevalence by demographic and socioeconomic factors

Table 3 presents the crude and corrected dengue IgG seroprevalence by demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. Corrected seroprevalence estimates were consistently lower than crude estimates across all categories, though both showed similar patterns. Seroprevalence was substantially higher in urban areas than in rural areas. Females had slightly higher seroprevalence than males, but CIs overlapped. A strong age gradient was observed with a seroprevalence that increased progressively in children aged 5–14 years to those aged 55 years and older, with a sharp rise starting in the 15–24 years’ group. By education level, the highest seroprevalence was among individuals with tertiary education, followed by those with secondary. Regarding marital status, seroprevalence was highest among married individuals, followed by single individuals, and lowest among those classified as non-applicable. A clear inverse socioeconomic gradient was observed indicating that the poorest group had the highest seroprevalence, followed by the middle group, while the richest group had the lowest.

Age showed a strong positive gradient with DENV IgG seropositivity in both univariable and multivariable analyses. Using the 5–14 years’ age group as the reference category, the odds of seropositivity increased progressively with age. All age groups above 14 years showed significantly higher odds of seropositivity compared with the youngest age group, with the highest odds observed among those aged 55 years and older.

The association between education level and DENV IgG seropositivity changed notably between univariable and multivariable analyses. In univariable analysis, compared with individuals with no formal education, those with primary education had lower odds of seropositivity, while those with secondary education had higher odds, and those with tertiary education had the highest odds. After adjustment for other factors, the direction of association changed for primary education; individuals with primary education had significantly higher odds of seropositivity compared with those with no education. Secondary education remained significantly associated with higher odds. However, tertiary education was no longer statistically significant in the multivariable model.

SES showed an inverse association with DENV IgG seropositivity. Using the poor category as the reference, both intermediate and rich socioeconomic groups had significantly lower odds of seropositivity in univariable analysis. These associations persisted after multivariable adjustment, although the magnitudes were attenuated. compared with the poor group, the intermediate and rich groups had lower odds of being DENV IgG seropositive.

Discussion

Key results

This study aimed to provide estimates of DENV exposure and to identify key demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with DENV IgG seropositivity using the CTK Onsite Dengue IgG Rapid Test. Overall dengue IgG seroprevalence was 39%, with substantial geographical heterogeneity across regions. Corrected seroprevalence was 27%, indicating thus the crude estimates likely included 12% of false positive. Seroprevalence was consistently higher in urban than in rural areas, both nationally and across regions. The highest seroprevalence was observed in the Centre, Centre-North and North regions. Urban residence was associated with dengue IgG seropositivity, with more than a twofold increase in odds compared with rural residence. Seropositivity increased strongly as age increased, reaching more than 10-fold higher odds among individuals aged 55 years or older compared with children aged 5–14 years. Higher SES was associated with lower odds of dengue seropositivity. Individuals with primary or secondary education were more exposed to dengue infection. Sex and marital status were not associated with dengue seropositivity.

Interpretation

Study findings highlight that dengue fever has emerged as a major public health concern in the country, alongside malaria, consistent with evidence from multiple previous studies.118,23 Dengue infection may contribute significantly to the onset, the persistence and the severity of fever in patients suffering from other infectious diseases, mainly malaria.24,27 A previous study reported a high prevalence of malaria-dengue coinfection across African regions ranging from 16 per 1000 febrile cases in West Africa to 47 per 1000 in Central Africa, with a marked increase over time from 0.9% during 2008–2013 to 5.5% during 2018–2021.28 In the absence of specific antiviral treatment for dengue, such coinfections are likely to complicate patient management. These findings underscore the importance for healthcare professionals to consider both malaria and dengue in the differential diagnosis of febrile illness. They also highlight the need for policy makers to strengthen health system capacity through training and provision of diagnostic tools for dengue infections. Following the 2017 outbreak, Burkina Faso adopted national guidelines for dengue case management; however, wider dissemination and comprehensive training of health workers remain necessary. Community sensitisation on dengue prevention, symptoms and its potential coexistence with malaria is also essential.

The results indicate that more than a third of the population in Burkina Faso has been exposed to the DENV. This finding is higher than the estimate reported by Ouattara et al in their study conducted in Ouagadougou, which focused on febrile patients and found an overall dengue seroprevalence of 16.5%, with anti-dengue IgG detected in 2.7% of cases.20 In contrast, our result is lower than the 75.4% seroprevalence reported in another Burkina Faso-based study that included patients with hepatitis.22 However, because these estimates are derived from clinical records rather than community-based samples, they inherently underestimate the true disease burden. Hospital data primarily capture symptomatic and often severe cases, which precludes accurate prevalence estimates. Consequently, while these figures reflect trends in hospital burden, they do not offer a representative picture of a broader epidemiological landscape.

The study estimated overall seroprevalence remains below the WHO threshold of 60% defining settings with high transmission intensity.14 With it, findings suggest moderate transmission, but dengue vaccine policy requires confirmation using standardised WHO thresholds. Despite this, adults living in highly urbanised cities may be suitable candidates for targeted dengue vaccination programmes. In parallel, strengthening vector control interventions, including environmental sanitation, reduction of mosquito breeding sites around households, appropriate insecticide use and installation of protective screens, remains a priority for the entire country as routine activities.

Higher seroprevalence in urban compared with rural areas across most regions and climate zones suggests that urban environments play a central role in sustaining dengue transmission. This finding is consistent with the ecology of Aedes vectors, which thrive in densely populated settings with abundant artificial breeding sites.222 29,31 Poor wastewater management, accumulation of water containers and household gardening practices most occurring in urban settings may favour mosquito proliferation and increase human–vector contact.29 30 However, some studies have reported homogeneous dengue exposure across rural and urban settings, suggesting that dengue transmission may no longer be exclusively urban.32 Further research is needed to clarify these patterns in different ecological contexts.

Dengue seropositivity increased steadily with age, showing a strong dose–response relationship and highlighting that age remains the strongest independent predictor of dengue exposure, in line with previous studies.819 22 32,35 This pattern is indicative of cumulative lifetime exposure and supports the presence of long-standing endemic transmission rather than sporadic outbreaks of dengue in the country context. Higher exposure among adults may reflect increased outdoor activities, occupational exposure, mobility and travel to dengue-endemic areas. Adults may spend more time in environments where mosquitoes are most prevalent, such as gardens, markets or construction sites. Ongoing urbanisation, climate changes and changes in mosquito populations may further amplify adult exposure, particularly in urban settings.

Education level and SES were independently associated with seropositivity. Primary and secondary education levels were associated with increased odds of dengue exposure, while intermediate and high SES appeared protective. These associations may reflect complex interactions between mobility, housing quality, living environments and access to vector control measures, although residual confounding factors cannot be excluded.

Strengths and limitations

This study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between explanatory factors and dengue seropositivity. Second, sampling was restricted to few clusters in the first stage, with only two clusters sampled per region. This may lead to unstable variance estimates and inflation, thereby reducing the precision of the estimates. Third, the use of anti-dengue IgG antibodies reflects past exposure and does not capture recent or acute infections. Consequently, infections detectable by IgM antibodies or NS1 antigen may have been missed, potentially misestimating the seroprevalence. This limitation is compounded by the low specificity (82%) of the rapid diagnostic tests used, which may also have cross-reacted with antibodies from related flaviviruses such as Zika or yellow fever, especially in regions where multiple flaviviruses co-circulate. Although the crude seroprevalence estimates are reported for comparability with other studies, sensitivity analysis using the Rogan-Gladen estimator indicated a lower true prevalence, highlighting the potential for misclassification bias due to imperfect test characteristics. Thus, the corrected estimates likely provide a more accurate reflection of population immunity.

Despite these limitations, this study provided nationally representative estimates of DENV seroprevalence in Burkina Faso, offering a comprehensive assessment of population-level exposure in a dengue endemic setting. The community-based design allowed detection of asymptomatic and past infections that are typically missed by routine facility-based surveillance systems. In addition, the study identified demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with dengue seropositivity at national level, thereby generating robust evidence to support evidence-based dengue surveillance, vector control and vaccination strategies.

Generalisability

The findings of this study are broadly generalisable to the whole population of Burkina Faso, given the community-based design, large sample size and national coverage across diverse regions, urban and rural settings, and climate zones. The use of a multistage probability sampling strategy enhances external validity and supports inference at the national level. However, some factors should be considered when extrapolating these results beyond the study context. First, dengue transmission dynamics are strongly influenced by local ecological, urbanisation and vector-control conditions. While the observed urban–rural gradient and age-related accumulation of exposure are likely applicable to other rapidly urbanising West African settings, seroprevalence levels may differ in countries with different histories of dengue circulation, vector density or housing infrastructure. Second, the cross-sectional design captures cumulative exposure rather than current transmission intensity, which may limit comparability with settings experiencing recent outbreaks or emerging transmission. Despite these considerations, the consistency of patterns observed across regions and climate zones suggests that the key associations (particularly the increased burden in urban areas and older age groups) are likely generalisable to similar epidemiological and socio-environmental contexts in sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion

This nationwide study demonstrates substantial exposure to DENV in Burkina Faso, with nearly half of the population showing evidence of past infection. Dengue transmission remains of moderate intensity but is widespread, with marked variation by region, climate zone, age and place of residence. Urban residence and increasing age were the strongest independent predictors of DENV IgG seropositivity, highlighting the central role of urban environments and cumulative exposure in sustaining dengue transmission. Although current overall seroprevalence level does not meet WHO criteria for dengue vaccine introduction, the high burden of exposure underscores dengue as an important public health concern alongside malaria. Adults residing in urban areas most affected may need dengue vaccine as supplementary strategy. Strengthening integrated surveillance, improving diagnostic capacity, expanding health worker training and reinforcing vector control interventions (particularly in urban settings) are essential. Targeted community sensitisation and sustained implementation of national dengue management guidelines will be critical to reducing dengue-related morbidity and improving febrile illness management in Burkina Faso.
