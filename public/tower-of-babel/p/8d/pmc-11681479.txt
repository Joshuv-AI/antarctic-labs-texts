Antiphospholipid Antibodies Modify the Prognostic Value of Baseline Platelet Count for Clinical Outcomes After Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Yinan Wang, Pinni Yang, Zhengbao Zhu, Hao Peng, Xiaoqing Bu, Qingyun Xu, Aili Wang, Jing Chen, Tan Xu, Yonghong Zhang, Jiang He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) have been reported to be involved in platelet‐mediated thrombosis and inflammation, but the impact on the prognosis of ischemic stroke remains unclear. We aimed to examine whether the association between baseline platelet count (PLT) and long‐term clinical outcomes within 2 years after ischemic stroke onset is modulated by aPLs.Methods and ResultsA total of 2938 patients with ischemic stroke were included in this prospective cohort study. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between the baseline PLT stratified by aPLs status and 2‐year clinical outcomes after stroke onset, and an interaction effect between PLT and aPLs on clinical outcomes was tested by likelihood ratio test. There was a significant interaction effect of aPLs and PLT on recurrent stroke (P interaction=0.002) and cardiovascular events (P interaction=0.001) within 2 years after stroke onset. After multivariate adjustment, high PLT was associated with increased risks of recurrent stroke (hazard ratio [HR], 2.78 [95% CI, 1.03–7.45]; P trend=0.039) and cardiovascular events (HR, 2.58 [95% CI, 1.12–5.90]; P trend=0.024) when 2 extreme tertiles were compared among patients with aPL positive, but not among those with aPL negative.ConclusionsThe aPLs had a modifying effect on the association between PLT and clinical outcomes within 2 years after ischemic stroke onset. Increased PLT was associated with recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events after ischemic stroke onset among patients with aPL positive, but not in those with aPL negative.Keywords: antiphospholipid antibodies, ischemic stroke, platelet count, prognosisSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Secondary Prevention, Ischemic Stroke

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and long‐term disability in China and worldwide, 1 , 2 and ischemic stroke is the most common subtype. 3 Despite intense preventative and therapeutic efforts, the rates of recurrent cardiovascular events, disability, and death after ischemic stroke remain high. 1 Therefore, accurate risk assessment for the prognosis of ischemic stroke at admission will be of great clinical significance in timely identifying patients at high risk of adverse prognosis and in guiding physicians to actively monitor patients and implement therapeutic interventions.

Platelets participate in the process of thrombosis and inflammation, which plays an essential role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and stroke. 4 , 5 , 6 Several studies also showed that platelet count (PLT) was associated with adverse prognosis after ischemic cerebrovascular diseases. 7 , 8 , 9 Antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) consist of a heterogeneous group of autoantibodies targeting phospholipid binding proteins. 10 Previous epidemiological studies suggested that aPL positive would be associated with adverse prognosis of stroke. 11 , 12 , 13 aPLs could contribute to inflammation, thrombosis, and atherogenesis by promoting platelet activation and aggregation. 14 , 15 However, to date, there have been no epidemiological studies exploring the effect of aPLs on the association between PLT and prognosis of ischemic stroke. Considering the potential close link between aPLs and platelets in thrombosis and inflammatory mechanisms, it is imperative to assess the role of aPLs in the relationship between platelets and prognosis of ischemic stroke in well‐designed prospective studies with large sample sizes.

Herein, we prospectively investigated the association between baseline PLT stratified by aPL status and clinical outcomes within 2 years after ischemic stroke onset on the basis of CATIS (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke).

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Participants

The study participants in this prospective cohort study were derived from CATIS, a multicenter, single‐blind, blinded end points, randomized clinical trial carried out in 26 hospitals across China from August 2009 to May 2013. Details of CATIS design and methods have been described previously. 16 Briefly, CATIS recruited 4071 patients with ischemic stroke aged >22 years within 48 hours of symptom onset and with an elevated systolic blood pressure (BP) between 140 and 220 mm Hg. Diagnosis of ischemic stroke was confirmed by either computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain. Patients with a BP ≥220/120 mm Hg, cerebrovascular stenosis (≥70%), acute myocardial infarction, severe heart failure, unstable angina, atrial fibrillation, aortic dissection, resistant hypertension, deep coma, or receiving intravenous thrombolytic therapy were excluded. In the present study, 1133 patients were further excluded because they did not offer blood samples or we failed to determine their baseline aPLs or PLT. Finally, a total of 2938 patients with ischemic stroke were included in this analysis (Figure 1).

This study was approved by the institutional review boards at Soochow University in China and Tulane University in the United States, along with ethical committees at the 26 participating hospitals. Written consent was obtained from all study participants or their immediate family members.

Data Collection and Measurements

Baseline data on demographic characteristics, medical history, medication history, clinical features, and therapy after admission were collected at the time of enrollment using a standard questionnaire by trained interviewers. Stroke severity was evaluated with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) by trained neurologists at baseline. 17 Three BP measurements were obtained at baseline by trained nurses when the patient was in the supine position, using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer according to a common method adapted from procedures recommended by the American Heart Association. 18 The mean of the 3 BP records was used in the analyses. TOAST (Trial of Org 10 172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria were applied to classify the ischemic stroke subtypes as large‐artery atherosclerosis (thrombotic), cardiac embolism (embolic), and small‐vessel occlusion (lacunar) on the basis of the clinical features and imaging data of the patients. 19

Blood samples were drawn within 24 hours of hospital admission after at least an 8‐hour fast. Routine laboratory determinations (blood glucose, blood lipids, PLT, etc) were conducted for all enrolled patients in each participating hospital at admission. Serum aPLs were tested centrally in the Central Laboratory of the School of Public Health at Soochow University with commercially available ELISA kits (INOVA Diagnostics, San Diego, CA). We measured 3 types of aPLs: antiphosphatidylserine antibodies (aPS), β2‐glycoprotein I‐dependent anticardiolipin antibodies (β2GPI), and anticardiolipin antibodies (aCLs). Patients were considered aPL positive if ≥1 of the 3 antibodies exceeded the cutoff values. Cutoff values for positive were IgG–aPS ≥11 GPS, IgG‐β2GPI >20 SGU, and IgG‐aCL >20 GPL, respectively. The aPLs were measured with stringent quality control procedures, and laboratory technicians were blinded to the baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of the study participants.

Outcome Assessment

The participants were followed up in person at 3 months, 1 year, and 2 years after stroke onset by trained neurologists who were unaware of the treatment assignment. The outcomes in this study included recurrent stroke, cardiovascular events (ie, vascular deaths, recurrent nonfatal stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction, hospitalized and treated angina, hospitalized and treated congestive heart failure, and hospitalized and treated peripheral artery disease), and death. All reported events were reviewed and adjudicated by the outcome assessment committee, which was blinded to treatment assignment. The follow‐up time was calculated from the date of stroke onset to the date of event occurrence or the date of the last follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

First, all included patients were divided into aPL‐positive and aPL‐negative subgroups. Then, the patients in each aPL subgroup were categorized into 3 groups according to the subgroup‐specific tertiles of PLT. Baseline characteristics among PLT tertile groups in each aPL subgroup were compared by Student t test (normal distribution) or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (skewed distribution) for continuous variables and χ 2 test for categorical variables.

The proportional hazards assumption was investigated by Schoenfeld residuals, and global tests for all Cox models showed no violations of the proportional hazards assumption (all P>0.05). The assumption of linearity was confirmed for all continuous covariates included in Cox models using Martingale residuals. Deviance residuals, symmetric transformation of the Martingale residuals, showed no influential observations in Cox models. Based on the above 3 methods, the assumptions of the Cox proportional hazards model in this study were deemed plausible. Therefore, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between the baseline PLT stratified by aPL status and 2‐year adverse clinical outcomes. Hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% CIs were calculated for upper tertiles of PLT with the lowest tertile as a reference. Tests for linear trend in risk across PLT tertiles were performed using the PLT category as continuous variables in each aPL subgroup. On the basis of our prior knowledge and baseline characteristics of the study participants, 3 multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were built. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was further adjusted for current cigarette smoking, current alcohol drinking, history of hyperlipidemia, time from onset to hospitalization, baseline NIHSS, admission systolic BP, body mass index, blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, ischemic stroke subtypes, and antihypertensive therapy after admission based on model 1. Considering that antiplatelet therapy might play a role in the association between aPLs and PLT with the risk of clinical outcomes after stroke, model 3 was further adjusted for antiplatelet therapy after admission based on model 2. To examine the effect modified by aPLs, the interaction between aPLs and PLT on study outcomes was tested using the likelihood ratio test in Cox proportional hazards regression models with multiplicative interaction terms, adjusting for the same covariates as model 3.

Restricted cubic splines with Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to explore the dose–response relationships between PLT and 2‐year adverse clinical outcomes after multivariable adjustment with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the subgroup‐specific distribution of PLT, the default parameters of the ‘rms’ package in R software [R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria]), 20 , 21 and were further tested with different numbers and locations of knots to verify the robustness of results. In addition, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and continuous net reclassification indexes (NRI) were generated to evaluate the improvement in predictive power of adverse clinical outcomes after stroke onset when baseline PLT was added to the basic model with conventional risk factors (covariates in model 3) in each aPL subgroup. 22 Finally, we further excluded the patients with a history of coronary heart disease in the sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our findings.

A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R software version 4.1.1.

Baseline PLT and Clinical Outcomes

As shown in Table 2, the incidences of recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events significantly increased as baseline PLT increased from the lowest tertile to the highest tertile among patients with aPL positive. In the aPL‐positive group, the multivariable‐adjusted HRs for the recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events within 2 years after stroke were 2.78 (95% CI, 1.03–7.45) and 2.58 (95% CI, 1.12–5.90), respectively, in comparison of the highest tertile with the lowest tertile. After adjustment for multiple potential covariates (model 2), the HRs for the recurrent stroke (P trend=0.037) and cardiovascular events (P trend=0.024) within 2 years after stroke onset increased significantly with elevated baseline PLT in the aPL‐positive group. The significant associations remained when further adjusted for antiplatelet therapy after admission (model 3). However, there was no significant association between baseline PLT and adverse clinical outcomes in patients with aPL negative (all P>0.05). aPLs significantly modified the prognostic value of baseline PLT for the recurrent stroke (P interaction=0.002) and cardiovascular events (P interaction=0.001) within 2 years after stroke onset.

Multivariate‐adjusted restricted cubic spline regression models showed positive linear dose–response relationships between baseline PLT and the risks of recurrent stroke (P linearity=0.034) and cardiovascular events (P linearity=0.015) within 2 years after stroke onset among patients with aPL positive (Figure 2). Restricted cubic splines with different numbers and locations of knots were consistent with the main analysis (Figures S1 and S2). In the aPL‐positive group, when PLT tertiles were added to the conventional model, risk prediction for the recurrent stroke was improved significantly (continuous NRI, 36.59% [95% CI, 2.83%–70.35%]; P=0.039). Similarly, adding PLT tertiles to the conventional model in the aPL‐positive group also significantly improved IDI by 0.88% (95% CI, 0.15%–1.61%; P=0.019) and continuous NRI by 35.86% (95% CI, 6.88%–64.85%; P=0.018; Table 3). After further excluding the patients with a history of coronary heart disease, our findings were consistent with the original results, confirming the robustness of our results (Table S1).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale prospective study to assess the prognostic value of the baseline PLT for long‐term clinical outcomes among patients with different aPL status after ischemic stroke onset. In this study, based on data of the CATIS, a large randomized clinical trial with strict quality controls in data collection and outcome assessment, we observed that increased baseline PLT was independently associated with increased risks of recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events within 2 years after stroke onset among patients with aPL positive, but not in those with aPL negative. This study highlighted the modification role of aPLs on the association between baseline PLT and clinical outcomes after ischemic stroke, suggesting that aPL positive may reinforce the association between PLT and adverse prognosis after ischemic stroke.

Previous studies on the association between PLT and prognosis of ischemic cerebrovascular diseases yielded inconsistent results, 7 , 8 , 9 , 23 , 24 although most studies supported increased platelets as a risk factor. For example, a cohort involving 16 842 patients with ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack from the China National Stroke Registry II revealed a U‐shaped relationship between baseline PLT and adverse prognosis within 1 year after ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, 7 but the association of increased PLT with adverse prognosis was stronger than that of decreased PLT with adverse prognosis. In addition, a retrospective cohort study based on 692 patients with ischemic stroke showed that higher PLT at admission was related to a higher 30‐day mortality rate after ischemic stroke. 8 Similarly, another retrospective cohort study including 9230 patients with acute ischemic stroke also demonstrated that increased PLT at admission was associated with increased death within 30 days or 90 days after stroke onset. 9 Antiplatelet therapy has been recommended as a major strategy to reduce the risk of adverse prognosis for patients with ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. 25 , 26 In contrast, in a post hoc analysis of a multicenter randomized clinical trial (DIRECT‐MT [Direct Intra‐Arterial Thrombectomy to Revascularize AIS Patients With Large Vessel Occlusion Efficiently in Chinese Tertiary Hospitals]) incorporating 654 patients with acute ischemic stroke, patients with higher baseline PLT had a decreased risk of adverse functional outcomes at 3 months after stroke. 23 However, a prospective study with 281 patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke found that PLT had no significant effect on prognosis of ischemic stroke at 30 days after stroke. 24 It must be mentioned that the above studies included mixed types of stroke, 7 , 23 used small sample sizes, 8 , 23 , 24 or had a relatively short follow‐up period. 8 , 9 , 23 , 24 Furthermore, these studies did not consider the effect of aPLs in investigating the relationship between PLT and prognosis of ischemic stroke.

The presence of aPLs is the laboratory feature of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), an autoimmune disorder characterized by the occurrence of thrombosis. 10 Ischemic cerebrovascular event is the most common arterial thrombotic manifestation in APS. 27 , 28 In addition, antiplatelet therapy was considered as an effective option for preventing the recurrence of arterial thrombosis in patients with APS. 29 Population‐based studies have shown that aPLs are independent risk factors for ischemic stroke, 30 , 31 , 32 and are also related to adverse clinical outcomes after acute ischemic stroke. 11 , 12 , 13 In vitro and in vivo studies using animal models, aPLs could promote platelet activation and enhance platelet‐mediated thrombosis. 33 , 34 , 35 The above evidence based on the experiment and population studies suggests that platelets play an important role in the pathogenesis of thrombosis and thrombosis recurrence in patients with aPL positive. In this prospective study including 2938 patients with ischemic stroke from CATIS, we observed a significant interaction between aPLs and PLT on the incidence of recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events within 2 years after stroke onset. Increased baseline PLT increased the risk of adverse clinical outcomes among patients with aPL positive but not in those with aPL negative. Nevertheless, previous studies have found that thrombocytopenia occurs in 20% to 50% of patients with confirmed APS, and thrombocytopenia has been reported to potentially increase the risk of thrombosis in patients with APS. 36 We speculated that the discrepancy between the results of the present study and those previously observed in patients with APS might be due to the following 2 main reasons: First, relevant studies have shown that there are 2 subtypes of aPLs, mainly β2GPI and aCL, that can cause thrombocytopenia by destroying and depleting platelets. 15 However, the number of patients with stroke with β2GPI positive or aCL positive in this study was relatively small. Second, the patients with ischemic stroke in this study tested positive for aPLs on admission, but they were not definitively diagnosed with persistent aPL positivity, which was different from the situation of patients with APS. Our findings showed that patients with ischemic stroke with aPL positive and elevated PLT on admission were at a greater risk of poor prognosis, suggesting that the coexistence of aPL positive and increased PLT may promote pathological progression after ischemic stroke by inducing thrombosis, which in turn increases the risks of stroke recurrence and vascular events.

Our findings have important scientific significance and clinical implications for better understanding pathological processes after ischemic stroke onset and further improving the prognosis. The mechanisms by which aPLs and platelets synergize to promote adverse outcomes after ischemic stroke may be explained as follows: aPLs can lead to thrombosis and atherosclerosis by directly promoting platelet activation 14 and are also able to increase the risk of thrombosis by upregulating the plasma active von Willebrand factor levels to mediate platelet adhesion and aggregation. 15 In addition, aPLs induce inflammatory reactions or activate the complement system to further stimulate platelet activation. 37 , 38 According to the latest Guideline for the Prevention of Stroke in Patients with Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack, although systematic aPL testing is not necessary for all patients with stroke, aPL testing is recommended for patients with stroke with a history of thrombosis or rheumatological disease. 39 Of note, our findings suggested that simultaneously testing for aPLs and PLT during the acute phase of ischemic stroke could help identify the patients at high risk of adverse prognosis. Moreover, this study provided further insights into the effective identification of adverse prognosis after stroke and personalized management for patients with stroke. In the future, these results need to be further validated in well‐designed prospective cohort studies and clinical trials with larger sample sizes.

This study also has several limitations. First, the patients with BP ≥220/120 mm Hg, severe cardiovascular disease, or intravenous thrombolytic therapy were excluded due to CATIS design. Therefore, there might exist a selection bias. However, the baseline characteristics of participants in the present study were similar to those from the China National Stroke Registry, 40 indicating that the selection bias may be minimal. Second, aPLs and PLT were tested only once at baseline without serial measurements, although aPLs were reported to be relatively stable within the first week after stroke onset. 41 Further evaluation of the effect of dynamic changes in aPLs and PLT on prognosis of ischemic stroke is needed in the future. In addition, with only 1 measurement of aPLs at admission, we were unable to determine whether the observed elevation of aPLs in these patients with ischemic stroke was just transient or indicative of APS. The exact differences between transient aPL positivity and persistent aPL positivity (eg, APS) on the role of the relationship between PLT and stroke prognosis also need to be further studied in the future. Third, other platelet‐related parameters that might be associated with the risk and prognosis of stroke were not measured in this study, such as mean platelet volume. 42 In the future, the role of aPLs in the association between other platelet parameters and poor prognosis of ischemic stroke needs to be further explored to better understand the impact of aPLs on the relationship between platelets and prognosis of ischemic stroke. Fourth, in the subgroup analysis for 3 types of aPLs, we found that increased PLT was associated with increased risks of adverse clinical outcomes after stroke among patients with aPS positive, which was consistent with the results of the main analysis. However, the number of patients with stroke who were β2GPI positive or aCL positive was too small to generate reliable results. Whether the exact role of different aPL subtypes in the relationship between PLT and stroke prognosis is different remains to be further investigated in studies with sufficient sample sizes for different aPL subtypes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the aPLs had a modifying effect on the association between PLT and clinical outcomes within 2 years after ischemic stroke onset. Increased PLT was associated with recurrent stroke and cardiovascular events within 2 years after ischemic stroke onset among patients with aPL positive, but not in those with aPL negative. In the future, our findings need to be further validated in well‐designed prospective cohort studies and clinical trials with larger sample sizes.
