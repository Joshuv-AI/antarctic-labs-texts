Silent pulmonary tuberculosis in patients with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus: Pathogenesis, clinical implications, and diagnostic challenges
Authors: Fumu Wang, Haiming Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coexistence of tuberculosis (TB) and diabetes mellitus (DM) presents a significant global health challenge, particularly in patients with poorly controlled glycemic levels. Subclinical or silent pulmonary TB in diabetic individuals—characterized by the absence of classic symptoms—often leads to delayed diagnosis, atypical clinical presentations, and suboptimal treatment outcomes. This review explores the underlying pathogenesis, unique clinical manifestations, and diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in this high-risk population. We highlight the interplay between hyperglycemia and immune dysfunction, the limitations of current diagnostic tools, and the need for tailored management strategies to improve TB control in diabetic patients.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Diabetes mellitus, Asymptomatic TB, Immune dysfunction, Diagnosis, Treatment challenges

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious cause of mortality worldwide, while diabetes mellitus—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—has reached epidemic proportions [1]. The bidirectional relationship between TB and DM exacerbates disease severity, complicates diagnosis, and increases the risk of poor treatment outcomes [2]. A critical yet understudied subgroup is patients with poorly controlled diabetes who develop asymptomatic or silent pulmonary TB. The term “silent TB” refers to subclinical infection with no typical TB symptoms (e.g., cough, fever, hemoptysis), though non-specific symptoms such as fatigue or mild weight loss may be present and are often misattributed to diabetes itself. Due to the absence of classic features, diagnosis is often delayed, leading to advanced disease and higher transmission rates. This review examines the mechanisms behind silent TB in hyperglycemic patients, atypical clinical presentations, and the challenges in diagnosis and treatment.

Pathogenesis: hyperglycemia and immune dysfunction

1. Impaired Innate and Adaptive Immunity

Chronic hyperglycemia in DM patients leads to immune dysfunction, including:

Neutrophil and macrophage dysfunction: Reduced phagocytic activity and impaired intracellular killing of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). Hyperglycemia promotes the formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which bind to receptors on immune cells and further suppress phagocytosis and chemotaxis[3].Altered cytokine profiles: Decreased Th1 responses (IFN-γ, TNF-α) and elevated anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10), facilitating Mtb persistence. Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β are often dysregulated in diabetic individuals, contributing to chronic inflammation and impaired bacterial clearance [4].

2. Microvascular Complications and Hypoxia

Diabetic microangiopathy reduces blood flow to lung tissue, creating a hypoxic environment that promotes Mtb survival. Additionally, poor glycemic control accelerates lung damage, increasing susceptibility to TB infection and progression [5].

3. Clinical Presentation: Why Silent TB is Common in Diabetics

Unlike immunocompetent individuals, diabetic patients with pulmonary TB frequently present with:Minimal or absent respiratory symptoms (e.g., no cough or hemoptysis) [6].Atypical radiographic findings (lower lung zone infiltrates, non-cavitary lesions).Non-specific constitutional symptoms (fatigue, mild weight loss) often attributed to diabetes rather than TB [7].This atypical presentation contributes to diagnostic delays, allowing for disease progression and increased transmission risk.

Diagnostic challenges

1. Limitations of Conventional Diagnostic Tools

Sputum smear microscopy: Lower sensitivity due to reduced bacillary load in asymptomatic patients [8].Tuberculin skin test (TST) and IGRA: False-negative results due to diabetic immunosuppression [9].Chest X-ray and CT: Atypical patterns (e.g., non-cavitary lesions, lower lobe involvement) may be misinterpreted.

2. Need for Advanced Diagnostics

Molecular assays (GeneXpert MTB/RIF, LPA): Higher sensitivity but limited in paucibacillary cases [10].Biomarker research: Exploration of host (e.g., inflammatory cytokines) and bacterial (e.g., Mtb-specific RNA signatures) markers for early detection.

3. Potential Screening Strategies for High-Risk Diabetic Populations

Given the high risk of silent TB in poorly controlled diabetics, targeted screening protocols are warranted. Annual chest radiographs may be considered for patients with HbA1c > 7.5 % or long-standing diabetes, especially in TB-endemic regions. Additionally, symptom screening—though limited—should include specific inquiries about subtle respiratory changes or unexplained fatigue.

Treatment challenges

1. Drug-Drug Interactions and Toxicity

Rifampicin accelerates the metabolism of oral hypoglycemic agents, worsening glycemic control [11].Increased risk of hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy due to overlapping drug toxicities [12].

2. Prolonged Treatment Duration and Relapse Risk

Poor glycemic control correlates with delayed sputum conversion and higher relapse rates [13].Extended TB regimens may be necessary in diabetic patients with persistent hyperglycemia.

3. Integrated Management Strategies

Glycemic optimization: Tight glucose control (HbA1c <7 %) improves TB treatment outcomes [14].Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM): Adjusting anti-TB drug doses based on pharmacokinetic variability in diabetics.Multidisciplinary care: Coordinated management between endocrinologists and TB specialists to address comorbidities [15].

Conclusion and future directions

Silent pulmonary TB in poorly controlled diabetic patients represents a critical diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. The interplay between hyperglycemia and immune dysfunction facilitates TB progression while masking typical symptoms. Improved diagnostic algorithms, biomarker development, and integrated diabetes-TB care models are urgently needed. Future research should focus on:

Novel diagnostic tools (e.g., breath-based assays, AI-assisted imaging to detect subtle non-cavitary lesions often missed in diabetic patients) [16].Personalized treatment approaches (e.g., host-directed therapies, optimized drug regimens) [17].Public health strategies (e.g., targeted screening in diabetic populations) [18].Addressing these challenges will be essential in reducing the dual burden of TB and diabetes globally.
