Socioeconomic disparities and severity of gunshot injuries in Israel: a retrospective review of National Trauma Registry data from public hospitals 2019–2022
Authors: Ahmad Awwad, Layalee Abo Naser, Arnona Ziv, Adi Givon, Jennifer Moriatis Wolf, Jason A Strelzow, Eli Farhat, Shai Luria, Israel Trauma Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic status (SES) is correlated with gunshot violence. This study examined the associations between SES and the severity of gunshot injuries in Israel.MethodsThe Israel National Trauma Registry was reviewed for all violence-related gunshot wound patients who were admitted to all public hospitals in Israel from 2019 to 2022. Self-inflicted injuries were excluded. This time frame preceded the regional military conflict that began in October 2023 and did not include any military-related activity. The registry records patient demographics including ethnicity. Severity was assessed on the Injury Severity Score (ISS), and SES was categorized into 10 clusters based on the patients’ home addresses using the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics index.ResultsA total of 1,848 patients who sustained a gunshot injury were extracted from the database for the period in question, and their SES was identified. Patients from lower SES areas (1–5) were typically young (mean age 30.87 vs. 31.33 in the higher SES group; p = 0.4059) and were almost all from the Arab sector (94.53% vs. 46.94% in the higher SES group; p < 0.0001). Males were injured more frequently, regardless of SES (1825 vs. 82 females). While most patients came from lower SES backgrounds (92% SES levels 1–5), severe injuries were more prevalent in those with higher SES levels (6–10), and had higher ISS scores (p < 0.0001), more admissions to the intensive care unit (p = 0.04), injuries to internal organs (p = 0.0026), and referrals to rehabilitation facilities (p = 0.04). No differences were found for mode of transport to the hospital or likelihood to undergo surgery as a function of SES.ConclusionsThe rate of gunshot-related injuries in Israel is significantly affected by socioeconomic status and ethnicity. The lower SES cohorts, with an ethnic majority of Arabs, experienced more frequent gunshot injuries, but these tended to be less severe. This discrepancy suggests a potential difference in the underlying injury mechanism between social and ethnic groups. Local authorities as well as community leaders would benefit from a clearer understanding of these differences in injury mechanisms, which can contribute to better management of this growing societal issue.Keywords: Gunshot injuries, Socioeconomic status, Violence, Gunshot wound, Trauma, Ethnicity

Background

Gunshot injuries and deaths have been linked to socioeconomic status (SES). Data from countries such as the United States, Brazil, and Mexico show that individuals from lower SES backgrounds face higher risks of severe gunshot injuries and fatalities [1]. In 2016, Nand et al. 2018 reported that 251,000 people died from firearm-related injuries worldwide, a marked increase from 209,000 in 1990. The authors relate this to an increase in access to firearms in impoverished areas [1]. In countries like Canada [2] the Netherlands [3] the United States [4] and the United Kingdom [5] severe injuries and hospitalizations are more frequent in low-income neighborhoods, mainly due to the higher prevalence of organized crime and increased access to firearms [2, 4, 6]. These findings suggest that socioeconomic deprivation and disentitlement exacerbate both the frequency and severity of gunshot injuries.

Gunshot injuries have become a growing concern in Israel over the past several years [6–8]. Between 2017 and 2020, reports of gun-related incidents to the Israeli police increased by 40%. This surge in violence has disproportionately affected Arab and Bedouin communities [9] which typically have lower SES, where the number of casualties has risen by 47% [6]. One report found that 81% of all gunshot fatalities in emergency departments involved Arab citizens [6]. Arab children and adolescents are 21 times more likely to be victims of gunshot injuries than their Jewish counterparts [6]. Injuries primarily occur in non-accidental settings, contributing to rising homicide rates among adults and children alike [4, 10]. The Arab and Bedouin communities have the highest frequency of calls to the police related to gunshot incidents, reflecting a national public health crisis.

This study aimed to examine the associations between socioeconomic status and the severity of gunshot injuries in Israel. We hypothesized that individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds would be more likely to experience more severe trauma.

Materials and methods

The Israeli National Trauma Registry database was searched retrospectively for all patients hospitalized from 2019 to 2022 with ICD9 E-codes E965.0 through E965.4, which code for gunshot injuries related to assault. Children under age 18, patients with disabilities, and pregnant women were included. By contrast, self-inflicted injuries, military-related injuries, patients found dead on arrival, and injuries caused by other mechanisms (such as blasts, stabbings, or pellet/air rifle guns) were excluded.

The Israel National Trauma Registry (INTR) collects information from all seven level-I trauma centers and 16 level-II trauma centers across the country [11]. This registry includes details on trauma patients with diagnosis codes 800–989.9 from the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM). These are patients who presented at an emergency department and were hospitalized, died in the ED, or were transferred to another hospital. It does not include individuals who died at the scene or during transportation, and excludes patients admitted more than 72 h after their injury. Data entry at each trauma center is handled by trained trauma registrars, overseen by a trauma unit director or coordinator, and the information is electronically submitted to the INTR for quality examination. To ensure privacy, identifying details such as names and national ID numbers are not included.

All patients with a gunshot injury listed in the registry were included in this study, regardless of age or ethnicity. In Israel, all major public hospitals are included in the registry, and all GSW patients are treated at these hospitals. Thus, virtually all GSW patients are admitted to the hospital and accounted for in this registry.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Gertner Institute’s ethical committee (SMC-5138-18), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived, given the anonymous, retrospective nature of this study.

Socio-economic status (SES)

The socio-economic status (SES) of patients in the trauma registry database was determined using the socio-economic index developed by the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics (ICBS) for 2019 [12]. This index classifies 255 local authorities in Israel into 10 clusters, with cluster 1 representing the lowest SES and cluster 10 the highest. Each local authority is assigned a socio-economic index value based on 14 variables, using factor analysis. These variables include age, the ratio of younger to older residents to the working population, percentage of families with 4 or more children, mean years of education, individuals with a college degree, employment, retirement and unemployment figures for men and women, percentage earning twice the national average or less than the minimum wage, average income per capita, number of vehicles owned and days abroad.

These index values are standardized, with the national average set at zero. Values above zero indicate higher than average SES, while negative values indicate lower than average SES. Although this index covers most of Israel’s population, it excludes small localities with fewer than 120 inhabitants and certain institutional residents such as those in nursing homes or prisons. The index values for 2019 ranged from − 2.844 for the lowest SES location to + 2.353 for the highest, and the 10 clusters were calculated based on these values. This method thus provided a standardized way to assess socio-economic disparities in health care utilization and outcomes across different regions nationwide.

Patients with gunshot wounds were associated with an SES as follows: (1) Each patient’s address from the trauma registry was matched to a corresponding local authority. (2) For analysis purposes, the 10 clusters were combined into low SES (clusters 1–5) and high SES (clusters 6–10). Given the small number of patients in the high SES clusters, they were pooled into one cluster labelled ‘group 6+’, and the analyses were run on 6 groups (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6+).

Patient demographics were collected from the registry, including age, self-identified gender and ethnic background, method of transportation to the hospital, injury severity, and treatment parameters. These included the Injury Severity Score (ISS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), injury site and type of injuries, vital signs at presentation, length of hospital stay, admission to the ICU and length of ICU stay, surgical treatment at first admission and number of surgical interventions performed, discharge information (home, medical facility, jail) or death.

Statistical analysis

SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used for the statistical analyses. Fisher's Exact Test and the Chi-square (χ2) test were used to assess the descriptive data. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The chi-square test was utilized to investigate the association between categorical variables, including SES and intensive care unit admission.

Results

A total of 1,908 patients who met the inclusion criteria were identified in the database. A total of 1,848 patients could be assigned to one of the SES groups, of whom 92% were in the lower SES clusters (Fig. 1), thus underscoring the high concentration of gunshot injuries among the socioeconomically disadvantaged. Most patients were taken to the hospital by a mobile intensive care ambulance (MICA) and (46.8%) and underwent at least one surgical procedure during their first hospital admission (57.3%). Only 5.3% were referred to rehabilitation units for further in-house care.

Distribution of patients by socioeconomic status (SES) cluster. The 1,848 patients with gunshot injuries are grouped into low SES (clusters 1–5) and high SES (clusters 6+), and by age

As shown, the patients in SES clusters 1–5 were typically young (mean age 30.87 vs. 31.33 for the higher SES clusters; p = 0.4059). As shown in Fig. 2, most self-reported their ethnicity as Arab (94.53% vs. 46.94% in the higher SES cluster; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). Males were injured more frequently in all clusters (1847 vs. 79 females).

Ethnic distribution by SES. Ethnic background differed significantly across SES clusters. Patients in the lower SES cluster were predominantly of Arab ethnicity (94.53%), while higher SES groups included more Jewish patients, with 46.94% identifying as Arab, thus illustrating the disparity in injury based on both SES and ethnicity

Injury severity (ISS score) was not associated with ethnicity in general (p = 0.6). However, when patients were stratified by SES, ethnicity was associated with higher ISS scores in the higher SES cluster (6+) (p = 0.0351). Specifically, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4, in the higher SES cluster, severe injuries (ISS ≥ 16) were more prevalent among Jewish patients (44.93% vs. 28.21%).

Injury Severity Score (ISS) by ethnicity and SES. ISS was analyzed by SES cluster and ethnic group. While the overall ISS did not differ significantly by ethnicity, among patients in the higher SES clusters (6+), Jewish ethnicity was significantly associated with higher ISS scores (p = 0.0351), indicating more severe injuries compared to patients with Arab ethnicity in the same SES clusters

Injury Severity Score (ISS) by SES. In patients in SES cluster 6+, Jewish patients had a significantly higher proportion of severe injuries (ISS ≥ 16) at 44.93%, as compared to 28.21% among Arab patients. This supports the association between ethnicity and injury severity within high SES populations

Higher SES was also correlated with lower GCS (p < 0.0001), higher death rates (12.93%), and more admissions to the ICU (p = 0.04). In SES cluster 1, 18.1% were admitted to the ICU vs. 26.5% in group 6+ (Fig. 5). In keeping with higher injury severity, there were also differences in injury patterns between SES clusters. Internal organ injuries were more prevalent in higher SES clusters (p = 0.0026) (Fig. 6), whereas there were no differences between groups for extremity, spine, skeletal, nerve, vascular or other injuries.

ICU admission rate by SES. Comparison of ICU admissions across SES clusters. ICU admission was more frequent in higher SES clusters (26.5%) than in lower SES clusters (18.1%) (p = 0.04). This difference reflects the higher clinical severity of patients in higher SES clusters

Injury site distribution by SES group. Internal organ injuries were significantly more frequent in patients in the higher SES cluster (p = 0.0026), suggesting a difference in the mechanism or impact of trauma. No significant differences were found for other types of injury, such as extremity, spine, vascular, or nerve injuries

Referrals to rehabilitation and the number of operations were not correlated with SES, although, as shown in Fig. 7, the number of operations and injury severity (ISS) were correlated (p < 0.0001). The method of transportation to the hospital was not correlated with ISS or ethnicity. Most patients were transported by a mobile intensive care ambulance (MICA) (Jews 52% vs. Arabs 46%), or in a private vehicle (Jews 21% vs. Arabs 25%).

Discussion

Understanding gunshot injuries and the confounders of these injuries is crucial to improving care and implementing harm reduction strategies. Although socioeconomic factors are critical to unpacking health epidemics, there is scant research on this topic in relation to gunshot injuries. The findings here underscore the complex relationship between SES, ethnicity, and injury patterns in Israel. They show that while lower SES clusters, predominantly self-identified Arab populations, experienced more frequent injuries, higher SES clusters, with a greater proportion of Jewish individuals, had more severe injuries based on ISS scores. Residence in lower SES clusters accounted for 92% of all injuries, with a disproportionate representation of individuals with Arab ethnicities (95%), although only 21% percent of Israeli citizens are Arab [13]. Despite these disparities, there were no significant differences in referrals to rehabilitation, method of transportation, surgical care, or number of operations between SES clusters, suggesting that Israel’s universal healthcare system mitigates many of the common issues related to access to care that can be exacerbated by socioeconomic disparities Previous studies have reported ethnic and socioeconomic disparities in injury rates, with individuals of Arab ethnicity experiencing higher rates of hospitalization for injuries, including gunshot wounds, traffic accidents, and hand trauma [8, 14–17]. Globally, lower SES clusters are more likely to face firearm-related injuries and fatalities due to increased exposure to violence and limited access to care [1]. However, few studies have reported more severe injuries in higher SES clusters. One exception is the Milwaukee Police Department and American Community Survey (2013-2017 [18]. By contrast, most studies highlight the greater prevalence of severe injuries in lower SES populations, thus emphasizing their vulnerability [1, 8, 19–21]. The increased severity of injuries in higher SES clusters in the current study may be attributed to the nature of the injuries, which often involved injuries to critical internal organs/systems requiring intensive care and longer recovery periods. This aligns with the findings here of higher ISS and lower GCS scores in higher SES clusters. On the other hand, injuries in lower SES clusters were more frequent but less severe, potentially reflecting the instrumental violence associated with organized crime, where fatalities are not the primary goal [22]. In addition, and contrasting with previous studies on the Arab sector in Israel, there was a drop in the use of private vehicles for transportation to the hospital (25% vs. 29%) [8]. This may suggest that the growing utilization of MICA among Arab patients in lower SES clusters, as observed here (46% vs. 37%) [8], may contribute to better outcomes despite the frequency of injuries [8] compared to global trends [8, 16, 23, 24]. Overall, these findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address the root causes of violence, particularly in lower SES and Arab communities. Strengthening the collaboration between local authorities, Arab leadership, and the police is essential to reducing crime and gun violence. Efforts should focus on illegal weapon collection, urban policing, and education programs targeting youth and offenders in rehabilitation [6]. Tailored financial support and enhanced public awareness are critical components, but must be complemented by broader strategies that promote societal integration, reduce social exclusion, and foster trust between marginalized communities and institutions. Globally, approaches such as improving equitable access to education and employment, strengthening community-based services, and ensuring respectful law enforcement engagement have proven essential in addressing socioeconomic disparities and lessening violence. These strategies are especially vital in structurally marginalized communities, where addressing social determinants of health—including racism, trauma, and neighborhood conditions—is fundamental to achieving lasting impact [25–28]. Addressing organized crime by targeting financial resources and ensuring local authority stability are also vital measures [6]. The current study has a number of limitations. Its retrospective design relied solely on database entries, which can be fraught with entry errors. However, when conducted under the supervision of experienced and well-trained staff, the likelihood of significant errors should be minimal. Further, this study did not include data on pre-hospital deaths or patients found dead on arrival, which may have inadvertently skewed the survivor data. Moreover, SES is complex, and unknown or undetected factors may not have been captured by using the patients’ address as the sole measure of the SES cluster Nevertheless, the findings highlight the complex interplay between socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and injury patterns in Israel. While lower SES groups, predominantly Arab populations, experienced more frequent injuries, higher SES groups, with a higher proportion of Jewish individuals, sustained more severe injuries. Despite these differences, the universal healthcare system in Israel provided similar treatment and outcomes, including rates of surgical care, time to presentation, method of transportation, and post-injury rehabilitation referrals, regardless of SES and ethnic disparities Further research is needed to better understand pre-hospital care and other factors influencing injury severity and outcomes across SES and ethnic groups [29]. The social mechanisms behind these differences should be explored, which can contribute to increasing the collaborative efforts of local authorities, community leaders, and law enforcement to reduce crime, improve public safety, and handle this surge in violence.
