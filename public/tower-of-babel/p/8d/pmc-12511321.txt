Terahertz time domain spectroscopy technique used for evaluation of thickness and optical cum dielectric properties of table and fertile eggshells
Authors: Juveria Iram, Naveen Periketi, Aleem Basha, A K Chaudhary, Kaleem Ahmed Jaleeli, R Purshotham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper reports the use of THz time domain spectroscopy for the measurements of thickness, refractive index and dielectric constants of chicken egg shells. Twelve numbers of table and fertile egg samples were selected. The experiment was performed in the reflection geometry using a fiber-coupled terahertz source between 0 and 4.0 THz range. The reflected time domain signal from the inner eggshell-membrane boundary and the outer eggshell interface was used, to determine shell thickness. The results were verified with Field Emission Scanning Electron-Microscopy (FESEM). In the next step, the obtained Time/frequency domain spectra data were used to ascertain the R.I., absorption coefficients, and real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constants for the first time. It was noticed that the values of R.I. of normal eggs at 0.8 THz were higher than 2.5 as compared to fertile eggs. Similarly, the absorption coefficient values for nonfertile eggs lie between 2 and 14 cm −1 compared to fertile eggs, which lie between 2 and 10 cm −1 range.The most important aspect of our study is related to measurements of real and imaginary parts of dielectric constants between 1.2 and 3.0 THz range the nonfertile eggs show a steep drop in values between 4 − 2.0 range while fertile eggs show an almost linear response of 4 0.0 in the given frequency range. The imaginary parts show nonfertile egg values between 7.0 and 3.75 while fertile eggs show a consistent result of the order of 5.0. It further opens a new channel to enhance the poultry industry. The results were verified using field emission scanning electron microscopy results. This non-destructive technique can play a very important role in the poultry industry in minimizing waste and maximizing efficiency.Subject terms: Biophysics, Molecular biology, Optics and photonics

Introduction

The eggshell is a protective barrier for the developing embryo from external contaminations and UV radiation. It facilitates gas exchange from the surface and acts as a source of minerals for the developing biological system in avian birds1. Thin eggshells cause cracks, infection, and dehydration, leading to higher embryonic mortality. The quality of the eggshells is essential for better hatching and preventing egg breakage2. The estimated economic losses from cracked and broken eggs constitute between 6% and 8% of the total eggs produced3. On one hand, the higher thickness provides higher mineral resources and protection while, on the other side, it makes the job of storage and transportation easier. Therefore, a non-destructive thickness measurement technique is highly desirable in meat and egg production. Also, eggshells are a barrier to many acoustic and optical spectroscopic research approaches, for instance, ovo sexing by optical spectroscopy. Determination of sex at an earlier stage is a prerequisite for solving unwanted male chick culling4. Because eggshells are a hindrance to embryo probes.

The measurement of eggshell thickness is a crucial exercise in quality control processes and investigating the effects of pollutants and environmental contamination on its thinning. Accurate data on eggshell thickness can quantify the hazardous effects of pesticides, chemical wastes, and pollutants on the egg quality of various types of species.

Different types of laboratory-based techniques have been employed to measure eggshell thickness, these are further divided into destructive and non-destructive categories. Eggshell thickness measurements are often done using destructive methods like drilling air holes with micrometer screws, impact fracture force, puncture force, or quasistatic compression5. The quality of the egg is influenced by the uniformity of eggshell thickness rather than the thickness itself6. Researchers measured eggshell thickness along longitudinal and latitudinal axes using a gauge, finding significant variations from blunt to sharp end. Non-destructive methods involve dipping eggs in salt solutions with different concentrations of salt, allowing for rapid testing7. However, these methods have the disadvantage of giving crude results. Ultrasound has also been shown to be successful in measuring eggshell thickness non-destructively8. Applying the photonic imaging modality of optical coherence tomography, intact egg thickness can be measured. The OCT can measure eggshell thickness and detect cracks up to 1.7 mm resolution. However, measuring thin and fragile eggshells with this method is a challenging task. Commercial ultrasonic sensors, such as the Egg Tester Precision Ultrasonic Egg Shell Thickness Gauge and non-ferrous material Thickness Gauge, are available for non-destructive measurements9. The Magna-Mike 8600 and Mini-Test 7200 FH Hall-effect thickness gauges (HETG) are used to measure the thickness of eggshells and membranes. These non-destructive devices measure the distance between a steel reference ball and a magnetic probe, providing precise and accurate digital measurements. HETG measurements are similar to Starrett thickness gauges but generally 0–9% thicker. As compared to pre-DDT-era eggshells, HETG measurements are about 12% thicker. The HETG is a useful device for accurate and repeatable measurements, especially for smaller, fragile eggs, without damaging the eggshells10. However, this process requires the careful handling of eggs. When electromagnetic waves are used for eggshell thickness probes, the brown-colored pigment of eggs shows high absorbance and scattering effects that make use of UV and visible electromagnetic waves unsuitable for non-destructive measurement.

Besides the thickness of the eggshells, extinction coefficient and dielectric properties are some other important parameters to evaluate the quality of egg, which diminishes during storage. The Haugh unit and yolk index are some useful indicators to ascertain the quality of the Egg. The dielectric constant and loss factor of egg albumen and yolk over the 10 to 1800 MHz frequency range during 5-week storage at 15 °C are reported. The reported results show that albumen had higher dielectric constants and loss factors than the yolk. The dielectric constants rose after 2 weeks in storage, remaining constant throughout the 5 weeks. Since the storage has less influence on the albumen or yolk loss factor. Therefore, no correlation is observed between dielectric properties and moisture content, ash content, Haugh unit, or yolk index11.

Dielectric characteristics are essential for detecting chemical structures and distinguishing molecule combinations. These characteristics can help to determine the quality of mixed-material complexes. Some other studies discuss the use of dielectric spectroscopic methods for evaluating the agricultural items, exploring connections between dielectric qualities and other physical and chemical data. Due to their high nutritional content, eggs can harbor and transmit harmful microorganisms, such as Salmonella enteritidis. According to FSIS-USDA (2006), heat pasteurization is often considered the most effective method for controlling these disease-causing microorganisms. A study was carried out to examine the impact of temperature and frequency on the dielectric characteristics of egg components. The results indicate that albumen exhibits greater dielectric values and faster rapid heating rates than yolk. Nevertheless, both egg components and egg yolks exhibit comparable heating rates. Microwave heating shows significant promise for the pasteurization of eggs while still in their shells. Furthermore, this paper presents models for determining heating rates13. Materials’ dielectric characteristics are important for assessing electromagnetic energy interactions, particularly in the food industry. These attributes depend on frequency, temperature, composition, and moisture. A review compared microwave or radio wave penetration depths across food classes based on their dielectric properties. Food type and frequency have an impact on temperature and composition. Finding the best dielectric heating approach requires more research. However, terahertz (THz) radiation offers the most promising solution due to its numerous advantages, including low power and deep penetration through the shell, which enables information to be transmitted through the different layers of the eggshell in reflection mode. It is also supported by the low water content and crystalline nature of eggshells. Khaliduzzama et al. used THZ-TDS for the thickness measurement of white infertile eggs. Terahertz-based spectroscopy is applied for the non-destructive analysis of samples in various domains, including industrial quality control23. Reflection, transmission, and attenuation processes are three phenomena that are crucial to take into account when THz waves propagate at each interface—the air-shell, shell-membrane, and membrane-albumen interfaces—of an intact egg.

In the present work, we have used Terahertz Time-domain Spectroscopy in reflection mode for evaluating the important optical properties such as thickness, refractive index, absorption coefficient, and dielectric constants (real and imaginary) of table and fertile chicken eggshells between 0.1 and 3.0 terahertz frequency range. We have adopted the Maximum Entropy Method (MEM) technique, which is another effective version of the S. Due to high pulse duration and repetition rate, the two signals can clearly be distinguished on a pico seconds time scale without any interference. The THz transmission and reflection were tested on broken and undamaged eggshells, respectively. Materials and methods

All samples were sourced from PVNR Veterinary University Hyderabad. Fertile eggs were obtained from housing units where female and male chickens were kept for mating. However, Table eggs were sourced from housing units with only female chickens. The carefully selected potential fertile eggs are incubated in the facility. All sourced samples are brown eggs. We have selected twelve egg samples for the present study. Out of which six numbers of fertile egg samples were incubated for a day, the remaining six infertile a week-old refrigerated table chicken eggs for the determination of their thickness and other important physical parameters such as R.I., absorption coefficients, and real and imaginary parts of dielectric constants. We have used two important physical techniques, such as (i) FESEM and (ii) Time Domain THz Spectroscopy to measure the different physical parameters of the Egg samples.

In the first part, a cross-section of the eggshells was ascertained by scanning with an electron microscope to verify the accuracy and precision. It also helped to measure the thickness of the eggshells. The membranes of the shell were removed mechanically, and the thickness of eggshells and shell membranes were measured separately. However, a cross-section examination was done by making the shell piece face up and aligned parallelly with the screen boundary. two fertile and two infertile eggs were taken for thickness measurement with FESEM (Model Ultra 55 Carl Zeiss).

In the second part of the experiment, Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy was performed to measure the thickness of intact eggs. A commercial tabletop THz time-domain system (model TeraFlash-Toptica Photonics, Germany) with a bandwidth of over 4.0 THz and a peak dynamic range exceeding 90 dB was used for the measurement. The system employed an optical fiber-coupled femtosecond laser featuring a voice coil-driven corner mirror with a position sensor and InGaAs/InAlAs photoconductive antennas for THz generation. The femtosecond fiber-coupled laser (Toptica FemtoFErb THz FDS) operated at a central wavelength of 1560 nm, with a pulse width of 60 fs, a repetition rate of 100 MHz, and an average output power of 80 mW. The incident THz radiation was used to irradiate the egg samples at an 8-degree angle in reflection geometry. The system is purged with dry air to eliminate the effect of atmosphere humidity. The reflected time domain signal from the eggshell was then converted into a frequency domain signal using a fast Fourier transform (FFT). The THz frequency spectra lie between 0.1 and 4.0 THz range. The terahertz spectroscopy system (Toptica TeraFlash) is designed such that reduces the discrepancies araised by the curved surface because it has a small angle of incidence of approximately 8 (degree), which helps to maintain a relatively consistent interaction between the THz waves and the curved surface. Furthermore, the system’s focal spot size is of the order of 2.5 mm which allows for better interaction with the surface and reducing distortions (Scattering losses) caused by surface irregularities. These design considerations enable us to improve the reliability and precision of THz reflectance measurements on curved surfaces such as eggshells. The micron-level positioning accuracy between the reference pulse (reflected from a gold-coated mirror) and the sample pulse (reflected from the egg surface) is achieved using a PI motorized stage ( Physik Instrumente). The vertical precise control using pitch of micro-meter ensures consistent and optimal alignment in the terahertz reflection geometry. Furthermore, any residual misalignment between the reference and sample pulses is effectively corrected using the Maximum Entropy Method (MEM), which enhances spectral accuracy by compensating for phase and amplitude discrepancies introduced by slight positioning errors.

An algorithm written in Python was used to automatically calculate the distance between the first two peaks in the frequency domain. THz time-domain signal reflected from the gold-coated mirror Eref is considered as the reference signal and THz signal reflected from the sample is considered as Esam. When THz waves propagate at each interface—the air-shell, shell-membrane, and membrane-albumen interfaces—of an intact egg. The crystalline CaCO3 structure of eggshells comprises mamillary cones and palisades32. A distinct, soft membrane known as the shell membrane, which is made of fibril protein and thin layers of albumen, covers the interior of the eggshell. The low absorption of the dry materials results in low attenuation of the terahertz pulse. Stated differently, the degree of attenuation is mostly determined by the water content of the medium. The material’s extinction coefficient (k) and refractive index (n) determine the strength of the transmitted and reflected pulses. The refractive index in the terahertz range is influenced by the material’s moisture content and degree of compactness, such as density and crystallinity. When light passes through a medium with a higher refractive index, it will reflect more light and move more slowly. The extinction coefficient of eggshells is much lower than egg albumen, indicating that the transmitted part of the terahertz pulse is largely absorbed by the egg white and attenuated soon. The intensity of the incident THz pulse decreases with increasing depth from the surface, as part of the incident THz pulse is reflected and absorbed by the mediums. Therefore, the reflected THz pulse intensity should be stronger from the shell-membrane interface than from the membrane-albumen interface22. Variations in intensity only influence the amplitude of the signal, not the oscillation phase of the interference signal. The refractive index of the shell membrane should also be high due to its collagen fibril content and tendon-like polypeptide structures. Separating the shell membrane with available devices is difficult, so a laser with a shorter pulse width is necessary.

The real and imaginary parts of dielectric constants are the very important parameters of a given sample. There are two important mechanisms associated with the dielectric medium (i) the Drude mechanism that accounts for the transport properties of free charge carriers in the material (ii) Debye relaxation are dominant process that governs the dielectric properties of solids at low-frequency regions below the microwave band. Secondly, at the higher frequency side, the mid-IR range or above the optical properties are attributed to lattice and optical phonon resonances, but the low energy tail of the spectrum is the major source of absorption. This is also known as Deby relaxation of vibrational modes in the THz range.

Conclusions

We have demonstrated the use of Time-domain THz spectroscopy for the characterization of optical properties of Table and Fertile Chicken’s eggshell. In addition, the optical technique to measure the thickness. Time/frequency domain spectra data were recorded between 0.1 and 3.0 THz range. R.I., absorption coefficients, and real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constants were measured. It was noticed that values of R.I. of table eggs at 0.8 THz were higher than 2.5 as compared to fertile eggs. Similarly, the absorption coefficient values for infertile eggs lie between 2 and 14 cm−1 compared to fertile eggs, which lie between 2 and 10 cm−1 range. The most important aspect of our study covers the measurements of real and imaginary parts of dielectric constants between the 1.2 to 3.0 THz range the infertile eggs show a steep drop in values between 4 − 2.0 range while fertile eggs show an almost linear response of 4 0.0 in the given frequency range. The imaginary parts show infertile egg values between 7.0 and 3.75 while fertile eggs show a consistent result of the order of 5.0. This study will significantly contribute to non-invasive in-vivo research as well. The ability to differentiate fertile eggshells from infertile eggs demonstrates the versatility and capability of terahertz spectroscopy in enhancing our understanding of biological samples.
