Fertility Preservation and Spontaneous Pregnancy in a Young Woman with Sertoli-Leydig Cell Tumor: A Five-Year Disease-Free Case Report
Authors: Wen-Qiang Hu, Yan-Yan Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSertoli–Leydig cell tumors (SLCTs) are uncommon ovarian sex cord-stromal neoplasms that predominantly affect young women and are frequently associated with hyperandrogenic features.Case DescriptionA 23-year-old nulliparous woman presented with a left ovarian mass, accompanied by amenorrhea, clinical signs of virilization, and significantly elevated α-fetoprotein levels. Surgical intervention via left salpingo-oophorectomy was performed. Histopathological evaluation confirmed a Stage IA moderately to poorly differentiated SLCT. Postoperative management included adjuvant chemotherapy with the etoposide and cisplatin, along with administration of a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist as an adjunctive measure. No evidence of tumor recurrence was observed over a five-year surveillance period. Subsequently, the patient achieved a spontaneous pregnancy and delivered a healthy male neonate via vaginal delivery.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that in young women with stage IA SLCT, fertility-sparing surgery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy and ovarian preservation strategies may be considered feasible in selected patients for five-year disease-free survival and subsequent spontaneous pregnancy.

Introduction

Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor (SLCT) is a rare subtype of ovarian sex cord–stromal neoplasm, accounting for approximately 1% of tumors within this category and fewer than 0.5% of all ovarian neoplasms. It primarily affects young women and is frequently characterized by hyperandrogenism, often presenting with elevated serum testosterone levels.1 Although virilization resulting from excessive androgen secretion is a typical clinical manifestation, cases associated with significantly elevated α-fetoprotein (AFP) levels remain exceedingly uncommon. SLCT is generally associated with a favorable prognosis; however, tumors exhibiting moderate to poor differentiation are linked to a higher risk of recurrence, thereby making fertility preservation a significant consideration in the treatment of young patients.2

Notably, reports of young patients with FIGO Stage IA, moderately to poorly differentiated, AFP producing SLCT who undergo fertility sparing surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy, and ovarian function protection with gonadotropin releasing hormone agonist (GnRH-a), and subsequently achieve long-term disease-free survival (5 years) and successful spontaneous pregnancy with vaginal delivery are extremely scarce in the literature.

This study describes the case of a 23-year-old nulliparous woman with FIGO Stage IA SLCT, severe virilization, and markedly elevated AFP, who underwent unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy followed by EP chemotherapy and GnRH-a therapy for ovarian preservation. The patient remained disease-free for five years and subsequently achieved spontaneous conception and full-term vaginal delivery of a healthy infant. This case is unique in its combination of early-stage (Stage IA) disease, high-risk pathological features, rare AFP elevation, successful fertility preservation, and long-term favorable outcomes, aiming to inform optimized fertility-sparing strategies in young patients with SLCT and highlight the value of multidisciplinary management for this rare ovarian tumor.

Case Report

General Data

A 23-year-old woman was admitted to the Department of General Gynecology I at The First Bethune Hospital of Jilin University in 2018, with the chief complaint of a left ovarian mass discovered 5.5 months earlier. Approximately 5 months prior to admission, right lower abdominal pain prompted evaluation at a local hospital. A whole-abdomen computed tomography scan indicated a left adnexal mass and appendicitis. Gynecological ultrasonography identified a cystic, mixed-echogenic mass in the left ovary, measuring approximately 2.8×3.1 cm, for which routine follow-up was recommended. Serial ultrasound examinations subsequently demonstrated slow progression in the size of the mass.

The patient experienced menarche at 14 years of age and had previously maintained regular menstrual cycles at 30-day intervals, lasting 5–7 days, with occasional mild dysmenorrhea. Following the onset of symptoms, amenorrhea developed. Three months prior to admission, the patient self-administered oral progesterone, resulting in a 4-day episode of scant, dark red vaginal bleeding, after which amenorrhea persisted.

On physical examination, the patient was alert and oriented. Signs of virilization were evident, including a prominent Adam’s apple, deepened voice, thickened eyebrows and eyelashes, facial hair above the upper lip, and increased hair growth on the extremities. Bilateral breast development was normal. No cardiopulmonary abnormalities were identified. Gynecological examination indicated dense pubic hair with an inverted triangular distribution and clitoromegaly, measuring approximately 3 cm in length (Figure 1). The vagina was patent with smooth mucosa. The cervix was of normal size and contour. The uterus was anteverted, normal in size, of medium consistency, with good mobility and no tenderness. A mass approximately the size of a hen’s egg was palpated in the left adnexal region, presenting with medium consistency, good mobility, and no tenderness. No abnormalities were noted in the right adnexal region. Vaginal discharge was scant, white, and odorless. Ancillary investigations yielded the following results: Gynecological ultrasonography revealed a 31×18 mm cystic echogenic mass in the left ovary with heterogeneous internal echoes and peripheral ring-shaped blood flow. The right ovary appeared normal in size and contained more than 12 small anechoic areas, with a maximum internal diameter of 6 mm. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 2) demonstrated a hypervascular space-occupying lesion in the left adnexal region, suggestive of a sex cord-stromal tumor, with a sclerosing stromal tumor or SLCT being the most likely differential diagnoses. Diagnosis and Treatment Course

From the medical history, physical examination findings, and results of ancillary investigations, a diagnosis of ovarian SLCT was strongly suspected. Considering the age of the patient (23 years) and her nulliparous status, a surgical plan was formulated to include left salpingo-oophorectomy in combination with pelvic adhesiolysis, omentectomy, and appendectomy. Intraoperative exploration indicated a right ovary measuring approximately 6 × 3 × 2 cm, with a smooth, white surface and multiple follicles ranging from 2 to 6 mm in diameter. The left ovary measured approximately 7 × 5 × 4 cm, also exhibiting a smooth, white surface and a solid consistency (Figures 3 and 4). A small volume of pale-yellow fluid was observed in the pelvic cavity. No enlarged lymph nodes were palpated within the abdominopelvic region. In accordance with the preoperative plan, left adnexectomy was performed. Intraoperative frozen section analysis identified a sex cord–stromal tumor in the left adnexa, with a preliminary diagnosis of a Sertoli-stromal cell tumor exhibiting moderate to poor differentiation. Postoperative Pathology

Postoperative pathological examination yielded the following findings: the left adnexa contained a moderately to poorly differentiated Sertoli-stromal cell tumor, measuring 5.5 × 3.8 × 3.2 cm. Chronic salpingitis was identified, with no significant pathological changes observed in the fallopian tube. The separately submitted appendix presented features consistent with chronic appendicitis and no evidence of tumor involvement. The separately submitted omental tissue consisted of adipose tissue without tumor infiltration. Final pathological stage: FIGO stage IA (confined to the left ovary, no capsular rupture, negative peritoneal cytology).Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining demonstrated the following results: Inhibin (+), Calretinin (+), CD99 (+), WT-1 (+), CD56 (+), CD10 (focally +), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) (-), CK7 (-), Oct3/4 (-), SALL4 (-), Vimentin (+), CK-pan (+), and SMA (-).

Postoperative Outcome

Serial postoperative measurements of testosterone and AFP levels demonstrated a gradual decline (Figures 5 and 6). On postoperative day 10, testosterone measured 2.940 nmol/L and AFP was 115.90 ng/mL. Wound healing was satisfactory, and the patient was discharged in stable condition. At three months postoperatively, recovery remained uneventful. At six months, pelvic examination revealed no evidence of tumor recurrence, and testosterone and AFP levels had declined to within normal reference ranges. At one-year postoperative follow-up, conducted via telephone, it was reported that four cycles of chemotherapy with the etoposide and cisplatin (EP) regimen had been administered at 21-day intervals at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. Concurrently, GnRH-a; Diphereline) was used for the preservation of contralateral ovarian function. Throughout the follow-up, testosterone and AFP levels remained within normal limits. At the most recent follow-up in December 2023, disease-free survival was confirmed. Fertility Outcome

In April 2022, a spontaneous vaginal delivery of a healthy male infant was achieved at Xing’an Meng Maternal and Child Health Hospital. The neonate had a birth weight of 3850 g and Apgar scores of 10 at both 1 and 5 minutes. At one-year postpartum follow-up, the infant’s feeding was reported to be normal.

Discussion

Clinical Features

SLCT is a rare sex cord-stromal neoplasm that primarily affects young individuals, with a mean age at onset of approximately 25 years.3 Clinical presentation is most often characterized by signs of virilization resulting from tumor-derived androgen secretion. Common manifestations include oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea, breast atrophy, acne, hirsutism, transient alopecia, voice deepening, and clitoromegaly. It is worth noting that voice changes and clitoromegaly may persist irreversibly, even following surgical resection of the tumor.1 A smaller subset of patients may present with estrogen-related symptoms, such as precocious puberty, menstrual irregularities, polymenorrhea, or postmenopausal bleeding.4–6 In certain cases, only nonspecific symptoms like abdominal distension or pain may be reported.7,8 Due to the heterogeneous and often nonspecific nature of its clinical manifestations, combined with its low incidence, SLCT is frequently underrecognized or misdiagnosed in clinical practice.

Laboratory Findings, Tumor Gross and Histological Features, and Differential Diagnosis

Pathologically, approximately 98% of SLCTs are unilateral and well circumscribed, with tumor sizes ranging from 2 cm to 35 cm and a mean diameter of 12–14 cm. Bilateral involvement is rare, occurring in only 1.5%–2% of cases, while extraovarian metastasis is observed in approximately 2%–3% of cases.9–11 Elevated serum testosterone levels are found in approximately 80% of SLCT cases, although a small proportion of patients may present with normal levels. As such, increased testosterone concentrations serve as a useful biomarker for this tumor type.1,12,13 In reproductive-aged individuals presenting with a well-circumscribed, mobile, solid mass in the unilateral adnexal region along with elevated serum testosterone, a diagnosis of SLCT should be strongly considered. Adrenal tumors should be excluded, as they usually present with markedly elevated 17-OHP and DHEAS, which are generally normal in SLCT.14,15

AFP is a classic marker for germ cell tumors and hepatocellular carcinoma, but elevated AFP can also occur in less than 5% of SLCTs, making SLCT the most common AFP‑producing ovarian non‑germ cell tumor.12,16 In this case, serum AFP was significantly elevated, which is a rare but important feature of SLCT. The origin of AFP in SLCT remains unclear; possible sources include Sertoli-Leydig cells or immature hepatocyte differentiation.17,18

SLCTs may present as solid, cystic, or mixed cystic-solid masses.2,9 From a histopathological perspective, SLCTs are classified into four subtypes based on the degree of tubular differentiation by Sertoli cells and the proportion of primitive gonadal stroma: well differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, and retiform.18 Heterologous components may be found in the latter three subtypes.2 These heterologous elements are categorized into two types: endodermal components most commonly gastrointestinal-type epithelium and mesenchymal components, such as cartilage or skeletal muscle. Histopathological features associated with an unfavorable prognosis include poor differentiation, a retiform growth pattern, and the presence of heterologous components.19 Among these, tumors containing mesenchymal heterologous elements have been associated with poorer clinical outcomes, whereas the presence of gastrointestinal epithelial components does not appear to influence prognosis.9

The definitive diagnosis of SLCT is primarily established through histopathological evaluation and IHC analysis. Typical IHC profiles frequently demonstrate positivity of Sertoli cell components for WT-1, inhibin-α, calretinin, CD99, and CD56. In a minority of cases, cytokeratin positivity may also be observed, whereas EMA is typically negative. In the present case, a preoperative suspicion of SLCT was raised, and the diagnosis was subsequently supported by postoperative histopathological findings, IHC results, and a significant decline in serum AFP levels following surgical intervention.

Genetics

The genetic mechanisms underlying SLCT remain incompletely characterized. Variants in the DICER1 and FOXL2 genes have been identified in a subset of patients, as reported by Karnezis.20 Based on molecular features, SLCT is classified into three subtypes: DICER1-variant, FOXL2-variant, and DICER1/FOXL2 wild-type. DICER1-variant tumors tend to occur in younger patients and are typically moderately to poorly differentiated. These tumors frequently contain retiform or heterologous components and are often associated with androgen excess.21,22

DICER1 variants play a critical role in the tumorigenesis of SLCT. Abnormalities in DICER1 lead to dysregulated miRNA processing and excessive androgen secretion, which are closely associated with the pathogenesis of SLCT.6,20 DICER1 variants are significantly more common in moderately to poorly differentiated SLCTs, whereas well-differentiated tumors rarely harbor such alterations.20,23 Both germline and somatic DICER1 variants have been identified in patients with SLCT.24 Importantly, germline DICER1 variants underlie DICER1 syndrome, an inherited tumor predisposition syndrome that confers a high risk of ovarian SLCT.10,25 The frequency of DICER1 variants in SLCT varies widely across studies and exhibits a striking age-dependent distribution, with a markedly higher prevalence in young patients.20,26,27

In the present case, disease onset occurred at 23 years of age, with no reported history of renal, thyroid, cerebral, pulmonary, or other tumors. However, based on the age of onset and the tumor’s degree of differentiation, the presence of an underlying DICER1 variant was considered likely, although genetic confirmation was not possible due to the decision of the family to decline testing. Large-scale studies assessing clinical prognosis based on DICER1 variant status remain limited. Although Thibault et al reported an increased risk of recurrence and poorer outcomes in patients with DICER1 variants, further investigation is necessary to inform postoperative treatment strategies and long-term follow-up protocols in this population.2,28

Treatment Strategy and Prognosis

In the management of SLCT, patient age and tumor stage are regarded as the most critical factors influencing treatment decisions.2,29 For young patients diagnosed with stage IA disease according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification, fertility-sparing surgery is considered both safe and effective, as bilateral ovarian involvement is rare.11,24,30,31 In contrast, for postmenopausal patients and those with FIGO stage IB or more advanced disease, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) recommends total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. During surgical management, careful intraoperative handling is advised to preserve tumor integrity and minimize the risk of tumor cell dissemination, which may influence recurrence rates.32

The role of lymphadenectomy in the surgical management of SLCT remains controversial. Brown et al reported that lymph node metastasis is rare in patients with SLCT.33 In their series of 31 patients, five underwent lymphadenectomy, and all postoperative pathological findings were negative. Among the nine patients who developed recurrence, no lymph node metastases were identified at the time of recurrence. These findings were further supported by data presented by Yasin et al31 Based on these observations, the current ESMO guidelines for ovarian tumors do not recommend routine lymphadenectomy during initial surgical management.32 In the present case, preoperative pelvic MRI revealed no suspicious lymph nodes, and intraoperative exploration identified no enlarged lymph nodes; therefore, lymphadenectomy was not performed.

For patients with early-stage disease, the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy remains uncertain due to the lack of prospective evidence. From the available data, adjuvant chemotherapy and long-term follow-up are recommended for patients with high-risk features, including poor differentiation, stage beyond IA, tumor rupture or intraoperative spillage, and the presence of heterologous components.34–36 According to the ESMO guidelines for the management of non-epithelial ovarian tumors, adjuvant chemotherapy is advised for patients with SLCT who exhibit heterologous components, poor differentiation, or FIGO stage IB–IV disease.32 The 2022 Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Ovarian Sex Cord-Stromal Tumors recommends either active surveillance or consideration of 3–6 cycles of chemotherapy for patients with stage IA or IB grade 1 (G1) disease who present with additional moderate- to high-risk factors, as well as for those with grade 2 (G2) disease.30 For patients with stage IA or IB grade 3 (G3) disease, stage IC disease, or more advanced stages, 3–6 cycles of postoperative chemotherapy are recommended.

In patients requiring postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, several chemotherapeutic agents are recognized for their gonadotoxic potential, including cyclophosphamide (high risk), as well as cisplatin and carboplatin (intermediate risk). Clinical studies have demonstrated that the administration of GnRH-a before and during chemotherapy can maintain ovarian tissue in a relatively quiescent state. This effect is associated with a reduction in the number of primordial follicles entering subsequent developmental stages and decreased ovarian perfusion, potentially mitigating chemotherapy-induced ovarian damage and improving post-chemotherapy menstrual recovery and pregnancy rates.37,38 However, findings from a multicenter study involving 227 patients with malignant ovarian germ cell tumors indicated that while the rate of menstrual resumption was higher in the GnRH-a group (100%) compared to the non-use group (90.9%), multivariate analysis did not identify GnRH-a as an independent protective factor. Furthermore, the pregnancy rate in the GnRH-a group (60%) was lower than that in the control group (68%), although the difference was not statistically significant.39 Additional studies have indicated that the protective effect of GnRH-a may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as age, ovarian reserve, and the chemotherapy regimen used. Limited benefit has been observed in patients with diminished ovarian reserve (eg., AMH < 1.1 ng/mL) or in those over 40 years of age.37

Prognosis

The overall prognosis for patients with SLCT is generally favorable, with a reported 5-year survival rate ranging from 70% to 90%. Late recurrence is relatively uncommon. Prognostic outcomes are closely associated with the degree of tumor differentiation, with poorly differentiated tumors linked to less favorable survival. Given that ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors exhibit low-grade malignant potential and the possibility of late recurrence, long-term postoperative surveillance is recommended.

Conclusion

SLCT shows diverse clinical and pathological features, and treatment should be individualized. For selected young patients with early-stage (FIGO IA) SLCT, fertility-sparing surgery combined with adjuvant therapy may help achieve long-term disease-free survival and subsequent spontaneous pregnancy. This case supports the feasibility of fertility preservation in appropriately selected patients, but the conclusions are limited by the nature of a single case report.
