Plant-derived N, S-Co-doped carbon dots enable reductive engineering of Ag–Fe bimetallic nanocomposites with broad-spectrum bioactivity
Authors: Yeluri Kumari, Shilpa Raina, Parshant Bakshi, Zahid Nabi Sheikh, Jameel M Al-Khayri, Othman Al-Dossary, Bader Alsubaie, Mohammed I Aldaej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractN, S-co-doped carbon dots (N, S-CDs) were synthesized from Calotropis gigantea leaves via a green hydrothermal method and used as dual-function reducing and stabilizing agents for the in situ fabrication of Ag–Fe bimetallic nanocomposites (Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs). Thus, this study presents a sustainable nanoplatform that combines heteroatom-doped carbon dots with bimetallic systems without the use of external chemical reducing agents. The N, S-CDs exhibited effective antioxidant activity with IC₅₀ values of 39.17 µg mL⁻¹ (DPPH), 42.26 µg mL⁻¹ (FRAP), and 15.30 µg mL⁻¹ (ABTS). In contrast, Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs showed improved anti-inflammatory activity with albumin denaturation inhibition of 59.48–77.38% and proteinase inhibition up to 93.63%, along with enhanced cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells (IC₅₀ = 90.15 µg mL⁻¹). Both nanomaterials exhibited effective antimicrobial activity with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values ranging from 31.25 to 250 µg mL⁻¹ against tested pathogens. These results indicate that N, S-CDs are more effective as antioxidant agents due to their rich surface functional groups, whereas Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs show superior anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic performance due to synergistic interactions between the metal components and the carbon framework. Therefore, the present study revealed a green and efficient strategy for designing multifunctional nanocomposites with potential biomedical applications.Keywords: Carbon Dots, Ag-Fe, Calotropis gigantea, Antioxidant, Anti-inflammatory and cytotoxicity

Introduction

Over the past two decades, rationally engineered nanomaterials have emerged as a highly dynamic interdisciplinary field owing to their wide-ranging applications in cosmetics, textiles, food packaging, electronics, and biomedicine [1, 2]. Their nanoscale dimensions, high surface reactivity, and tunable electronic properties have enabled remarkable technological advancements. However, conventional physical and chemical synthesis methods often involve toxic reagents, harsh reaction conditions, and high energy consumption, raising serious environmental and biological concerns. Consequently, green synthesis approaches aligned with the principles of sustainable chemistry have gained increasing attention due to their reduced toxicity, cost-effectiveness, operational simplicity, and improved biocompatibility [3]. Among green synthesis routes, plant-mediated strategies are widely explored, where phytochemicals act as natural reducing and stabilizing agents [4]. Nevertheless, traditional phytochemical extraction methods rely on prolonged heating and solvent-intensive processes that can degrade bioactive compounds and suffer from poor reproducibility [5]. To address these limitations, biomass-derived carbon dots (CDs), also known as carbon quantum dots (CQDs), have recently emerged as efficient green reductants and stabilizers [6]. CDs are quasi-spherical, zero-dimensional carbon nanomaterials (< 10 nm) enriched with surface functional groups such as O–H, N–H, and C = O, which confer excellent redox activity and nucleation capability while preventing nanoparticle agglomeration [7–9]. Heteroatom doping, particularly with nitrogen and sulphur, further enhances their electronic structure, reducing efficiency, and photoluminescence properties [10–12], as nitrogen improves electron-donating ability while sulfur introduces additional surface defect sites that facilitate metal ion coordination and catalytic activity. Bottom-up hydrothermal synthesis is especially favored for producing high-quality CDs from renewable biomass precursors due to its simplicity, eco-friendliness, and ability to utilize intrinsic phytochemicals without the need for additional chemical reducing agents [13–16]. Importantly, biomass-derived CDs exhibit superior metal-ion reducing efficiency compared to crude plant extracts [17]. In this context, the present study explores the use of Calotropis gigantea-derived N, S co-doped carbon dots (CG@N, S-CDs) as a green nanoplatform for the synthesis of Ag–Fe bimetallic nanocomposites (Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs). Calotropis gigantea was selected due to its rich phytochemical composition, including alkaloids, flavonoids, and proteins [18], which facilitate carbonization and heteroatom incorporation during hydrothermal processing. The Ag–Fe bimetallic system was chosen due to the complementary properties of Ag and Fe, where silver provides strong antimicrobial activity and iron contributes redox-active behavior through Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ transitions, enabling enhanced electron transfer and biological interactions [19]. Although Ag–Fe bimetallic nanoparticles have been synthesized using various precursors [20–23], the utilization of heteroatom-doped carbon dots as both reducing and stabilizing agents for Ag–Fe nanocomposite formation remains limited [24–26]. Therefore, the novelty of this work lies in utilizing plant-derived N, S-co-doped carbon dots as a sustainable nanoreactor for the in situ fabrication of Ag–Fe nanocomposites, rather than in the synthesis of carbon dots themselves.

This study aims to systematically elucidate the structural, compositional, optical, and surface properties of both CG@N, S-CDs and Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs and to evaluate their in vitro biological activities, thereby establishing a green and functionally relevant nanobiotechnological approach.

Materials and methods

Materials

The chemicals used in this study are analytical grade, which do not require further purification. Silver nitrate (AgNO₃), ferric nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃), L-cysteine, ABTS, DPPH, TPTZ, ferric chloride (FeCl₃), potassium persulfate, methanol, hydrochloric acid (HCl), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), bovine serum albumin (BSA), trypsin, casein, trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and EDTA were used as received. Culture media including Luria–Bertani broth, Sabouraud dextrose broth, Muller-Hinton agar, and Sabouraud dextrose agar were used for antimicrobial studies. DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), antibiotics, and MTT reagent were used for cytotoxicity. All the above chemicals and reagents were obtained from Himedia, Merck India and CHD Pvt. Ltd.

Collection and preparation of Calotropis gigantea leaves

Fresh, healthy leaves of Calotropis gigantea (L.) Dryand. (Family: Apocynaceae) were collected from naturally growing wild populations in Rajamahendravaram, Andhra Pradesh, India (16.9891°N, 81.7780°E). The species is abundantly distributed in the region and commonly grows as a weed in open and roadside habitats. The plant material was not sourced from private agricultural land, protected areas, or commercial suppliers. Calotropis gigantea is not listed as an endangered or protected species under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) or under the Wildlife Protection Act of India. Therefore, no specific permits or collection licenses were required for sampling from non-protected public areas. Identification of the plant material was confirmed by Dr. A. Matta Reddy, Associate Professor, School of Life and Health Sciences, Adikavi Nannaya University, Rajamahendravaram. The herbarium was stored in the School of Life and Health Sciences, Adikavi Nannaya University, Rajamahendravaram with Name and Deposition Number of CG & AKNU-SLHS-03/2025. The collected leaves were thoroughly washed under running tap water to remove adhering debris and shade-dried at ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for five days. The dried material was pulverized using an electric laboratory blender to obtain a fine powder, which was stored in an airtight container in a moisture-free chamber until further experimental use.

Synthesis of N, S co-doped carbon dots (CDs)

The synthesis of N, S co-doped CDs was carried out according to the studies of Aladesanmi and Samuel 2023; Kang and Kang 2021 with slight modifications [27, 28]. In this method, the first 10 g of pulverized Calotropis gigantea leaves were dispersed in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred at 100 °C to ensure homogenous dispersion of particles. Then, this mixture was transferred to a hydrothermal autoclave, and an experiment was carried out for 12 h at 180 °C in a hot air oven (REMI RDHO-50). The obtained solution was cooled down to room temperature and filtered using Whatman filter paper No. 1 to remove larger undissolved particles, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min (REMI R-12 C Plus). Based on the typical rotor radius (approximately 14 cm) of this instrument, the corresponding relative centrifugal force (RCF) is estimated to be in the range of ~ 14,000 ×g. The purification of CDs was done using 0.22 μm PVDF membrane filters (HiMedia) and labelled as CG CDs. These purified CG CDs were co-doped with nitrogen and sulphur using the amino acid L-cysteine. In the co-doping process, 5 g of L-cysteine was added to the purified CG CDs, and the same procedure was followed from hydrothermal treatment to purification and the observed pH of the final solution is 6.8. These reactions were repeated multiple times to achieve the required quantity and labelled as CG@N, S-CDs.

Synthesis of Ag-Fe @N, S CDs nanomaterials

The seedless synthesis of Ag-Fe@N, S CDs was done according to the protocol of G. Raman et al. 2025 [29]. In this method, the aqueous solution containing silver nitrate (AgNO₃) and ferric nitrate (FeNO₃) (HiMedia), with each concentration of 0.01 M, was prepared and stirred at 70 °C. The prepared CG@N, S-CDs were released slowly into the above solution, and stirring was continued for the next 2 h to ensure complete reduction of Ag@Fe BMNPs. The unreacted nanoparticles were removed using a centrifugation process for 5 min at 10,000 rpm, and the obtained pellet was dried at 70 °C. The observed pH of the final solution of Ag@Fe BMNPs is 9.2.

Optical and structural properties

In this study, the optical and structural properties of synthesised CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs were analysed using various analytical techniques and referred from studies of Kota et al. 2024; Sudhakar et al. 2024 [30, 31].

The functional groups associated with CG@N, S-CDs, and responsible for the synthesis of Ag-Fe @N, S-CDs, are studied using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Bruker Alpha II) from the region of 500–4000 cm⁻¹, and prior to the experiment, the samples were dried with KBr pellets. The UV-Vis spectroscopy analysis of both samples is used to evaluate the Tauc’s plot, and measurement was done using the Shimadzu UV-2600 in the 200–800 nm wavelength region. The photoluminescence excitation and emission of CG@N and S-CDs are studied using a fluorescence spectrophotometer with wavelength regions of 300–800 nm (PERKIN ELMER FL-6500). The phase analysis of both CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs is evaluated using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD at 2θ angle from 0 to 120°) (Bruker D8 Discover). The surface morphology and elemental composition of Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs were studied using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDAX) analysis (Zeiss Gemini 500). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) is used to study the particle size distribution of both CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs (Thermo Fisher F200).

In vitro antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and cytotoxicity activities

In vitro antioxidant activity

Three assays were carried out to determine the free radical neutralizing capacity of CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs using ABTS, DPPH and FRAP assays. In the ABTS assay, the reagent of ABTS was prepared by using 7 mM of ABTS and 2.45 mM of potassium persulfate dissolved in distilled water and incubated in the dark for 16 h, and then the working solution was prepared by dissolving the stock reagent into methanol and by adjusting the absorbance between 0.700 and 0.750 at 734 nm. The samples of CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs are dissolved in 1 mL of methanol with concentrations of 25 –100 µg mL⁻¹ and sonicated for 5 min. 1 mL of ABTS reagent was added to all the concentrations of each sample and incubated for 30 min in the dark, and absorbance was taken at 734 nm. In the DPPH assay, CG@N, S-CDs, Ag-Fe @ N, and S-CDs were dissolved in 1 mL of methanol and sonicated for 5 min, and the DPPH reagent was prepared by dissolving 3 mM of DPPH in methanol, and the absorbance was adjusted between 0.600 and 0.650 at 517 nm. The working DPPH reagent was added to the sample and incubated for 30 min in the dark, and absorbance was taken at 517 nm. In the FRAP assay, CG@N, S-CDs and Ag-Fe @ N, S CDs are dissolved in 1 mL of 5% DMSO and sonicated for 5 min. The FRAP reagent was prepared by using acetate buffer (pH 3.6), TPTZ (10 mM) in HCl (40 mM) and 20 mM of FeCl₃, and the samples were added to the 1 mL of FRAP reagent. After 30 min of incubation in the dark, the absorbance was taken at 593 nm.

In vitro anti-inflammatory activity

The anti-inflammatory activity of CG@N, S-CDs, and Ag-Fe@N, S-CDs was evaluated using three in vitro assays: inhibition of albumin denaturation, inhibition of proteinase activity, and inhibition of RBC haemolysis. These assays were conducted using test concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 µg mL⁻¹, and IC50 values are calculated using a linear regression curve and Eq. (2).

The inhibition of albumin denaturation was carried out as follows: Both the samples were dissolved in 5% DMSO with respective concentrations and added to the 1% bovine serum albumin and prepared in phosphate buffered saline (PBS pH 6.4). The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min and transferred to a water bath with a temperature of 70 °C and incubated for 5 min to induce denaturation. The absorbance of final reaction mixtures was recorded at 660 nm.In the inhibition of proteinase enzyme activity assay, trypsin was taken as the model enzyme. In this assay, the reaction mixture consists of 0.06 mg/mL of trypsin, 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), and test samples of CG@N, S- CDs and Ag-Fe@CD NCs of different concentrations. These mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, and casein (0.8%) was added and further incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. To stop the reaction between enzyme and substrate, 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added and centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm, and absorbance was recorded at 280 nm. In the inhibition of RBC haemolysis, the freshly collected chicken blood in EDTA tubes was centrifuged and diluted in PBS buffer. Both the samples of different concentrations were added to the RBC suspension along with the PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and then further incubated at 54 °C for 5 min and then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min. The absorbance was determined at the 540 nm wavelength region.

In vitro cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity of both samples was evaluated on MCF-7 cell lines using the standard MTT assay. Prior to treatment with samples, cell lines were seeded with a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics in a 96-well plate, which was incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were treated with both the samples of various concentrations (12.5 to 200 µg mL⁻¹) and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, 20 µL of MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Then, 100 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA plate reader.

The activity/inhibition (%) and IC50 values of in vitro antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and cytotoxicity properties are calculated using Eqs. 1 & 2.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of CG@N, S-CDs, and Ag-Fe@CD NCs was evaluated against six pathogenic species, including two Gram-positive (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus), two Gram-negative (Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium), and two fungal pathogens (Aspergillus niger, Candida albicans). The assays were conducted using two methods: broth microdilution and well diffusion. For the broth microdilution assay, antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). A two-fold serial dilution of CG@CDs and Ag-Fe@CD NCs was prepared in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth for bacterial species and Sabouraud dextrose broth for fungal pathogens, with concentrations ranging from 3.9 µg mL⁻¹ to 1000 µg mL⁻¹. Each well contained 100 µL of test solution with a microbial inoculum of approximately 105 CFU mL⁻¹. MIC values were recorded as the lowest concentration where no visible growth occurred after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C for bacterial strains and 28 °C for fungal species. For the well diffusion assay, microbial cultures were plated on Muller-Hinton agar for bacterial pathogens and Sabouraud dextrose agar for fungal pathogens. Wells were created on the agar surface and filled with 50 µL of CG@CDs or Ag-Fe@CD NCs at concentrations of 500 and 1000 µg mL⁻¹. Streptomycin (25 µg mL⁻¹) was used as a positive control for bacteria, and Amphotericin B (25 µg mL⁻¹) for fungi.

Result and discussion

Physicochemical Characterization of CG@N, S-CDs and Ag–Fe@N, S-CD Nanocomposites. The structural, optical, and surface chemical properties of the biosynthesized CG@N, S-carbon dots (CDs) and Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites (NCs) were comprehensively investigated using a suite of spectroscopic techniques, followed by evaluation of their in vitro biological activities.

UV-vis spectroscopy and Tauc’s plot

The UV–visible absorption spectrum of CG@N, S-CDs exhibited two prominent absorption bands centered at approximately 230 nm and 276 nm, which are attributed to π–π* transitions of conjugated C = C bonds and n–π* transitions associated with carbonyl (C = O) groups, respectively. Carbon dots typically display characteristic absorption features within the 200–400 nm region, often accompanied by a long absorption tail extending into the visible–near-infrared region due to surface defect states and heteroatom doping [32]. Upon incorporation of CG@N, S-CDs into the precursor metal salt solutions, the resulting Ag–Fe@CD NCs exhibited distinct absorption peaks at 218 nm and 229 nm. The emergence and shift of these absorption features can be ascribed to strong electronic interactions between the carbon dot surface functional groups and the Ag and Fe metal species, as well as metal-induced modifications in the electronic structure of the carbon matrix [33]. The optical band gap energies of both samples were estimated using Tauc’s plots for direct and indirect electronic transitions. The direct band gap energies were determined to be 3.87 eV for CG@N, S-CDs and 5.15 eV for Ag–Fe@CD NCs (Fig. 1a), while the corresponding indirect band gap energies were 3.01 eV and 4.76 eV, respectively (Fig. 1b). The observed modulation of band gap energy upon metal incorporation reflects altered electronic density states, enhanced light absorption behavior, and improved charge carrier mobility, which are advantageous for optoelectronic and biofunctional applications [34].

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence spectrum of CG@N, S-CDs showed multiple emission peaks in the visible region with excitation initiated approximately at 340 nm (Fig. 1c). The Gaussian deconvolution of the cumulative emission profile revealed multiple emission peaks at 511 nm, 529 nm, 563 nm, 618 nm, and 629 nm wavelength regions. The multiple emission centres observed are due to the distribution of various surface functional groups and emissive traps for heteroatom doping [35]. Photoluminescence quenching is a common phenomenon that occurs when CDs are doped to the metal ions [36]. The mechanisms include charge transfer, energy transfer and modifying the CDs into non fluorescent compounds leads to photoluminescence quenching [37].

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the surface functional groups and bonding interactions present in CG@N, S-CDs and Ag–Fe@CD NCs (Fig. 1d). The FTIR spectrum of CG@N, S-CDs exhibited characteristic absorption bands at 3411 cm⁻¹ (O–H stretching), 1776 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), 1621 cm⁻¹ (C = C stretching), 1352 cm⁻¹ (C–N stretching), and 1149 cm⁻¹ (C–O–C stretching), along with peaks at 826 and 688 cm⁻¹ corresponding to C–H and C–S bending vibrations. The abundance of hydroxyl, carbonyl, amine, and ether groups imparts hydrophilicity, colloidal stability, and biocompatibility to the CDs. Following interaction with metal precursors, the FTIR spectrum of Ag–Fe@CD NCs revealed significant alterations in vibrational features. A broad transmittance band spanning 2887–3780 cm⁻¹ was observed, corresponding to overlapping O–H stretching vibrations, while narrower bands in the range of 2055–2380 cm⁻¹ and distinct peaks at 1362 and 1132 cm⁻¹ were attributed to C = O, C–N, and C–O functional groups. Additionally, absorption bands appearing below 996 cm⁻¹ are tentatively assigned to metal–oxygen (M–O) vibrations, confirming the formation of Ag–Fe oxide-related bonding within the carbon dot matrix. The presence of oxygen- and nitrogen-rich functional groups, such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl moieties, plays a pivotal role in the in situ reduction of metal ions and the stabilization of the resulting nanocomposites by preventing agglomeration and facilitating uniform metal dispersion [38].

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to investigate the crystalline structure, phase purity, and average crystallite size of the synthesized CG@N, S-carbon dots (CDs) and Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites. The diffraction pattern of CG@N, S-CDs (Fig. 2a) exhibited multiple broad diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 24.5°, 32.9°, 37.6°, 47.8°, 52.1°, 58.5°, 63.6°, 72.1°, and 78.5°, which were indexed to the (112), (202), (131), (312), (420), (114), (440), (513), and (353) lattice planes, respectively. These reflections showed good agreement with the standard JCPDS card no. 00-053-0518, confirming the structural consistency of the synthesized CDs. Carbon dots are generally characterized by a predominantly amorphous or poorly crystalline nature, with a broad diffraction feature typically observed around the (002) plane, corresponding to turbostratic or graphitic carbon domains [39]. The appearance of multiple diffused reflections in the present CG@N, S-CDs can be attributed to partial graphitization, heteroatom (N and S) incorporation, and the presence of short-range ordered carbon domains formed during hydrothermal carbonization of the Calotropis gigantea biomass. The broadness of the peaks further indicates nanoscale dimensions and structural disorder, which are characteristic features of doped carbon nanostructures. In contrast, the XRD pattern of Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites (Fig. 2b) displayed well-defined and sharper diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 27.5°, 32.0°, 34.5°, 36.3°, 38.2°, 45.0°, 48.3°, 55.2°, 58.5°, 62.8°, and 66.1°. These peaks were indexed to the (112), (200), (004), (211), (220), (301), (312), and (323) crystallographic planes and matched well with the standard JCPDS card no. 03-065-2736, corresponding to the tetragonal crystal system of silver iron sulfide. Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites were also matched with carbon peaks and the determined 2θ values are 26.60, 43.45, 46.33, 56.66 and 77.69 corresponds to crystallographic planes of (0 0 3), (1 0 1), (0 1 2), (1 0 4) and (1 1 0) matched with the standard JCPDS carbon no. 00-026-1079. The absence of additional impurity peaks confirms the high phase purity and successful formation of the Ag–Fe bimetallic phase anchored onto the CD matrix. The enhanced crystallinity observed in the Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites compared to the pristine CDs suggests effective nucleation and growth of the bimetallic phase facilitated by the functionalized carbon dot surface. The oxygen-, nitrogen-, and sulfur-containing functional groups on the CDs act as coordination sites, promoting homogeneous metal ion reduction and stabilizing the bimetallic nanostructures while preventing agglomeration. The average crystallite sizes of both CG@N, S-CDs and Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites were estimated using the Debye–Scherrer equation, yielding values in the range of 10–15 nm. The nanoscale crystallite size, coupled with high phase purity and intimate metal–carbon interactions, is expected to enhance surface reactivity and contribute significantly to the observed biological and functional performance of the synthesized nanomaterials.

FESEM and EDAX

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was employed to investigate the surface morphology, particle distribution, and microstructural features of the synthesized Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites. Owing to their ultrasmall size (< 10 nm) and predominantly amorphous carbonaceous nature, the pristine CG@N, S-CDs were not amenable to reliable FESEM imaging, and hence morphological analysis was primarily focused on the Ag–Fe@N, S-CD system (Fig. 3a–d). This limitation is commonly observed for carbon dots, as their dimensions fall below the spatial resolution of conventional FESEM techniques and lack sufficient electron density contrast. The FESEM micrographs of Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs revealed predominantly quasi-spherical nanoparticles with moderate size uniformity, along with a small fraction of irregularly shaped nanostructures. The observed morphological heterogeneity may be attributed to the seedless green reductive synthesis route, wherein nucleation and growth processes are governed by the availability and distribution of surface functional groups on the CG@N, S-CDs. The absence of external surfactants or capping agents further contributes to the formation of irregular morphologies during bimetallic nucleation.

Morphology of Ag-Fe@ N, S CDs (a) & (b) FESEM micrograph (c) EDAX spectrum and (d) Mapping

Notably, the nanoparticles exhibited moderate clustering without extensive agglomeration, indicating effective surface passivation and stabilization by the CG@N, S-CDs. The abundant oxygen-, nitrogen-, and sulfur-containing functional moieties present on the CD surface act as coordination sites for Ag⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions, facilitating controlled reduction and anchoring of the bimetallic phase. This interaction plays a crucial role in tailoring nanoparticle morphology and suppressing uncontrolled particle growth. The elemental composition and spatial distribution of the Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites were further confirmed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDAX) coupled with elemental mapping. The EDAX spectrum exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to Ag at 3.00 keV (Lα) and 3.15 keV (Lβ), Fe at 6.40 keV (Lα) and 0.70 keV (Kα), C at 0.28 keV, N at 0.39 keV, O at 0.50 keV, and S at 2.30 keV. The presence of these elements unequivocally confirms the successful formation of Ag–Fe bimetallic nanostructures embedded within the N, S co-doped carbon dot matrix. Elemental mapping analysis further demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of Ag and Fe across the nanocomposite surface, indicating uniform nucleation and growth of the bimetallic phase without phase segregation. The weight percentages (%) of each element are Ag (4.77%), Fe (4.40%), C (10.81%), N (27.47%), O (51.41%), and S (1.14%) found in EDAX analysis. The co-existence of C, N, O, and S signals substantiates the effective incorporation of heteroatom-doped CDs as both a reducing scaffold and stabilizing matrix. The intimate interfacial interaction between the metallic components and the functionalized carbon framework is expected to enhance electron transfer processes and contribute significantly to the observed biological and functional properties of the Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites.

HRTEM analysis

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was employed to investigate the particle size, morphology, dispersion, and structural features of CG@N, S-CDs and Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites, while selected area electron diffraction (SAED) provided complementary insights into their crystallinity (Fig. 4a–f).

HRTEM analysis of CG@N, S CDs and Ag-Fe@N, S CDs (a) SAED pattern of CG@N, S CDs (b) SAED pattern of Ag-Fe@N, S CDs (c) HRTEM micrograph of CG@N, S CDs (d) HRTEM micrograph of Ag-Fe@N, S CDs (e) Particle size histogram of CG@N, S CDs and (f) Particle size histogram of Ag-Fe@N, S CDs

The SAED patterns (Fig. 4a–b) revealed distinct structural characteristics of the two systems. The CG@N, S-CDs exhibited diffused rings, consistent with an amorphous carbonaceous structure, typical of heteroatom-doped carbon dots synthesized via hydrothermal carbonization. In contrast, the Ag–Fe@N, S-CD nanocomposites displayed a polycrystalline diffraction pattern, indicative of the successful nucleation and growth of Ag–Fe bimetallic domains within the carbon dot matrix. The formation of discrete diffraction rings confirms the presence of multiple crystalline planes corresponding to the bimetallic phase, while the surrounding amorphous halo arises from the carbonaceous scaffold. HRTEM micrographs revealed that CG@N, S-CDs were highly aggregated, forming dense clusters due to strong interparticle interactions driven by surface functional groups. In comparison, Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs exhibited moderate agglomeration, with nanoparticles more uniformly distributed on the CD surface, reflecting the stabilizing role of heteroatom functional groups during metal nucleation. The particle size distribution analysis indicated that the CG@N, S-CDs ranged from 1 to 10 nm, with an average particle size of 4.27 nm. In contrast, Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs exhibited a broader size distribution spanning 0 to 40 nm, with an average size of 15.18 nm, reflecting the incorporation of metallic domains into the carbon dot framework. The increase in average particle size upon bimetallic formation can be attributed to the nucleation and growth of Ag–Fe nanoparticles facilitated by the surface functional groups of the CDs. The HRTEM analysis also revealed lattice fringes in the Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs, suggesting well-defined crystalline domains within the polycrystalline matrix, which are likely responsible for enhanced electronic conductivity, catalytic activity, and biofunctional performance. The combination of nanoscale dimensions, controlled agglomeration, and polycrystalline metal incorporation underscores the potential of these nanocomposites for applications requiring high surface area, efficient electron transfer, and robust stability.

In vitro antioxidant activities

During normal cellular metabolism and under hypoxic conditions, aerobic cells generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), reactive nitrogen species (RNS), and nitric oxide (NO), which, if not adequately neutralized, can lead to oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [40]. Cells possess endogenous antioxidant enzymes to mitigate such stress; however, persistent or chronic oxidative stress may result in cellular dysfunction, somatic mutations, genomic instability, and contribute to the onset of chronic disorders, inflammatory pathologies, and tumorigenesis [41, 42]. Notably, the synergy between oxidative stress and chronic inflammation exacerbates autoimmune disorders and promotes carcinogenesis. Carbon dots (CDs), owing to their abundant surface functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, amino, and thiol moieties, exhibit significant free radical scavenging potential. These functional groups can donate electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize ROS and RNS, thereby mitigating oxidative damage and potentially preventing downstream cellular complications. In the present study, N,S co-doped CDs derived from Calotropis gigantea (CG@N, S-CDs) and their Ag–Fe bimetallic nanocomposites (Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs) were subjected to in vitro evaluation of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxicity activities. The outcomes are expressed as percentage activity and IC50 values to quantify their biofunctional potency. The antioxidant efficacy was assessed using three complementary assays—DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS—each targeting distinct free radical species and providing a comprehensive overview of radical scavenging capacity. Sample concentrations ranged from 25 to 100 µg mL⁻¹ and were benchmarked against standard ascorbic acid (Fig. 5a–c). CG@N, S-CDs demonstrated robust antioxidant activity across all assays, with DPPH radical scavenging ranging from 71.79% to 81.38% (IC50 = 39.17 µg mL⁻¹), FRAP values between 90.69% and 94.87% (IC50 = 42.26 µg mL⁻¹), and ABTS scavenging from 91.85% to 93.96% (IC50 = 15.30 µg mL⁻¹). In contrast, the Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs exhibited relatively lower DPPH activity (15.23%–41.33%, IC50 = 154.51 µg mL⁻¹), but retained moderate FRAP (80.78%–91.46%, IC50 = 38.55 µg mL⁻¹) and ABTS (72.50%–87.50%, IC50 = 47.99 µg mL⁻¹) activity. The effective antioxidant performance of CG@N, S-CDs can be attributed to the high density of surface-active functional groups capable of efficient electron and hydrogen donation. The modest reduction in activity observed in Ag–Fe@N, S-CDs is likely due to the coordination and partial bonding of these functional groups with metal ions during nanocomposite formation, which reduces their availability for radical neutralization. Nevertheless, the bimetallic incorporation provides structural and electronic benefits that may enhance other catalytic or biofunctional properties, highlighting the trade-off between surface chemistry and metal ion integration 44. Overall, these findings underscore the critical role of surface chemistry in modulating antioxidant behaviour and demonstrate that N, S co-doped CDs, both as standalone entities and as metal-integrated nanocomposites, possess significant potential for mitigating oxidative stress in biomedical applications.

In vitro anti-inflammatory activity

The in vitro anti-inflammatory potential of both nanomaterial systems was systematically evaluated using three complementary biochemical assays, namely inhibition of albumin denaturation, inhibition of proteinase activity, and stabilization of erythrocyte membranes against RBC haemolysis. In each assay, the test concentrations ranged from 25 to 100 µg mL⁻¹ and were directly benchmarked against the standard anti-inflammatory drug aspirin, as illustrated in Fig. 6 (a–c). The CG@N, S CDs exhibited concentration-dependent anti-inflammatory responses, achieving inhibition ranges of 34.88–49.85% for albumin denaturation, 63.81–89.97% for proteinase activity, and 39.98–51.14% for RBC haemolysis, with corresponding IC₅₀ values of 106.07, 21.67, and 113.89 µg mL⁻¹, respectively. In comparison, Ag-Fe@N, S CDs demonstrated moderately enhanced efficacy across all three assays, with inhibition values ranging from 59.48 to 77.38% for albumin denaturation, 69.0–93.63% for proteinase inhibition, and 40.02–64.25% for RBC haemolysis, accompanied by lower IC₅₀ values of 101.0, 16.44, and 108.95 µg mL⁻¹, respectively. The improved anti-inflammatory performance of Ag-Fe@N, S CDs may be attributed to the synergistic redox activity of Ag and Fe species, heteroatom (N, S) doping-induced surface defects, and enhanced interaction with inflammatory mediators, which collectively improve protein stabilization and membrane integrity.

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxic potential of CG@N, S CDs and Ag-Fe@N, S CDs was further assessed using MCF-7 human breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 6d), employing a broader concentration range of 12.5 to 200 µg mL⁻¹. The CG@N, S CDs displayed cytotoxicity ranging from 24 to 79.11%, whereas Ag-Fe@N, S CDs induced higher growth inhibition ranging from 31.7 to 87.44%, with IC₅₀ values of 469.92 and 90.15 µg mL⁻¹, respectively. The significantly lower IC₅₀ value observed for Ag-Fe@N, S CDs indicates anticancer efficacy, potentially arising from enhanced cellular uptake, reactive oxygen species generation, and mitochondrial dysfunction induced by metal-decorated carbon nanostructures. Overall, the findings demonstrate that both nanomaterials possess effective in vitro antioxidant and cytotoxic activities, alongside moderate anti-inflammatory efficacy. Previous studies on plant-mediated synthesis of Ag-Fe bimetallic nanoparticles have reported comparable biological activities; however, the present Ag-Fe@N, S CDs exhibit improved homogeneity, nanoscale uniformity, and surface functionality, which may account for their effective biological performance relative to earlier Ag-Fe BMNP systems [21, 23, 26]. Similar enhancements in bioactivity due to heteroatom-doped carbon dots and metal–carbon hybrid nanostructures have also been reported in recent literature, reinforcing the mechanistic plausibility of the observed results.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial efficacy of both CG@N, S CDs and Ag-Fe@N, S CDs demonstrated pronounced and effective inhibition against a broad spectrum of microbial strains, showing performance comparable to antibiotic standards and aligning well with previously reported carbon dot-based systems and Ag-Fe BMNPs. In the present investigation, antimicrobial activity was systematically evaluated against six microbial species employing two complementary methodologies—agar well diffusion and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays—as summarized in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 7. The CG@N, S CDs exhibited substantial antimicrobial potency, recording a maximum zone of inhibition (ZOI) of 20.5 mm and 17 mm against the fungal strains Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger, respectively, as well as against Salmonella typhimurium, at a concentration of 1000 µg mL⁻¹. Likewise, Ag-Fe@N, S CDs displayed marked antimicrobial performance, with the highest ZOI values observed against Streptococcus pneumoniae (19.6 mm), Escherichia coli (18 mm), Candida albicans (18 mm), and Aspergillus niger (19.2 mm) at the same concentration of 1000 µg mL⁻¹. Further substantiating these findings, MIC analysis revealed effective microbial growth inhibition at relatively low concentrations ranging from 31.25 to 250 µg mL⁻¹ for both samples, underscoring their strong antimicrobial efficiency. In comparison, N-, NS-, and NB-co-doped CDs synthesized via chemical precursor routes have previously been reported to exhibit MIC values between 300 and 500 µg mL⁻¹, indicating comparatively reduced efficacy relative to the present system [43]. Additionally, several studies have documented the effective antimicrobial performance of CDs, reporting inhibition trends consistent with those observed in the present investigation [44, 45]. Green-synthesized Ag-Fe BMNPs derived from diverse biological sources have also been shown to exert antimicrobial activity comparable to the results obtained in this study, further validating the effectiveness of the present nanostructures [19, 31]. Notably, the N, S CDs and nanocomposites evaluated herein exhibited effective antimicrobial performance relative to our previous work involving CDs and Ag-Fe@CD nanocomposites, emphasizing the enhanced bioactivity achieved through the present compositional and structural optimization [46, 47].

Conclusion

In the present study, the Calotropis gigantea leaves were used to synthesize N, S co-doped CDs using the hydrothermal-assisted method. These N, S co-doped CDs are used as natural reducing agents in the synthesis of Ag-Fe@ N, S CDs. Both the CG@ N, S CDs and Ag-Fe@ N, S CDs were subjected to evaluation using various analytical techniques and in vitro biological activities. The optical properties of CG@N, S CDs showed a specific peak between 200 and 400 nm, which is typical for CDs; similarly, Ag-Fe@N, S CDs also had a peak in the UV range, likely due to the metal CD nanocomposites. It was also observed that CG@N, S CDs showed multiple emission peaks, and the absence of the Ag-Fe@N, S CDs photoluminescence property is due to electron quenching. Respective functional groups of CG@N, S CDs, and their metal nanocomposites were observed using FTIR, and phase was confirmed using XRD. The CG@N, S CDs showed high agglomeration as compared to the Ag-Fe@N, S CDs, and particle size distribution ranged from 1 to 10 nm and 0 to 40 nm, respectively. The findings clearly establish that the tested system possesses robust and significant in vitro biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cytotoxic, and antimicrobial effects, underscoring its potential for diverse biomedical applications. The present study was only limited to the in vitro biological activities, and nonhomogeneous distribution of nanocomposites was observed. Therefore, further studies are required to improve the homogeneity of nanocomposites for better efficacy and efficiency.
