Trends in the incidence of lung cancer in never smokers in Eastern China: a retrospective population-based cohort study using regional electronic health records
Authors: Xiao Ge, Xing Liu, Wang Hong Xu, Yexiang Sun, Hongbo Lin, Peng Shen, Haiquan Chen, Na He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAlthough lung cancer in never smokers (LCNSs) accounts for an estimated 25% of all lung cancer cases, the temporal trends in LCNS incidence and its broader epidemiological patterns remain poorly understood. Our study examines the temporal trends in LCNS incidence and analyses key epidemiological characteristics, specifically, the trends in mortality rates, survival rates and changes in age at onset to illuminate the reasons for temporal trends in LCNS incidence.DesignRetrospective population-based cohort study.SettingRegional electronic health record (EHR) database linked to the cancer registry in eastern China.ParticipantsA total of 1 080 317 adults who have never smoked were included, among whom 4061 incident lung cancer cases were identified between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2020.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe temporal trends in LCNS incidence in a large population-based dynamic cohort were estimated, overall and separately by sex, age group and histological types. The broader epidemiological patterns of LCNS, such as trends in mortality rates, survival rates and changes in age at onset, were analysed.ResultsFrom 2009 through 2020, the incidence of LCNS among men increased significantly from 9.51 to 43.4 per 100 000 (average annual percentage change (AAPC) 10.51%; 95% CI (6.88% to 14.26%)) whereas a sharper increase was observed among women from 3.57 to 51.68 per 100 000 (AAPC 22.39%; 95% CI (18.36% to 26.55%)). The 2-year survival of LCNS improved from 39% in 2010 to 64% in 2018 for men, and from 57% in 2009 to 86% in 2020 for women, while the mortality from LCNS remained stable from 2014 through 2020 (men: AAPC −1.77%; 95% CI (−8.95% to 5.97%); women: AAPC 2.19%; 95% CI (−4.09% to 8.89%)). The increasing trend in LCNS incidence was observed across most of the age groups except the elderly population aged ≥85 years in both sexes and the men aged 45–54 years, but the increase was most evident in the younger population aged <45 years (men: AAPC 21.10%; 95% CI (3.57% to 41.60%); women: AAPC 32.05%; 95% CI (23.73% to 40.92%)). The incidence of adenocarcinoma increased sharply in both sexes, whereas the incidence of non-adenocarcinoma histological types either increased slightly or remained stable. The standardised average age at onset of LCNS decreased significantly over time among women (β=−0.58, robust SE=0.16, p=0.005) but not among men (β=−0.21, SE=0.15, p=0.189).ConclusionsThere is a substantial increase in LCNS incidence, especially among women and young populations. However, the reason for these observed trends remains unclear and warrants investigation.Keywords: China, Electronic Health Records, Epidemiology

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide. More than one-third of the world’s lung cancer cases and lung cancer-related deaths occur in China (37.0% and 39.8%, respectively).1 2 Although cigarette smoking is the predominant risk factor for the development of lung cancer, it’s estimated that 25% of cases occur in individuals who have never smoked or who have smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.3 Lung cancer in never smokers (LCNSs) has been associated with East Asian descent, female sex and adenocarcinoma histology.4,6

As the decline in cigarette smoking prevalence has led to considerable reductions in lung cancer,7 8 the proportion of individuals who have never smoked among patients with lung cancer has increased alarmingly.9 10 The increase in proportion frequency may not necessarily mean the real risk of lung cancer occurring in individuals who have never smoked (the LCNS incidence) is going up. The temporal trends in LCNS incidence remain debatable.11 To estimate and understand the incidence trends in LCNS, it is important to assess the individual smoking history. Because most cancer registries don’t collect the smoking status and are not linked with population-based smoking information, limited studies reported the temporal trends in LCNS incidence, with inconsistent results.12,16 In Europe, one Swedish cohort study found evidence of an increased LCNS incidence in men between 1971 and 1995.12 Another two studies in Finland (1972–2015)13 and the UK (1998–2018)14 reported decreased LCNS incidence in men, yet slightly increased or stable LCNS incidence in women. In the USA, Thun et al found no temporal trends when comparing the incidence among US women aged 40–69 years in the 1930s with female populations in other Western countries where few women smoked in the 1980s.15 A recent study using linked electronic health record (EHR) and cancer registry data between 2007 and 2018 in northern California reported that the LCNS incidence was stable within age, sex and ethnic groups, except among Asian and Pacific Islanders, for whom it increased at an average of 2.0% annually.16 However, to our best knowledge, there are no previous studies reporting the LCNS incidence trends in East Asia, where LCNS is more prevalent.

East Asians tend to be more susceptible to LCNS. Studies have generally reported that 30%–40% of lung cancer cases occur in individuals who have never smoked,17 18 with two times higher incidence rates of LCNS in East Asians15 16 19 compared with other ethnic groups. In East Asia, with a continuous decrease in smoking rates and extensive introduction of low-dose CT (LDCT) for lung cancer screening, there’s an increasing trend in the proportion of LCNS,18 20 a substantial stage shift towards earlier lung cancer diagnoses,20,22 and a sharp increase in incidence rates in women and young populations.23 24 These observations, in contradiction to classical concepts, prompt the study of sex-specific and age-specific lung cancer incidence rates in individuals who have never smoked.

In this study, we used the regional EHR database in the Yinzhou District of Ningbo city, linking individual smoking information with lung cancer registry data at a population level. Our study aims to examine the temporal trends in LCNS incidence by sex, age group and histological types in a large population-based dynamic cohort. Additionally, we analyse the epidemiological patterns of LCNS, such as trends in mortality rates, survival rates and changes in age at onset, to illuminate reasons for temporal trends in LCNS incidence.

Methods

Study design

Data were collected from the regional EHR database in the Yinzhou District of Ningbo City, an economically developed area in eastern China, consisting of approximately 1.6 million residents in 2020.25 Since 2006, Yinzhou District has been collecting EHRs of residents from all the hospitals and community health centres in the district. Since 2009, this regional EHR database has been integrated with data from various administrative databases, including general demographic characteristics, health check information, inpatient and outpatient electronic medical records, the health insurance database, the cancer registry and the death certificate database. This public health information and clinical data are seamlessly interconnected through unique personal identifiers. Presently, 98% of the population in Yinzhou has successfully enrolled in the regional EHR with a valid healthcare identifier. This regional EHR database has encompassed nearly all health-related activities of residents within the specified area, spanning from birth to death. A detailed description of the EHR database in Yinzhou has been published elsewhere.26 27

Leveraging the population-based EHR database in Yinzhou District, this retrospective dynamic cohort study included all never-smoking participants aged 18–100 years with available follow-up data from 1 January 2009 through 31 December 2020 and collected incident lung cancer cases through the cancer registry and deaths through the death certificate database. Participants entered the study at the date of first registration in the EHR database, at 18 years of age or on 1 January 2009, whichever came first. Each cohort member contributed person-years of observation from the date of entering the study until the date of lung cancer diagnosis, death, reaching 100 years of age, emigration or 31 December 2020, whichever came first.

When the participants were included in the EHR database, their smoking status at recruitment was collected by the primary healthcare professionals trained by the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention in Yinzhou using a standard questionnaire. The smoking status was recorded in the health behaviour module of the EHR database, with mutually exclusive categories of ‘current smoker’, ‘former smoker’, ‘quit smoking’ and ‘never smoker’. The EHR retained detailed information on the number of cigarettes smoked per day, starting age and quitting time for individuals who currently smoke and individuals who formerly smoked. We excluded individuals who currently smoke (21.55%), individuals who formerly smoked (3.39%) and those with unknown smoking information (0.03%). The final study included a total of 1 080 317 (75.02%) self-reported individuals who have never smoked (online supplemental S1 eFigure 1).

Lung cancers (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, code C34) diagnosed from 2009 through 2020 were derived from the cancer registry, with the morphologic verification reaching 85.82%. Five main histological groupings (online supplemental S1 eTable 1) were created: adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, small cell carcinoma, other specified carcinoma and unspecified types, based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, third edition morphology codes.28 We used 2014 as the first year in the histological type-specific incidence trend analysis because more than 80% patients were recorded with histological type information since 2014, as a result of evolving clinical practice, diagnostic methods and classification. The cancer registry monitors the occurrences of all types of cancer and captures all the lung cancer diagnoses from regional residents. Diagnosis of lung cancer made in the regional hospitals will be automatically captured. If a local resident is diagnosed with lung cancer outside the region, the corresponding information can still be captured if the participant visits regional hospitals for medical consultation or treatment. In addition, primary healthcare professionals actively follow up with enrolled residents and query the occurrence of cancer.

The death certificate data are linked with the lung cancer incidence data through a unique personal healthcare identifier, which allows the calculation of incidence-based mortality.29 Considering the lungs are a common site of metastasis for other cancers at diagnosis, using death certificate data directly may cause the misattribution or overattribution of deaths to lung cancer. The incidence-based mortality rates are calculated based on the incident cases, where some deaths may occur many years after cancer diagnosis; therefore, incidence-based mortality rates are underestimated in the initial years of cancer registration. For this reason, we required 5 years of data on incident cases before each year of mortality data (sometimes known as the ‘burn-in’ period for incidence-based mortality) to ensure capture of almost all deaths that occur from lung cancer. Thus, we presented the incidence-based mortality from 2014 to 2020 for trend analysis. The death certificate database captures complete and timely mortality for local residents, mandated by law.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Statistical analysis

We described the characteristics of the cohort population and incident lung cancer cases with continuous variables presented as median (IQR) and categorical variables described by frequencies and proportions. Crude, age-specific and age-standardised incidence rates were calculated per 100 000 person-years at risk. Age-standardised rates (ASRs) of incidence were standardised to the 2000 China Standard Population using 15 age groups spanning from 18 to 100 years. Annual incidence rates were computed by splitting data into 1-year intervals based on age and calendar year, providing corresponding age and person-years at risk as denominators each year. Annual age-specific incidence rates were calculated for each of the 15 age groups by dividing the annual incident cases of lung cancer by the annual person-years at risk, subsequently standardised to the 2000 China Standard Population to derive annual ASRs of incidence. The same methodology was applied for crude rates and ASRs of incidence-based mortality. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were calculated as the ratio of incidence rates between men and women by 10-year age group. The 95% CIs were computed using the natural logarithm transformation method, assuming Poisson-distributed case counts.30 Temporal trends in incidence and mortality were analysed using Joinpoint regression to estimate the average annual percentage change (AAPC) and corresponding 95% CI. We provided estimates of the 2-year survival rate among patients with LCNS. The standardised average age at onset for LCNS, standardised to the 2000 China Standard Population, was calculated.31 A linear regression model was employed to investigate the relationship between average age at onset and calendar year. Postregression residual diagnostics, including Durbin-Watson, Shapiro-Wilk, residual plots and QQ plots, were applied to validate model assumptions. Newey-West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) robust SEs were provided where potential autocorrelation was detected.

A two-sided p value of<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software, V.17.0 (StataCorp) or Joinpoint regression program software, V.4.9.0.0 (National Cancer Institute). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the secular trend whereby participants who outflowed in their last follow-up date were censored. There’s no meaningful effect because a low percentage (1.1%) of the cohort migrated out of the region.

Discussion

Using the individual smoking information in the regional EHR database, this population-based cohort study analysed the temporal trends in incidence, mortality, survival and changes in age at onset of LCNS over a 12 year period in China. There is a significant increasing trend in LCNS incidence from 2009 through 2020, especially in women and the young population. We found a rapid rise in adenocarcinoma incidence, with relatively stable trends in other histological types. Corresponding 2-year survival among patients with LCNS improved, and the trend in mortality for LCNS was stable. The standardised average age at onset for LCNS decreased significantly over time among women but not among men.

Individuals who have never smoked have experienced a high burden of lung cancer incidence. The high incidence of LCNS makes it the second most commonly diagnosed cancer among men and the fourth among women, if LCNS is considered as a separate cancer entity and rank-ordered with other cancers.32 The LCNS incidence in our study is comparable with the lung cancer incidence from the national cancer registry in China. As expected, lung cancer incidence in never-smoking men (28.71 per 100 000) was lower than overall lung cancer incidence in men (50.29 per 100 000)32 because smoking-related lung cancer incidence is higher. In contrast, the lung cancer incidence in never-smoking women (23.51 per 100 000) represents the majority of lung cancer cases in women (26.29 per 100 000),32 probably due to the extremely low prevalence of cigarette smoking among Chinese women (2.1%).33 Existing literature on incidence rates of LCNS is sparse, from Europe and the USA, and mainly based on cohorts with smoking information.13,1619 34 35 We can’t easily compare the incidence rates in our study with existing ones because of the different population standards used for age standardisation. Additionally, cohort studies are subject to selection bias, impacting the generalisability. Our study is the first population-based study conducted in East Asia, besides one population-based study in the USA.34 Previous studies generally indicated that East Asians are vulnerable to LCNS. Two studies from the USA indicated that Asian and Pacific Islanders had the highest LCNS incidence rates among all ethnicities.16 34 DeRouen et al specifically pointed out that Chinese American women had the highest LCNS incidence at 22.8 per 100 000 (standardised to the 2000 US Standard Population) compared with the incidence in Asian American (Japanese, Filipinx and Other Asian), Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander women.19 Nevertheless, our study provided the urgently needed incidence rates of LCNS to quantify the disease burden of LCNS in East Asia.

In this study, we observed a significant increase in lung cancer incidence among individuals who have never smoked, with a fivefold increase in men (AAPC 10.51%) and a 14-fold increase in women (AAPC 22.39%) between 2009 and 2020. This rise in the incidence among LCNS contrasts with findings from studies in Europe and the USA, which have not reported a similar upward trend over time.13,1634 However, differences in ethnicity, study period and risk exposures are likely to account for the variations in temporal trends observed in LCNS incidence between Western countries and China. Our findings align with those from East Asia. In Shanghai, two population-based studies observed a rapid increase in lung cancer incidence among women, who have a low smoking prevalence, with an AAPC of 11.98% in Pudong New Area23 and 19.84% in Xuhui District36 since 2011. Although these studies did not stratify by smoking status, they reported stable or slightly increasing trends in lung cancer incidence among men, which supports our interpretation: a rise in LCNS may be offset by a decline in LCNS in men.

In addition to the significant increase in incidence, we noted the stable mortality, despite the lack of mortality data during the initial 2009–2014 period (a 5 year ‘burn-in’ phase). This epidemiological pattern—marked by a dramatic rise in incidence and stable mortality—suggests the potential for overdiagnosis of lung cancer. This trend has been widely observed in East Asian women, particularly after the introduction of LDCT screening.23 24 36 However, a multicentre cohort study from South Korea did not find a significant sex difference, suggesting that both men and women are equally susceptible to overdiagnosis.21

The 2 year survival rates for LCNS in our study were notably higher than those reported in randomised clinical trials,37 further reinforcing our assumption of overdiagnosis. Previous research has shown that early detection of early-stage indolent lung cancers via LDCT can lead to exceptionally high survival rates (eg, 90% at 10 years).38 The increased 2-year survival observed in our study is primarily attributed to the early detection rather than advancements in treatment. Early detection of early-stage lung cancers improves 2-year survival rates; changes in mortality take longer to manifest. Conversely, advancements in treatment typically lead to improved survival rates and mortality for late-stage cancers within the same timeframe.

The substantial increase in LCNS incidence is likely due to the increased detection of early-stage, indolent lung cancers and their correspondingly increased proportion. Since the publication of the benefits of LDCT screening in 2011,39 China has incorporated LDCT as part of its national lung cancer screening programme. Yinzhou District, one of China’s most economically developed regions, has been part of the Cancer Screening Programme in Urban China since 2012,40 providing residents with easy access to LDCT imaging covered by health insurance. The widespread implementation of LDCT has led to a notable shift in lung cancer diagnosis, with more cases being detected at an earlier stage.41 A recent study in Taiwan found that the proportion of patients diagnosed with early-stage lung cancer (stage 0/I/II) increased dramatically from 19.3% in 2006 to 62.8% in 2019.42 A population-based study in Shanghai reported that early-stage (stage I) lung cancer incidence rose over 19-fold in Chinese women and approximately fivefold in men.23 Notably, early-stage lung cancer was detected at a higher rate among individuals who have never smoked compared with the general population.22

Our study revealed a higher vulnerability to LCNS in women at younger ages, contrasting with a higher incidence of LCNS in men aged 55 years and older. The question of whether women are more susceptible to LCNS has been under discussion, yielding varied findings. Two previous studies found significantly higher incidence of LCNS in women (aged 40–79 years and 30+ years, separately) compared with men,16 35 while others reported a non-significant difference between the two sexes aged 40+ years.15 34 Similar to our study results, Pinheiro et al identified that LCNS was more common among men than women, but with women’s rates exceeding men’s in younger ages than 54 years.34 Our study further showed the temporal trends in LCNS incidence by age groups and sex. Until around 2013, the LCNS incidence in young men aged <54 years was generally higher than that in women. The rapid increase in LCNS incidence among women resulted in higher incidence rates among women aged <54 years compared with men after 2013. Among the elderly populations aged 55 years and above, however, the LCNS incidence was consistently higher in men than in women from 2009 through 2020 despite the rapider increase in women. Importantly, the absolute risk remained relatively low in the younger population, but it escalated significantly with age. The incidence of LCNS peaked in age groups of 65–84 years for both sexes.

The adenocarcinoma is the predominant histological subtype among individuals who have never smoked, comprising 61.13% of LCNS cases in men and 90.31% in women in our cohort. The rising incidence of adenocarcinoma, compared with other histological types, appears to be driving the overall increase in LCNS. Women are more susceptible to the risk of adenocarcinoma compared with men.43 44 Our study demonstrates that the risk of adenocarcinoma increases more rapidly among never-smoking women than among never-smoking men. These sex differences in histological subtype distribution and the pace of risk escalation associated with these subtypes likely contribute to the more rapid increase in LCNS incidence among women compared with men.

Interestingly, the average age at onset of LCNS notably decreased among women, but not among men. Specifically, the average age at onset decreased significantly for adenocarcinoma while remaining unchanged for other histological types. This observed age trend appears to be influenced by LDCT screening, which tends to detect lung adenocarcinomas with ground glass opacity features more frequently.20 These features are particularly more prevalent among young, never-smoking women than men.

The increasing incidence of LCNS across various age groups suggests increased risk exposure. Traditionally, factors such as second-hand smoke, occupational carcinogens, cooking fumes, ambient air pollution or genetic susceptibility are believed to increase the LCNS risk.45 In China, the declining prevalence of active smoking and implementation of anti-smoking laws in public spaces have reduced exposure to second-hand smoke.46 Evidence on second-hand smoke does not seem to explain the growing incidence of LCNS.

Occupational carcinogens and cooking fumes have likely declined due to the shift from traditional occupations to novel green productivity, improved indoor ventilation and increased use of powerful hood fans, and are not likely to play a major role in the upward trend in LCNS incidence in the population. Ambient air pollution may lead to an increased risk of LCNS, especially adenocarcinoma.47 However, this may not be the case in the area, where environmental air quality has improved significantly, with concentrations of particulate matter with a diameter of <2.5 µm decreasing from 61 µg/m3 in 2013 to 25 µg/m3 in 2020, after overcoming the air pollution challenges in the early 2010s.48 Genetic factors, particularly among women, contribute to increased susceptibility to lung cancer, with evidence suggesting higher frequencies of mutations in critical driver genes, such as TP53 and the KRAS oncogene.49 Nevertheless, it is unlikely that these genetic factors explain the increase in LCNS incidence in Yinzhou, Ningbo. Hence, further investigations are essential to identify factors underlying the increase in LCNS and deepen our understanding of LCNS aetiology.

Linking contemporary individual-level EHR and cancer registry data, this analysis examined 12-year trends (2009–2020) in LCNS incidence and associated epidemiological patterns in a cohort of 1 080 317 individuals who have never smoked. The study benefits from population-level smoking information enabled by extensive EHR coverage of local residents. Among the cohort, 4061 incident lung cancer cases were identified. The cancer registry further ensures relatively complete case ascertainment by capturing extra-regional cancer diagnoses.

Nevertheless, our study has inherent limitations. First, we defined never-smoking status based on self-reports rather than using more objective biochemical tests, which could lead to misclassification of smoking status. However, studies assessing the validity of self-reported smoking status have generally found low misclassification rates among individuals who have never smoked (<3%).50,55 Self-reporting proved reliable for identifying individuals who have never smoked, although it tended to misclassify some individuals who currently smoke as individuals who formerly smoked rather than as individuals who have never smoked when compared with cotinine measurements.53,55 Second, we included participants aged 18–100 years, and excluding certain age groups may affect comparability across studies. However, we believe the impact of excluding individuals aged under 18 years and over 100 years is minimal, given the very low incidence of lung cancer before 18 years and low proportion in the general population above 100 years. Third, there is a potential for loss to follow-up for participants who migrated out of the region, with lung cancer occurring thereafter. However, only 1.1% of the cohort outflowed in their last follow-up. Last but not least, the study population was from a developed city in eastern China, mainly Han Chinese. Thus, the conclusions could not be generalised to other populations.

Conclusions

In summary, this study found a significant rise in lung cancer incidence among individuals who never smoked from 2009 to 2020, especially in women and younger populations. The incidence of adenocarcinoma increased in individuals who never smoked, whereas non-adenocarcinoma histological types showed minimal change. The use of LDCT imaging for early detection may potentially contribute to the increase in LCNS incidence and the disparities by sex, age and histological type. Further research is imperative to identify the aetiologic reasons.
