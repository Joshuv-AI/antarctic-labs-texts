Interlimb training improves motor function in partial-hand but not necessarily transradial simulated prosthesis use
Authors: Bennett L. Alterman, Saif Ali, Emily Keeton, Katrina Binkley, William Hendrix, Perry J. Lee, John T. Johnson, Shuo Wang, James Kling, Lewis A. Wheaton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of empirical approaches for functional rehabilitation during the acute and subacute stages following upper limb amputation is needed. For persons with unilateral limb loss, interlimb training is a potentially attractive approach, as it may allow for the translation of learned function from the unaffected to affected side while awaiting prosthesis fitting. Persons with sound limbs performed simple and complex reach-to-grasp motor tasks using a body-powered transradial or partial-hand prosthesis simulator in a 5-day interlimb training paradigm. Prior work in a single-session paradigm found improvments in motor control with prosthesis simulators, with an especially strong effect in partial-hand device use. Here, we hypothesized that participating in interlimb training would result in improvements in motor control with the prostheses, particularly in partial-hand device use and during complex tasks. Overall, partial-hand prosthesis users showed greater performance adaptations with interlimb training than transradial prosthesis users, who tended to show no improvements following training in either task. We also found that partial-hand users showed significantly higher reach peak velocities compared to transradial users following training in both the simple and complex task. These results suggest that interlimb training may provide tangible benefits during early-stage rehabilitation following upper limb amputation, especially in cases of partial-hand loss.

Introduction

Transradial and partial-hand amputation represent the two most common levels of upper limb loss1. For both groups, learning to use a prosthesis is an important tool in the arsenal of recovery. However, learning how to use prostheses can be challenging2,3. Investigations into the reasons for the nonuse of prosthetic limbs have identified factors related to comfort and function4–8, but despite abundant technological advances in design, durability, and materials—paired with routine clinical implementation—there is no empirical evidence of an impact on device acceptance9,10. Similarly, data recommending any single approach for implementation or device acclimation are limited11, nor is there evidence for any single training protocol, intensity, treatment setting, or service delivery model for persons with upper limb amputation to gain function with prostheses12.

Two commonly prescribed types of prostheses are body-powered and myoelectric devices. Body-powered devices rely on flexion/extension of the remaining joints or limb to activate the opening or closing of the effector. In transradial devices, this often relies on a harness and cable system to translate movements from the shoulders into control of the effector. These devices require effort from the contralateral shoulder, and are thus operated via bilateral control, as opposed to partial-hand devices which require only unilateral input. Myoelectric devices are externally powered and operate through the detection of electrical signals from muscles of the residuum via electrodes in contact with the skin. These devices rely on programmed actions based on predefined muscle activation patterns to generate corresponding actions (e.g., opening or closing of the effector). A systematic review revealed no superiority between myoelectric devices and body-powered devices13, and data from Medicare Durable Medical Equipment files revealed that body-powered prostheses were prescribed at a higher rate (43%) than myoelectric prostheses (27.6%) in 20–67-year-old patients from 2013 to 201414. One study evaluating advanced multi-grip vs. standard myoelectric prosthetic hands reported that in all of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health-Model (ICF-Model) categories, there were no clear benefits of advanced devices15. In some measures, the standard device even outperformed the multi-grip device. Thus, body-powered devices are still seemingly popular, and are being considered in this work.

Patients are not typically fitted with a prosthesis for at least 3‒6 months after amputation, which can negatively affect rehabilitation outcomes16,17. There is a clinical need for improved interventional paradigms to provide optimum translational benefit to device users. Providing tools to allow therapists to engage in rehabilitative interventions sooner after amputation surgery is critical in creating the opportunity for earlier functional benefit for patients. Prosthesis training regimes vary tremendously, and monitoring and quantifying progress during recovery and rehabilitation remains inconsistent18. Interlimb training or interlimb transfer (ILT)—training one limb to induce changes in brain organization and behavior corresponding not only to the trained limb but also to the untrained limb—has shown some promise as an early interventional technique to enhance functional outcomes following unilateral limb loss19, leveraging the pre-prosthesis fitting period to train prosthesis use in unilateral amputation. A study conducted in able-bodied individuals revealed that a significant transfer effect occurred from the nondominant hand to the dominant hand and vice versa when trained in dexterity-based tasks20. Studies into ILT using a body-powered transradial prosthesis simulator (split-hook effector) have revealed effects, including decreasing movement time19, a valid measure for quantifying motor adaptation21. A series of articles authored by Romkema, Bongers, and van der Sluis22–24 examined behavioral changes (error frequency, grip force production, and the duration of hand opening) via a myoelectric transradial prosthesis simulator (hand effector), controlled by electrodes detecting changes in electrical activity related to muscle contractions from wrist flexors and extensors. When reach, grasp, and grip force production are limited in interlimb training, only varying grip forces have been shown to have a significant transfer effect across limbs24. Furthermore, improvements in movement time were transferred across limbs25. Weeks, Wallace, and Anderson19 also reported reductions in movement time, but only in a later retention test, not in the experimental post-test. This group also reported that tasks involving the transport of an object, as opposed to the manipulation of a fixed object, resulted in more performance errors. Finally, a study of experienced myoelectric prosthesis users revealed that movement time, hand opening, and functional performance improved, but force control did not26. These findings suggest a mixed benefit of ILT, highlighting the need for further clarity on this approach.

While the abovementioned results suggest a mix of benefits in both myoelectric and body-powered systems, a critical question may be related to the level of prosthesis use being studied. Prior work has evaluated patterns of behavior with partial-hand prosthesis use compared with transradial use27. This study revealed that, specifically, partial-hand body-powered prosthesis users showed increases in reach peak velocity within a single-day session and leveraged multiple strategies to accomplish the motor task, whereas transradial device users tended to use only a single strategy. Furthermore, neural activation patterns were compared between partial-hand and transradial body-powered prosthesis simulator use on the dominant (right) limb28. It was found that partial-hand prosthesis users especially engaged bilateral parietofrontal circuits with prosthesis use, whereas transradial users showed decreased neural activity that was more limited to ipsilateral motor areas. Owing to the bilateral trunk control of the body-powered transradial device, ipsilateral activity was suspected29–31. However, the findings with the partial-hand device were more surprising given the unilateral control of the partial-hand prosthesis. This may suggest that neural representations of action with prostheses in the non-dominant hemisphere might better support interhemispheric motor planning in partial-hand prosthesis users32. Investigations into neural activity during myoelectric device use may yield comparable results given the similar operation through unilateral control. As the previously described studies have evaluated ILT in prosthesis use higher than the wrist, it is unclear how ILT may differentially impact persons across major levels of body-powered prosthesis use. The goal of this study is to evaluate whether ILT is beneficial for partial-hand users relative to transradial device users. Here, participants underwent 3 days of prosthesis training with a prosthesis simulator worn on their left limb, preceded and followed by motor testing with a prosthesis simulator worn on their right limb in a Pre-Post design. While the testing and training tasks were different, they both involved reaching and grasping various objects (see Methods). The testing days specifically sought to evaluate how motor task difficulty might impact results via translation (easier) and rotation (harder) tasks. The translation task involved moving a metal disc between two targets. The rotation task involved lifting and rotating a marker 90 degrees and placing it on a target. We hypothesize that interlimb training will lead to improvements in motor control, as measured by reach peak velocity, movement duration, reach duration, and placement error, and that these effects will be most notable in partial-hand users. Further, these improvements will be seen across the range of task difficulties.

Results

For both the translation and rotation tasks, the raw values for reach peak velocity (m/s), movement duration (ms), reach duration (ms), and placement error were compared both between the first testing session (“Pre”) and final testing session (“Post”) and between transradial and partial-hand prosthesis simulators (TrPS and PhPS, respectively) within each task.

Testing

Reach peak velocity

The translation task showed a main effect of group (χ2(1) = 17.260, p < 0.0001, ɛ2 = 0.30), session (χ2(1) = 11.509, p = 0.0007, ɛ2 = 0.22), and a group × session interaction effect (χ2(3) = 29.320, p < 0.0001, ɛ2 = 0.68) (Fig. 1A). This revealed a significant increase in the peak velocity over training in the partial-hand group (p = 0.0008, d = -1.1) but not in the transradial group (p = 0.2). The peak velocity following training by partial-hand users was greater than that following training by transradial users (p = 0.03, d = 0.7).

The rotation task showed a main effect of group (χ2(1) = 7.120, p = 0.008, ɛ2 = 0.14), session (χ2(1) = 15.008, p = 0.0001, ɛ2 = 0.27), and a group × session interaction effect (χ2(3) = 26.193, p < 0.0001, ɛ2 = 0.65) (Fig. 1B). While the partial-hand users had higher peak velocities than did the transradial users before (p = 0.002, d = 1.0) and after training (p = 0.0007, d = 1.1), they also showed a significant improvement because of training (p = 0.04, d = -0.7), whereas the transradial users did not (p = 0.2).

Placement error

Overall, there was no effect of placement error in either group. The translation task showed no main effect of group (χ2(1) = 1.554, p = 0.21), session (χ2(1) = 0.002, p = 0.96), or a group × session interaction effect (χ2(3) = 1.627, p = 0.65) (Fig. 4A). The rotation task showed no main effect of group (χ2(1) = 0.529, p = 0.47), session (χ2(1) = 2.912, p = 0.09), or a group × session interaction effect (χ2(3) = 3.938, p = 0.27) (Fig. 4B). As participants were not instructed to improve placement performance or given feedback on placement performance, we had no clear expectation of placement error change.

Training

Both the TrPS and PhPS training groups presented an increase in the number of trials completed, a decrease in trial duration, and a decrease in the number of errors committed per trial during training sessions from day 2 to day 4 (Fig. 5). According to post hoc t-tests, there were no significant differences between device levels across trials completed (p = 0.41), trial duration (p = 0.48), or number of errors per trial (p = 0.80).

Discussion

In this work, we investigated how ILT affects motor control during prosthesis use and whether the level of simulated prosthesis use and task complexity affect the outcomes of ILT during action execution. Overall, the findings suggest that ILT may be beneficial for prosthesis use, particularly in partial-hand device users, who showed kinematic benefits of ILT for both tasks, apart from reach duration for the rotation task. Overall, the most notable and consistent effects across participants were seen in partial-hand users. It is unlikely that this effect was due to training intensity, as there was no difference in training data between groups. This result may clarify the mixed results of interlimb training in transradial and higher levels of device usage reported in the literature19,23 and expand our knowledge of distinctions in outcomes on the basis of task specificity and the possibility that ILT may be more robust for individuals using partial-hand prostheses.

For the translation task, partial-hand prosthesis users experienced improvements in all kinematic measures (peak velocity, movement duration, and reach duration). The translation task (metal disc translation) was similar to one training task (wooden disc translation). That transfer happened for a similar task for a different object in all kinematic measures in partial-hand users cannot be explained simply by the similarity in the two tasks, as there was no improvement noted on any kinematic measures for the translation task in the transradial device users. Despite being a partial-hand prosthesis, no participants used parts of their biological limbs to aid in performing the task. Research has demonstrated the generalizability of interlimb transfer for a variety of tasks regardless of task difficulty33. Recent studies have shed light on how motor function might be transferred between different effectors. For example, initial sensorimotor learning can transfer between the upper and lower limbs34. As the partial-hand prosthesis likely affords more movement variability trial to trial27, it remains unclear whether this might impact learning rates in partial-hand users beyond transradial device users35.

For the rotation task, while the reach duration did not improve, the peak velocity and movement duration significantly improved for the partial-hand users only. Reach duration has often been associated with motor planning associated with action goals36,37. Thus, the improvement in reach duration for the translation task, but not for the rotation task, may indicate that the partial-hand users have undergone a task-dependent change in their motor planning to the action goal to afford improvements in reach duration. Although the reach duration did not improve in the partial-hand group in the rotation task, the movement duration improved. These findings suggest that ILT produced a primary benefit in the post-grasp phase of movement. We were unable to confidently analyze the post-grasp phase, as small changes in the initial grasp, avoiding dropping the object, and determining a confident grasp of the object impacted the ability to quantify this phase with certainty compared with our prior studies38. Further studies are needed to evaluate separate phases of reach-to-place tasks.

The peak velocity data revealed that for both tasks, partial-hand users presented higher peak velocities than did transradial users. Furthermore, nearly all partial-hand users who underwent ILT showed an increase from Pre to Post, whereas the transradial users as a group appeared to converge around a central reach peak velocity (as well as movement duration and reach duration to an extent) at Post on the Rotation task, with some improving, while others did not (Fig. 1B). This may highlight the interparticipant variability in motor learning based on the level of the device. Given that the transradial device hinders numerous degrees of freedom and muscular activities, it may cause more variable responses to the dynamics of muscle control in the upper limb39. Studies have demonstrated that limb immobilization can impact motor performance through altered kinematics40. Studies in transradial amputation have revealed common adaptations during prosthesis use (prepositioning, posture, and range of motion), which present different challenges in different tasks and can lead to increased interparticipant variability41. The participants occasionally reported that the use of the transradial device was particularly fatiguing. Variability in the fatiguing nature of prosthesis use might increase interparticipant variability42, which could impact motor learning43. Understanding these variable responses in transradial users (but not partial-hand users), which are uncovered through training, might support further understanding of the impacts of device level on motor adaptations. This behavior may indicate device-induced constraints on performance that exist in environments of both high and low task demands.

In the context of functional coordination of movement, hemispheric coordination of excitation and inhibition remains a crucial component in motor control and motor learning strategies, and is modulated in a task-dependent manner44–46. Studies have revealed that decreases in motor excitability can also suppress skill acquisition with the contralateral hand, thus leading to increased learning of the ipsilateral hand47,48. Action encoding pathways exist bilaterally49, and thus a training paradigm that focuses on not one, but both hemispheres may provide sufficient interhemispheric encoding of motor representations, that will translate to functional benefit. It has been argued that there is a laterality to brain function in that the left hemisphere (in right-handers) has become specialized in movement dynamics, while the right hemisphere controls more stable steady-state processing50,51. Kelly, Mizelle, & Wheaton52 proposed that translation of visually-perceived action for motor simulation occurs at the visual level, and thus lateralized hemispheric encoding would occur prior to inputting at the motor cortex. Recent work has demonstrated that motor skill acquisition engages both hemispheres to facilitate interlimb transfer in complex tasks53. These results suggest the possibility of interhemispheric interactions driving a shift from lateralized to bilateralized encoding of action goals which could be optimal for acquiring skills for a new prosthesis early in motor training. Further longitudinal studies of prosthesis learning with ILT in combination with neuroimaging will be useful to understand this possibility.

There are inherent limitations in this study. Firstly, this work was conducted using prosthesis simulators in able-bodied adults. Although prosthesis simulation has been validated as a means of studying prosthesis use, there remains a need to evaluate interlimb training effects in true prosthesis users. Additionally, the comparison of the two prosthesis simulator devices may be problematic, as they function through different mechanisms of control. The partial-hand device is controlled via unilateral control (active opening and closing of the effector through wrist flexion and extension), whereas the transradial device requires bilateral control (requires active opening of the effector through biscapular flexion). The differences between the two devices may create an inadvertent trade-off between maneuverability and grasp tightness. Furthermore, the study had limited sample size and did not control for the age of the participants, which may play a role in the degree of ILT20. Additionally, any nonsignificant effects in the kinematic data may be due to occlusion from the effects of grasp strategy variability explored by Alterman et al.27. This unanticipated confound limited the sample size for the statistical analysis. It remains unclear whether the lack of effect is due to methodological limitations or a genuine absence of effect; thus, with additional data collection, we may be able to begin to tease apart the effects of grasp variability and evaluate the efficacy of ILT mediated by the choice of grasp posture.

Future work will consider a longer translational study with a secondary follow-up test to examine how long the effects of ILT persist to ensure that the results found here are not simply after-effects but true results due to interlimb training. Furthermore, a longer or more intensive ILT regimen may show greater benefit; however, the appropriate dosage of training is presently unknown in upper limb amputation12. There is a need to assess these effects in persons with amputation to validate results in a clinical setting. Additionally, future studies should consider the inclusion of myoelectric prostheses. This study provides some early evidence of the promise of kinematic improvements possibly due to ILT in body-powered partial-hand prosthesis use while indicating possible challenges in transradial prosthesis use.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty right-handed, able-bodied adults (age = 23.2 ± 3.1 years, 12 females) with intact upper extremities were included. A subset of these data was used in a previous study by Alterman et al.27, in which kinematic data were examined after one day of prosthesis use. All participants provided written, informed consent, and the Georgia Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board approved all methods. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. The participants completed the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory54 to confirm right-handedness. The participants were placed pseudorandomly into one of two groups: TrPS training (n = 10) and PhPS training (n = 10) (Fig. 6).

Devices

The participants enrolled in this study wore either a transradial prosthesis simulator (TrPS) or a partial-hand prosthetic simulator (PhPS) that fit over their intact right limb (Fig. 6). By restricting movement of the hand and wrist joints, the TrPS imitates a below-elbow amputation55. Additionally, sensory feedback is restricted by a soft fabric within the palm of the hand that forces an open hand posture. Using a figure-of-nine harness, the TrPS features a body-powered opening that allows for on-demand, active opening and passive closing (at rest) of the split-hook end-effector (Vari-Pinch Prehensor™ (V2P™, ToughWare PRX, Westminster, CO, USA)) via glenohumeral flexion and scapular/bi-scapular abduction. The figure-of-nine harness was adjusted so that the northwestern ring was inferior and contralateral to C7 on each participant. This adjustment was necessary to account for the varying body sizes and arm lengths of the participants. These adjustments ultimately allowed for a consistent elbow angle of 130–135° across all participants when the prosthesis effector was initially opened27.

Fig. 6Overall study design.

Overall study design.

The other device used in this study was the partial-hand prosthesis simulator (PhPS), which is a modified tenodesis brace (U-17RM, Becker Orthopedic, Troy) that simulates the loss of digits 1–3 at the metacarpophalangeal joint. The middle and fore fingers are fastened to a plate proximal to the distal joint of each finger, and the thumb is restricted at a right angle secured along the palm56,57. Like the TrPS, the PhPS functions via body-powered voluntary opening. Unlike the TrPS, the PhPS opens via wrist flexion and closes via wrist extension (Fig. 6) and requires voluntary action to both open and close the device grasp. To imitate the functionality of the human finger, artificial digits were assembled in a 3-jaw chuck pinch grasp. Importantly, the PhPS does not alter the length of the effector, which could result in a change in the perception of limb length and ultimately function.

Experimental tasks

Testing

The testing tasks included a translation and a rotation task. The translation task involved reaching and grasping a small metal disc and translating it to a series of specified targets. The rotation task involved reaching and grasping a marker sitting horizontally on a cradle and rotating it to place it vertically on a series of specified targets. Testing tasks mimicked aspects of the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT)58. The ARAT categorizes tasks by action type and performance difficulty, and has become a common assessment of upper limb function. Participants completing these tasks needed to account for object-specific properties such as shape, weight, and object material, while also considering object manoeuverability (translation and rotation), spatiotemporal precision, and aim.

Training

The training tasks included the wooden disc translation (Fig. 7A), tubes and rods (Fig. 7B), key insertion and locking/unlocking (Fig. 7C), and “operation” (Fig. 7D) tasks. The wooden disc translation task involved reaching and grasping a small wooden disc and translating it to a series of specified targets. The tubes and rods task involved removing tubes of varying diameter from the dowels and translating and placing them onto the target dowels. The locking/unlocking task involved removing a key from a lock as well as inserting the key into the lock and turning the key to lock/unlock the deadbolt. The “operation” task involved removing a cylinder from within a hole and then placing it into a specified hole, both while attempting to avoid contact with the walls of the hole. Training sessions involved tasks which mimicked various kinematic aspects of the testing tasks with slight modifications. For example, the wooden disc translation task resembles the translation task, however the task objects (the discs themselves) were of a different material which weighed less than the discs in the testing task. This allowed us to examine generalizability of training as participants were not performing explicit repetition of the testing tasks.

Experimental design

The experimental paradigm is shown in Fig. 6. On day 1 (“Pre”), the participants used a prosthesis simulator (TrPS or PhPS) worn on their right limb to complete motor execution tasks (testing tasks), as described by Alterman et al.27, to assess the dexterity and precision of prosthesis use. The participants then wore the simulator on their left limb for the next three days to complete a separate group of training tasks. After three days of training (total training time: 96 min), the participants returned on day 5 (“Post”) for testing via a prosthesis simulator on their right limb, with the testing protocol identical to that used on day 1. This study design allows baseline measurements to be taken on day 1 from the right limb and post-training measurements to be taken on day 5 after three days of training.

For each testing task, after verbal instructions, participants were shown a demonstration video of an actor correctly performing the task using the device with slightly exaggerated motions. Tasks consisted of a prescribed number of paced movements (translation: 80 movements; rotation: 40 movements). A 5-minute rest followed each task. Participants were not given feedback regarding their performance, but were not prevented from making their own visual assessment of performance while completing each trial. Additionally, they were not told what outcome measures were being assessed.

For each training task, participants were given verbal instructions and a demonstration by the experimenter and were asked to complete as many movements as possible within an 8-minute period. There was no external pacing during the training sessions, and participants were informed that if they desired, they were allowed to pause to rest during the task. For each task, data were collected on the number of trials completed during each task, the duration of each trial, and the number of errors committed during each trial.

Electromagnetic motion capture (Ascension 3D Guidance TrakStar™ (MotionMonitor, Innovative Sport Training, Chicago, IL)) was used to collect 3-dimensional positional data at a sampling rate of 100 Hz on testing days. Sensors were taped to both sides of the end-effector of the prosthesis simulator as well as the right scapula.

Statistical analysis

Testing

Custom MATLAB and Arduino scripts allowed the addition of event markers to synchronize with The MotionMonitor to assess individual movement events. The outcome measures for the testing data include the reach peak velocity, movement duration, reach duration, and placement error. Movement duration was calculated as the time in milliseconds (ms) starting from the moment the participant released the start position button after receiving the “Go” signal for initiating the reach and ending when the participant returned and pressed the same button upon completing the movement. The reach velocity follows a regular bell-shaped velocity curve59. Thus, the peak velocity was found via the findpeaks function in MATLAB, which allowed for visual confirmation of the peak velocity. The reach duration was calculated as the time in milliseconds between the velocity minima before the peak velocity was reached and the velocity minima after the peak velocity was reached. The placement error was determined for the translation and rotation tasks by determining how close to the center of the target the object was placed. This was quantified by using a series of concentric rings that surround the target. The participants were not given verbal or any other form of feedback when an error was committed. The participants were not encouraged during task execution.

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were carried out via Microsoft Excel 2023 version 16.71 (Microsoft, 2023), GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA; www.graphpad.com), and RStudio 2009–2021 version 2021.09.1 + 372 (R Core Team, 2017). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and an epsilon squared value > 0.26 was considered a large effect size60.

One participant was removed from the kinematic evaluation because of a lack of data on day 5. These data were removed because of issues with data integrity. This left n = 9 for the PhPS group and n = 10 for the TrPS group analyses.

Linear mixed effects models were used to determine the contributions of fixed effects of group (2: TrPS, PhPS) and session (2: Pre, Post) for the translation and rotation tasks, respectively. The lme function was used to evaluate baseline differences (null model) in outcome measures due solely to random effects (Participant). Subsequent models incorporated fixed effects (group, session) to determine significant main and interaction effects. The aov function was used to calculate the likelihood ratio comparing the null model from those incorporating fixed effects. Post hoc comparisons were calculated using the emmeans package with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Cohen’s d was calculated using Microsoft Excel, and d ≥ 0.8 was considered a large effect size61.

Training

Data from all four training tasks were averaged together to examine the percent change across training from the first day (day 2) to the final day (day 4) of training. Two-tailed t-tests of unequal variance were performed in Excel to compare PhPS to TrPS for the dependent variables: number of trials completed, trial duration, and number of errors per trial.
