Visually Guided Saccades in Amblyopia and Strabismus: The Roles of Sensory Deficits and Nystagmus
Authors: Ibrahim Quagraine, Busra Cakir, Archana Mandava, Jordan Murray, Zaina Albirini, Aasef Shaikh, Fatema Ghasia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine how amblyopia subtype, visual acuity, stereoacuity, and nystagmus influence visually guided saccades across viewing conditions in subjects with amblyopia and strabismus.MethodsVisually guided saccades were recorded using video‑oculography in controls, anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, and strabismus without amblyopia subjects under binocular and monocular viewing. Saccadic latency, gain, disconjugacy, movement strategies, and main sequence relationships were quantified. Linear mixed effects models assessed group and viewing condition effects, and χ2 tests compared saccadic strategies and diagnostic distributions by nystagmus status.ResultsSaccadic latency varied with viewing condition and was most strongly associated with amblyopic eye visual acuity, with prolonged latencies during amblyopic eye viewing in anisometropic and strabismic amblyopia. Subjects with strabismic amblyopia also showed increased latencies during binocular viewing. Reduced saccadic gain and increased reliance on multiple‑step saccades were most pronounced in strabismic amblyopia and were strongly associated with nystagmus. Saccadic disconjugacy was greatest in subjects with reduced stereoacuity and was most evident during binocular viewing. Main sequence relationships were preserved across all cohorts, including those with nystagmus.ConclusionsVisually guided saccade abnormalities in amblyopia and strabismus reflect distinct contributions of sensory deficits and nystagmus. Although saccadic latency primarily indexes sensory access to the target, reduced gain and altered movement strategies are closely associated with nystagmus. Preservation of main sequence kinematics indicates intact saccadic motor execution. These findings identify nystagmus as an important modifier of saccadic accuracy and underscore the need to account for nystagmus when interpreting oculomotor behavior in amblyopia and strabismus, with relevance to visuomotor tasks such as reading and visual scanning.

Amblyopia arises from abnormal visual experience during critical developmental periods, most commonly due to anisometropia or strabismus.1 Traditionally defined by reduced high‑contrast visual acuity in one eye, amblyopia is now recognized as a disorder with broader sensory and motor consequences that extend beyond the amblyopic eye. Both amblyopic and fellow eyes can exhibit degraded visual function, with associated impairments in binocular vision.2–4 Strabismus may occur with or without amblyopia—it similarly disrupts binocular integration and stereopsis and introduces disconjugacy, or interocular differences in movement amplitude—thereby degrading binocular coordination.5–9 Together, these conditions are associated with long-term alterations in visual feedback, extending beyond cortical processing to subcortical structures implicated in eye movement regulation.10–20 Thus, oculomotor abnormalities are common across amblyopia and strabismus. Saccades—rapid, ballistic movements that redirect gaze—exhibit prolonged latencies and reduced precision, particularly under monocular amblyopic eye viewing, with deficits amplified by greater amblyopia severity and absence of stereopsis.21,22 Because saccades are essential for everyday visual behaviors such as fluent reading, accurate visual tracking, and spatial navigation, their disruption can have meaningful functional consequences.23–25

Fixation instability in amblyopia or strabismus arises from altered physiological fixation eye movements or nystagmus.8,26–31 We have previously shown that amblyopic individuals with nystagmus exhibit greater deficits in binocular contrast sensitivity and binocular visual acuity.32–34 Nystagmus introduces continuous retinal slip and spatial uncertainty, which can degrade visually guided oculomotor behavior by impairing target localization and gaze stabilization.35–37 Despite these effects, most prior studies of saccades in amblyopia and strabismus have either excluded individuals with nystagmus or failed to stratify analyses by nystagmus status, limiting insight into how oscillatory eye movements interact with saccadic control.8,21,38 This gap is critical given that the neural circuitry mediating nystagmus quick phases substantially overlaps with that underlying visually guided saccades.23,39,40 To date, no study has systematically examined how nystagmus modulates saccadic performance across amblyopia and strabismus subtypes.

In this study, we systematically quantify horizontal, vertical, and oblique visually guided saccades in individuals with (1) anisometropic amblyopia, (2) strabismic amblyopia, and (3) strabismus without amblyopia and evaluated the modulatory effects of nystagmus. We assessed the latency, gain, disconjugacy, and main sequence relationships of saccades quantified under binocular and monocular viewing conditions. We anticipate that saccadic abnormalities will be evident not only during amblyopic eye viewing, but also during fellow eye and binocular viewing. We hypothesize that control participants will demonstrate a binocular advantage, characterized by shorter latencies and higher gain, relative to the amblyopic and strabismic groups. We further expect individuals with anisometropic amblyopia to retain relative binocular facilitation like controls and to exhibit less disconjugacy than strabismic subtypes with or without amblyopia. We predict that nystagmus will reduce saccadic gain due to interference from spontaneous quick phases during movement execution. Finally, we expect greater deficits under amblyopic eye viewing—particularly in participants with poorer amblyopic eye visual acuity—reflecting the combined burden of sensory loss and oculomotor instability, along with increased disconjugacy in subjects with poorer stereopsis.

Methods

This study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their parent/legal guardian, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 70 subjects were recruited (21 controls and 49 amblyopia/strabismic subjects). All participants underwent comprehensive eye examinations, including cycloplegic refraction, ocular motility assessment, strabismus evaluation, and stereoacuity measurements using the Titmus stereoacuity test. Subjects with absent fly response were assigned log stereopsis value of 3.84.32,41 Amblyopia was defined as reduced corrected distance visual acuity without structural abnormalities of the optic nerve, retina, or visual pathways. Subjects in the amblyopic cohort demonstrated at least a two-line difference in visual acuity between the two eyes at the time of diagnosis. Amblyopia types were classified according to the Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group criteria.42 Anisometropic amblyopia was defined based on meeting at least one of the following criteria: (a) 0.50 diopters (D) or greater difference between both eyes in spherical equivalent or 1.50 D or greater difference between both eyes in astigmatism at any meridian (n = 15). Strabismic amblyopia based on meeting at least one of the following and criteria is not met for mixed amblyopia (described elsewhere in this article): (1) heterotropia at distance (with or without spectacles), (2) a history of strabismus surgery, or (3) a history of strabismus that has resolved with glasses and/or surgery (n = 3). Mixed amblyopia based on meeting both of the following criteria: (1) criteria for strabismus, and (2) a 1.00 D or greater difference between both eyes in spherical equivalent or a 1.50 D or greater difference between eyes in astigmatism in any meridian (n = 14). Given the smaller sample size, we combined the strabismic/mixed amblyopia subjects into the strabismic amblyopia group. We recruited 17 strabismic subjects who had neither current amblyopia nor any history of treated amblyopia. The exclusion criteria included any coexisting ocular or systemic disease, congenital infections or malformations, and developmental delay. We also excluded subjects with idiopathic infantile nystagmus, characterized by an increasing velocity slow phase, or alternatively, pendular nystagmus.43 For all measurements, participants wore optical corrections based on their cycloplegic refraction, with hyperopic adjustments made symmetrically for both eyes as clinically necessary to achieve the best-corrected visual acuity.

Eye Movement Measurements

Binocular horizontal and vertical eye positions were recorded using a high-resolution eye tracker (EyeLink 1000 Plus, SR Research, Ottawa, Ontario, Canada) with 500-Hz sampling frequency and a spatial resolution of 0.01°.32,44,45 A forehead sticker was used to track head movements. Calibration and validation were performed using a five-point horizontal and vertical target constellation. Calibration was performed before data acquisition and was followed by validation following the manufacturer-recommended procedure. Calibration quality was assessed using the EyeLink validation display and was accepted when validation error met standard research‑grade criteria, typically an average error of less than 0.5° to 1.0°. Eye tracking in the presence of nystagmus poses known challenges for absolute spatial calibration, because fixation instability during calibration and validation can introduce additional uncertainty.43 Accordingly, small differences in absolute calibration accuracy across participants—particularly between individuals with and without nystagmus—cannot be fully excluded. Subjects supported their head on a chinrest 55 cm away from the liquid crystal display screen. We used an infrared permissive filter to capture binocular eye positions in monocular viewing. The filter transmitted infrared light for video oculography while blocking visible light, thereby preventing vision in the covered eye. We recorded a sequence of visually guided saccades from the center position to 1 of 14 eccentric locations (5°,10°, and 15° to the right and left; 5° and 10° up and down, and in oblique directions [combinations of 10° to the right or left and up or down]). Each trial was performed twice, for a total of 28 visually guided saccades (12 horizontal, 8 vertical, and 8 oblique). Subjects were instructed to move their eyes as quickly as possible to the target and keep their eyes on the target until the end of a trial. The recordings were obtained under both eye viewing (BEV) and monocular viewing. For analysis purposes, for control subjects the left eye viewing (LEV) trials were pooled together with fellow eye viewing (FEV) for amblyopic and dominant eye viewing (DEV) for strabismic subjects without amblyopia, Similarly, the trials of right eye viewing (REV) in controls, amblyopic eye viewing (AEV) in amblyopic subjects, and nondominant eye viewing (NDEV) for strabismic subjects without amblyopia were pooled together. In addition, three trials, lasting 45 seconds each, were performed when the subjects were fixing on the 0.5° circular dot in primary position under BEV, FEV/LEV/DEV, and AEV/REV/NDEV conditions, performed in a randomized order.

Data Analysis

Binocular eye positions were analyzed. Eye velocity was computed by differentiating eye position signals, applying a Savitzky–Golay filter for smoothing and used for identifying and excluding blinks and partial blinks.46,47 Saccades, microsaccades, saccadic intrusions, and quick phases were detected using the Engbert and Kliegl velocity-based algorithm.48,49 For primary analyses, microsaccades, larger saccades, and saccadic intrusions were not analyzed separately but were grouped together as saccadic events, consistent with prior evidence that these fast eye movements share overlapping kinematic properties and common neural generators and form a physiological continuum.50–53 The saccades were further confirmed by visual inspection in the interactive data analysis program. Saccade onset and offset were defined using velocity‑based detection, with postsaccadic dynamic overshoot events identified by their short latency (<20 ms) and incorporated into the same saccadic event rather than classified as separate saccades, Thus, saccade amplitude was defined as the absolute difference between the eye positions at the start and the end of the saccade (which comprised the dynamic overshoot). We computed the latency and amplitude of the first saccade after stimulus onset. Saccadic gain was defined as the ratio of measured saccade amplitude (from eye position at saccade onset to the final postovershoot position) to target step amplitude. Saccadic disconjugacy was defined as the interocular difference in saccade amplitude for this initial saccade. Peak saccadic velocity was measured for each eye during the first saccadic event. We also quantified the number of saccades required for the eyes to reach the target.

Statistical Analysis

Group differences in age between control and amblyopic/strabismic participants were assessed using unpaired t-tests. We used χ2 tests to compare gender distributions between controls and amblyopic/strabismic participants and to assess differences in strabismus subtype between strabismic subjects with and without amblyopia. Statistical analyses were conducted to examine group- and condition-dependent differences in saccadic metrics and saccadic strategies. Continuous saccadic metrics, including gain, latency, and disconjugacy, were analyzed using linear mixed effects models (SPSS) to account for repeated measurements within subjects. Fixed effects included group (control, anisometropic amblyopia, strabismus without amblyopia, and strabismic amblyopia) and viewing condition (BEV, FEV/LEV/DEV, and AEV/REV/NDEV). Covariates included logMAR visual acuity (amblyopic eye, or nondominant or right eye) and log stereoacuity. Model assumptions were evaluated by inspection of residual distributions. Additional linear mixed effects models were used to examine the effect of nystagmus on saccadic metrics. Fixed effects included nystagmus status (control, no nystagmus, and nystagmus) and viewing condition (BEV, FEV/LEV/DEV, and AEV/REV/NDEV). Covariates included logMAR visual acuity (amblyopic eye, or nondominant or right eye) and log stereoacuity. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of less than 0.05 with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple pairwise comparisons. Saccadic strategies were classified as primary saccades only, primary saccades followed by a corrective saccade, or multiple-step saccades. Differences in strategy distributions across groups and viewing conditions were evaluated using χ2 tests of independence. Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding a corrected alpha of 0.012 for main analyses and 0.0028 for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Group differences based on nystagmus status were similarly assessed, with corrected alpha levels of 0.016 for main analyses and 0.0056 for post hoc comparisons.

Results

There were no differences in mean age between controls (22 ± 19.1 years) and amblyopic/strabismic subjects (17.5 ± 16.2 years) (P = 0.25) or gender distribution (controls: 42% males; amblyopic/strabismic subjects, 46% males; P = 0.75). Among the 17 participants with strabismic amblyopia, 8 (47.0%) had infantile strabismus, 7 (41.2%) had accommodative esotropia, and 2 (11.8%) had intermittent or constant exotropia. In contrast, among the 17 participants with strabismus without amblyopia, 5 (29.4%) had infantile strabismus, 4 (23.5%) had accommodative or acquired esotropia, and 8 (47.1%) had intermittent or constant exotropia. A χ2 analysis demonstrated a trend toward statistical significance in the distribution of strabismus subtypes between participants with amblyopia and those without amblyopia (P = 0.07).

Saccadic Latency

A linear mixed effects model showed significant effects of group, F(3,5130) = 13.2, P < 0.001, viewing condition, F(2,5130) = 27.5, P < 0.001, and amblyopic eye acuity, F(1,5130) = 11.2, P < 0.001, as well as a significant Group × Viewing interaction, F(6,5130) = 9.4, P < 0.001, on the latency of the first saccadic event after stimulus onset. The stereoacuity deficit was not associated with latency, F(1,5130) = 0.0, P = 0.992. There was a significant main effect of viewing condition, with latencies longest during AEV/REV, intermediate during FEV/LEV, and shortest during BEV. All pairwise comparisons were significant (AEV–BEV: Δ = 26.0 ms; AEV–FEV: Δ = 14.6 ms; FEV–BEV: Δ = 11.5 ms; all P ≤ 0.006). For groups, the anisometropic group demonstrated longer latencies than controls, the strabismic amblyopia group, and strabismus without amblyopia group (all Δ ≥18.9 ms; P < 0.001). The strabismic amblyopia group exhibited slightly longer latencies than strabismus without amblyopia group (Δ = 11.5 ms; P = 0.045). Consistent with the significant Group × Viewing interaction, the effect of viewing condition differed across groups. In the anisometropic amblyopia, latency was markedly prolonged during AEV compared with BEV and FEV (Δ = 72.9 and 31.2 ms, respectively; both P < 0.001), with FEV also slower than BEV (Δ = 41.7 ms; P < 0.001). In controls, latency was modestly longer during REV than BEV (Δ = 18.2 ms; P = 0.012). In strabismic amblyopia, latency was greater during AEV than FEV (Δ = 17.8 ms; P = 0.004), whereas comparisons involving BEV were not significant. In contrast, no effect of viewing condition was observed in strabismus without amblyopia. Between-group comparisons within each viewing condition further reflected this interaction. During AEV, anisometropic amblyopia exhibited longer latencies than all other groups (Δ = 45.7–62.7 ms; all P < 0.001), with the strabismic amblyopia having greater latencies than strabismus without amblyopia (Δ = 17.0 ms; P = 0.027). During BEV, group differences were attenuated, with only the strabismic amblyopia group showing a longer latency than controls (Δ = 22.6 ms; P = 0.008). During FEV, the anisometropic amblyopia remained slower than controls, strabismus with and without amblyopia groups (Δ = 31.6–33.9 ms; all P < 0.001).

Saccadic Gain

A linear mixed effects model showed significant effects of group, F(3,5130) = 9.62, P < 0.001, as well as a significant Group × Viewing condition interaction, F(6,5130) = 3.70, P = 0.001, on the gain of first saccadic event after stimuli onset. Gain was not significantly associated with amblyopic eye visual acuity deficit, F(1,5130) = 1.77, P = 0.183, but was significantly associated with stereoacuity deficit, F(1,5130) = 8.15, P = 0.004. Reduced gain was seen in the strabismic amblyopia group compared with the anisometropic amblyopia group (Δ = −0.041; P = 0.013), controls (Δ = −0.058, P < 0.001), and the strabismus without amblyopia group (Δ = −0.065, P < 0.001). No other pairwise group differences were significant. Consistent with the significant Group × Viewing interaction, the effect of viewing condition differed across groups. In the strabismic amblyopia group, gain was significantly lower during BEV than during AEV (Δ = 0.067; P = 0.004), with AEV–FEV (Δ = 0.036; P = 0.094) and BEV–FEV (Δ = −0.031; P = 0.428) not attaining significance. In the strabismus without amblyopia group, gain was highest during BEV, exceeding both NDEV (Δ = 0.072; P = 0.006) and DEV (Δ = 0.064; P = 0.020), whereas NDEV–DEV did not differ (Δ = −0.008; P = 1.000). In controls, there was no evidence of a viewing effect (all P ≥ 0.945). In the anisometropic group, the omnibus simple effect of viewing was significant, F(2,5130) = 3.17; P = 0.042, but none of the pairwise viewing comparisons reached significance (AEV–BEV: Δ = 0.040, P = 0.310; AEV–FEV: Δ = 0.044, P = 0.054; BEV–FEV: Δ = 0.004, P = 1.000). Between-group comparisons within each viewing condition further reflected this interaction. During BEV, group differences were pronounced, F(3,5130) = 10.62, P < 0.001, with the strabismic amblyopia group showing lower gain than controls (Δ = −0.095; P < 0.001) and strabismus without amblyopia (Δ = −0.143; P < 0.001). In contrast, during AEV/REV/NDEV there were no significant between-group differences, F(3,5130) = 1.74, P = 0.156, and during FEV/LEV/NDEV the between-group differences did not attain significance, F(3,5130) = 2.47, P = 0.061.

Saccadic Disconjugacy

A linear mixed effects model showed significant main effects of group, F(3,5130) = 4.56, P = 0.003, with no significance on viewing condition, F(2,5130) = 2.10, P = 0.122, with no significant Group × Viewing condition interaction, F(6,5130) = 1.23, P = 0.287, on saccadic disconjugacy. Saccadic disconjugacy was strongly associated with stereoacuity deficit, F(1,5130) = 46.98, P < 0.001, and was also significantly associated with amblyopic eye visual acuity deficit, F(1,5130) = 4.84, P = 0.028. The strabismus without amblyopia group showed the greatest saccadic disconjugacy, followed by the strabismus with amblyopia group, the anisometropic group, and controls. Pairwise comparisons indicated that controls had significantly lower saccadic disconjugacy than both the strabismic amblyopia group (Δ = −0.217°; P = 0.035) and the strabismus without amblyopia group (Δ = −0.253°; P = 0.003); no other between-group differences were significant. Consistent with the nonsignificant Group × Viewing interaction, the effect of viewing condition did not differ meaningfully across groups: within-group simple effects of viewing were not significant for anisometropic, F(2,5130) = 0.69, P = 0.502, strabismic group with amblyopia, F(2,5130) = 2.26, P = 0.104, or strabismic group without amblyopia, F(2,5130) = 0.90, P = 0.405. Although controls showed a significant omnibus viewing effect, F(2,5130) = 3.47, P = 0.031, no Bonferroni-adjusted within-control pairwise viewing contrasts attained significance. Between-group comparisons within each viewing condition similarly showed limited divergence: a significant group effect emerged during AEV/REV/NDEV, F(3,5130) = 2.75, P = 0.041, driven by lower saccadic disconjugacy in controls compared with the strabismic amblyopia group (P = 0.038); no group differences were significant within BEV, F(3,5130) = 2.05, P = 0.104, or FEV/LEV/NDEV, F(3,5130) = 1.85, P = 0.136.

Impact of Nystagmus on Saccade Dynamics

The eye position traces obtained during the three viewing conditions were evaluated to categorize the amblyopic/strabismic participants per their FEM waveforms; that is, subjects were classified based on the presence or absence of nystagmus. Figure 3 illustrates the FEM traces obtained under BEV, FEV/LEV/DEV, and AEV/REV/NDEV conditions for 5 seconds. Compared with the control subject (Fig. 3A), the subject who did not exhibit nystagmus demonstrated increased frequency of saccadic intrusions (Fig. 3B) across all three viewing conditions. Subjects with nystagmus were assessed for the presence of fusion maldevelopment nystagmus (FMN), which serves as an indicator of disruption of binocularity in early infancy. FMN is characterized by a nasally directed slow phase and temporally directed fast phase during monocular viewing. We have found that amblyopic subjects can have nystagmus that does not meet the criteria for FMN. These patients exhibited jerk nystagmus with dynamic quick phase overshoots predominantly observed during FEV. Additionally, there is no reversal direction of nystagmus between AEV and FEV. A notable difference that sets them apart from patients with infantile nystagmus syndrome is that the slow phase velocities are either decreasing or constant, in contrast with the increasing eye velocity commonly seen in infantile nystagmus.

Amblyopic participants and those with strabismus without amblyopia were classified according to their fixation eye movement characteristics as either without nystagmus (n = 27) or with nystagmus (n = 22). In the full cohort (n = 49), nystagmus was absent in 55.1% of subjects and present in 44.9%. The distribution of diagnostic categories differed significantly by nystagmus status, χ²(2) = 10.51, P = 0.005. Among subjects without nystagmus (n = 27), anisometropic amblyopia (n = 11,40.7%) and strabismus without amblyopia (n = 12,44.4%) accounted for similar proportions of cases, whereas strabismic amblyopia was less common (n = 4 [14.9%]). In contrast, among subjects with nystagmus (n = 22), strabismic amblyopia predominated (n = 13 [59.1%]), compared with anisometropic amblyopia (n = 4 [18.2%]) and strabismus without amblyopia (n = 5 [22.7%]). Given the smaller sample sizes within each diagnostic category, we were unable to analyze the effects of nystagmus separately within each amblyopia subtype and the strabismus without amblyopia group. However, we examined the impact of the presence versus absence of nystagmus on saccadic metrics across the cohort.

Saccadic Gain

A linear mixed effects model showed a significant effect of group, F(2,5130) = 14.06, P < 0.001, on primary saccadic gain, with no significant main effect of viewing condition, F(2,5130) = 2.77, P = 0.063, and no significant Group × Viewing condition interaction, F(4,5130) = 1.83, P = 0.120. A significant main effect of group was observed, driven by reduced gain in the strabismic/amblyopic with nystagmus group relative to both controls (Δ = 0.059, P < 0.001) and strabismic/amblyopic without nystagmus (Δ = 0.061, P < 0.001), whereas controls and strabismic/amblyopic without nystagmus did not differ (Δ = 0.003; P = 1.000). In contrast, strabismic/amblyopic subjects without nystagmus exhibited a small but significant viewing effect, F(2,5130) = 3.33, P = 0.036, with gain higher during BEV than FEV/DEV (Δ = 0.054; P = 0.039), whereas contrasts involving AEV/NDEV were not significant (Δ = −0.030; P = 0.48), with no differences between AEV/NDEV–FEV/DEV (Δ = 0.024; P = 0.35). Between-group comparisons within viewing conditions mirrored the overall group effect, with differences most evident when compared with the nystagmus group. During BEV, both controls and subjects without nystagmus exhibited higher gain than nystagmus subjects (Δ = 0.080 and 0.107, respectively; both P < 0.001). During FEV/DEV, the group without nystagmus again showed higher gain than the nystagmus group (Δ = 0.039; P = 0.05) and during AEV/NDEV, the group without nystagmus showed higher gain than the nystagmus group (Δ = 0.039; P = 0.040).

A linear mixed effects model demonstrated a main effect of group, F(2,5130) = 7.10, P < 0.001, a main effect of viewing, F(2,5130) = 4.72, P = 0.009, and no significant Group × Viewing interaction, F(4,5130) = 1.68, P = 0.152, on saccadic disconjugacy. Strategies of Visually Guided Saccades Across Viewing Conditions

Given that subjects with strabismus and amblyopia, as well as those with nystagmus, were more likely to exhibit reduced saccadic gain—particularly under binocular viewing—we next examined the strategies used to execute visually guided saccades. Three saccadic strategies were identified. The first was a primary saccade only, in which the initial saccade landed on or near the target without the need for further adjustment. The second strategy consisted of a primary saccade followed by a corrective saccade, defined as a single additional saccade occurring 80 to 750 ms after completion of the primary saccade.54–56 The third strategy comprised multiple-step saccades, characterized by more than two rapid eye movements.57 Multiple-step saccades included saccadic intrusions or quick phases preceding the primary saccade, as well as sequences of multiple saccades associated with pronounced saccadic hypometria or hypermetria, in which successive corrective components were required before the eyes reached or approached the target.

Discussion

The present study shows that visually guided saccade behavior varies systematically with amblyopia subtype, visual acuity, stereoacuity, and the presence of nystagmus. By jointly examining saccadic latency, gain, disconjugacy, movement strategy, and main sequence relationships across viewing conditions, we demonstrate that nystagmus is associated with distinct alterations in saccadic accuracy and efficiency that are not fully explained by visual acuity loss or ocular misalignment alone. These findings emphasize the importance of explicitly accounting for nystagmus when characterizing oculomotor behavior in amblyopia and strabismus.

Similar to fixation instability, visually guided saccades depend on both the quality and stability of sensory input, and saccadic metrics therefore provide a functional readout of visual access and target localization.45,58,59 In the present study, saccadic latency varied as a function of viewing condition and diagnostic group and was most strongly associated with amblyopic eye visual acuity. Prolonged latencies during amblyopic eye viewing were consistently observed in subjects with anisometropic and strabismic amblyopia, whereas subjects with strabismus without amblyopia showed minimal viewing‑dependent latency effects. These findings are in agreement with previous studies21,38,60,61 and indicate that reduced monocular sensory input delays saccade initiation, whereas ocular misalignment in the absence of amblyopia is insufficient to disrupt saccadic timing. Thus, latency appears to be particularly sensitive to sensory access and target acquisition efficiency, rather than to motor execution per se.

An additional latency effect emerged under binocular viewing in subjects with strabismic amblyopia, who exhibited longer latencies compared with controls. This effect was not observed in anisometropic amblyopia or strabismus without amblyopia. Reduced binocular summation in subjects with coexisting strabismus and amblyopia likely contribute to this pattern,21,34,61 indicating that binocular viewing does not uniformly confer a latency advantage in populations with abnormal binocular development.

Consistent with prior reports, nystagmus was more prevalent in subjects with strabismic amblyopia than in those with anisometropic amblyopia or strabismus without amblyopia. Previous work has shown that monocular viewing can exacerbate nystagmus, particularly in FMN, likely reflecting difficulty maintaining stable spatial reference frames when binocular cues are removed.43,57,62 In the present data, however, the relationship between nystagmus, viewing condition, and latency was not monotonic. Although binocular viewing provides richer spatial information, effective use of these signals requires intact binocular integration, which is often compromised in strabismic amblyopia with nystagmus.3,45,63 Thus, saccadic latency appears to reflect a balance between sensory signal quality and the demands of integrating that information, rather than being determined by nystagmus amplitude alone.

In contrast with latency, saccadic gain and movement strategy were most strongly associated with nystagmus. Reduced gain was most pronounced in subjects with strabismic amblyopia and persisted across all viewing conditions, including binocular viewing. Although previous studies have reported increased variability in saccadic accuracy in amblyopia and strabismus, few have explicitly examined the contribution of nystagmus. In the current cohort, a substantial proportion of strabismic amblyopia subjects exhibited nystagmus, and the persistence of reduced gain under binocular viewing suggests that ongoing retinal image motion may interfere with spatial calibration during saccade execution. Similar reductions in saccadic accuracy have been reported in congenital nystagmus.35,64 In addition, prior case-based work in FMN has described prolonged latencies and multiple-step saccadic trajectories, supporting the notion that nystagmus disrupts spatial calibration rather than the strength of motor output.57

An analysis of saccadic strategies further illustrated how nystagmus alters functional oculomotor behavior. Control subjects predominantly executed primary-only or primary-plus-corrective saccades, whereas subjects with anisometropic and strabismic amblyopia showed an increased reliance on multiple-step saccades. These differences were most evident during binocular and amblyopic eye viewing and were accentuated in subjects with nystagmus, with fewer differences observed during fellow eye viewing. Multiple-step saccades consist of closely spaced hypometric movements with short intersaccadic intervals and are commonly interpreted as reflecting ongoing internal error correction rather than delayed visual feedback.56,65 In the context of nystagmus, increased use of this strategy likely reflects repeated adjustments in the presence of spatial uncertainty rather than a primary motor deficit.

Despite abnormalities in latency, gain, and strategy, main sequence relationships between saccade amplitude and peak velocity were preserved across all cohorts, including subjects with nystagmus. Preservation of the main sequence indicates intact saccadic burst generation and brainstem motor execution,58,66 localizing the observed abnormalities to disrupted sensory integration, spatial calibration, and error correction processes rather than to primary deficits in the saccadic motor generator. This dissociation is consistent with forward model–based accounts of sensorimotor control.67

Saccadic disconjugacy showed a distinct pattern, being most strongly associated with stereoacuity deficits and most pronounced under binocular viewing. Minimal differences between fellow eye and amblyopic eye viewing suggest that disconjugacy reflects a relatively stable deficit in binocular motor coordination rather than a moment to moment sensory degradation. Interestingly, subjects with nystagmus showed less disconjugacy than those without nystagmus, possibly because nystagmus produces largely conjugate rhythmic eye movements. This finding highlights that different components of binocular coordination may be differentially affected by nystagmus and stereoacuity loss. Spatial calibration accuracy is particularly relevant for analyses of saccadic disconjugacy. Disconjugacy was derived from binocular, within‑subject comparisons of simultaneously recorded saccades rather than absolute gaze position relative to the display. These relative measures are less sensitive to global calibration offsets. Nevertheless, eye‑tracker calibration is inherently more challenging in the presence of nystagmus, because involuntary oscillations violate the assumption of stable fixation during calibration and validation. As a result, small differences in absolute calibration accuracy across individuals—especially between participants with and without nystagmus—could influence estimates of saccadic disconjugacy. However, not all forms of nystagmus are equivalent in this regard: in FMN syndrome, slow phase eye movements are typically linear or decelerating and binocularly conjugate, which may reduce differential calibration error between the two eyes compared with a more irregular, higher accelerating velocity infantile nystagmus waveforms.43 Nonetheless, absolute spatial calibration inaccuracies may still be present and represent a limitation of the study.

Besides this, another limitation is the relatively modest sample size, which limited detailed subgroup analyses within clinical types based on the presence or absence of nystagmus. Future studies with larger, subtype-balanced cohorts are required to disentangle the independent and interactive contributions of strabismus, amblyopia severity, and nystagmus characteristics. Nevertheless, the present findings highlight nystagmus as a central modifier of saccadic accuracy and efficiency, amplifying the impact of amblyopia and binocular dysfunction on visually guided saccades while leaving core motor execution intact. Failure to account for nystagmus risks obscuring distinct mechanisms underlying reduced saccadic accuracy and altered corrective strategies. More broadly, these oculomotor abnormalities have important implications for real-world visuomotor tasks such as reading and visual search, which depend on rapid, accurate saccades and stable fixation, particularly in individuals with strabismic amblyopia and nystagmus.
