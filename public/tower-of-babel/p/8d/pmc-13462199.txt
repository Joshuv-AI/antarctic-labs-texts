Current concepts in arthroscopic treatment for chronic lateral ankle instability: a clinical practice review
Authors: Shohei Sano, Yuichi Hoshino, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Noriyuki Kanzaki, Ryosuke Kuroda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) is a frequent sequela of acute lateral ligament injury, causing recurrent sprains, persistent pain, and functional limitations, particularly among athletes and active individuals. While most acute ankle sprains respond well to conservative treatment, approximately 20–30% of patients experience persistent mechanical or functional instability requiring surgical intervention. In recent years, arthroscopic techniques have grown in popularity due to their minimally invasive nature, ability to address concomitant intra-articular pathology, and favorable recovery profiles compared to open surgery. This clinical practice review integrates recent evidence from randomized controlled trials, comparative cohort studies, and technical reports with expert clinical experience to provide practical guidance on the arthroscopic management of CLAI. For patients with adequate ligament tissue quality, arthroscopic Broström repair remains the preferred first-line option, offering high return to sport (RTS) rates and low complication rates. For cases involving revision, poor-quality tissue, or generalized ligamentous laxity, arthroscopic ligament reconstruction using autografts or allografts is recommended. Concomitant procedures such as synovectomy, loose body removal, and cartilage debridement or microfracture can be performed during the same operation. A structured postoperative rehabilitation program is essential for achieving optimal outcomes. Early protected mobilization is followed by progressive range of motion (ROM) and strengthening phases, with sport-specific training typically initiated at about 3 months postoperatively. Current evidence suggests that arthroscopic management yields comparable clinical results to open procedures, while enabling the simultaneous treatment of associated joint pathologies and potentially facilitating faster recovery. Arthroscopic management of CLAI should be regarded not merely as a surgical alternative, but as a comprehensive clinical approach that integrates diagnosis, surgical decision-making, and rehabilitation for optimized patient outcomes. This review aims to provide an evidence-informed framework to guide orthopedic surgeons and sports medicine clinicians in the individualized, evidence-based management of CLAI.

Introduction

Background

Chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) commonly follows inadequately healed injuries of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) and manifests as recurrent sprains, pain, and functional limitations in athletes and active individuals (1,2). Although most acute sprains improve with nonoperative management, approximately 20–30% of patients develop persistent mechanical or functional instability that may ultimately require surgical treatment (3,4).

The traditional open Broström or Broström-Gould repair has long been considered the gold standard surgical approach. It reliably restores ankle stability and enables a return to sport (RTS). Long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated that both augmented direct anatomical repair and lateral ligament reconstruction provide durable restoration of ligament laxity and functional stability for up to 15 years after surgery (5). However, arthroscopic techniques provide a minimally invasive approach that allows for the management of concomitant intra-articular findings, including synovitis and periarticular soft-tissue adhesions (6), and achieve clinical outcomes comparable to those of open procedures (1,7-9). Recent comparative reviews and meta-analyses have reported similar stability and complication profiles between arthroscopic and open Broström-Gould repairs (1,7), with some data suggesting faster RTS after arthroscopy (3,8,10).

Additionally, advancements in arthroscopic instrumentation and suture anchor technology have made possible various modified techniques possible, such as all-inside repairs, double-row constructs, and anatomic reconstructions using autografts or allografts under arthroscopic guidance (11,12). These innovations have broadened the scope of arthroscopic procedures, allowing them to be tailored to each patient’s specific pathology and functional demands. Consequently, arthroscopy is now viewed not only as a minimally invasive alternative, but also as a versatile platform for repair and reconstruction strategies in CLAI.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Despite the expanding evidence base, important practice questions remain. Meta-analyses show broadly equivalent functional outcomes between arthroscopic and open repair (1,7), but considerable variation remains regarding surgical indications (13), rehabilitation protocols (14), and reporting of RTS timelines (3). Recent cohort studies suggest that arthroscopy may facilitate earlier RTS in selected patients (3), but consistent superiority over open repair remains inconclusive (1,7).

Furthermore, there are no clear recommendations for cases with poor remnant tissue, generalized ligamentous laxity, or revision surgery—settings in which anatomic reconstruction with an autograft or allograft may be preferable (12,15). In these contexts, the optimal surgical strategy is often left to the surgeon’s discretion, underscoring the importance of reaching a consensus and establishing standardized treatment frameworks. Similarly, although arthroscopy allows for the treatment of intra-articular lesions at the same time, the prognostic significance of these lesions and how they influence outcomes remains understudied (16,17).

Diagnostic pathways continue to evolve as well. History and physical examination remain central, but stress radiography, ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are increasingly employed as adjunctive modalities to refine patient selection and preoperative planning (18,19). However, there is no universally accepted diagnostic framework, and the lack of a clear distinction of mechanical versus functional instability complicates clinical practice and research comparisons.

Finally, postoperative rehabilitation remains an area of persistent variability, and further research is needed to improve postoperative outcomes. Although early mobilization, weight-bearing progression, and sport-specific retraining are recognized as key factors in functional recovery, there is limited high-level evidence defining optimal timelines (14,20,21). Therefore, clinicians would benefit from a pragmatic, stepwise framework that integrates diagnosis, patient selection, surgical technique, and rehabilitation milestones into a single cohesive continuum of care (2,19).

Objective

This clinical practice review summarizes the latest evidence and expert recommendations on the arthroscopic management of CLAI to inform daily decision-making. We aim to: (I) outline a practical diagnostic strategy and patient selection criteria; (II) summarize indications, key steps, and expected outcomes for arthroscopic Broström repair versus anatomic ligament reconstruction; and (III) present rehabilitation principles and RTS expectations that are most relevant to orthopedic surgeons and sports medicine clinicians.

Diagnosis and patient selection

An accurate diagnosis of CLAI requires a combination of medical history, physical examination, and imaging studies. Patients typically present with recurrent ankle sprains, lateral ankle pain, swelling, and a subjective feeling of instability (18,22). In addition to symptom assessment, functional performance tests have been proposed to objectively evaluate neuromuscular control and postural stability. Jump battery tests may help detect functional deficits not identifiable on imaging studies and assist in distinguishing patients with CLAI from healthy individuals (23). Anterior drawer and talar tilt tests confirm mechanical instability by demonstrating excessive translation or tilt compared to the contralateral side (24,25). Stress radiography typically demonstrates increased anterior talar translation and talar tilt compared with the contralateral side, providing objective quantification of mechanical instability (26,27). Pathological thresholds have been reported as ≥10 or ≥3 mm side-to-side difference for anterior talar translation and ≥10° or ≥6° side-to-side difference for talar tilt (18). Dynamic ultrasound enables real-time visualization of ATFL elongation, discontinuity, and increased anterior talar translation under stress, allowing functional assessment of ligament laxity (28,29). However, unlike stress radiography, no universally accepted cutoff values have been established for ultrasonographic diagnosis of CLAI. Although increased ATFL length under stress and higher ATFL stress-to-rest ratios have been reported in unstable ankles, consensus thresholds have not yet been clearly defined (18). MRI provides detailed assessment of the ATFL, CFL, and associated intra-articular pathology such as osteochondral lesions or synovitis (30-32). In patients with CLAI, MRI commonly demonstrates ligament attenuation and wavy or irregular morphology of the ATFL and CFL rather than complete discontinuity. Attenuated appearance is the most frequent finding. (32)

Surgical intervention is generally recommended when CLAI persists for 3 to 6 months despite structured rehabilitation programs focusing on proprioceptive training, strengthening, and bracing support (13,22,33). High-demand athletes and patients with recurrent injuries despite optimized nonoperative management are prime candidates for surgical treatment, as persistent instability can impair performance and predispose to progressive chondral and osteochondral damage (19,34). The preoperative evaluation should also consider factors specific to the patient that influence the choice of surgery. Generalized ligamentous laxity and comorbidities such as collagen disorders may compromise the outcomes of direct repair and should be considered during surgical planning (35-37). In selected patients, augmentation techniques such as suture tape augmentation (internal brace) may provide additional mechanical stability (38). In addition, patients with poor-quality ligament remnants after multiple sprains or revision cases may not be ideal candidates for arthroscopic Broström repair and may benefit from graft-based reconstruction (39,40). Therefore, careful intraoperative assessment of ligament tissue quality is essential for selecting the appropriate surgical strategy, as poor remnant quality may necessitate ligament reconstruction rather than direct repair.

In terms of procedure selection, isolated ATFL repair is generally considered in patients with predominant ATFL insufficiency and preserved CFL integrity (13,15). Combined ATFL and CFL repair is recommended when severe CLAI is accompanied by a CFL injury or when intraoperative findings suggest combined ligament insufficiency (41-43). Anatomic reconstruction using autografts or allografts may be preferred in patients with generalized ligamentous laxity, poor ligament tissue quality, or revision cases (12,40). In selected patients, augmentation techniques such as suture tape augmentation (internal brace) may be considered to provide additional mechanical stability and facilitate early rehabilitation (38).

Arthroscopic surgical techniques

Arthroscopic Broström repair (with knotted suture anchors)

The arthroscopic Broström repair using knotted suture anchors has become a well-established, minimally invasive alternative to the open Broström-Gould procedure. Standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals are created to allow direct visualization of the ATFL footprint and any concomitant intra-articular pathology. The lateral gutter is debrided to expose the bleeding bone at the origin of the fibula, which promotes ligament healing. One or two knotted suture anchors are placed at the origin of the ATFL on the fibula. Sutures are subsequently passed through the ligament remnant using a suture passer. Knots are tied securely to anatomically reattach and tension the ligament.

Clinical outcomes have shown that this technique provides reliable stability and favorable functional results. Corte-Real et al. (44) reported that arthroscopic repair of CLAI provided good clinical results and high patient satisfaction. Similarly, Kim et al. (45) demonstrated favorable clinical results using a suture anchor technique for arthroscopic ATFL repair in chronic ankle instability, supporting the reliability of this procedure.

Clinically, this technique offers a reproducible and anatomically precise method for restoring lateral ankle stability while minimizing surgical morbidity, making it an effective option for patients seeking a balance between minimal invasiveness and durable functional recovery.

Arthroscopic Broström repair (with knotless suture anchors for capsuloligamentous complex fixation)

Recent advances in implant design have led to the use of knotless suture anchors for the arthroscopic Broström procedure, aiming to simplify the repair and eliminate complications related to knot prominence, irritation, or impingement. After creating the standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals, the ATFL’s fibular footprint is prepared through debridement. A knotless anchor is then inserted at the fibular origin, and the suture is passed through the ATFL remnant and capsule, after which it is cinched within the anchor to complete fixation—without extracapsular knot tying. This approach minimizes soft tissue dissection and the amount of implanted material, which can reduce postoperative pain, swelling, and nerve irritation.

Kanzaki et al. (46) investigated the clinical outcomes of arthroscopic Broström repair using knotless suture anchors in patients with CLAI. They reported a significant improvement in Japanese Society for Surgery of the Foot (JSSF) ankle-hindfoot scores and a marked increase in all Self-Administered Foot Evaluation Questionnaire (SAFE-Q) subscales, with no recurrent instability observed. The authors emphasized that the use of knotless fixation reduced knot-related complications, enabled early rehabilitation, and allowed a safe RTS activities. Similarly, Shimabukuro et al. (47) reported favorable mid-term outcomes following arthroscopic lateral ligament repair using a knotless suture anchor, with significant improvements in JSSF and SAFE-Q scores and no major complications or recurrent instability. Building on this, Teramoto et al. (48) reported that an accelerated rehabilitation protocol following arthroscopic ankle lateral ligament repair using knotless anchors enable early weight-bearing and jogging, with 75% of patients returning to sports within 8 weeks maintaining improved mechanical stability.

Collectively, these findings establish the arthroscopic Broström repair with knotless suture anchors as a safe, efficient, and minimally invasive alternative to conventional knotted techniques, providing strong fixation, reduced soft-tissue irritation, and the potential for accelerated rehabilitation and early RTS.

Clinically, this technique offers a simplified surgical procedure that minimizes soft-tissue irritation and provides stable anatomic fixation, thereby promoting early mobilization, accelerating functional recovery, and enabling a safe, timely RTS for active patients.

All-inside arthroscopic repair

The all-inside arthroscopic Broström repair is a minimally invasive, fully intra-articular evolution of lateral ankle ligament repair that eliminates the need for extracapsular knot-tying because the entire procedure is performed from within the joint. Isolated all-inside arthroscopic ATFL repair is generally indicated in patients with predominant ATFL insufficiency and preserved CFL function. Zhou et al. (49) compared their all-inside arthroscopic modified Broström technique, in which both the ATFL and inferior extensor retinaculum are sutured under direct visualization, with the conventional open Broström-Gould procedure. At a minimum follow-up of 2 years, both techniques achieved comparable improvements in pain visual analog scale (VAS), American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS), and Karlsson scores, as well as similar anterior drawer test results. The arthroscopic procedure significantly reduced operative time without increasing complications, demonstrating that it is a safe, reproducible, and minimally invasive alternative to the open approach. Pereira et al. (50) described an all-soft, all-knotless arthroscopic Broström repair using suture-based anchors, which reduce hardware prominence and foreign-body reaction risk while providing reliable fixation and favorable functional recovery. Won et al. (51) reported that arthroscopic all-inside ATFL repair resulted in significant improvements in clinical outcomes at a minimum 24-month follow-up. Postoperative VAS, foot and ankle outcome score (FAOS), and Karlsson scores improved significantly, indicating favorable outcomes for patients with chronic ankle instability.

Combined ATFL and CFL repair

CFL repair should be considered in patients with severe CLAI accompanied by a CFL injury, particularly when intraoperative assessment—such as stress radiography after ATFL repair or hook palpation demonstrating loss of mechanical resistance of the ATFL—suggests combined ATFL and CFL insufficiency (52,53). Recent clinical studies have reported favorable outcomes after combined ATFL and CFL repair in patients with chronic ankle instability involving both ligaments. Yoshimoto et al. (42) demonstrated that combined ATFL and CFL repair significantly reduced the incidence of recurrent ankle instability compared with ATFL-only repair in patients with severe CLAI accompanied by a CFL injury. Similarly, Mao et al. (43) reported superior functional outcomes and improved ankle stability after combined ligament repair compared with isolated ATFL repair. In addition, Vega et al. (41) showed that arthroscopic all-inside repair of the ATFL and CFL provided excellent functional outcomes and reliable restoration of ankle stability with a low complication rate. Clinically, this technique offers a safe and effective method for restoring lateral ankle stability in patients with combined ligament insufficiency and may reduce the risk of recurrent instability.

Clinically, this technique offers a safe, fully intra-articular, and minimally invasive alternative to open repair, enabling precise anatomic restoration with reduced soft-tissue trauma, fewer complications, and faster functional recovery that facilitates an early RTS.

Arthroscopic ligament reconstruction

Arthroscopic ligament reconstruction has been developed as an alternative to Broström repair techniques for patients with generalized ligamentous laxity, poor ATFL tissue quality, or failure after primary repair. Reconstruction procedures typically utilize autografts or allografts to restore ankle stability, and recent advances in arthroscopic instrumentation have enabled minimally invasive graft passage and fixation.

Several techniques have been described in the literature. Alayane et al. (40) described arthroscopic anatomic lateral ankle reconstruction using an extensor hallucis longus (EHL) allograft, which enables precise graft tension control safe anatomic reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL. Similarly, Pettit et al. (54) reported that arthroscopic-assisted reconstruction using suture tape augmentation and knotless all-suture anchors may enhance fixation stability while reducing soft-tissue irritation. Feng et al. (55) reported that arthroscopic lateral ligament reconstruction using a semitendinosus autograft demonstrated favorable mid-term functional outcomes, including improvements in AOFAS and Karlsson scores, suggesting reliable mechanical stability.

Taken together, these studies suggest that arthroscopic ligament reconstruction is a safe and effective option in cases unsuitable for direct ligament repair, providing reliable stability and favorable clinical outcomes while minimizing soft-tissue dissection.

From a clinical perspective, this technique offers a valuable solution for complex or revision cases, enabling anatomic restoration of both the ATFL and CFL through a minimally invasive approach that preserves soft tissue, reduces donor-site morbidity, and promotes early functional recovery.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation is essential for achieving optimal outcomes after surgical treatment for CLAI. Most programs follow a structured, phased approach that progresses from management of swelling and pain to restoring function and performing sport-specific training. Although considerable heterogeneity exists among published protocols, many studies have described progression based on functional criteria rather than fixed timelines, allowing each phase to begin once specific recovery goals are achieved (14,56,57).

Deltoid ligament (DL) involvement in CLAI

In addition to lateral ligament insufficiency, concomitant injury to the DL has recently gained increasing attention in patients with CLAI (19,58). Recent imaging and arthroscopic studies suggest that DL abnormalities may be present in a substantial proportion of patients with CLAI (59), indicating that medial ligament pathology may contribute to persistent instability in some cases. However, the optimal management of these injuries remains controversial (58). While most surgical procedures focus primarily on lateral ligament repair, careful evaluation of the medial structures is recommended, and DL repair or reconstruction may be considered in selected patients with persistent medial instability (58).

Rationale and key considerations

The overarching goals are to (I) protect the surgical construct during the early healing phase, (II) restore range of motion (ROM), (III) regain peroneal and global ankle strength, (IV) enhance proprioception and neuromuscular control, and (V) facilitate a safe and efficient RTS. Early protected mobilization and progressive weightbearing have been shown to promote functional recovery, though excessive acceleration may increase the risk of laxity or complications (14,56,60).

Evidence summary and rationale

Immobilization and early protection

Most studies recommend cast, boot or splint immobilization for 10–14 days to promote wound healing and minimize edema (14,56). Pearce et al. (56) emphasized that a brief immobilization period followed by early mechanical stimulation supports tendon-to-bone healing without increasing complications. However, several studies have reported favorable outcomes with immediate or early full weight-bearing when protected with functional bracing after ligament repair (20). Therefore, postoperative weight-bearing protocols should be individualized depending on fixation strength, concomitant procedures, and patient-specific factors.

Controlled mobilization

Early mobilization beginning 2–3 weeks postoperatively has been associated with improved AOFAS and Karlsson scores compared with delayed protocols, though at the expense of slightly increased laxity (60). Therefore, gradual dorsiflexion–plantarflexion exercises under protective support are recommended beginning after the initial immobilization period, while inversion-eversion movements should be limited to a restricted ROM (approximately 10–15°) and avoided without protection until 6 weeks postoperatively (56).

Strengthening and neuromuscular recovery

From 6 weeks postoperatively, progressive closed-chain strengthening and proprioceptive training—including standing heel-rise and foot intrinsic (‘short-foot’) exercises—are recommended to enhance postural control and restore functional stability (56,57).

RTS

RTS represents the final phase of postoperative rehabilitation following surgical treatment for CLAI. The optimal timing and criteria for RTS remain subjects of debate; however, most evidence supports a criterion-based progression rather than a strictly time-based schedule. Meta-analyses and cohort studies have demonstrated that RTS is typically achieved between 12 and 16 weeks after anatomic repair, while reconstruction protocols extended up to 20 weeks (14,56,57). In contrast, several protocols incorporating suture-tape augmentation recommended RTS at 12–16 weeks postoperatively (14).

Current recommendations

Based on current evidence, anatomic direct repair (Broström or modified Broström-Gould procedure) remains the first-line surgical treatment for CLAI. Arthroscopic Broström-Gould repair provides clinical outcomes comparable to those of the open approach while offering the advantages of minimal invasiveness and the ability to simultaneously address concomitant intra-articular pathology. Therefore, it is widely supported as a preferred alternative in appropriately selected patients (1,15). Treatment strategies should be individualized according to ligament tissue quality, patient activity level, and associated intra-articular findings. Optimal outcomes depend on accurate diagnosis, meticulous surgical technique, and adherence to a structured postoperative rehabilitation protocol.

Future perspectives

Although arthroscopic management of CLAI has advanced considerably, several important areas warrant further investigation. First, high-quality multicenter randomized controlled trials directly comparing arthroscopic repair, anatomic reconstruction, and augmentation techniques are needed to clarify optimal surgical indications and long-term durability. Standardization of outcome reporting—particularly with regard to RTS criteria, objective functional performance testing, and patient-reported outcome measures—would enhance comparability across studies and strengthen the evidence base. Second, refinement of patient selection remains a priority. Future research should aim to integrate structural imaging findings with dynamic functional assessments, including neuromuscular control, postural stability, and biomechanical analysis. A clearer distinction between mechanical and functional instability would improve diagnostic consistency and treatment algorithms.

Third, emerging biologic and augmentation strategies—such as suture-tape reinforcement, biologic scaffolds, and combined intra-articular procedures—require long-term validation in larger prospective cohorts. While early outcomes appear promising, robust data are necessary to determine their true added value over conventional repair or reconstruction. Finally, rehabilitation protocols should move toward more individualized, criterion-based progression models supported by objective monitoring tools. Advances in wearable technology, motion analysis systems, and performance metrics may enable data-driven rehabilitation and more precise RTS decision-making.

Continued collaborative research efforts will be essential to establish standardized treatment frameworks and optimize patient-specific management strategies in arthroscopic CLAI surgery.

Conclusions

Arthroscopic treatment for CLAI has evolved into a reliable and versatile surgical approach, offering the advantages of minimally invasive access, the ability to address concomitant intra-articular pathology, and functional outcomes comparable to those achieved with traditional open techniques. For patients with good-quality ligament tissue, arthroscopic Broström repair is widely used as one of the standard treatment options, demonstrating high rates of RTS and low complication rates. For more complex cases, such as revision surgery, generalized ligamentous laxity, or poor-quality ligament remnants, arthroscopic anatomic reconstruction using autografts or allografts may represent an alternative that shows favorable mid-term results. Achieving optimal outcomes likely depend on careful patient selection, meticulous surgical technique, and adherence to evidence-based rehabilitation protocols. Future high-quality studies with long-term follow-up are necessary to evaluate emerging techniques, such as suture-tape augmentation, biologic scaffolds, and combined procedures, and to develop standardized rehabilitation guidelines.

Arthroscopic management of CLAI should be regarded not merely as a surgical option, but as a comprehensive clinical approach that integrates diagnostic assessment, procedure selection, and postoperative rehabilitation. This review provides a practical, evidence-based framework that connects diagnostic evaluation, surgical decision-making, and postoperative rehabilitation, aiming to support individualized management of CLAI.

Ultimately, individualized treatment strategies grounded in current evidence and tailored to each patient’s specific functional demands may help support and promote durable ankle stability.
