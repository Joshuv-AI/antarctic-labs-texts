Suppressing electron-phonon coupling in perovskite nanoplatelets for efficient pure-red light-emitting diodes with narrow spectral emission
Authors: Qingzhao Hua, Zirui Liu, Sheng Wang, Mengyang Xia, Chenglin He, Liang Liu, Zheyuan Xu, Zilan Tang, Wenlong Jiang, Ying Jiang, Fan Cao, Xiaoxia Wang, Xuyong Yang, Anlian Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerovskite light-emitting diodes are promising for next-generation ultrahigh-definition displays, yet efficient pure-red emission (620-650 nm) with high spectral purity remains challenging, with emission linewidths commonly exceeding 30 nm. CsPbI3 nanoplatelets feature the potential for narrow-linewidth emission owing to strong vertical quantum confinement and atomic-level flat surface. Nonetheless, the dynamic motion of native long-chain oleylamine exacerbates lattice vibrations in ultrathin soft lattices, inducing strong electron-phonon coupling and undermining optical advantages. Here, we introduce the rigid 4-(2-aminoethyl)benzenesulfonamide molecule, which partially replaces oleylamine and vertically anchors on the nanoplatelets, sterically restricting the dynamic motion of neighboring oleylamine, thereby mitigating electron-phonon coupling. The resulting light-emitting diodes achieve pure-red electroluminescence at 641 nm with a narrow linewidth of 24 nm, an average external quantum efficiency of 23.2 ± 2.4% (maximum 29.8%), and an operational lifetime of 128.2 h. This work underscores the critical role of electron-phonon coupling modulation in optimizing perovskite optoelectronic devices.Subject terms: Inorganic LEDs, Structural properties

Introduction

Perovskite materials possess tunable band gaps, high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs), and can be fabricated via low-cost solution processes, making perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) promising for advanced display technologies1–11. Although considerable progress has been made in improving external quantum efficiency (EQE)1,6,12, pure-red PeLEDs still face key challenges. These devices are typically based on Br/I mixed-halide perovskites5,13,14, tending to undergo ion migration and phase segregation under device operation15. This instability results in emission redshift and spectral broadening, which hinder the realization of narrow electroluminescence (EL) full width at half maximum (FWHM)16. As a result, their practical use in next-generation display technologies remains limited. Quantum-confined low-dimensional pure-iodide-based perovskites offer an effective solution to circumvent the above challenges. Among them, quasi-2D films and small-sized quantum dots are the most widely studied systems4,6,17. However, the rapid nucleation and growth kinetics process hinders size uniformity, causing inhomogeneous emission broadening (FWHM > 30 nm) due to multi-center recombination18,19. By contrast, the perovskite nanoplatelets (NPLs) exhibit distinct structural advantages. The thermodynamic preference against vertical growth in NPLs yields atomically flat basal planes, characterized by uniform thickness20. Given that their emission is primarily determined by strong vertical quantum confinement and is relatively insensitive to lateral size variations, NPLs intrinsically favor narrowband emission20–27.

Despite these merits, pure-red NPLs-based PeLEDs still suffer from broad EL linewidths and unsatisfactory device performances28,29. This limitation primarily stems from the ultrathin geometry of NPLs, which amplifies the inherent lattice softness of perovskites, increasing susceptibility to structural fluctuations20,30–35. Furthermore, the long-chain oleylamine (OLA) ligands, essential for stabilizing the layered structure, introduce significant dynamic disorder due to high conformational freedom, thereby exacerbating lattice vibrations20,36,37 (Fig. 1a). The resulting strong electron-phonon (EP) coupling intensifies non-radiative recombination, promotes carrier scattering, and broadens emission linewidths, ultimately constraining the potential of NPLs for high-performance LEDs30,35,36,38–42.

In this work, we design a rigid-ligand pinning strategy using 4-(2-aminoethyl)benzenesulfonamide (AEBS) (Fig. 1a). The rigid AEBS molecules vertically anchor on NPL surfaces and sterically restrict the dynamic motion of neighboring OLA ligands, thereby mitigating OLA-aggravated EP coupling. This enables high-performance PeLED devices with a maximum EQE and an operational lifetime of 29.8% and 128.2 h, respectively. Our work may inspire the design of NPLs for optoelectronic applications.

Results

Effect of rigid ligand pinning

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that AEBS adopts a nearly perpendicular configuration on the NPLs surface through strong coordination of sulfonyl ( − S( = O)2 − ) group with Pb2+ ions and −NH2 groups with I- (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1). The rigid and upright molecular geometry on the NPLs surface sterically restricts the dynamic motion of adjacent OLA ligands (Supplementary Fig. 2), effectively reducing ligand-induced lattice vibrations. Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrate the smaller temporal variations in the bond length of Pb−I in AEBS-treated NPLs (AEBS-NPLs, ~2.9-3.2 Å) compared to those without AEBS anchoring (Control-NPLs, ~2.9-3.6 Å) (Fig. 1b), indicating a more stable inorganic [PbI6]4- octahedral framework.

Due to the high polarity of AEBS, direct post-treatment leads to rapid ligand exchange and structural degradation of perovskites, inducing NPLs aggregation and severe photoluminescence (PL) quenching (Supplementary Fig. 3). Herein, we pre-load AEBS molecules into mesoporous silica (SBA‑15) as a slow‑release medium (Supplementary Figs. 4–7). This controlled delivery retained structural integrity and square platelet morphology of NPLs during post-treatment (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 8–13). As shown in Fig. 1d, AEBS-NPLs exhibit a sharp emission peak centered at 640 nm with a FWHM of ~24 nm, representing the narrowest PL linewidth reported for Pb-based pure-red perovskite to date1,4,6,9,43. The ligand pinning effect of AEBS is further supported by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The νas(CH2) and νs(CH2) vibrational bands are highly sensitive to the conformational order of hydrocarbon chains. Compared with Control-NPLs, AEBS-NPLs exhibit a marked decrease in the νas(CH2)/νs(CH2) intensity ratio and a pronounced shift of both bands to lower wavenumbers, accompanied by reductions in their fitted bandwidths, indicating enhanced alkyl-chain ordering and suppressed conformational dynamics of the OLA ligand44–46 (Fig. 1e). Oleylammonium iodide (OLAI) and 3-Amino-1-propanesulfonamide (APSA) are introduced to clarify the role of ligand structure. OLAI increases OLA surface coverage and passivates defects without introducing new ligand species, whereas APSA possesses the same head and tail groups as AEBS but lacks a rigid aromatic backbone (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). The more pronounced alkyl-chain disorder induced by higher OLAI content, and the limited conformational constraint of APSA, collectively underscore the pinning effect of rigid ligands (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Modulation of electron-phonon coupling by ligands

To further elucidate the effect of AEBS ligands on EP coupling in CsPbI3 NPLs, we conduct a comprehensive set of transient and steady-state spectroscopic measurements. Since strong EP coupling accelerates hot carrier cooling and increases energy loss, hot carrier relaxation dynamics serve as a sensitive probe to evaluate EP interactions47–50. We first examine the early-stage relaxation process via time-resolved PL (TRPL), where the initial rise in TRPL traces reflects the dephasing of initial polarization and the hot carrier relaxation51,52. As shown in Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. 17, the temperature-dependent rise rate (krise) of both NPLs follows a power-law dependence krise ∝ Tγ (γ > 0). A markedly lower exponent for AEBS-NPLs (γ = 0.89) compared to Control-NPLs (γ = 1.29) is to some extent associated with the weaker EP interaction in AEBS-NPLs41,51 (Fig. 2c). Consistent results are provided by the prolonged rise time of the ground-state bleach (GSB) in transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy, which increases from 0.73 ps in Control-NPLs to 1.43 ps in AEBS-NPLs (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Fig. 18). Further, the slower hot carrier temperature (Tc) dynamics decay and narrower Raman peaks provide strong evidence for weaker EP interaction in AEBS-NPLs47,48,53–57 (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Finally, temperature-dependent steady-state PL measurements provide a quantitative evaluation of EP coupling strength. As shown in Fig. 2g–i and Supplementary Fig. 21, the dominant PL peak in AEBS-NPLs exhibits a smaller linewidth variation with increasing temperature (from 80 K to 300 K) compared to that in Control-NPLs. Fitting the temperature-dependent linewidth variation reveals that AEBS-NPLs exhibit the lowest exciton-longitudinal optical (LO) phonon coupling strength (ΓLO) of 36.6 meV (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 22). The markedly lower ΓLO in AEBS-NPLs indicates that AEBS modification effectively suppresses EP coupling, accounting for the narrow emission linewidth of the AEBS-NPLs30,54,58 (Fig. 1d), in agreement with the reduced Huang-Rhys factor (S) (Supplementary Fig. 23).

Enhanced optoelectronic properties

EP coupling in CsPbI3 NPLs plays a critical role in governing their electrical and optical behavior. Space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements reveal that both electron and hole mobilities are markedly increased in AEBS-treated NPLs compared to Control-NPLs (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 24). Consistently, the electrical conductivity (σ) of AEBS-NPLs film reaches 9.42 × 10-6 S m-1, 2.2-fold higher than that of Control-NPLs (4.25 × 10-6 S m-1) (Fig. 3c). These improvements are primarily attributed to suppressed carrier scattering due to weakened EP coupling, further assisted by the structural advantages of the short-chain conjugated AEBS ligands and defect passivation30,35,38,59 (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, optical characterizations further underscore the benefits of EP coupling suppression. As shown in Figs. 2g, h and 3d, the weakened EP coupling in AEBS-NPLs suppresses exciton dissociation, thereby achieving a high exciton binding energy (Eb) and effectively preventing thermal degradation60,61. In addition, the combination of AEBS passivation and weakened EP coupling effectively reduces non-radiative recombination (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), leading to an overall improvement in PLQYs51,55,58,62 (Fig. 3f). These combined effects endow AEBS-NPLs with superior structural and photophysical robustness under diverse operational and environmental conditions (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Figs. 26–29).

Electroluminescence performance and operational stability

Encouraged by the enhanced optoelectronic properties, we further fabricate PeLEDs with a multi-layer structure of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/PTAA/NPLs/TPBi/LiF/Al (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). The AEBS-NPLs-based PeLEDs deliver voltage-invariant EL spectra centered at ~641 nm, featuring the narrowest EL linewidth of ~24 nm among all the reported Pb-based pure-red PeLEDs (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Table 3). The corresponding Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) chromaticity coordinates of (0.702, 0.296) satisfy the Rec.2020 standard, outperforming the control device (Supplementary Fig. 33). Angle-dependent EL intensity follows a Lambertian profile (Fig. 4c). The current density-voltage-luminance (J-V-L) and EQE-J characteristics of a representative AEBS-NPLs-based PeLED demonstrate a maximum EQEmax of 23.7% and a maximum luminance (Lmax) of 2060 cd m-2. In contrast, the control device achieves a significantly lower EQEmax of 9.5% and Lmax of 550 cd m-2 (Fig. 4d, e). AEBS-NPLs-based PeLEDs exhibit suppressed efficiency roll-off, arising from weakened EP coupling that mitigates exciton dissociation induced quenching and improved energy-level alignment that promotes balanced charge injection and suppresses Auger recombination7,60,61 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 30). Statistical analysis of 30 devices reveals a distribution of EQE ranging from 19.3% to 29.8% for AEBS-NPLs based PeLEDs, with an average of 23.2 ± 2.4% (Supplementary Figs. 34–36). In comparison, PeLEDs based on OLAI-NPLs and APSA-NPLs achieve limited EQE enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 37). A summary of reported EQEs and EL linewidths for pure-red PeLEDs is provided in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 3. The operational stability of the devices is further evaluated. Under continuous operation at an initial luminance of ~100 cd m-2, the AEBS-NPLs based device achieves a half-lifetime (T50) of ~128.2 h, ~15-fold longer than the control device (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 38). This represents one of the longest operational lifetimes reported for pure-red PeLEDs (Supplementary Table 3). We further investigate the charge dynamics and thermal behavior of the devices to understand the origin of the improved device performance. Transient EL (Tr-EL) reveals that AEBS-NPLs-based devices exhibit shorter EL rise times (tr) and fall times (tf) than the control counterparts (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Table 4), indicative of more efficient carrier injection and radiative recombination4,58. These findings are consistent with the higher current density observed at identical biases and the lower turn-on voltage in these devices11 (Fig. 4d). Moreover, thermal imaging reveals that the AEBS-NPLs based device undergoes significantly less Joule heating as voltage increases to 5 V, exhibiting a surface temperature rise of only 5.6 °C, in contrast to 13.9 °C observed in the control device (Supplementary Fig. 39). The reduction in Joule heating originates from mitigated EP coupling and improved energy-level alignment, promoting efficient charge injection and transport while reducing series resistance and nonradiative losses. Reduced Joule heating, the elevated ion-migration activation energy, and the stable lattice enabled by AEBS ligand pinning together lead to a marked improvement in the stability of AEBS-NPLs based devices (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 39 and 40).

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate a ligand-pinning strategy to achieve high-performance pure-red-emitting PeLEDs based on CsPbI3 NPLs with a narrow emission of 24 nm. Rigid AEBS ligands anchor vertically on the NPL surface, effectively restricting the dynamic motion of native OLA ligands. This suppresses lattice vibrations and mitigates EP coupling, simultaneously enabling enhanced radiative recombination and charge transport, as well as a reduced emission linewidth. As a result, the devices exhibit a peak EQE of 29.8% and an operational lifetime of 128.2 h. These findings represent a key step forward in advancing perovskite NPLs for high-efficiency light-emitting applications.

Methods

Materials

Cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3, 99.9%) and chlorobenzene (C6H5Cl, 99.9%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Lead(II) acetate trihydrate (Pb(OAc)2·3H2O, 99.99%), oleylamine (OLA, 80–90%), oleic acid (OA, 90%), ethyl acetate (99.8%), isopropyl alcohol (99.9%), 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%) and 4-(2-aminoethyl)benzenesulfonamide (AEBS) were purchased from Aladdin. Zinc iodide (ZnI2, 99.99%) and n-hexane (99%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. The mesoporous particles (SBA-15) were purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Co. Ltd. 1,3,5-Tris(1-phenyl-1H-benzimidazol-2-yl)benzene (TPBi) and lithium fluoride (LiF) were purchased from Luminescence Technology Corp. Poly(ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS; Heraeus-Clevios PVP AI4083) and poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA) were purchased from Xian Yuri Solar Co, Ltd. All chemicals were used directly without any further purification.

Preparation of precursor

Cs2CO3 (0.21 g), Pb(OAc)2·3H2O (0.475 g), OA (2.5 mL), and ODE (10 mL) were loaded into a 50 mL three-necked flask, and the mixture was degassed for 15 min at 100 °C under argon. Subsequently, the mixture was heated at 120 °C under argon atmosphere for 30 min until a transparent solution was obtained. The resulting precursor solution was heated at 100 °C under argon before injection.

Preparation of molecule-loaded mesoporous silica

To synthesize the nanocomposite, 50 mg of AEBS powder was dissolved in 10 mL of isopropanol, and the SBA-15 was impregnated with the solution, followed by ultrasonication to facilitate the infiltration of the solution into the mesopores. The solid product was then separated, washed three times with isopropanol, and vacuum-dried to obtain the final material.

Synthesis and purification of nanoplatelets

For the typical synthesis of CsPbI3 NPLs, 0.26 g ZnI2, 2.3 mL OA, 1.8 mL OLA, and 6 mL ODE were loaded into a 50 mL three-neck flask, which was degassed and dried under vacuum for 30 min at 120 °C. Then the temperature was reduced to 100 °C, and 1.5 mL precursor solution was quickly injected. After waiting for 1 min, the reaction mixture was immediately cooled down to room temperature by an ice-water bath.

The crude solution was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and 15 mL of ethyl acetate was added. The mixture was centrifuged at 9,800 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was dissolved in 1 mL of n-hexane, followed by the addition of 3 mL of ethyl acetate. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 9,800 × g for 5 min, and the precipitate was collected. The precipitate was dissolved in 1 mL of hexane and centrifuged at 2,460 × g for 3 min to retain the supernatant. Ethyl acetate (3 mL) was added to the supernatant, and the mixture was centrifuged at 9800 × g for 5min to isolate the precipitate. Finally, the precipitates were redispersed in n-hexane. AEBS-functionalized NPLs were prepared by ultrasonicating a dispersion of NPLs in n-hexane containing AEBS/SBA-15 ( ~ 5 mg). A sonication time of ~3 min was employed to achieve the desired surface AEBS-to-OLA ratio (Supplementary Fig. 7). The final product was collected from the supernatant after centrifugation at 9800 × g for 1 min. For OLAI- and APSA-modified NPLs, the ligand was added to the NPL dispersion in n-hexane, stirred for ~20 min, and centrifuged at 9800 × g for 1 min to collect the supernatant. All n-hexane solutions containing NPLs are filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE filters. The resulting NPLs solutions were stored for further characterization and LED fabrication.

Device fabrication

PeLEDs were fabricated on ITO-coated glass substrates with a sheet resistance of ~17 Ω sq-1. Before using, these coated substrates were cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min, respectively. Then these substrates were treated with oxygen plasma for 15 min. A PEDOT:PSS solution was spin-coated onto the ITO substrate at 4,000 rpm for 40 s and then baked at 150 °C for 20 min in ambient air. Subsequently, the ITO substrate was transferred into a nitrogen-filled glovebox. PTAA (8 mg mL-1 in chlorobenzene) was spin-coated onto PEDOT:PSS layer at 4000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 120 °C for 20 min. Perovskite was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 40 s. After that, TPBi (40–50 nm), LiF (1 nm) and an Al electrode (100 nm) were thermally evaporated with a vacuum pressure below 4 × 10-4 Pa.

Material characterizations

High-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) images and HAADF-STEM images were obtained by Themis Z microscope, and the accelerating voltage was 200 kV. AFM images of NPL films were measured by a Dimension Icon instrument. XRD patterns of perovskite films were obtained using a MiniFlex 600 X-ray diffractometer with a Cu-Kα source (λ = 1.54056 Å). The FTIR spectra of NPLs were measured by an IRTracer-100 Fourier infrared spectrometer. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) measurements were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400 spectrometer. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a NETZSCH STA 449 F3 instrument. The samples were heated from 30 °C to 800 °C at a rate of 20 °C·min-1 under a nitrogen flow. Grazing incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS) data were collected using an X-ray wavelength of 0.154 nm. XPS and UPS were measured by a spectrometer system (Nexsa G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A monochromatic Al Kα line (1,486.6 eV) was used for XPS, and He I radiation (21.2 eV) was used for UPS. The conductivity of a film was measured by a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley, 4200) with a probe station. A 100 nm-thick Au layer was deposited onto a glass/perovskite film by thermal evaporation. A field-effect transistor (FET) exhibits a channel length of 50 μm and a channel width of 1 cm. The thickness of the perovskite film is 30 nm.

Optical characterizations

UV-visible absorption spectra were acquired using a Lambda 1050+ spectrophotometer. The Raman spectra were obtained using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution confocal Raman microscope. A 785 nm laser was used as the excitation source. The PLQY values were measured using a system that included a xenon flash lamp, a QE Pro spectrometer, and a custom-built integrating sphere. Temperature-dependent steady-state PL spectra were recorded using a confocal microscope (Witec Alpha300R) equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled cryostat, with a 405 nm laser as the excitation source.

TRPL measurements were conducted with the confocal microscope coupled to a streak camera (C10910, Hamamatsu). A Ti: Sapphire pulsed laser operating at 400 nm (pulse width: 80 fs; repetition rate: 80 MHz) was employed as the excitation source.

Time-resolved TA spectra were acquired using femtosecond broadband pump-probe microscopy. The light source was a regenerative amplifier system (Spitfire-ace, Spectra-Physics) operating at 800 nm with a pulse width of 80 fs and a repetition rate of 250 Hz. The output from the amplifier was divided into two paths; one was frequency-doubled to produce a 400 nm pump beam. The other beam was focused into a sapphire plate (C-cut, 2 mm thick) to form the continuous white light with a range of 450–750 nm as the probe beam. Both beams were directed into a custom-built microscope and focused onto the samples. The sub-picosecond time delay between the pump and probe pulses was controlled using a mechanical delay line (Newport, M-ILS250CC, 250 mm). A spectrometer (Avantes, AvaSpec-2048L) positioned after the microscope was used to capture the transient signals. All measurements were performed under comparable experimental conditions.

Device characterizations

The device's active area was defined as 4 mm2. Electroluminescence characterization was performed at room temperature in a nitrogen-filled glovebox using a Keithley 2400 source meter coupled with an integrating sphere (50 mm diameter, Shenzhen Pynect) and an Ocean Optics QE Pro spectrometer. The angular dependence of the emission intensity was collected using a Konica Minolta CS-200 detector. The operational lifetimes of PeLEDs were evaluated via a commercial organic LED aging system (ZJZCL-1) in a nitrogen environment. Thermal images of the samples were acquired using a Fluke Ti401 PRO camera. The Tr-EL signal was collected by a high-speed photomultiplier tube (Hamamatsu, H10721-20) and transformed into a current signal. The current signal was sent to a transimpedance amplifier (Femto, DHPCA-100) and then collected by a digital oscilloscope (MXR108B).

The ion-migration activation energy was measured using a FET device architecture. A 10 V bias was applied to the electrodes for ~20 s with a source meter (Keithley 2400) to induce ion migration. Upon abrupt voltage removal, a negative current arising from ion-vacancy redistribution was recorded and decayed exponentially, reflecting the ionic transport dynamics. Measurements were conducted from 220 to 310 K using a liquid-nitrogen-cooled cryostat. The transient currents were fitted with a double-exponential function, and the slower decay constant (τ), corresponding to the ion redistribution recovery time, was used to extract the activation energy.

Optical simulation

Both random orientation (isotropic dipole distribution) and fully face-down orientation of emitters are assumed in this model. The following optical channels are considered in the simulation: nonradiative mode, surface plasmon polariton (SPP) mode, waveguide mode, absorption mode, substrate mode, and air mode. The air mode corresponds to the theoretically simulated EQE limit.

Density functional theory calculation

In this work, the calculations are conducted within the framework of DFT using Vienna Ab Simulation Package (VASP)63,64. The generalized gradient approximation exchange-correlation function is described by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE), and the projector augmented wave pseudopotential is introduced with a cutoff of 400 eV (Ref. 65). The models are relaxed until the force convergence criterion is less than 0.01 eV A-1 per atom and the energy convergence criterion is less than 10-5 eV. To avoid interactions between the two nearest-neighbor unit cells, a vacuum of 20 Å is set in the Z direction. A 4 × 4 × 1 r-centered k-point mesh is used for Brillouin-zone sampling.
