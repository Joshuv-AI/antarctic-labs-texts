Behavioral responses of healthcare providers to prospective payment systems: a systematic review using the WHO framework (2000–2024)
Authors: Anahita Behzadi, Zohreh Bagherinezhad, Maliheh Ghobadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProspective payment systems (PPS) have become increasingly prevalent in healthcare globally, yet their impact on provider behavior remains complex and context-dependent. This systematic review examines the behavioral responses of healthcare providers to PPS using the World Health Organization’s four-function framework.MethodsA comprehensive systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Electronic searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase for studies published between January 2000 and December 2024. Studies assessing the impact of PPS on healthcare provider behavior were included. Methodological quality was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklists. Data were synthesized through narrative synthesis and thematic analysis aligned with the WHO framework (governance, service delivery, financing, and resource generation).ResultsFrom 8,615 identified records, 168 studies met the inclusion criteria. Among these, Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG)-based systems (32.1%) and pay-for-performance models (26.2%) were the most frequently examined payment mechanisms. The included studies covered 25 countries, with the United States (33.9%), China (28.0%), and European countries (24.4%) accounting for the largest shares. Most studies (64.9%) were published between 2016 and 2024. Hospitals (51.8%) and physicians (28.0%) were the primary provider groups investigated. Analysis within the WHO framework revealed distinct behavioral patterns across all four system functions. In the governance domain, PPS was associated with improvements in quality indicators and cost efficiency, alongside concerns regarding risk selection and potential reductions in care quality. Service delivery responses included greater emphasis on preventive care and shifts from inpatient to outpatient services, but also evidence of induced demand and selective patient provision. Financing-related outcomes demonstrated cost containment effects, accompanied by cost-shifting behaviors. In the resource generation function, PPS encouraged clinical process optimization and more efficient resource utilization.ConclusionsPPS implementation generates complex and multidimensional behavioral responses among healthcare providers. Although these systems generally support cost containment, they may also prompt strategic behaviors that risk undermining care quality and equity. The overall effectiveness of PPS depends on payment design, regulatory context, and provider characteristics. Incorporating behavioral insights into future payment system design is essential to enhance intended effects and mitigate unintended consequences.

Introduction

Rising healthcare costs have emerged as a major global challenge. Factors such as rapid technological advancement, escalating pharmaceutical expenditures, increased utilization of healthcare services, population ageing, and the growing burden of chronic diseases have substantially contributed to this trend [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, global health expenditure has been growing at an annual rate of approximately 4%, reaching nearly 6% in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. Beyond these structural drivers, weaknesses in financial governance and inefficiencies in resource allocation, reflected in poor health system efficiency and low value for money, have been widely recognized as important contributors to pressures on health system spending, particularly in settings with limited oversight and accountability [4].

In response to these pressures, many health systems have introduced prospective payment systems (PPS) as a core financing reform. PPS aim to control costs and improve efficiency by determining reimbursement rates prior to service delivery. Empirical evidence suggests that PPS can improve hospital cost efficiency compared with retrospective or per diem payment methods [5, 6]. However, the impact of these systems on quality of care remains uncertain. While some studies report improvements in efficiency, others highlight risks such as adverse selection, patient cream-skimming, and strategic coding practices [7, 8]. The effectiveness of PPS appears to depend critically on the alignment between provider incentives, social welfare objectives, and underlying cost structures [9, 10].

In high-income countries, recent payment reforms have increasingly shifted away from purely volume-based reimbursement toward hybrid and value-oriented models [11]. Evidence indicates that such reforms may reduce healthcare costs, hospital length of stay, and readmission rates in certain contexts [12, 13]. At the same time, financial pressure under prospective payment arrangements may incentivize providers to engage in risk selection, reduce service intensity for high-cost patients, or compensate for revenue constraints through increased provision of low-value or unnecessary services [14, 15]. These dynamics may be amplified in health systems with weak regulatory capacity, fragmented governance structures, or limited purchaser–provider accountability [16].

Another important but less consistently examined dimension concerns the interaction between payment systems and financial protection. In settings with weak social security arrangements or incomplete insurance coverage, payment reforms may be associated with increased out-of-pocket expenditures, shifting financial risk to households rather than achieving genuine cost containment [17, 18]. Such effects raise concerns about equity and access, particularly in LMICs.

Despite a growing body of empirical research, significant gaps remain in understanding how healthcare providers behaviorally respond to prospective payment systems across different institutional and economic contexts. Existing evidence is largely fragmented, country-specific, and focused on isolated outcomes, offering limited insight into broader behavioral patterns or system-wide interactions [19]. To date, no study has comprehensively synthesized two decades of evidence on provider behavioral responses to PPS through an integrated health system framework. This study addresses this critical gap by conducting the first systematic review synthesizing evidence from 2000 to 2024 on healthcare provider behavioral responses to prospective payment systems, using the WHO health systems framework to capture governance, financing, service delivery, and resource generation dimensions. In addition, the study provides an evolutionary analysis of PPS reforms, mapping temporal trajectories and identifying dominant behavioral patterns across diverse payment models and system contexts.

Methods

Study design

A systematic review was conducted to analyze the effects of prospective payment systems on healthcare provider behavior by the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [20].The study protocol was developed and finalized by the research team before implementation.

Conceptual framework

For analyzing the effects of prospective payment systems on provider behavior, the World Health Organization’s four-function health system framework was utilized [21]. This framework was selected due to its comprehensiveness and widespread acceptance in health system studies. The framework encompasses financing (resource collection, resource pooling, and service purchasing), resource generation (human resources, equipment and technology, pharmaceuticals, and physical facilities), service delivery (access, coverage, quality, and safety of services), and stewardship (policy design, coordination, regulation, and accountability).

This framework enabled the classification of prospective payment system impacts across different levels of the health system and revealed the interconnections among provider responses at various levels. It also facilitated a comprehensive analysis of behavioral changes across different health system contexts.

Search strategy and information sources

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in collaboration with a specialist medical librarian, to identify relevant studies. The search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase databases on December 31, 2024. The time limit for the search was set from January 2000 to December 31, 2024.

Keyword identification followed a structured three-stage process: (i) use of controlled vocabularies (MeSH and Emtree), (ii) consultation with subject-matter experts, and (iii) review of related literature. Core concepts included prospective payment systems, healthcare providers, behavioural responses, and their synonyms.

To ensure an appropriate balance between sensitivity and precision, the search strategy was designed to prioritise conceptual relevance rather than maximal retrieval volume. Methodological evidence indicates that highly sensitive strategies may substantially increase non-relevant records without proportional gains in eligible studies, thereby reducing screening efficiency [22]. Accordingly, the final strategy was selected to maximize relevance while maintaining adequate coverage of the literature.

Search strings were adapted to the specific indexing and search functionalities of each database, and the full search strategy is provided in Appendix 1.

Reasons for article exclusion at this stage were systematically recorded. In case of disagreement, final decisions were made through discussion and consensus. Quality assessment of studies

For assessing the quality of included studies, standard checklists from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) appropriate to each study’s design were utilized [23].Each study was independently assessed by two researchers. Each checklist item was scored as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” In case of disagreement, the disputed item was first reviewed by a third assessor, and then the final decision was made through three-person consensus. For each study, the quality score percentage was calculated (the number of “yes” responses divided by the total number of applicable items multiplied by 100). Studies with scores below 50% were classified as low quality, between 50% and 70% as moderate quality, and above 70% as high quality.

The results of the study quality assessment were considered in the interpretation of findings and the strength of evidence. Still, by PRISMA recommendations for systematic reviews, no study was excluded from the review solely based on quality score.

Data synthesis and analysis

Given the heterogeneity of studies in terms of design, population, intervention, and outcomes examined, a narrative synthesis method was used to combine findings. This synthesis was conducted using a thematic analysis approach.

The analysis process included initial coding of extracted data by two researchers, grouping similar codes and extracting sub-themes, and final organization of themes within the framework of the four health system functions. To ensure analysis accuracy, the MaxQDA version 2020 software was utilized.

Assessment of publication bias

Given the narrative review nature of this study and the significant heterogeneity of studies in terms of design, study population, intervention type, and measured outcomes, quantitative assessment of publication bias was not feasible. Therefore, statistical methods such as funnel plot or Egger and Begg tests were not used. Thus, qualitative assessment of publication bias was conducted based on the following:

Findings

Study selection process

The systematic search in four central databases from the beginning of 2000 to the end of December 2024 resulted in the identification of a total of 8,615 articles, including: PubMed/MEDLINE (3,712 articles), Web of Science (2,653 articles), Scopus (1,323 articles), and Embase (927 articles). After removing 1,899 duplicates, 6,716articles remained for title and abstract screening.

In the initial screening stage, 6,520 articles were excluded due to non-compliance with inclusion criteria. The full text of 196 remaining articles was thoroughly reviewed, ultimately resulting in the selection of 168 studies for final analysis. The whole selection process as illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

Descriptive characteristics of included studies

The present systematic review analyzed 168 eligible studies published during the 2000–2024 period. These studies were conducted in 25 countries and cover a comprehensive range of prospective payment systems.

Temporal distribution of publications

The distribution of 168 final studies across three time periods was as follows: the first period (2000–2007) included 22 studies (13.1%), the second period (2008–2015) included 37 studies (22.0%) with 68% growth compared to the previous period, and the third period (2016–2024) included 109 studies (64.9%) with 195% growth compared to the previous period. More than two-thirds of all studies were published in the last 9 years (Fig. 2).

Hospitals were the most frequently studied provider type, examined in 87 studies (51.8%). Physicians were the focus of 47 studies (28.0%), primarily including primary care physicians and specialists. Quality assessment of studies

Quality assessment was conducted using the JBI critical appraisal checklists for all 168 included studies. For each study, a quality score percentage was calculated based on the proportion of checklist items rated as “yes” among all applicable items. Based on these percentages, 129 studies (76.8%) were classified as high quality, 22 studies (13.1%) as moderate quality, and 17 studies (10.1%) as low quality. Overall, 151 studies (89.9%) were of moderate to high methodological quality. Inter-rater agreement between reviewers was 91.2%.

Common methodological limitations included the absence of an appropriate control group (24% of studies), limited generalizability of findings (36%), and insufficient assessment of long-term effects (29%). In contrast, major strengths included the use of appropriate statistical analyses (94%), adequate sample sizes (88%), and clearly defined research objectives (97%). Methodological quality improved over time, with studies published between 2020 and 2024 demonstrating higher quality on average, while studies from 2000 to 2009 showed greater variability; during 2010–2019, moderate to high quality predominated. Complete details of the quality assessment for each study are presented in Appendix 2.

Dominant Provider Behavioral Responses to Prospective Payment Systems Across WHO Health System Functions

Governance function

Economic and quality outcomes

Providers demonstrated significant changes in quality and efficiency in response to prospective payment systems. Multiple studies documented improvements in quality indicators across various clinical domains following the implementation of DRG and P4P systems [24–30]. These systems were associated with significant improvements in chronic disease management [34, 35] reduced post-operative complication rates [34, 35], improvements in antibiotic prescribing and prevention of medical errors [34], reduced error rates in medical records [29], and improved quality of therapeutic services due to financial incentives [36, 37].

Cost-saving behaviors and better resource management were reported. Provider financial responses included behavioral adaptation to new economic incentives, with cost reduction reported alongside maintaining or improving service quality [72]. Additionally, increased compliance with complete recommended care bundles [28]and a shift in provider approach from service quantity to service quality were observed [73].

Changes in service delivery patterns were extensively documented, including increased provision of preventive services [52, 53], improved performance in adult and pediatric preventive care [35], improved performance in admissions sensitive to outpatient care [51], and a shift in service delivery model from inpatient to outpatient. These changes also included enhanced access to services and reduced referrals to hospitals [54].Positive changes were also reported in specialized domains, including increased adequacy rates in dialysis and reduced number of blood transfusions [74]. The combination of global budget with performance-based incentives also demonstrated the potential of hybrid systems in simultaneously improving efficiency and quality [27, 38].

In contrast, some studies raised concerns about the unintended consequences of prospective payment systems. Under-treatment for sicker patients [75], focus on diagnosis rather than patient need [31], unfairness of the system for vulnerable patients [76], lack of significant change in patient selection [72], and no change in emergency and inpatient service utilization [77]were reported.

Organizational and professional outcomes

Competitive behaviors were observed as an essential behavioral response to prospective payment systems. The impact of competition between providers under global budgeting on performance indicators [78] and different hospital responses to performance-based incentives based on organizational characteristics were documented [79].

Impacts on physician professional autonomy and changes in professional behaviors were reported. Performance-based payment systems were associated with reduced potential for innovation and minor modifications [33] and a lack of incentive for efficiency improvement in some cases [31].Response to financial incentives varied and was dependent on organizational and individual factors. Additionally, regulatory responses and changes in quality-oriented participation were observed.

Positive changes in team behaviors and collaboration were also documented, leading to improved care coordination and more effective organizational responses. Focus shifted from service quantity to service quality [73], maintaining care continuity that led to reduced risk of complications [80] and improved inter-team collaboration in care delivery were reported.

Service delivery function

Service quality-related outcomes

Approach and process changes in service delivery included changes in service delivery approach, changes in administrative and regulatory processes, and the development of new managerial and executive mechanisms. Redesign of clinical methods for optimal resource utilization and improvement of care processes in value-based payment programs was reported. Quality management mechanisms were affected by these systems [27, 81, 82]. Application of DRG systems in improving medical record management was documented [32, 83, 84].

Changes in service quality encompassed a wide range of outcomes. Positive changes in quality and efficiency were reported in some studies [27, 29, 32, 39, 47, 54, 65, 66, 82–87], while quality decline and threats to care comprehensiveness were documented in other studies [34, 88–90]. Neutral or unclear changes in quality were also reported in some contexts. Productivity changes and functional changes were identified as essential dimensions of service quality affected by payment systems. Strategies for service coding enhancement, information and documentation manipulation, and data manipulation and documentation strategies were also identified as quality-related behaviors [81, 91–93].

Service quantity-related outcomes

Changes related to financial incentives and motivational and behavioral changes led to diverse outcomes in service delivery quantity. Significant reduction in patient length of stay following DRG reforms and the impact of case-based pricing on reducing length of stay for standard surgical procedures were extensively documented [39, 41, 94]. Influential or non-influential behavioral changes on service delivery were observed [91, 95, 96].

Service management and clinical decision-making were significantly affected by payment systems, including discretionary treatment decisions and changes in diagnostic and therapeutic service delivery patterns. Selective patient selection was documented as one of the strategic behaviors of providers [40, 43, 44]. Inpatient and discharge management also underwent significant changes, including admission management, changes in admission patterns, changes in visit volume, patient management, and follow-up management [67, 97–100]. Changes with unclear impact and implementation challenges were also reported.

Induced demand

Induced demand was identified as one of the most critical behavioral outcomes of prospective payment systems. The significant impact of payment methods on clinical decision-making and how payment systems affect clinical uncertainty and conflict of interest in specialized decision-making were documented [44, 45, 101]. Discretionary treatment decisions by providers were reported, accompanied by changes in service demand patterns [31, 42, 46].

Financing function

Cost reduction

Cost management and optimization, as one of the main objectives of prospective payment systems, encompasses diverse strategies. Reduction in per capita cost per service and cost-saving strategies were extensively documented in various studies [64, 81, 99, 102, 103]. The impact of case-based pricing on reducing length of stay and costs [61, 63, 64], reduction in low-value services in surgical patients under DRG systems [35, 62], and significant reduction in various types of costs were reported.

Reduction in overall treatment costs at the system level [67, 78, 104, 105]and reduction in direct service costs were documented [48, 64, 94, 106, 107].Reduction in pharmaceutical costs and prescribing under global budgets [55–58, 60] and reduction in hospital inpatient costs in bundled payments were reported [47–50, 108].Additionally, reduction in medical and diagnostic expenses [25, 48, 94, 106, 107]and reduction in outpatient costs and visits were also documented [61, 63, 99, 105].Long-term evaluations also showed a significant decrease in total care costs in advanced models such as alternative quality contracts. Reduction in general expenses and reduction in non-medical service costs were also reported in some contexts.

Cost shifting

Cost-shifting behaviors were identified in some contexts, including behavioral outcomes of payment systems and the creation of cost compensation mechanisms. Cost-shifting phenomena affecting service intensity and complex cost-shifting patterns under payment system reforms were documented [31, 56, 59].Strategic adaptation behaviors of providers to maintain income were observed. Greater acceptance of uninsured patients [56], increased length of stay [59], conflict between individual and collective interests (48), and cost compensation through increased other services [109]were among the identified mechanisms for cost-shifting. Additionally, a reduction in the number of therapeutic procedures [44], reduced pressure for diagnosis recording and lower performance (72), and management of referrals on an outpatient basis [99] were observed. Changes in service delivery type and changes in prescribing patterns and treatment selection were also reported as cost-shifting tools [102, 110].

Financial and payment outcomes

Cost outcomes and financial and payment outcomes included a wide range of changes in the economic structure of the health system. Changes in treatment costs [55, 58, 60, 94] and changes in direct patient payments were reported [43, 102, 111]. Changes in revenues and expenses were also documented [54, 112, 113]. Revenue maximization behaviors were observed in studies, including upcoding practices, strategic discharge timing for reimbursement maximization, and other financial techniques for income optimization [91].

Resource generation function

Clinical process optimization

Optimization of clinical processes for optimal resource utilization was identified in the health system. Enhancement of clinical processes with a focus on optimal resource utilization included redesign of clinical methods for optimal resource utilization and improvement of care processes for high-need patients in value-based payment programs [66–68]. This optimization included various items such as increased referral to rehabilitation centers and long-term care hospitals [67], increased use of aspirin in appropriate treatments [66] and the impact of disease severity on length of stay increased [68]. Additionally, improved ambulance exit time [66], reduced oxygen index assessment in patients with chronic asthma [114] and reduced time spent in medical visits [115]were reported.

Human resource development and management

Human resource management strategies were observed in response to payment incentives. Increased physician training [65], increased number of working days [45] and increased development and modification of clinical protocols [65]were among the reported strategies for optimizing human resource utilization. Strategic allocation of physician time based on financial incentives and shifting primary care toward team-based care under high capitation payment levels were documented.

Physical and financial resource management

Optimization of physical resources was also observed. Resource optimization at various levels of the health system [69–71], more optimal use of medical materials and equipment [39], and optimization of hospital resource consumption [41] were reported. Reduced use of technology-based medical services under global budgets and improved resource allocation in hospitals were documented.

Cost reduction strategies were identified as an essential part of financial resource management. Changes in prescribing behavior toward alternative prescribing [116], increased referral to lower-cost service providers, and increased optimal use of complementary and synergistic treatments for cost reduction [117]were documented. Improved financial resource management as a result of introducing prospective payment systems was reported, leading to more efficient use of healthcare budgets. Financial-service strategies also included direct technology connection with financial rewards [51], increased services for elderly patients [118], and increased return rates [119].

Discussion

Qualitative assessment of publication bias in the 168 included studies revealed several patterns that are critical for interpreting the findings of this review. A pronounced geographical imbalance was observed, with North America (71 studies, 42.2%) and Europe (41 studies, 24.4%) accounting for two-thirds of the evidence base, while Africa and South America were minimally represented. This concentration in high-income settings likely reflects differences in research capacity, scientific infrastructure, and policy prioritization. From a behavioural ecology perspective—defined here as the interaction between provider incentives, institutional constraints, and adaptive decision-making within specific health system contexts—this imbalance indicates that the behavioural patterns identified in this review predominantly reflect provider responses shaped by specific institutional arrangements, governance structures, and financing environments characteristic of high-income health systems, rather than a universally generalisable ecology of prospective payment systems. Consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to health systems operating under different economic or institutional conditions [120].

The temporal distribution of publications showed a marked increase over time, rising from 37 studies (22.0%) published between 2008 and 2015 to 109 studies (64.9%) published between 2016 and 2024. While this upward trend reflects growing scientific and policy interest in prospective payment systems, it may also be influenced by greater visibility and accessibility of more recent studies in major bibliographic databases, contributing to temporal publication bias. An additional asymmetry was observed in reported outcomes, with approximately 70% of studies reporting at least some favorable effects of prospective payment systems and only 30% reporting predominantly negative or neutral findings. This pattern may reflect selective publication of positive results, while unfavorable or null findings receive less attention. Moreover, inclusion of only English-language publications may have resulted in omission of relevant evidence published in local languages, particularly from Asian and Latin American contexts where innovative payment reforms have been implemented [121, 122].

Taken together, these patterns suggest that the behavioural responses synthesized in this review may be systematically biased toward experiences from developed health systems and toward positive or mixed outcomes. Because behavioural ecology is inherently context-specific, provider responses to prospective payment systems are shaped not only by financial incentives but also by regulatory capacity, decision space, enforcement mechanisms, market structure, and baseline financing arrangements.

Evidence from low- and middle-income settings suggests that limitations in institutional capacity, centralised governance, and constrained provider autonomy can substantially alter how financial incentives translate into behavioural change [122], implying that the behavioural ecology described here may differ meaningfully in single-payer or resource-constrained systems [123].

Beyond issues of generalizability, the WHO four-function framework enabled a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which prospective payment systems influence provider behaviour. Within the governance function, institutional transformation—understood as shifts in power relations, organizational routines, and professional norms triggered by changes in payment architecture—emerged as a recurring pattern across contexts [36, 73, 82, 124]. Rather than responding solely to direct financial signals [86, 125, 126], providers revise their internal definitions of appropriate care and desirable performance, helping to explain why similar payment reforms yield divergent outcomes across settings [37, 73, 127].

Findings related to the service delivery function revealed a recurrent pattern of multidimensional quality diversion, defined as the simultaneous improvement of incentivized dimensions of care alongside deterioration or neglect of non-incentivised or less measurable aspects of quality. While numerous studies documented gains in targeted quality indicators such as chronic disease management, including diabetes-related process and outcome measures [27, 28, 32, 65, 78, 83–85, 87, 97, 101, 112, 128–131], others reported gaming behaviours, neglect of holistic care, or erosion of non-incentivized services [27, 33, 37, 88, 90, 97, 114, 127, 129, 132] This pattern indicates that providers strategically reallocate effort and resources in response to embedded priorities within payment structures, rather than uniformly improving overall quality, a finding consistent with theories of incentive-driven behavioural adaptation [133]. The evolutionary analysis further demonstrated that provider responses to prospective payment systems unfold over time through increasing levels of adaptive complexity. Initial responses tend to focus on technical efficiency and rule compliance, followed by quality-oriented adaptations as system expectations become clearer. In later stages, providers may engage in what can be described as creative adaptation, developing hybrid strategies that include both desirable innovations and unintended behaviours such as patient selection or upcoding [33, 41, 51, 65, 81, 91, 98, 99, 130, 134–138], This progression suggests that payment systems themselves function as learning environments in which both regulators and providers continuously adjust to one another [101, 139].

Within the financing function, while cost containment objectives were largely achieved across contexts [25, 31, 33, 36, 47, 50, 67, 71, 78, 79, 82, 86, 96, 103, 106, 110, 117, 125, 126, 128, 140–144] the mechanisms underlying these reductions were not always socially optimal. Observed cost savings, ranging from 5% to 25% [145]. were often accompanied by cost-shifting behaviours rather than absolute efficiency gains [31, 33, 44, 56, 59, 99, 109] underscoring the importance of adopting a systems-level perspective when evaluating effectiveness.

One important contribution of this review is the identification of adaptive induced demand, defined as provider-generated demand that emerges in response to payment constraints and may encompass both revenue-seeking behaviours and clinically appropriate expansion of services, such as preventive care or long-term management of chronic conditions [35, 52, 61].While such strategies may increase short-term expenditures, they may contribute to improved outcomes and lower long-term costs, highlighting the need to distinguish between appropriate and inappropriate forms of induced demand [146].

Finally, analysis of the resource generation function revealed a shift toward multidimensional productivity, in which performance is evaluated not solely through volume-based metrics but through integrated consideration of quality, patient experience, and long-term outcomes [147]. The effectiveness of such transformations appears strongly conditioned by institutional absorptive capacity, defined as the ability of health systems to recognize, integrate, and operationalise complex reforms [139, 148].

In high-capacity environments characterised by robust information systems and organisational learning cultures, prospective payment systems were more likely to generate sustained improvements [26, 95], whereas low-capacity settings experienced greater unintended consequences.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that prospective payment systems function not merely as economic instruments but as powerful drivers of behavioural and institutional change. The behavioural ecology emerging from this review underscores the limitations of static incentive models and highlights the need for adaptive, context-sensitive policy design, particularly as payment systems evolve toward more dynamic and self-regulating forms [139, 149, 150].

Policy recommendations

The findings of this review suggest that the effectiveness of prospective payment systems cannot be ensured through financial incentive design alone, but rather depends on how these incentives interact with broader institutional, behavioural, and organizational contexts. From a behavioural ecology perspective, provider responses emerge from a complex environment shaped by regulatory capacity, professional norms, information systems, and historical financing arrangements. Consequently, policy design should move away from one-size-fits-all payment models and instead adopt context-sensitive strategies that recognise these interacting forces.

A central implication of this review is the need for adaptive and phased implementation of prospective payment systems. Given the evolutionary nature of provider behaviour identified in this study, large-scale and immediate implementation increases the risk of unintended and difficult-to-reverse consequences. Pilot-based implementation, combined with continuous monitoring of behavioural responses and iterative adjustment of payment parameters, allows policymakers to learn from early signals and adapt system design accordingly. Such an approach also facilitates trust-building and reduces resistance among providers, which is critical for long-term system stability.

The review highlights that the success of payment reforms is strongly conditioned by institutional absorptive capacity. In settings with weak information infrastructure, limited managerial autonomy, or low organizational learning capacity, even well-designed payment systems may generate counterproductive outcomes. Therefore, strengthening health information systems, investing in workforce training, enhancing transparency and accountability mechanisms, and fostering participatory governance structures should be viewed not as complementary actions but as foundational prerequisites for payment reform.

The findings further demonstrate that narrowly targeted incentives may generate multidimensional quality diversion, whereby improvements in incentivised indicators are accompanied by neglect of non-measured or non-incentivised dimensions of care. To mitigate this risk, payment systems should evolve beyond narrowly defined pay-for-performance schemes toward bundled or integrated incentive structures that reward care continuity, coordination, and patient-reported outcomes. Such designs can better align provider behaviour with broader system goals and reduce strategic neglect of complex or less measurable aspects of care.

Another important implication concerns adaptive induced demand. This review shows that provider-induced demand is not inherently detrimental; under certain institutional conditions, it may be redirected toward socially desirable objectives such as preventive care or long-term management of chronic conditions. Policymakers should therefore distinguish between inappropriate and value-enhancing induced demand and design payment mechanisms that encourage the latter while constraining exploitative behaviours.

Finally, sustained stakeholder engagement emerges as a cross-cutting requirement for successful reform. Active involvement of providers in the design, implementation, and evaluation of payment systems enhances perceived legitimacy, reduces resistance, and improves system responsiveness. Given the potential of prospective payment systems to reshape professional norms and organizational cultures over time, policymakers should adopt a long-term strategic perspective that prioritizes sustainable behavioural change over short-term cost containment. The ultimate success of prospective payment systems lies in their ability to generate durable, system-wide transformations that align efficiency, quality, equity, and professional integrity.

Study limitations

This study, despite providing a comprehensive and integrative synthesis of provider behavioural responses to prospective payment systems, has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the review is based on secondary data, which limits the ability to control for heterogeneity in primary study design, data quality, and methodological rigor. Substantial variation in study designs, populations, outcome measures, and implementation contexts may have influenced the comparability of findings and constrained the generalisability of the results. In addition, differences in reporting quality and the level of methodological detail provided across studies limited the depth of analysis for some behavioural mechanisms.

A further limitation relates to the heterogeneity in how prospective payment systems were operationalised and defined across studies. Temporal, geographical, and institutional differences in payment design may have contributed to variation in observed behavioural responses, complicating cross-study comparisons. Moreover, most included studies focused on short-term effects, with relatively limited evidence on long-term behavioural adaptation and sustainability, restricting insights into the full lifecycle of provider responses.

Importantly, the evidence base was predominantly drawn from high-income settings—particularly the United States and China—which may have shaped the behavioural patterns identified in this review. Because provider behaviour is highly sensitive to institutional arrangements, regulatory capacity, and financing structures, the behavioural ecology described here may not fully reflect responses in single-payer systems or resource-constrained health systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to settings with different governance structures, market dynamics, and baseline resource availability.

Finally, the limited number of in-depth qualitative studies represents an additional constraint. While this review aimed to provide a mechanistic interpretation of behavioural responses, the predominance of quantitative evidence may not fully capture contextual nuances, decision-making processes, and informal adaptation strategies employed by providers under prospective payment systems.

Future research directions

Based on the findings of this study and identified limitations, several priority areas for future research can be identified. The priority is conducting long-term longitudinal studies that can track the complete cycle of behavioral evolution of providers in response to prospective payment systems. These studies should cover at least 5–10 years to evaluate the final impacts and the sustainability of changes and identify long-term adaptation patterns.

Development and validation of standard measurement tools for assessing the “behavioral ecology” of payment systems is another urgent need that can provide the possibility of systematic comparison between studies and different contexts. These tools should be able to simultaneously measure various dimensions of impact, including efficiency, quality, equity, and unwanted behaviors, while having sufficient sensitivity to identify subtle behavioral changes.

Cross-cultural and cross-institutional comparative research is another necessity that can help better understand the role of contextual factors in determining the success or failure of these systems. These studies should specifically focus on identifying institutional, cultural, and structural characteristics that cause differences in outcomes and provide the possibility of developing context-appropriate design guidelines.

Conducting in-depth qualitative studies using methods such as case study, semi-structured interviews, and organizational ethnographic methods is essential for discovering precise mechanisms of provider decision-making and processes of adaptive behavior formation. These studies can shed more light on hidden and non-measurable quantitative aspects of behavioral impacts.

Research in the field of “intelligent payment systems” that can learn from behavioral responses and automatically adjust parameters is an emerging area that has revolutionary potential for the future of these systems. The development of machine learning algorithms that can recognize complex behavioral patterns and provide adaptive suggestions based on them can be a solution to many current problems.

Additionally, research on “generational impacts” of these systems and how new professional norms and values are formed in future generations of providers is an important area that has received less attention. Understanding these processes can help design systems that ensure not only short-term effectiveness but also long-term sustainability and desirability.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive evolutionary–behavioral analysis of the impacts of prospective payment systems on healthcare providers over the 2000–2024 period. It opens new horizons for understanding the complexity of these systems. The findings demonstrate that prospective payment systems have evolved beyond simple cost-containment instruments into complex, multi-objective mechanisms capable of producing fundamental transformations across multiple dimensions of the health system.

The identification of “behavioural ecology” and the delineation of a three-stage evolutionary pattern of provider responses represents key theoretical contributions of this research. These concepts deepen understanding of the causal mechanisms through which payment systems influence provider behaviour and offer a novel conceptual lens for the design and evaluation of future payment reforms. Analysis grounded in the four WHO health system functions confirms that the impacts of prospective payment systems on governance, service delivery, financing, and resource generation are simultaneous, interactive, and mutually reinforcing, and therefore cannot be meaningfully interpreted in isolation.

The findings underscore the critical role of contextual factors and institutional absorptive capacity in shaping the success or failure of prospective payment systems. This evidence highlights that direct transplantation of payment models across settings is unlikely to be effective and may generate unintended consequences. Instead, an adaptive, context-sensitive approach that priorities institutional strengthening and alignment with local regulatory, organizational, and financing environments emerges as essential for sustainable reform.

The identification of multidimensional quality diversion and adaptive induced demand illustrates the sophisticated strategic adaptation capabilities of providers. These behavioural responses can generate both opportunities for performance improvement and significant challenges for system oversight. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of balanced incentive structures and the development of advanced, multidimensional monitoring systems capable of detecting and managing both desirable and undesirable behavioural adaptations.

The evolutionary perspective adopted in this study further suggests that prospective payment systems are progressing toward forms of adaptive intelligence, in which learning from behavioural feedback and dynamic adjustment of system parameters become feasible. While this trajectory offers new opportunities for enhancing system effectiveness, it also introduces additional layers of complexity that warrant further empirical investigation and institutional preparedness.

The policy implications derived from this analysis emphasize the necessity of a systematic, gradual, and participatory approach to payment reform. Success depends not only on technical incentive design but also on institutional capacity-building, continuous monitoring, and sustained stakeholder engagement. Such an approach increases the likelihood of achieving intended objectives while mitigating the risk of unintended consequences.

Finally, this study demonstrates that prospective payment systems function not merely as economic instruments but as powerful agents of social and organizational transformation, capable of reshaping professional norms, values, and care practices over time. This insight highlights the importance of adopting a long-term and generational perspective in payment system reform. Ultimately, the success of prospective payment systems lies in their ability to foster sustainable transformations that jointly advance efficiency, quality, equity, and stakeholder satisfaction, contributing to more responsive and human-centred health systems.
