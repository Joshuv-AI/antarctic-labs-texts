Effect of dihydroquercetin on the quality and microbial diversity of duck meat during storage
Authors: Haibin Zhong, Wenwen Dong, Guiming Li, Xiaoyuan Yuan, Kai Meng, Jina Han, Zhengrong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effectiveness of dihydroquercetin (DHQ) in preserving duck meat stored at 4 °C. The impact of different DHQ concentrations on meat color, pH, thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), sensory attributes, flavor compounds, and microbial diversity was assessed. Results showed that DHQ maintained a* values, delayed pH decline, reduced TBARS formation, and improved sensory acceptability. TVB-N analysis showed that DHQ at 625 μg/mL extended the shelf life from 6 (control) to 8 days, while DHQ at 1250 μg/mL extended it to 10 days. GC–MS analysis further revealed that DHQ slowed the degradation of umami-related compounds and reduced the accumulation of spoilage-related metabolites. 16S rRNA sequencing identified Pseudomonas as the predominant spoilage bacterium, with DHQ application reducing its abundance. These findings suggest DHQ's moderate potential as a natural antibacterial agent for maintaining duck meat quality during storage.

Introduction

Duck meat has seen a steady increase in global consumption and has become an important meat source (Xu et al., 2023). It is rich in nutrients, including unsaturated fatty acids, amino acids, and vitamins, all of which are beneficial for human health (Huo et al., 2021). However, high levels of water activity and nutrients in duck meat create an ideal environment for spoilage microorganisms and foodborne pathogens (Zhou et al., 2010). The perishable nature of poultry necessitates effective preservation methods to ensure quality and safety during storage (Hashim et al., 2025). Most duck meat is frozen to prevent waste and economic loss (Biswas et al., 2019). Therefore, extending the shelf life of fresh duck meat while preserving its sensory qualities remains a significant challenge.

Food preservatives that inhibit microbial growth and delay lipid oxidation are commonly incorporated. However, consumer concerns about the potential side effects of synthetic chemical preservatives have led to reluctance in their use (Yu et al., 2021). As a result, researchers and meat producers are exploring natural preservatives as alternatives (Yu et al., 2021). Natural plant extracts, for instance, can serve as antimicrobial agents, effectively inhibiting microbial growth while meeting the growing consumer demand for environmentally friendly and health-oriented products (Wu et al., 2024). Quercetin is known for its antibacterial properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Tavassoli et al., 2021). Dihydroquercetin (DHQ), a related compound, contains two extra hydrogen atoms, making it more saturated and stable while maintaining similar functional characteristics.

DHQ is an effective flavonoid belonging to the flavonol subgroup (Weidmann, 2012). In 1951, Kurth and Chan identified DHQ as an effective antioxidant found in lard, cottonseed oil, and butter (Kurth & Chan, 1951). DHQ has been applied to extend the shelf-life of various types of meat and meat products. After 0.02% DHQ solution was used to treat veal stored in modified atmosphere packaging at 0 °C, its quality and shelf life could be maintained and extended to 15 d. (Dragoev et al., 2014). It has been reported that DHQ could inhibit foodborne bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Unver, 2024). In 2021, the National Health Commission of China approved DHQ as a novel food ingredient and additive. The European Union and Russia have also authorized its use as a food additive, further substantiating its safety profile. However, the potential of DHQ in duck meat preservation remains underexplored, particularly with regard to systematic evaluation.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of DHQ treatment on the quality, sensory attributes, and bacterial community structure of duck meat during refrigerated storage. The findings are intended to provide more effective preservation methods for meat processing industry, improve the shelf life of duck meat, product quality and consumer satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

A total of 300 duck breast meat samples in two batches were obtained from a duck slaughterhouse in Shandong, China. Samples were transported on ice to the laboratory within one hour. Among them, 288 duck breasts were randomly divided into four treatment groups (72 ducks per group): (A) uncoated (control, without DHQ), (B) sprayed with DHQ solution at 312.5 μg/mL, (C) sprayed with DHQ solution at 625 μg/mL, and (D) sprayed with DHQ solution at 1250 μg/mL for physicochemical, sensory, and microbial analysis on days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 of storage. The remaining 12 breast meat without any DHQ treatment were used as the control group for analyses on day 0. The day of processing and treatment allocation was denoted as day 0. Samples were treated by uniformly spraying their surfaces with the respective DHQ solutions, air-drying for 10 min, and immediately placing in polypropylene trays (Sealed Air Corp., Danbury, USA). On each sampling day, one tray was chosen at random from each treatment group to undergo analyses of microbes, total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), and lipid oxidation. Another tray was used for other indicators. All the treatment groups were stored at 4 °C and each consisted of six biological replicates.

Color measurement

Color was measured using a modified method adapted from Yang et al. (2022). The L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values of duck meat samples stored for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 days were assessed using a precision colorimeter (NR20XE). The instrument was calibrated using standard white and black reference tiles before measurements were taken perpendicularly to the meat surface. Three measurements were taken at different locations on each sample, and the average of the results was obtained.

pH measurement

pH was measured using a portable pH meter (Han et al., 2021). The meter was calibrated using standard buffer solutions at pH values of 7.00 and 4.00 before use. A small incision was made on the meat surface, and a pH probe was inserted to record the reading. Three repeated measurements were conducted at different positions on each sample, and the mean value was calculated.

Thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS)

TBARS content was determined using a modified version of the spectrophotometric method described in GB 5009.181–2016. Briefly, 5.00 g of minced duck meat was homogenized with 45 mL of 7.5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution containing 0.05 g disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate at 10,000 r/min for 1 min. The homogenate was filtered twice through double-layered filter paper, discarding the initial filtrate and retaining the subsequent filtrate for analysis. A 5 mL aliquot of the filtrate was transferred into a 25 mL stoppered test tube. Then, 5 mL of 0.02 mol/L thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution was added and mixed. The mixture was heated in a 90 °C water bath for 30 min, cooled, and its absorbance measured at 532 nm. A blank consisting of 5 mL of TCA solution and 5 mL TBA solution was prepared concurrently. A standard curve was created with 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane (y = 0.6337× + 0.00006; R2 = 0.9991). TBARS values were reported as milligrams of malondialdehyde (MDA) per kilogram of meat (mg/kg).

Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N)

TVB-N was determined using the microdiffusion method described in the Chinese National Food Safety Standard Method GB 5009.228–2016. Duck meat was finely minced, and 20.00 g portions were transferred to a conical flask, combined with 100.0 mL of distilled water, and macerated at 25 °C for 30 min to achieve homogeneous dispersion. The mixture was then filtered. A water-soluble grease was applied to the rim of a diffusion dish. The central inner chamber received 1 mL of 20 g/L boric acid solution and one drop of methyl red-bromocresol green ethanol mixed indicator. The outer compartment contained 1 mL of the prepared filtrate and 1 mL of saturated potassium carbonate solution. The dish was covered with its glass lid, gently rotated to ensure complete mixing, and incubated in a temperature-controlled chamber at 37 °C ± 1 °C for 2 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the boric acid solution in the central chamber was titrated to its endpoint using a standardized hydrochloric acid solution (concentration = c mol/L). The volume of HCl consumed for the sample was recorded as V₁, and for the blank as V₂. TVB-N content in duck meat was calculated using Eq. 1:(1)TVB−Nmg/100g=V1−V2×c×14m×V/V0×100where m is the sample weight (g), V is the accurately pipetted filtrate volume (mL), and V₀ is the total filtrate volume (mL). Fourteen is the molar mass of nitrogen.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was conducted following the method described by Cheng et al. (2024). A group composed of 10 members trained in sensory evaluation technology was formed voluntarily. The panel assessed the sensory attributes of the duck meat throughout the storage period, focusing on color, odor, texture, and overall acceptability. A standardized nine-point scale was used, where 9 represented the highest quality (most desirable) and 1 represented the lowest (least desirable). All evaluations were conducted independently, and panelists were prohibited from communicating during assessments. The final daily sensory score for each storage interval was the average of the scores from all panel members. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

The flavor quality of chilled duck meat was analyzed on a GC–MS apparatus (Agilent 7890B-5977 A, USA). Samples were derivatized with BSTFA. The chromatographic conditions are as follows: Separation was achieved on a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). High-purity helium (≥ 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injector temperature was set at 260 °C. The gas chromatographic column temperature was 60 °C held for 30 s, increased to 210 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min, then to 270 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, followed by a rise to 305 °C at 20 °C/min, and finally maintained for 5 min. Mass spectrometry was performed with electron impact ionization at 70 eV, with an ion source temperature of 230 °C, a quadrupole temperature of 150 °C. Data were acquired in the full scan mode over a mass range of m/z 50–500 and compared with the NIST mass spectral library for identification.

Microbial analysis

Microbial colony counts were determined using the method described by Li et al. (2023), with minor modifications. A 10.0 g sample from the top surface of the duck meat was taken under aseptic conditions and transferred into a sterile stomacher bag containing 90.0 mL of sterile physiological saline (0.85% NaCl). The sample was homogenized for 2 min, and serial ten-fold dilutions were prepared for microbiological analysis. Total viable counts (TVC) were enumerated on Plate Count Agar following incubation at 36 °C ± 1 °C for 48 h ± 2 h. Coliform bacteria were determined on Violet Red Bile Agar following incubation at 36 °C ± 1 °C for 18–24 h. Blank controls were prepared by adding 1 mL of sterile saline solution to two sterile Petri dishes for each medium type. Results for all bacterial counts are expressed as log CFU/g.

DNA extraction, PCR reaction, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from duck meat using the CTAB method. The extracted DNA served as a template for PCR amplification focused on the V3-V4 segments of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene. The amplification protocol comprised an initial denaturation step and elongation. PCR products were observed using a 2% agarose gel, following which target bands were cut out and purified with the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit. The purified amplicon was quantified and then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The sequencing depth of each sample was about 50,000 reads. The sequencing coverage of all samples reached more than 99%, ensuring that the microbial community diversity was fully captured.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using a two-way ANOVA in IBM SPSS Statistics 20 software, with DHQ concentration, storage time and their interaction fitted as fixed factors, and duck meat as the random factor. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Alpha diversity indices were determined with the use of Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2), whereas beta diversity analysis was performed using R and QIIME2. Figures were generated using SigmaPlot 12.0 and Origin 2021 software.

Results and discussion

Color

Meat color serves as a critical indicator of freshness, as consumers often associate discoloration with spoilage, leading to rejection of the product. As depicted in Fig. 1, the L* value of the control group gradually increased during storage, remaining higher than that of the DHQ-treated groups. In contrast, the DHQ-treated groups maintained relatively stable L* values during storage. The a* values showed a downward trend in all groups; however, the DHQ-treated groups consistently exhibited higher a* values compared to the control group. Notably, the rate of decrease in a* values was lower in the DHQ-treated group. The b* values fluctuated among the treatment groups without a clear consistent pattern. The initial ranges of a* and L* values were comparable to those reported by Sun et al. (2025a). These results also align with the description by Liu et al. (2024), wherein duck samples exuded an increased purge during storage, enhancing surface light reflection and thus increasing L* values. The gradual decrease in myoglobin over time also contributed to the observed discoloration in a* values. pH

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the duck meat had an initial pH of 5.84, which decreased slightly between days 2 to 4. The pH then increased in the control group, rising from 6.31 to 6.60 from days 6 to 12. The initial drop was likely due to lactic acid production from glycogen glycolysis. The subsequent increase in pH can be attributed to protein degradation by microbial metabolism and endogenous enzymes, leading to the accumulation of alkaline compounds like bioamines and ammonia (Liu et al., 2022). This trend indicates a gradual deterioration in meat quality. In contrast, the DHQ-treated group exhibited a relatively slower and lower pH increase compared to the control group (P < 0.05). This suggests that DHQ inhibited microbial growth in duck meat, slowing the pH elevation and reducing the formation of alkaline nitrogen-containing compounds. These results demonstrate that DHQ treatment contributes to the maintenance of duck meat quality during storage.Fig. 2Effects of dihydroquercetin and storage time on pH value of duck meat. TBARS

MDA is produced during the decomposition of unsaturated fatty acids. The TBARS assay is a well-established method for evaluating lipid oxidation levels (Abeyrathne et al., 2021). TBARS values are positively correlated with the degree of lipid oxidation (Holman et al., 2021). Small molecular substances including ketones, aldehydes, acids, and other compounds are generated by lipid oxidation in duck breast meat, making the meat less acceptable for consumption (Keykhosravy et al., 2022). The initial TBARS value was 0.05 mg MDA/kg (Fig. 3), and it progressively increased during storage. After 8 days, the TBARS values increased by 5.91 times for the control group, 4.52 times for the 312.5 μg/mL DHQ group, 3.57 times for the 625 μg/mL DHQ group, and 2.96 times for the 1250 μg/mL DHQ group. Liu et al. (2024) reported an initial TBARS value of approximately 0.25 mg MDA/kg. With the extension of storage time, the available antioxidants in the samples were gradually exhausted, leading to an increase in TBARS values. In comparison, the DHQ-treated groups consistently exhibited lower TBARS values than the control group (P < 0.05). These results show that DHQ possesses effective antioxidant activity, with higher concentrations conferring superior antioxidant effects. Supporting this, Shagaeva et al. (2021) demonstrated that the use of DHQ reduced peroxide and acid numbers by two-fold and the thiobarbituric acid value by 1.8-fold in semi-finished product samples after 216 days of analysis. Flavonoids exhibit antioxidant activity by donating hydrogen atoms, quenching singlet oxygen, and chelating pro-oxidant metal ions (Olvera-Aguirre et al., 2023).Fig. 3Effects of dihydroquercetin and storage time on lipid oxidation of duck meat. TVB-N

The formation of TVB-N in fresh duck meat is predominantly influenced by enzymatic and microbial activities, which degrade proteins into amine compounds, such as tyrosine, histidine, and tryptophan (Duan et al., 2020). According to the National Food Safety Standard for Fresh (Frozen) Livestock and Poultry Products (GB 2707–2016), the maximum permissible TVB-N content in fresh meat is 15 mg/100 g. As depicted in Fig. 4, the initial TVB-N level on day 0 was 7.05 mg/100 g, indicating high freshness. Throughout the storage period, the control group exhibited progressively increasing TVB-N values, whereas the DHQ-treated samples showed lower TVB-N levels than the control group (P < 0.05). On day 8, the TVB-N value in the control group reached 20.01 mg/100 g, exceeding the regulatory limit and indicating spoilage. In contrast, the DHQ-treated groups at concentrations of 312.5 μg/mL, 625 μg/mL, and 1250 μg/mL maintained TVB-N values of 17.42 mg/100 g, 14.73 mg/100 g, and 11.41 mg/100 g, respectively. Li et al. (2019) also demonstrated that specific spoilage microorganisms, such as Pseudomonas, significantly contribute to the increase in TVB-N. These findings are similar to those of Cheng et al. (2024), who observed significant changes in TVB-N values in duck meat mince over 4 to 12 days, and the shelf life could be extended from 4 days to 6 days after using Litsea essential oil nanoemulsion. Consumer panel evaluation

Sensory quality is a critical indicator for consumers to assess freshness and acceptability. As shown in Fig. 5, the sensory scores (including color, odor, texture, and overall acceptability) of duck meat from both the control and DHQ-treated groups decreased progressively with prolonged storage time. The DHQ-treated group consistently received significantly higher scores than the control group. By day 6 of storage, the control group began to exhibit signs of spoilage, including off-odors, dull color, and a sticky texture, resulting in scores below the acceptable threshold according to the sensory panel. By day 8, the DHQ-treated group also became unacceptable to the panel. When the TVB-N value exceeded 15 mg/100 g, the sensory score significantly decreased, indicating that the off-odors and mucus produced by protein degradation are key factors affecting consumer acceptability. Consumer perceptions of spoilage can be influenced by various factors, including cultural preferences, economic considerations, and individual sensitivity. Typically, consumers reject meat products that show discoloration, strong off-odors, or slime formation. The growth of spoilage microorganisms, such as P. fluorescens and P. putida, which thrive at low temperatures, is a key factor in spoilage. These bacteria secrete collagenase, which hydrolyzes muscle connective tissue and leads to mucus formation and odor production (Ala & Shahbazi, 2019; Mohareb et al., 2015). These results confirm that spoilage directly impacts the overall quality of meat products (Tironi et al., 2007). The antioxidant mechanism of DHQ mainly involves scavenging free radicals, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity. Research conducted by the I. M. Sechenov Moscow Medical Academy shows that DHQ is non-toxic, poses no physiological harm to human health, and does not bring foreign taste or odor to products.Fig. 5Effects of dihydroquercetin and storage time on sensory evaluation of duck meat. Profiles of the flavor compounds by GC–MS

GC–MS was employed to analyze the flavor components in duck breast meat. The base peak chromatograms are provided in the supplementary data. As shown in Fig. 6, 36 flavor compounds were upregulated and 13 downregulated by day 4, rising to 66 upregulated and 22 downregulated by day 8. Compared to the control group, DHQ treatment resulted in 8 and 4 metabolites upregulated on days 4 and 8, respectively, and only 1 was downregulated on day 8. GC–MS analysis revealed that after duck meat was refrigerated for 4 days, freshness substances (adenosine monophosphate, 3’-AMP, and inosinic acid), water-soluble vitamins (ascorbic acid and niacinamide), and sulfur-containing amino acid metabolic intermediates (cystathione) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 7). These substances are important components that affect the quality of meat (Xu et al., 2025a). Concurrently, the accumulation of monosaccharides (d-fructose and sedoheptulose), biogenic amines (cadaverine), and microbial metabolites (2,3-butanediol) was observed, reflecting the initial nutritional degradation and formation of spoilage precursors. After 8 days of storage, the deterioration further intensified. This was characterized by the continuous loss of metabolites such as cytidine monophosphate, citric acid, and uric acid, alongside a substantial increase in spoilage-related amines (1-butylamine), glucose oxidation products (gluconolactone), and lipid oxidation derivatives (5-phenoxy-1-pentanol). These changes indicated profound disorders in nucleotide metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and carbohydrate-lipid oxidation pathways. Compared with the control group, DHQ-treated samples showed elevated levels of metabolites including cystathione, adenine, l-glutamine, and timonacic after 4 days of storage, which effectively protected amino acid and nucleotide metabolic pathways and enhanced the antioxidant capacity of the system. After 8 days, DHQ treatment restored the steady-state level of citric acid, reduced the accumulation of diglycerol, and increased key metabolic intermediates such as guanosine and β-glycerophosphoric acid. Consequently, it achieved the repair and protection of TCA cycle, lipid metabolism, and nucleic acid metabolism. Microbial counts

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the initial TVC of fresh duck meat was 5.10 log CFU/g, which is higher compared to a similar study, where the initial colony count was about 3.4 log CFU/g (Xu et al., 2025b). This suggests the importance of implementing measures to reduce the initial microbial load. Over the course of storage, the TVC gradually increased, with the DHQ-treated groups consistently showing lower counts compared to the control group. On day 8, the TVC in the control group reached 7.79 log CFU/g, surpassing the threshold of 7 log CFU/g, indicating the onset of spoilage. In contrast, the 1250 μg/mL DHQ treatment group maintained a lower count of 6.83 log CFU/g. These findings are consistent with previous research by Dragoev et al. (2014), which demonstrated that combining 80% O₂/20% CO₂ modified atmosphere packaging with surface treatment using a 0.02% DHQ solution enhanced veal quality and extended its shelf life. This approach reduced acid values by 27.72%, pH by 6.18%, peroxide values by 64.74%, and TBARS by 65.71%, all while maintaining acceptable microbiological quality for up to 15 days when stored at 0 ± 0.5 °C. Additionally, Sun et al. (2025b) reported that DHQ effectively inhibited lipid oxidation and microbial growth in fish, further supporting its role as a preservative in food storage. Bacterial communities identified by sequencing

The Chao 1 and Shannon indices are commonly used to assess microbial community richness and diversity (Yang et al., 2018). Rarefaction curves for both indices in all samples showed a gradual approach to a plateau (Fig. 10A, B), suggesting that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the microbial community diversity throughout the storage period. Both indices exhibited a general decline with prolonged storage, indicating a reduction in microbial richness and diversity. Venn diagram analysis of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) further supported these findings (Fig. 10C). The number of OTUs reflects microbial species richness. Throughout the storage period, 58 OTUs were shared among all samples. On day 0, duck meat exhibited 4537 unique OTUs. By day 4, the DHQ treatment groups had 896–2310 OTUs, which further declined to 223–463 by day 8. These results indicate that competition among microorganisms intensifies during storage, leading to a gradual decrease in diversity, and only some microorganisms gain dominance and become the preponderant microbiota. This finding is consistent with the results of the Chao 1 and Shannon indices.Fig. 10The diversity index rarefaction curve of Chao1 (A) and shannon (B) of dihydroquercetin treated duck meat during storage at 4 °C. A cluster analysis was performed on the top 16 genera with the highest relative abundance (Fig. 11A). The results revealed that samples from different periods formed distinct clusters, with high similarity in bacterial community structures within the same storage time. However, significant differences in the microbiota structure were observed across different storage periods. The initial microbial community in the duck meat was mainly composed of Acinetobacter, Aeromonas and Streptococcus. The relative abundances of Carnobacterium and Pseudomonas increased up to the 4th day of storage and decreased after DHQ treatment. The relative abundance of Pseudomonas and Brochothrix increased up to the 8th day of storage, with Pseudomonas becoming the dominant genus. After DHQ treatment, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas decreased, with higher DHQ concentrations leading to more pronounced reductions. These results indicate that DHQ treatment significantly influences the microflora of duck meat. The observed differences in the composition of the dominant flora in the later stages of storage may also explain the inconsistency in the shelf life of the different treatment samples. Further analysis of the differential flora at the species level, specifically in the control group, highlighted Pseudomonas as the predominant bacterium in the middle and late storage stages (Fig. 11B). It has been reported that the initial predominant bacteria in chilled chicken leg-quarters were Acinetobacter, Aeromonas, Shewanella and Pseudomonas (Chen et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with the study by Xu et al. (2025b), who also detected a large presence of Pseudomonas in duck meat during the middle and late storage stages. Notably, the dominant Pseudomonas in the mid-storage period was P. putida, while P. fragi became the dominant species during the late storage period. Both Pseudomonas fragi and Brochothrix are considered major spoilage bacteria under low-temperature aerobic conditions (Nychas et al., 2008). Proteolytic activity facilitates the penetration of Pseudomonads into meat (Gill & Penney, 1977; Gupta & Nagamohini, 1992). Among them, Pseudomonas putida can degrade muscle proteins, amino acids, and oligopeptides. A positive correlation may exist between its spoilage capacity and its adhesive properties (Shang et al., 2025). In contrast, P. fragi is characterized by a fast growth rate, high nutrient utilization, and produces volatile substances such as alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes, which lead to increased TVB-N and sensory rejection (e.g., off-odors). Spearman correlation analysis showed (Fig. 12) that the relative abundance of Pseudomonas exhibited a strong positive correlation with the TVB-N value (ρ = 0.87, P = 0.002), thereby confirming that the role of the strain in protein degradation. Conclusion

In this study, the effects of DHQ treatment on the quality and microbial community of duck meat during refrigerated storage were systematically investigated. According to the results, DHQ addition inhibited lipid oxidation and microbial growth, as reflected by lower TBARS values and reduced total viable counts. Based on TVB-N analysis, the shelf life was extended from 6 days (control) to 8 days with 625 μg/mL DHQ, and to 10 days with 1250 μg/mL DHQ. Notably, DHQ treatment showed a more pronounced effect on quality parameters such as color stability, pH control, and sensory acceptability in the earlier stages of storage. Metabolomic analysis further revealed that DHQ treatment mitigated the degradation of key flavor-related compounds (e.g., nucleotides and amino acids) and reduced the accumulation of spoilage-associated metabolites, including biogenic amines and lipid oxidation products. These metabolic changes corresponded to improved freshness retention and delayed quality deterioration. Furthermore, microbial community analysis indicated that DHQ treatment suppressed the dominance of spoilage-related bacteria, particularly Pseudomonas spp., which are closely associated with protein degradation and TVB-N accumulation. However, the degree of preservation achieved by DHQ alone suggests that its application may need to be combined with other strategies to achieve more robust shelf-life extension. In summary, while DHQ demonstrates potential as a natural antibacterial agent for duck meat, effectively maintaining certain quality attributes and microbial dynamics, its standalone use provides only moderate shelf-life improvement. Future research should focus on integrating DHQ with complementary preservation technologies, to develop more effective and practical preservation systems for meat products.
