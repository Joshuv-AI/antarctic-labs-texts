A combined model based on lactate clearance rate and SOFA score in prognostic assessment of patients with severe pneumonia
Authors: Ying Chen, Chuankai Liu, Duandan Li, Jun Yan, Man Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the role of lactate clearance rate (LCR) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score in prognostic assessment of patients with severe pneumonia (SP).MethodsA retrospective study of 185 SP patients was conducted. Baseline characteristics were compared between survivors (n = 128) and non-survivors (n = 57). Following univariate and correlation analyses, significant variables were used to build a combined prediction model via binary logistic regression. Model calibration and predictive performance for 28-day mortality were assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow and ROC curve analyses, respectively. Dynamic changes in LCR and SOFA scores at admission (T0), 6 h (T6), and 24 h (T24) were compared.ResultsThe 28-day mortality rate was 30.81% (57/185). Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in age, chronic lung disease, procalcitonin (PCT), LCR, and SOFA score (P < 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis showed a moderate negative correlation between LCR and SOFA score (r = -0.401, P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified LCR [0.911 (0.861–0.965)] and SOFA score [1.508 (1.278–1.779)] as independent predictors (P < 0.05). The combined model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.882, 95% CI: 0.833–0.932) compared to LCR (AUC: 0.793) or SOFA score (AUC: 0.832) alone (Delong test, P < 0.05). Survivors exhibited significantly higher LCR and lower SOFA scores at T6 and T24, with improving trends over time, unlike non-survivors.ConclusionThe combined model integrating LCR and SOFA score outperforms either parameter alone in predicting 28-day mortality risk in SP, offering a potential tool for early risk stratification.Keywords: Severe pneumonia, Lactate clearance rate, Sequential organ failure assessment score, Prediction model, Prognostic mortality

Introduction

Severe pneumonia (SP) is one of the most common reasons for adult admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and a key disease spectrum leading to in-hospital mortality and healthcare resource consumption. Epidemiological studies show that approximately 10%-20% of patients with community-acquired pneumonia progress to SP requiring ICU care due to respiratory failure, septic shock, or multiple organ dysfunction, with a mortality rate as high as 20%-40%, significantly exceeding that of general ward patients [1–3]. The 2019 American Thoracic Society/Infectious Diseases Society of America (ATS/IDSA) guidelines for adult community-acquired pneumonia explicitly identify SP as a high-risk phenotype requiring priority identification and intervention, emphasizing that early identification of high-risk patients, appropriate antimicrobial selection, and optimization of organ function support are core pathways to reducing mortality [4]. However, in clinical practice, even with standard anti-infective and organ support therapy, some SP patients still rapidly progress to irreversible multiple organ failure. This suggests that relying solely on traditional clinical assessments makes it difficult to promptly and accurately determine prognostic risk, highlighting an urgent need for more sensitive and actionable early prognostic assessment tools.

Several scoring tools are currently used clinically to assess pneumonia severity and prognosis, including the pneumonia severity index (PSI), CURB-65, quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) and SOFA scores [5, 6]. Concurrently, studies have indicated that the discriminative power of SOFA can vary with the site of infection, underscoring the importance of specific validation in populations with focal infections like pneumonia [7]. Beyond organ function scores, blood lactate, as an integrated signal of tissue perfusion and cellular metabolic status, holds a central position in risk stratification for patients with severe infection and shock. While a single lactate measurement can indicate tissue hypoperfusion or metabolic disturbance, dynamic monitoring of lactate trends over time, particularly the lactate clearance rate (LCR), is considered more reflective of resuscitation and treatment effectiveness than static values. Nguyen et al. [8] first demonstrated in patients with severe sepsis and septic shock that early LCR ≥ 10% was associated with significantly reduced in-hospital mortality, proposing the concept of lactate clearance-directed resuscitation. Subsequent studies further confirmed that a higher LCR within 24 h of ICU admission was closely associated with lower 28-day mortality and often independently correlated with adverse outcomes in multivariate models [9]. Systematic reviews also indicate that elevated lactate levels and insufficient lactate clearance are significant predictors of short-term mortality in critically ill patients, regardless of infection status [10].

Lactate-related indicators also demonstrate important prognostic value in pneumonia populations. Zhou et al. [11] found in an emergency department cohort of community-acquired pneumonia patients that combining admission lactate levels with severity scores like CURB-65, PSI, and SOFA significantly enhanced the predictive power for 28-day mortality. Other studies suggest that in SP patients, insufficient lactate clearance often indicates inadequate resuscitation and persistent tissue hypoperfusion, predisposing them to develop respiratory failure and multiple organ dysfunction [12–14]. However, existing research predominantly focuses on single biomarkers or single scoring systems. Evidence on how dynamic metabolic indicators (e.g., LCR) and quantitative organ function tools (e.g., SOFA) complement each other within the same SP population to build a combined mathematical model for improved prognostic prediction remains relatively limited. Particularly in real-world critical care cohorts from tertiary general hospitals in China, there is a lack of concise combined prediction models based on LCR and SOFA scores, and systematic analysis of their temporal trajectories in relation to prognosis is also insufficient.

Theoretically, with effective resuscitation and infection control, lactate levels should decrease over time (LCR increases), while organ function gradually recovers (SOFA score decreases). Conversely, persistently low LCR coupled with a persistently high SOFA score suggests ongoing hypoperfusion and progressive organ injury, indicating a significantly increased mortality risk. Therefore, integrating information from these two dimensions into a combined prediction model may enable earlier and more accurate identification of truly high-risk SP patients. Based on this background, this study aims to investigate the role of LCR and SOFA score in prognostic assessment of SP patients, and construct a combined prediction model, providing a simple and reliable tool for early risk stratification and precision treatment in SP patients.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

Based on relevant study report [15, 16], the 28-day mortality rate for SP patients is 17–49%. This study aimed to construct a logistic regression model predicting mortality in SP patients, planning to include 5–6 predictors that were determined with reference to a similar study [15] and the evaluation system of this research. According to the principle of requiring at least 10 events per variable (EPV) in logistic regression analysis [17], a conservative estimate using EPV = 10 yielded a minimum required number of death events of 50. Combining this with the expected mortality rate (33%), the calculated minimum total sample size was approximately 152 patients. To enhance model stability, this study ultimately included 185 SP patients treated at our hospital, meeting the sample size requirement.

Study subjects

A total of 185 SP patients admitted to the ICU of the Department of Critical Care Medicine, the Respiratory ICU (RICU) of the Department of Respiratory Medicine/Pulmonology, and the Emergency Department ICU of our hospital between October 2022 and December 2024 were retrospectively enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: ① Meeting the diagnostic criteria for SP as defined in the ATS/IDSA 2019 guidelines [4]; ② Age ≥ 18 years, directly admitted to one of the aforementioned ICUs upon hospitalization, with an ICU stay ≥ 24 h; ③ Arterial blood lactate measurement completed within 24 h of admission, allowing calculation of LCR using the formula; ④ Collection and assessment of all indicators required for the SOFA score completed within 24 h of admission; ⑤ Complete clinical medical records, including demographic information, vital signs, laboratory tests, imaging reports, and 28-day survival outcome; ⑥ Time from symptom onset to admission ≤ 72 h; ⑦ Signed informed consent obtained from the patient or legal representative (the retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital; for cases where informed consent could not be obtained, inclusion was permitted after ethical review and exemption). Exclusion criteria included: ① Comorbid end-stage underlying diseases, such as advanced malignancy (expected survival < 3 months), decompensated liver cirrhosis, chronic renal failure in uremic stage (on regular dialysis ≥ 3 months), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) stage IV; ② Concurrent other severe infections, such as infective endocarditis, purulent meningitis, intra-abdominal abscess, etc. (to avoid confounding of prognosis and indicators by multi-site infections); ③ Having received standardized antimicrobial therapy targeting the current pneumonia for ≥ 48 h prior to admission (affecting the authenticity of baseline lactate levels and SOFA scores); ④ Organ dysfunction caused by non-infectious diseases, such as acute cerebrovascular disease, acute myocardial infarction, severe trauma, acute pancreatitis, etc. (to exclude factors increasing SOFA score unrelated to pneumonia); ⑤ Hospital stay < 24 h (discharged against medical advice, transferred, or died), preventing completion of post-treatment 6-hour and 24-hour indicator monitoring and 28-day outcome follow-up; ⑥ Non-infectious causes of elevated lactate, such as diabetic ketoacidosis, rhabdomyolysis, severe anemia, liver failure, etc. (interfering with the specificity of LCR assessment); ⑦ Pregnancy or lactation; ⑧ Concurrent participation in other clinical trials that may interfere with the outcome assessment of this study. Observation indicators

LCR: Arterial blood lactate concentration was measured via arterial blood sampling for all patients at admission (T0), 6 hours post-treatment (T6, starting from the initiation of first fluid resuscitation or vasoactive drug use), and 24 hours post-treatment (T24). LCR was calculated using the formula: (Admission lactate value - Lactate value at a specific time point)/Initial lactate value × 100%. A higher LCR value indicates better lactate clearance capacity. In this study, LCR values calculated at T6 (6 hours post-treatment) and T24 (24 hours post-treatment) were primarily compared between the survival and death groups to assess the dynamic changes and prognostic value of LCR in early risk stratification. The decision to use LCR at T6 was based on its clinical relevance as an early indicator of treatment response and infection resolution.

SOFA score: The SOFA score quantifies the severity of organ dysfunction, assessing six domains: respiratory system (PaO₂/FiO₂), coagulation system (platelet count), liver system (bilirubin), circulatory system (mean arterial pressure and vasoactive drug use), nervous system (Glasgow Coma Scale score), and renal system (serum creatinine or urine output). The total SOFA score (range 0-24) was calculated at T0, T6, and T24. A higher score indicates worse prognosis.

The total SOFA score (range 0-24) was calculated at T0, T6, and T24. A higher score indicates worse prognosis.

Time zero definition: In this study, “time zero” is defined as the initiation of first fluid resuscitation or vasoactive drug use for each patient. This time point is chosen because it represents the start of active treatment aimed at restoring metabolic balance and organ perfusion, which are critical in the early management of severe pneumonia (SP). We acknowledge that “time zero” may vary slightly between patients depending on their individual clinical presentations, but it remains a clinically relevant threshold to assess early treatment response. Additionally, by anchoring time zero to treatment initiation, we can better track how LCR and SOFA scores change in response to therapy, allowing for more accurate early risk stratification.

Missing data. Patients with incomplete key data were excluded at screening according to the inclusion criteria. Consequently, there were no missing values in the final analytic dataset, and no imputation was performed.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and curves were plotted. Categorical data were expressed as [n (%)], with group comparisons performed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with group comparisons made using the independent samples t-test (equal variances) or Welch’s t-test (unequal variances). Within-group comparisons across different time points were made using the paired samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as M (P25, P75), with group comparisons made using the Mann-Whitney U test and within-group comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For repeated measures data at T0, T6, and T24, repeated measures analysis of variance was used if sphericity and normality assumptions were met; otherwise, the Friedman test was used, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. To construct the prediction model, potential factors associated with patient mortality were first screened through univariate analysis, and the correlation between LCR and SOFA score was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in a binary logistic regression to construct the combined prediction model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to evaluate model calibration. Predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Comparisons of the area under the curve (AUC) between different models were performed using the Delong test. Bootstrap resampling (500 iterations) was used to estimate the stability of the model’s performance and calculate the standard error of AUC values. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the net benefit of the combined model across a range of threshold probabilities, comparing it with LCR, SOFA, treat-all, and treat-none strategies.

Results

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis within 28 days of hospitalization

Among the 185 included SP patients, 57 (30.81%) died within 28 days. All patients were included in a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, with deaths as the event and survivors censored at day 28. The survival curve showed a progressive decline in survival probability over time, with an estimated 28-day survival rate of 69.2%. The median time to death among the 57 non-survivors was 12 days (95% CI: 8.724–15.276), but this did not represent the overall median survival because the cumulative mortality did not reach 50% (Fig. 2).

Prediction model construction

Based on the aforementioned binary logistic regression, a clinical prediction model was further constructed. Using 28-day mortality as the dependent variable, the core variables LCR and SOFA score were included in the binary logistic regression model based on clinical importance. The model formula was: Logit (P) = -7.212–0.093 × LCR + 0.411 × SOFA score. The Omnibus test of model coefficients confirmed the model’s statistical significance (P < 0.001). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed χ² = 10.835 and P = 0.211, indicating good model calibration. ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC (95% CI) for LCR alone in predicting SP patient mortality was 0.793 (0.729–0.857). At the maximum Youden’s index of 0.559, sensitivity was 96.50% and specificity was 59.40%. The AUC (95% CI) for SOFA score alone was 0.832 (0.771–0.892). At the maximum Youden’s index of 0.479, sensitivity was 87.70% and specificity was 60.20%. The AUC (95% CI) for the combined model was 0.882 (0.833–0.932). At the maximum Youden’s index of 0.656, sensitivity was 78.90% and specificity was 86.70%. The Delong test showed that the AUC of the combined model was greater than that of LCR or SOFA score alone (Z = 3.545, 2.763, P < 0.05), indicating excellent diagnostic efficacy for the combined model (Tables 4, 5 and 6; Fig. 5). In 500 bootstrap resamples, the mean AUC of the combined model was 0.881 (standard error = 0.026), significantly higher than that of the LCR model (AUC = 0.792, SE = 0.032) and the SOFA model (AUC = 0.830, SE = 0.031). These results further demonstrate the stability and higher clinical applicability potential of the combined model in predicting 28-day mortality risk.

Discussion

This single-center critical care cohort study included 185 SP patients meeting the ATS/IDSA 2019 guideline diagnostic criteria and directly admitted to the ICU. The 28-day in-hospital mortality rate was 30.81%, consistent with previously reported mortality rates of 20%-30% in domestic and international studies on SP or infection-related ICU populations, suggesting the representativeness of the study sample. The main findings of this study include: First, both LCR and SOFA score were confirmed as independent influencing factors for 28-day mortality in SP patients by multivariate logistic regression, whereas age, chronic lung disease, and PCT lost significance after adjustment. Second, the combined prediction model based on LCR and SOFA score achieved an AUC of 0.882, significantly superior to LCR alone (0.793) or SOFA score alone (0.832). Third, regarding dynamic changes, survivors exhibited a continuous increase in LCR and a continuous decrease in SOFA score from T0 to T24, whereas non-survivors had overall lower LCR and showed no significant improvement in SOFA score across the three time points. These results jointly validate, from the dimensions of metabolic homeostasis and organ function, the important role of LCR and SOFA score in the early prognostic assessment of SP patients and suggest a clear synergistic effect from their combined application.

LCR improves the prediction of 28-day mortality in SP by offering a dynamic, real-time measure of metabolic response and tissue perfusion. While SOFA primarily reflects organ dysfunction, LCR indicates how well the body is recovering from metabolic disturbances, particularly related to infection. The improvement in LCR during the early stages of resuscitation correlates with infection resolution, as lower lactate levels suggest better tissue oxygenation and perfusion [18]. In contrast, fluid resuscitation and vasopressors can also influence LCR by improving hemodynamics, but LCR remains a sensitive marker of infection resolution, as its improvement reflects the body’s ability to restore metabolic balance, which is essential for recovery from infection [19]. Regarding the prognostic value of lactate and its clearance rate, our findings are highly consistent with previous literature on sepsis and severe infection. As early as 2004, Nguyen et al. [8] reported that early LCR ≥ 10% was associated with significantly reduced in-hospital mortality in patients with severe sepsis and septic shock, proposing the incorporation of lactate clearance into resuscitation goals. Marty et al. [9] further confirmed that lactate clearance within 24 h of ICU admission was one of the most powerful predictors of 28-day mortality, with an AUC reaching 0.79. Recent systematic reviews indicate that in various critical care scenarios, elevated lactate and insufficient clearance are closely associated with short-term mortality risk, with LCR having a predictive advantage over single lactate measurements [10]. This study found that LCR at T6 and T24 was significantly lower in the death group than the survival group among SP patients. In the combined model, the OR for LCR was 0.911, indicating that for every 1% increase in lactate clearance, the 28-day mortality risk decreases by approximately 9%. This suggests that even at the same level of organ function score, the adequacy of metabolic resuscitation can provide additional information for mortality risk stratification.

Unlike previous studies predominantly focused on sepsis/septic shock, this study specifically targeted patients with severe disease caused by pulmonary infection. Zhou et al. [11] found in emergency department community-acquired pneumonia patients that admission lactate levels could supplement the risk stratification of CURB-65, neumonia Severity Index (PSI), and SOFA scores, significantly increasing the AUC in distinguishing high-risk patients. Several validated tools, such as the PSI, CURB-65, and SMART-COP, are widely used for early risk stratification. CURB-65 is simple and practical for rapid triage but may not fully capture the evolving organ dysfunction and treatment response in critically ill SP patients [20]. PSI offers broader prognostic utility but is complex and was developed for community-acquired pneumonia rather than ICU cases [21]. SMART-COP, designed to predict the need for intensive support, requires specific measurements that may not be available in routine datasets and reflects a more static assessment [22]. In contrast, our combined model integrates a dynamic metabolic marker (lactate clearance) with an organ dysfunction score (SOFA), capturing both early resuscitation effectiveness and multi-organ impairment. This two-dimensional approach is particularly relevant in ICU settings, where clinical conditions can change rapidly. While our study did not perform head-to-head comparisons against these tools, future multicenter studies should assess the comparative value and potential for reclassification (e.g., DeLong test, NRI/IDI) of our combined model. This study further integrated two-dimensional indicators (dynamic LCR and total SOFA score) into the same Logit model, demonstrating that in a pure pulmonary infection ICU population, LCR is not only an independent predictor but also exhibits a moderate negative correlation with the SOFA score. This correlation and complementarity provide a statistical basis for constructing a combined prediction tool.

The SOFA score is one of the most widely used organ function scoring tools in critical care medicine [23–25]. Raith et al. [6], using big data from over 180,000 infection-related ICU patients, found that sepsis defined by a SOFA score increase ≥ 2 points had a significantly higher AUC (0.753) for predicting in-hospital mortality compared to systemic inflammatory response syndrome and qSOFA score. Jones et al. [26] also confirmed a good dose-response relationship between SOFA score and in-hospital mortality in patients with severe sepsis. Concurrently, Pawar et al. [7] suggested that the discriminative power of SOFA was not entirely consistent across different infection sites (e.g., pneumonia, intra-abdominal infection, urinary tract infection), and infection type was a background variable that cannot be ignored when interpreting SOFA scores. This study, in a pure SP population, confirmed that early ICU SOFA score remained an important independent predictor of 28-day mortality, with an AUC of 0.832, similar to or slightly better than previous reports in infection-related ICU cohorts. This result supports the rationale of Sepsis-3 using “increase in SOFA score” as the core diagnostic criterion for sepsis, while also providing supplementary evidence for the contextualized application of SOFA in pulmonary infection populations [27–29].

Notably, similar to other studies, this research found that a single SOFA score often faced the challenge of balancing sensitivity and specificity when selecting thresholds [30]. In this study, even at the SOFA threshold corresponding to the maximum Youden’s index (sensitivity 87.7%, specificity 60.2%), there remained instances where some high-risk patients were underestimated or some low-risk patients were over-alerted. In contrast, the combined model, after selecting the optimal cut-off point, showed a slightly decreased sensitivity (78.9%) but a significantly increased specificity to 86.7%, with an overall AUC also significantly higher than that of single indicators. This suggests that in clinical practice, when the goal is to identify truly high-risk patients requiring intensified monitoring or escalated treatment, high specificity is particularly important to avoid resource waste and unnecessary invasive interventions [31, 32]. The combined model constructed in this study achieves a better balance between sensitivity and specificity while maintaining high overall predictive performance, indicating good potential for clinical application.

From a temporal perspective, this study suggested that the dynamic trajectories of LCR and SOFA score were highly correlated with outcomes. Survivors had significantly higher LCR at T6 and T24 compared to non-survivors, and their LCR at T24 was higher than at T6, reflecting gradual improvement in tissue perfusion and metabolic status with active fluid resuscitation, anti-infective therapy, and organ support. In contrast, although non-survivors showed a slight increase in LCR at T24 compared to T6, their overall levels remained significantly low, suggesting persistent microcirculatory disturbance and metabolic dysregulation. Regarding SOFA scores, survivors showed significant decreases at T6 and T24 compared to T0, whereas non-survivors showed no significant differences among the three time points, indicating a sustained state of organ function injury. These observations align with the pathophysiological principles of lactate kinetics and organ function recovery and are consistent with previous research conclusions that faster lactate clearance and better organ function recovery are associated with better prognosis [33–35]. This suggests that clinicians should not focus solely on single-point lactate or SOFA values at admission but should emphasize repeat measurements and their trends within 6–24 h, incorporating dynamic information into early treatment evaluation and regimen adjustment.

The clinical significance of this study is mainly reflected in the following aspects. First, both LCR and SOFA score are derived from routine clinical tests and records, requiring no additional expensive examinations, facilitating promotion and application across different levels of hospitals. Second, the combined model structure is concise, involving only two continuous variables, and can be directly integrated into electronic medical record systems or implemented via simple spreadsheets or mobile applications for bedside automatic calculation, providing clinicians with real-time individualized mortality risk assessment. Third, the model can be uniformly applied to SP patients admitted via different pathways, such as the Department of Critical Care Medicine, RICU, and Emergency Department ICU, facilitating cross-departmental and cross-time-point risk communication and unified treatment decision-making. Fourth, for patients identified as high-risk early on, clinical teams can initiate intensified anti-infective strategies, optimize hemodynamic management, assess indications for mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy earlier, and engage in timely prognosis communication and treatment goal discussions with patients and families, potentially improving overall outcomes and rationally allocating ICU resources.

Clinical integration and proposed decision algorithm. To facilitate bedside implementation, we propose a pragmatic clinical decision algorithm based on the combined model. The predicted probability of 28-day mortality can be calculated using: Logit(P) = -7.212–0.093×LCR + 0.411×SOFA, and P = 1/(1 + e^{−Logit(P)}). Using the ROC-derived optimal cut-off (maximum Youden index), patients may be categorized as high-risk (P ≥ 0.360) versus lower-risk (P < 0.360) for early escalation of care. High-risk patients may warrant intensified monitoring, early reassessment of perfusion and organ support needs, and timely multidisciplinary discussions regarding advanced respiratory/hemodynamic support. Lower-risk patients may continue standard management with scheduled reassessment of lactate kinetics and SOFA trajectory. A sample calculation is provided to improve usability. This algorithm is proposed for clinical facilitation and requires prospective multicenter validation before routine adoption.

Certainly, this study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective design, with cases from the ICUs of a single general hospital, which may introduce selection bias. The conclusions require further external validation in multi-center populations. Second, although various clinical and laboratory variables were included, differences in treatment strategies (e.g., antimicrobial initiation, fluid resuscitation, ventilation strategies) may affect prognosis and could not be fully adjusted. Third, this study only observed lactate and SOFA score changes at T0, T6, and T24, and did not assess longer-term dynamic data or functional outcomes. Fourth, emerging biomarkers (e.g., interleukin-6, procalcitonin) or imaging severity indicators were not included, and their combined value with LCR and SOFA warrants further exploration. Fifth, variable selection was based on both clinical relevance and statistical significance (P < 0.05), which may lead to the omission of important confounders. While this approach is commonly used, it can be unstable and may not fully account for complex relationships between variables. In future studies, we plan to use prespecified predictors, assess nonlinearity for continuous variables, and explore penalized regression methods such as LASSO or Ridge regression. Finally, this study did not conduct stratified analyses based on different etiologies or immune statuses. Future studies could construct stratified models on larger samples to improve prediction accuracy for different subgroups.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that in SP patients admitted to the ICU, RICU, or Emergency Department ICU, both LCR and SOFA score are important independent predictors of 28-day mortality, with complementarity in their pathophysiological dimensions. The combined logistic regression model based on LCR and SOFA score demonstrated superior prognostic assessment performance compared to single indicators and offers advantages such as simplicity, low cost, and ease of promotion. Future research is necessary to externally validate this model in multi-center prospective cohorts and to further optimize the prediction tool by incorporating more biomarkers and imaging information, thereby providing more reliable evidence-based support for early precision stratification and individualized treatment of SP patients.
