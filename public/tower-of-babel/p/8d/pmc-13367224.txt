A Combined Experimental and Predictive Modeling Study of Diltiazem Release From Natural Polymer Microcapsules in Different Media
Authors: Zehra Üstün, Ümit Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe release behavior of diltiazem‐loaded microcapsules prepared from gelatin B and gum arabic was evaluated experimentally and mechanistically in distilled water and simulated saliva. Release amounts were quantified by liquid chromatography over 5–720 min and used directly as input for kinetic and mechanistic modeling. Kinetic analysis was performed using different models, and model performances were compared using the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC, BIC). The Weibull model provided the best fit in both media (R 2 = 0.989 for distilled water, R 2 = 0.994 for simulated saliva). A two‐compartment mechanistic model developed in SimBiology/MATLAB yielded release rate constants of 0.0111 min−1 (water) and 0.0026 min−1 (saliva). Agreement between the Weibull mechanistic model and experimental results was R 2 = 0.989 for distilled water (RMSE = 0.524 mg) and R 2 = 0.994 for simulated saliva (RMSE = 0.348 mg). Model validation was performed using Visual Predictive Check (VPC) with N = 200 virtual individuals, confirming that all observed data points fell within the 5%–95% percentile prediction interval. The validated model was further extended to a sublingual pH variability scenario (pH 6.2–7.4), demonstrating that saliva pH influences cumulative release through its effect on the unionized fraction of diltiazem.

Introduction

In emergency department practice, diltiazem is one of the most frequently used non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers for acute ventricular rate control, particularly in patients with atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter and other supraventricular tachycardias. Both clinical guidelines and recent studies indicate that intravenous diltiazem is among the first selected therapeutic options in hemodynamically stable patients presenting with rapid ventricular response, owing to the need for rapid onset of action [1, 2]. However, especially during tachycardia attacks, there is currently no established clinical strategy aimed at mitigating the risks of panic attacks or cerebrovascular events in the period before patients reach the emergency department. The absence of a sublingual diltiazem formulation in clinical use therefore represents an important gap in current practice.

Oral formulations of diltiazem remain limited in acute tachycardia settings due to their dependence on gastrointestinal absorption, exposure to first‐pass hepatic metabolism, and delayed onset of action. In addition, patient non‐compliance becomes a significant concern in conditions such as nausea, altered consciousness, or dysphagia [3, 4]. In this context, the sublingual route of administration offers distinct pharmacokinetic advantages, including bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and hepatic first‐pass metabolism, achieving more rapid and predictable plasma concentrations, and allowing ease of administration by the patient [5].

In recent years, favorable clinical and pharmacokinetic outcomes reported for short‐acting calcium channel blockers administered via intranasal or sublingual routes suggest that the development of a sublingual formulation of diltiazem may represent a rational area for both theoretical and clinical investigation [6]. Taken together, while the efficacy of diltiazem in ensuring patient safety during acute tachycardia is well established, these considerations highlight that the gap between the need for rapid therapeutic onset and the limitations of oral administration may, in the future, be addressed through alternative pharmaceutical approaches such as sublingual delivery.

In recent years, studies have demonstrated that biorelevant test environments based on simulated saliva more realistically reflect sublingual drug release and mucosal contact time [7, 8]. Accordingly, in the development and evaluation of sublingual formulations, the use of biorelevant dissolution and release models that account for saliva release behavior is of critical importance for more accurate prediction of clinical performance [9].

Although numerous in vitro release studies on diltiazem are available in the literature [10, 11, 12], the majority of these studies remain limited to the description of release profiles and do not generate predictive information. In most existing studies, release data obtained under different polymer systems or formulation parameters are evaluated based on percentage release–time relationships, while kinetic analyses rely on limited outcomes. Although this approach provides a general classification of the release mechanism, it offers limited contribution in terms of predicting the in vivo performance of the formulation [13, 14, 15]. This situation highlights the need for biorelevant and predictive kinetic models, particularly for sublingual formulations developed for diltiazem.

Pharmacokinetic modeling tools such as SimBiology enable the direct integration of experimental release data into mathematical models, the systematic estimation of parameters and the virtual simulation of different populations or physiological conditions. In the literature, particularly for diltiazem, integrated studies in which experimental in vitro release data are systematically used for pharmacokinetic parameter extraction, these parameters are validated and subsequently extended to different physiological scenarios are highly limited or practically nonexistent [16, 17]. Although platforms such as SimBiology provide a suitable and flexible infrastructure for such integrative analyses, the use of release data based approaches remains limited, with applications largely focusing on different drug classes or alternative pharmaceutical systems. In this context, the development of a predictive approach that combines experimental release data of diltiazem with simulation based analysis emerges as a clear gap in the literature.

This study demonstrates an analytically driven workflow that integrates LC quantified release amounts (mg) with mechanistic SimBiology modeling and scenario based simulations. Present work includes the steps of microcapsule preparation, determination of release profiles in different media by liquid chromatographic method, comparative analysis of release kinetics using mathematical and statistical approaches, mechanistic release simulations with Visual Predictive Check validation, and predictive scenario analyses under physiologically relevant sublingual saliva pH conditions. Materials and Methods

Materials

Diltiazem HCl was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA). Gelatin B and gum arabic were used as natural polymers for microcapsule preparation. HPLC grade ethanol (Isolab, Germany) was used as the organic solvent in all chromatographic quantification analyses.

Experimental Procedures and Data Acquisition

Microcapsules were prepared at concentrations of 20% (w/v) gelatin and 5% (w/v) gum arabic, with a ratio of 2:1. In vitro release studies were conducted in distilled water and simulated saliva media under shaking incubator conditions at 37°C. Release studies were conducted over a time range of 5 to 720 min, with sampling at 12 times (5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 360, 480, and 720 min). All experiments were performed under sink conditions, and release profiles were calculated using cumulative correction.

The simulated saliva medium was prepared in the laboratory to establish a biorelevant model for sublingual conditions. The pH of the simulated saliva medium was measured as 6.810. Distilled water was selected as a reference medium to establish baseline release kinetics under sink conditions, enabling direct comparison with the biorelevant saliva medium. The amount of diltiazem in samples collected during the release experiments was determined using a liquid chromatographic method. HPLC grade ethanol (Isolab, Germany) was used as the organic solvent in all chromatographic quantification analyses. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Gemini NX‐5u C18 column and all analyses were carried out using a Shimadzu HPLC system.

Although the experimental release data were converted into instantaneous and cumulative percentage release values, cumulative amounts of released drug (mg) were used as input data in mechanistic modeling and simulation studies. Instantaneous release values were calculated as the difference between consecutive cumulative release measurements, representing the amount of drug released within each individual time interval. EDS (Energy Dispersive X‐ray Spectroscopy) analysis was used as supportive physicochemical characterization to confirm the presence and distribution of the drug in the microcapsules. EDS data were used to support the mechanistic interpretation of the experimentally observed release behavior. Specifically, EDS elemental mapping confirmed the presence and uniform distribution of diltiazem within the microcapsule matrix, providing physicochemical evidence consistent with the encapsulation efficiency value used to initialize the mechanistic model (Q 0 = 19.31 mg).

Kinetic Modeling and Model Selection

To quantitatively describe the kinetic behavior of diltiazem release profile and to comparatively evaluate the release mechanisms observed in different media, three different kinetic models (Higuchi [18], Korsmeyer–Peppas [19], Weibull [20]) were applied to the experimental release data. All calculations were performed using SciDAVis software, and the parameters were estimated by regression analysis. The Higuchi model was evaluated under the assumption that release proceeds through a diffusion‐controlled process and was calculated using Equation (1). (1) Qt=kHt1/2

Here, Q t represents the cumulative amount of drug released at time t; k H denotes the Higuchi release constant. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model is particularly used to describe early‐stage release behavior in polymeric systems. Calculations were performed using Equation (2). (2) QtQ∞=kKPtn

Here, Q∞ represents the total amount released at infinite time, k KP the kinetic constant, and n describes the release mechanism. The Weibull model was applied to describe release profiles and to capture time‐dependent changes in the release rate. The cumulative fractional release F(t) was calculated using the equation given in Equation (3). (3) Ft=1−exp−tλβ

In Equation (3), β is defined as the shape parameter, λ represents the scale parameter. To determine the most appropriate model, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) [21] and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [22] were calculated.

Mechanistic Release Modeling and Simulation

To enable a mechanistic interpretation of the experimental release data and to provide a basis for simulation studies, the release behavior of diltiazem was evaluated using a modeling approach. Mechanistic modeling and simulation studies were carried out using the SimBiology Model Builder tool in the MATLAB software. Cumulative amounts of released drug (mg) obtained from the experimental release studies were used as input data for the model.

The model structure was defined as two compartments representing the transfer of diltiazem from the capsule matrix to the dissolution medium. The first compartment represents the polymeric matrix containing the encapsulated drug, initialized at Q 0 = 19.31 mg based on the measured encapsulation efficiency. The second compartment represents the dissolution medium, initialized at zero. Drug transfer between compartments was modeled under first‐order kinetics, governed by the rate constant k release. This framework was selected for its parsimony and interpretability. While more complex mechanisms such as polymer swelling, erosion, and anomalous diffusion may contribute to the observed release behavior, the first‐order model provided a systematic basis for parameter estimation and scenario transfer across different physiological environments.

Model parameters were separately estimated using experimental release data obtained in water and simulated saliva, based on a mass‐action reaction scheme. Parameter estimation and model fitting were performed through a regression‐based optimization process implemented in the SimBiology. During model simulations, parameters were iteratively adjusted to minimize the discrepancy between experimental release profiles and simulated outputs. As a result, medium‐specific parameter sets representing the release behavior in both water and simulated saliva were obtained. During mechanistic modeling, EDS results were considered only as qualitative supportive information regarding capsule composition and drug presence.

Internal Model Validation and Experimental–Simulation Comparison

Simulation outputs obtained from the mechanistic model were compared with the experimental release data obtained in distilled water and simulated saliva. Comparisons were performed at identical sampling time points for both media, and experimentally measured cumulative amounts of released drug (mg) were compared with the values predicted by the model. To quantitatively evaluate model performance, the difference between experimental and simulated data was calculated using the coefficient of determination (R 2) and the root mean square error (RMSE) parameters. In addition to R 2 and RMSE metrics, model adequacy was further evaluated using Visual Predictive Check (VPC). For VPC, N = 200 virtual release profiles were generated by sampling Weibull model parameters from log‐normal distributions with a coefficient of variation of 10% for Q max and λ, and 5% for β. The 5%, 50%, and 95% percentiles of simulated profiles were computed and compared against observed data points to assess the predictive performance of the model at the population level. It should be noted that the validation performed in this study constitutes an internal validation, as the same dataset used for model development was also employed for VPC evaluation. The use of independent external datasets for validation is recommended in future studies.

Sublingual Saliva pH Variability Scenario and Population Analysis

To evaluate the influence of physiological saliva pH variability on diltiazem release from sublingual microcapsules, a population level simulation was performed using the validated Weibull model. Saliva pH in the sublingual environment varies among individuals within the range of 6.2–7.4 under normal physiological conditions. Since diltiazem is a weak base with a pKa of 7.7, its unionized fraction is influenced by local pH according to the Henderson‐Hasselbalch equation.

For each of N = 200 virtual individuals, a saliva pH value was randomly sampled from a uniform distribution between 6.2 and 7.4. The unionized fraction at each pH was calculated as 1/(1 + 10(pH−pKa)), and the ratio relative to the reference pH (6.8) was used to scale the Q max and λ parameters of the Weibull model. Additional log‐normal biological variability was incorporated (CV = 8% for Q max and λ, CV = 4% for β). Cumulative release at 720 min was selected as the primary output parameter. Population‐level simulations were implemented in MATLAB using a fixed random seed (rng = 42) to ensure reproducibility.

No unexpected or significant hazards were encountered during the experimental procedures. Acidic, basic, and oxidative reagents were handled in accordance with institutional safety guidelines and all experiments were conducted using appropriate personal protective equipment, including laboratory coats, gloves, and safety glasses.

Results and Discussion

Experimental Release Data in Different Media

Experimental release studies of diltiazem loaded microcapsules were conducted in distilled water and simulated saliva media. In distilled water, cumulative release reached a plateau of approximately 17.31 mg at 720 min, corresponding to 89.6% of the encapsulated drug amount. In simulated saliva, cumulative release was slower, reaching 13.77 mg at 720 min (71.3% of encapsulated amount), reflecting the influence of saliva viscosity and ionic composition on drug diffusion. The resulting time release data were used as input for kinetic modeling and simulation studies, and the raw experimental data are provided in Table S1.

As shown in Table 1, for the distilled water medium, the Weibull model provided the highest R 2 value (0.989) along with the lowest AIC (−9.49) and BIC (−8.03) values, and the lowest RMSE (0.524 mg). These findings indicate that the release behavior cannot be explained by a single diffusion controlled mechanism and that time dependent changes in the release rate are involved. The lower goodness‐of‐fit obtained with the Higuchi model further supports that the release process is not governed solely by diffusion. The n value of 0.273 obtained from the Korsmeyer–Peppas model suggests a diffusion dominated release mechanism in the early stage, accompanied by limited polymer relaxation [19].

Notably, the Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas models yielded identical R 2 values (0.9628) in simulated saliva, suggesting that diffusion‐based models alone cannot adequately distinguish the release mechanism in this medium. In the simulated saliva medium, the Weibull model again yielded the best performance, exhibiting the highest R 2 value (0.994), lowest AIC (−19.36) and BIC (−17.90), and lowest RMSE (0.348 mg). The n value of 0.601 calculated from the Korsmeyer–Peppas model indicates the presence of a non‐Fickian transport related release behavior [19, 27]. This difference suggests that the viscosity, ionic composition, and biorelevant components of the simulated saliva medium influence the release kinetics.

The consistent identification of the Weibull model as the best model based on AIC and BIC values for both media demonstrates that this model is the most appropriate approach for describing the release behavior of diltiazem loaded microcapsules. Accordingly, Weibull model outcomes were used as reference parameters for the mechanistic modeling and simulation studies.

The estimated Weibull parameters were as follows: for distilled water, Q max = 17.15 mg, λ = 65.53, β = 0.674; for simulated saliva, Q max = 14.17 mg, λ = 203.10, β = 0.930. A comparative visualization of all three kinetic model fits against experimental data is provided in Figure S1. The k release values obtained from the two‐compartment SimBiology model were 0.01112 min−1 for distilled water and 0.00263 min−1 for simulated saliva, confirming that drug transfer from the capsule matrix to the dissolution medium proceeds significantly more slowly in the saliva environment.

Experimental–Simulated Agreement of Release Profiles

To evaluate the extent to which the mechanistic model agrees with the experimental data, experimental cumulative release profiles obtained in water and saliva media were compared with the simulation outputs generated by the model.

The agreement between experimental and simulated cumulative release data was assessed using the root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R 2). For the distilled water medium, the calculated RMSE and R 2 values were 0.524 mg and 0.989, respectively; for the simulated saliva medium these values were 0.348 mg and 0.994, respectively (Table 2).

Sublingual Saliva pH Variability Scenario

Population based simulations performed under physiologically relevant saliva pH conditions (6.2–7.4) demonstrated that inter‐individual pH variability shows a meaningful influence on cumulative diltiazem release from sublingual microcapsules. At 720 min, the median cumulative release across N = 200 virtual individuals was 13.45 mg (mean ± SD: 13.32 ± 1.83 mg), with the 5%–95% percentiles corresponding to 10.05 mg and 16.05 mg, respectively.

pH group revealed a consistent inverse relationship between saliva pH and cumulative release, individuals in the pH 6.2–6.5 group showed a median release of 14.76 mg (n = 56), the pH 6.5–7.0 group showed 13.66 mg (n = 80), and the pH 7.0–7.4 group showed 11.58 mg (n = 64). This pattern is consistent with the Henderson‐Hasselbalch relationship for diltiazem (pKa = 7.7) at lower pH values; the unionized fraction of the drug is higher, facilitating greater membrane permeation and release.

These findings suggest that saliva pH represents a physiologically relevant source of interindividual variability in sublingual diltiazem delivery [28, 29, 30, 31]. The first‐order two‐compartment SimBiology model, while representing a simplified mechanistic framework, provided a systematic basis for parameter estimation. It is acknowledged that microcapsule release may involve additional mechanisms such as polymer swelling, matrix erosion, and anomalous diffusion. In contrast, the adopted model offered sufficient predictive capacity for the purposes of this study, as confirmed by VPC analysis. Furthermore, it is acknowledged that the VPC‐based validation performed in this study is internal, utilizing the same experimental dataset used for model development. Independent external validation datasets were not available within the scope of this study. Future studies should incorporate multi‐mechanism models to more accurately capture the complex release behavior of natural polymer microcapsules.

Conclusion

The Weibull model provided the best fit in both media (R 2 = 0.989 in water, R 2 = 0.994 in saliva), and VPC validation confirmed adequate predictive performance. Extension to a sublingual pH variability scenario demonstrated meaningful variation in cumulative diltiazem release, with median release ranging from 11.58 mg at higher pH to 14.76 mg at lower pH values. This variability is mechanistically linked to the pH‐dependent unionized fraction of diltiazem (pKa = 7.7) and represents a relevant source of interindividual differences in sublingual drug delivery. In this context, the presented approach indicates that quantitatively obtained chromatographic data may serve as the building blocks of predictions related to drug release behavior. Through mechanistic models in which experimental data are used as input, it becomes possible to more rationally predict the release kinetics and formulation performance of different drugs and carrier systems under physiologically variable conditions.
