Impact of inflammatory bowel disease on perioperative complications in patients undergoing hysterectomy
Authors: Haihua Liu, Lihui Chen, Nanfeng Huang, Zifeng Wang, Hao Xie, Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe global prevalence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is escalating rapidly, with its associated systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation potentially compromising surgical outcomes. Nevertheless, IBD’s influence on perioperative complications following hysterectomy remains unreported. This investigation sought to examine the relationship between IBD and perioperative complications in hysterectomy patients utilizing the National Inpatient Sample database while analyzing associated risk factors.MethodsData for patients who underwent hysterectomy between 2016 and 2022 were obtained from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes. Patients younger than 18 years were excluded. Patients were stratified according to the presence or absence of IBD, and demographic characteristics, hospital characteristics, comorbidities, and perioperative outcomes were compared between groups. Statistical analyses included Pearson’s χ² test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 187,401 hysterectomy patients, 853 (0.46%) had comorbid IBD. The prevalence of IBD increased from 0.40% in 2016 to 0.51% in 2022. After multivariable adjustment, IBD was associated with higher odds of sepsis (OR = 3.060; 95% CI = 2.095–4.470), shock (OR = 3.031; 95% CI = 1.605–3.722), acute kidney injury (AKI) (OR = 1.643; 95% CI = 1.235–2.184), urinary tract infection (UTI) (OR = 1.882; 95% CI = 1.332–2.659), and in-hospital mortality (OR = 3.827; 95% CI = 1.855–7.893).ConclusionsIBD prevalence among hysterectomy patients increased over time. IBD was associated with higher odds of selected perioperative outcomes, including sepsis, shock, AKI, UTI, and in-hospital mortality. These associations should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective administrative database design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04670-1.Keywords: Hysterectomy, Crohn's disease, Complications, Inflammatory bowel disease, National inpatient sample database, Ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease represents a constellation of chronic inflammatory conditions that progressively impact the gastrointestinal tract, primarily encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis [1, 2]. These disorders exhibit alternating patterns of acute inflammatory episodes and quiescent periods, frequently manifesting with debilitating symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and fatigue [3, 4]. Presently, IBD affects over 6.8 million people globally, with prevalence exceeding 0.3% in Western nations [5, 6]. While IBD incidence has reached a plateau in these regions, it remains a substantial public health burden [6–8].

IBD is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory disease with extraintestinal involvement affecting the immune, circulatory, nervous, and endocrine systems [9–11]. Such widespread inflammatory responses generate pathophysiological alterations that amplify surgical risks and complexities [12, 13]. Numerous IBD therapeutic agents compromise immune function, consequently elevating infection susceptibility—particularly concerning for surgical candidates [7]. Investigations by Tanenbaum et al. establish that IBD precipitates extended hospitalizations, increased healthcare expenditures, and unfavorable surgical outcomes [14], emphasizing the paramount importance of optimizing perioperative care for patients with this substantial comorbidity.

Data regarding perioperative outcomes of hysterectomy in patients with inflammatory bowel disease remain scarce. This research leveraged the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database to evaluate patient demographics, hospital characteristics, hospitalization expenses, length of stay (LOS), in-hospital mortality, comorbidities, and perioperative complications in IBD patients receiving hysterectomy. The objective was to examine the association between IBD and perioperative outcomes after hysterectomy and to provide evidence that may inform perioperative risk assessment in this patient population.

Methods

Data source

This investigation employed data from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) database, a Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project component administered by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. NIS constitutes the most comprehensive all-payer hospital admission database in the United States. Through sampling from over 1,000 hospitals, NIS compiles data representing approximately 20% of nationwide annual hospitalizations [15]. Retrieved data included patient demographics (age, sex, race), hospital characteristics (insurance type, admission type, hospital bed size, teaching status, location, region), LOS, economic parameters, and diagnostic plus procedural codes derived from International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10). Institutional review board approval was unnecessary given the utilization of publicly available, de-identified data.

Study population

Following incomplete record exclusion, the final analysis encompassed 187,401 cases. The patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Patient categorization occurred based on IBD comorbidity status (ICD-10 codes: K50/K51). Evaluated patient characteristics included race, age group, insurance type, and comorbidity burden. Hospital characteristics encompassed location, bed size, and teaching status. For assessing IBD’s impact on perioperative outcomes, we examined 13 common hysterectomy-related perioperative complications, including AKI, sepsis, UTI, and shock.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses utilized R version 3.5.3 with appropriate packages for complex survey data analysis accommodating NIS sampling methodology. Descriptive statistics characterized the study population. Patient stratification occurred by IBD status, with inter-group comparisons using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the independent association between IBD status and perioperative complications. In these models, IBD status was defined as the primary independent variable/exposure, and each perioperative complication was modeled separately as a binary dependent variable/outcome. The models were adjusted for age group, race, insurance type, hospital characteristics, admission type, comorbidity burden, and frailty. This cohort was further categorized into two groups (FRAIL and nFRAIL) basedon the ACG frailty-defining diagnoses clusters which included malnutrition, dementia, falls, abnormal weightloss, severe visual impairment, decubitus ulcer, urinaryincontinence, fecal incontinence, social needs support, and difficulty walking. Frailty was identified using the Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups frailty-defining diagnosis indicator. Comorbidity burden was defined as the total number of recorded comorbid conditions and categorized as 0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3 comorbidities (Table S1). Additional stratified analyses were performed according to IBD subtype, including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were reported. P-values were reported descriptively, and p ≤ 0.001 was used as a conservative threshold to highlight more robust associations in this large administrative dataset. Interpretation focused primarily on ORs and 95% CIs.

Results

Incidence of IBD in patients who underwent hysterectomy

During 2016–2022, 187,401 hysterectomy patients were included in the analysis. Overall, 853 patients had IBD, representing a 0.46% incidence (Table 1). IBD incidence among hysterectomy patients rose from 0.40% in 2016 to 0.51% in 2022 (Fig. 2).

Patient demographics

Patients with IBD and those without IBD had similar median ages, although the difference was statistically significant (48.0 (42.0, 62.0) vs. 48.0 (42.0, 60.0) years; P < 0.001). White patients accounted for a higher proportion of the IBD cohort than of the non-IBD cohort (77.5% vs. 54.7%; P < 0.001) (Table 1) (Fig. 3).

Hospital characteristics

Elective admission rates were comparable between patients with and without IBD (84.6% vs. 86.4%; P = 0.143). A higher proportion of patients with IBD were treated at urban teaching hospitals than patients without IBD (82.1% vs. 75.3%; P < 0.001). Regional differences were also observed, with patients with IBD more commonly treated in the Northeast (21.7% vs. 17.5%) and Midwest or North Central regions (25.7% vs. 19.0%), and less commonly treated in the South (36.7% vs. 41.6%) and West (15.9% vs. 21.8%) (P < 0.001) (Table 1) (Fig. 4).

Perioperative complications

In the unadjusted comparison, patients with IBD had higher incidences of several perioperative complications compared with patients without IBD, including sepsis (4.1% vs. 1.0%; P < 0.001), shock (1.2% vs. 0.3%; P < 0.001), AKI (6.9% vs. 3.2%; P < 0.001), UTI (4.3% vs. 1.9%; P < 0.001), postoperative delirium (1.1% vs. 0.3%; P < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (0.9% vs. 0.2%; P < 0.001).

After multivariable adjustment, IBD remained associated with higher odds of sepsis (OR = 3.060; 95% CI = 2.095–4.470; P < 0.001), shock (OR = 3.031; 95% CI = 1.605–3.722; P = 0.001), AKI (OR = 1.643; 95% CI = 1.235–2.184; P = 0.001), UTI (OR = 1.882; 95% CI = 1.332–2.659; P < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (OR = 3.827; 95% CI = 1.855–7.893; P < 0.001). No significant adjusted associations were observed for deep vein thrombosis, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, cardiac arrest, pneumonia, hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, blood transfusion, or postoperative delirium. The adjusted association for acute myocardial infarction was not estimable because there were no events in the IBD group (Table 2).

Stratified analysis by IBD subtype

Stratified analyses were further performed according to IBD subtype, including CD and UC. In the unadjusted comparison, both CD and UC patients showed higher incidences of selected perioperative complications compared with patients without IBD.

After multivariable adjustment, CD was associated with higher odds of sepsis (OR = 3.587; 95% CI = 2.272–5.665; P < 0.001), AKI (OR = 1.939; 95% CI = 1.438–2.728; P < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (OR = 6.170; 95% CI = 2.847–13.368; P < 0.001). In contrast, UC was not associated with any perioperative outcome at the prespecified statistical significance threshold of P < 0.001, although numerically increased odds were observed for sepsis, shock, UTI, and postoperative delirium.

The adjusted associations for acute myocardial infarction were not estimable because there were no events in either the CD or UC group. Similarly, the adjusted association between UC and cardiac arrest was not estimable because no cardiac arrest events occurred in the UC group (Table 3).

Discussion

This national administrative database study found that IBD was associated with higher odds of selected adverse perioperative outcomes among patients undergoing hysterectomy. The main findings were observed for sepsis, shock, AKI, UTI, and in-hospital mortality, which are discussed below.

Acute kidney injury

Prior studies have reported higher rates of postoperative AKI among patients with IBD in other surgical settings [16]. Magruder et al. [17] reported increased AKI risk following primary total hip arthroplasty in patients with IBD, and Yang et al. [16] reported increased AKI incidence among IBD patients undergoing colectomy. In the present study, IBD was associated with higher odds of AKI after multivariable adjustment (OR = 1.643; 95% CI = 1.235–2.184; P = 0.001).

Several factors may contribute to this association, including systemic inflammation, fluid and electrolyte disturbances, anemia, malnutrition, comorbidity burden, and potential exposure to nephrotoxic medications [16, 18, 19]. However, the NIS does not provide laboratory data, baseline renal function, medication exposure, intraoperative blood loss, or perioperative fluid management; therefore, the mechanisms underlying AKI could not be directly evaluated [18, 20].

Sepsis

Several studies have reported an association between IBD and increased susceptibility to infectious complications, including sepsis. Mårild et al. [21] demonstrated 1.66-fold increased sepsis risk during hospitalization in IBD patients, while Yan et al. [22] reported 1.51-fold elevated sepsis risk in cervical fusion surgery. Remily et al. [23] identified 1.43–1.52 times higher sepsis risk following total knee replacement. In our cohort, IBD was associated with higher odds of postoperative sepsis (OR = 3.060; 95% CI = 2.095–4.470; P < 0.001).This association may reflect the systemic inflammatory nature of IBD, immune dysregulation, comorbidity burden, frailty, nutritional impairment, or immunosuppressive medication use [24]. Immune dysregulation in IBD may extend beyond the intestinal mucosa and may contribute to impaired host defense in the perioperative period [12, 13]. Although immunosuppressive therapies such as corticosteroids and biologics may influence infection risk, medication exposure was not available in the NIS database [25–27].

Urinary tract infections

IBD was associated with higher odds of postoperative UTI (OR = 1.882; 95% CI = 1.332–2.659; P < 0.001). This association may be related to chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, comorbidity burden, or urinary tract susceptibility in patients with IBD. The known predisposition to urolithiasis in IBD may also contribute to urinary stasis and infection susceptibility [16, 28, 29]. Clinically, these findings suggest that clinicians may need heightened awareness of postoperative UTI in patients with IBD. Future studies should examine whether UTI risk varies according to IBD disease activity, medication exposure, catheter duration, and perioperative urinary management.

Shock

In our multivariable model, IBD was associated with higher odds of shock (OR = 3.031; 95% CI = 1.605–3.722; P = 0.001). While Naseem et al. [30] reported increased sepsis risk among hospitalized patients with IBD, supporting the broader association between IBD and severe infectious complications. This association may reflect greater perioperative vulnerability among patients with IBD, potentially related to infection, systemic inflammation, anemia, frailty, volume depletion, or comorbidity burden. IBD patients frequently receive glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, or biologics [25], which compromise immune function and enhance postoperative infection susceptibility. The observed association between IBD, sepsis, and shock suggests that infection-related pathways may contribute, although this could not be directly evaluated. These findings support the need for careful perioperative assessment and postoperative hemodynamic monitoring in patients with IBD, particularly in those with additional risk factors.

Mortality

Crude in-hospital mortality was higher among patients with IBD than among those without IBD (0.9% vs. 0.2%; P < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment, IBD remained associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality (OR = 3.827; 95% CI = 1.855–7.893; P < 0.001). The higher mortality observed among patients with IBD may partly reflect baseline vulnerability, including older age, greater comorbidity burden, frailty, and emergency admission. In addition, IBD was associated with higher odds of sepsis and shock, which may partly contribute to worse in-hospital outcomes. These findings suggest that selected patients with IBD may benefit from careful preoperative assessment and multidisciplinary perioperative planning. For elective procedures, optimization of overall clinical status and disease control may be considered when clinically appropriate.

IBD phenotypic heterogeneity

Treating IBD as a single entity may obscure clinically relevant differences between Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis. In stratified analyses, Crohn’s disease was associated with higher odds of sepsis (OR = 3.587; 95% CI = 2.272–5.665; P < 0.001), AKI (OR = 1.939; 95% CI = 1.438–2.728; P < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (OR = 6.170; 95% CI = 2.847–13.368; P < 0.001). In contrast, ulcerative colitis was not associated with any perioperative outcome at the prespecified statistical significance threshold of P < 0.001, although numerically increased odds were observed for selected outcomes. These findings suggest that the perioperative vulnerability observed in the overall IBD cohort may be more pronounced among patients with Crohn’s disease. Potential explanations may include transmural inflammation, penetrating or fistulizing complications, nutritional impairment, prior abdominal disease burden, and greater systemic inflammatory burden.

Comorbidity burden and multidimensional risk stratification

Comorbidity burden and frailty may be important contributors to perioperative vulnerability in patients with IBD [31]. In addition to chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation, diabetes may further increase susceptibility to postoperative complications through impaired immune response, delayed wound healing, metabolic stress, and increased risk of infection and renal injury. These mechanisms may partly explain the higher odds of sepsis, AKI, and in-hospital mortality observed in the IBD cohort [32, 33].

Sarcopenia and malnutrition are also clinically relevant in patients with IBD and may reduce physiological reserve, impair recovery, and increase postoperative risk. Psychiatric comorbidities may further affect perioperative outcomes through reduced functional status, poorer treatment adherence, altered pain perception, and delayed postoperative mobilization [34]. Together, these findings support a multidimensional risk-stratification approach that incorporates IBD status, comorbidity burden, frailty, diabetes, nutritional vulnerability, and psychiatric comorbidity.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to retrospective administrative database analyses. First, reliance on ICD-10-CM codes may introduce coding bias and misclassification, particularly regarding IBD subtype and disease severity. We excluded K52 from the IBD definition to reduce non-IBD inclusion; however, some coding inaccuracy remains unavoidable.

Second, although the multivariable models adjusted for demographic factors, hospital characteristics, admission type, comorbidity burden, and frailty, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The NIS lacks information on important clinical and surgical variables, including IBD disease duration, disease activity, medication exposure, operative approach, operative time, estimated blood loss, intraoperative management, and postoperative care. Therefore, the observed associations may partly reflect unmeasured baseline frailty, surgical complexity, or perioperative management factors.

Third, the absence of laboratory data, validated disease severity indices, nutritional measures, glycemic control, sarcopenia assessment, and psychiatric disease severity limited further risk stratification. In addition, some outcomes were rare, particularly in subtype analyses, which may have resulted in unstable estimates or non-estimable odds ratios.

Finally, the NIS captures only inpatient events during the index hospitalization and does not allow assessment of long-term complications, readmissions, post-discharge mortality, or sustained postoperative outcomes.

Conclusions

Comorbid IBD was associated with higher odds of sepsis, shock, AKI, UTI, and in-hospital mortality after hysterectomy. Patients with IBD may warrant careful perioperative assessment and postoperative monitoring, although these associations should not be interpreted as causal.
