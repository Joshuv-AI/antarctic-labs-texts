Identifying the Best Predictive Biomarker in Pharmacogenomics Through Multiple Comparisons With the Best
Authors: Song Zhai, Judong Shen, Jason C Hsu, Xinping Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSingle gene mutations are increasingly being adopted as clinical biomarkers for the optimal application of various therapeutic areas (such as cancer and cardiovascular disease). A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), the most common type of genetic variation in human populations, can affect the abundance and function of gene products at the molecular level. In practice, a single‐predictor model is usually built using the best single‐SNP (or single‐gene) biomarker with the most significant drug–SNP (or drug–gene) association in randomized clinical trials. Current statistical methods for the best single‐SNP biomarker selection rely on a variety of ranking procedures. However, these existing approaches (i) cannot accurately distinguish drug–SNP interaction effects (i.e., predictive effects) from SNP main effects (i.e., prognostic effects); (ii) do not necessarily yield the true ordering or provide a confidence interval for ranking. In this paper, we propose a novel method called Multiple comparisons with the best using Marginal Means (3M). Specifically, 3M first calculates unbiased estimates of SNPs’ predictive effects using marginal means (i.e., least squares means). To select the best predictive SNP (i.e., SNP with the largest unbiased drug–SNP interaction effect), 3M uses a nonparametric bootstrap method to construct constrained simultaneous confidence intervals under the framework of Multiple Comparisons with the Best. Simulation studies demonstrate that our proposed method is more reliable in distinguishing predictive effects from prognostic effects, and thus more powerful to identify the true best predictive SNP than existing methods. Finally, we applied our method to the IMPROVE‐IT (IMProved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytroin Efficacy International Trial) pharmacogenomics genome‐wide association study (GWAS) data to search for the best predictive SNP across the genome. One single best predictive SNP rs114462013 (STAG1) on chromosome 3 was detected, with the association previously identified in the literature.Keywords: drug–SNP association, least squares means, multiple comparisons with the best, multiple testing, predictive biomarker

Introduction

There is compelling evidence that the likelihood of a patient's response to a drug can be significantly impacted by genetic variations in a single gene. Specifically, a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)—a single‐base inheritable variation at a particular genomic location—can have profound biological consequences (Ding et al. 2018). Such SNPs can alter protein function, drug transport, metabolism, or reception, consequently influencing drug efficacy and patient safety (Collins et al. 2003; Lieberman and Marks 2009).

Single‐SNP biomarkers have been widely adopted for drug response prediction and patient stratification in pharmacogenomics (PGx), particularly in clinical settings involving drug metabolism, hypersensitivity, and treatment efficacy. Their widespread use is largely due to their simplicity, interpretability, and direct clinical applicability, which enable cost‐effective testing and clear therapeutic decision‐making (Relling and Klein 2011; Dunnenberger et al. 2015). Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have incorporated many single‐SNP markers into drug labeling, and clinical guideline bodies like CPIC (Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium) have developed standardized protocols for their use in practice (Caudle et al. 2014; https://www.fda.gov/drugs/science‐and‐research‐drugs/table‐pharmacogenomic‐biomarkers‐drug‐labeling). One widely recognized example of a single‐SNP biomarker used directly in clinical practice is rs1057910(C), a variant in the CYP2C9 gene that defines the CYP2C9*3 allele. This allele leads to reduced enzymatic activity of CYP2C9, which is responsible for metabolizing Warfarin, a commonly prescribed anticoagulant (Steward et al. 1997). Individuals carrying the CYP2C9*3 allele metabolize Warfarin more slowly, resulting in higher drug levels and increased risk of bleeding when standard doses are used. Clinical guidelines and FDA labeling recommend incorporating CYP2C9 genotype, alongside other factors such as VKORC1 and patient characteristics, into personalized Warfarin dosing algorithms. Notably, most early FDA‐approved companion diagnostic (CDx) tests were developed under a linear “one drug, one biomarker” paradigm, linking a specific genetic alteration to a corresponding targeted therapy (Naylor 2010; Khoury and Catenacci, 2015; Jørgensen 2015; Rosenbaum and Weisman 2017; Twomey et al. 2017).

In early‐stage pharmacogenomic discovery, researchers frequently construct single‐predictor models using the most statistically significant SNPs or other genomic features associated with drug response. This approach offers a practical and interpretable means of assessing the predictive value of individual variants and is commonly employed to prioritize candidate biomarkers for downstream validation in experimental or clinical settings (Garnett et al. 2012; Iorio et al. 2016; Seashore‐Ludlow et al. 2015). Because only genotype frequencies (e.g., AA, Aa, aa) are observed, a biologically causal SNP may be statistically indistinguishable from neighboring SNPs in strong linkage disequilibrium (LD). Consequently, multiple linked SNPs, including the causal variant, may show significant associations with drug response and are collectively referred to as predictive SNPs or biomarkers (Bush and Moore 2012).

The predictive effect, often modeled as a genotype‐by‐treatment interaction, quantifies differential treatment outcomes—such as relative risk, odds ratio, hazard ratio, or mean response—between biomarker‐defined subgroups (e.g., AA or Aa vs. aa) (Simon 2010; Oldenhuis et al. 2008). Predictive biomarkers influence the probability of a clinical event occurring in response to a specific treatment and are thus identified through statistically significant interactions between genotype and treatment arm. In contrast, prognostic biomarkers affect disease risk or progression independent of treatment and are identified through the genotype's main effect. While prognostic markers are useful for risk stratification, they do not directly inform therapeutic decisions (Sechidis et al. 2018; Simon 2010). Distinguishing between predictive and prognostic effects is therefore essential for selecting biomarkers that can guide targeted therapy development and precision prescribing.

A notable real‐world example of a predictive single‐SNP biomarker is rs2395029(G), a variant located in the HCP5 gene, which is in strong LD with the HLA‐B*57:01 allele in certain populations, particularly those of European ancestry. HLA‐B*57:01 is associated with a potentially life‐threatening hypersensitivity reaction to the antiretroviral drug Abacavir, and clinical guidelines require direct HLA‐B*57:01 genotyping before prescribing the drug (Mallal et al. 2008). Although rs2395029(G) is not used clinically due to LD variability across populations, it played a pivotal role in early pharmacogenomic discovery by serving as a proxy marker that helped elucidate the genetic basis of Abacavir hypersensitivity. This association facilitated the development of validated HLA‐B*57:01 testing protocols, ultimately enabling safer prescribing. The rs2395029 example illustrates the broader value of single‐SNP biomarkers in early‐stage discovery, particularly when such variants point to functionally important loci with established clinical relevance.

Although many existing methods can be used to identify predictive SNPs, such as multiple testing procedures (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Ding et al. 2018) and machine learning approaches (Tibshirani 1996; Su et al. 2018), few are specifically designed to select the single best predictive SNP. In principle, if predictive effects could be estimated without bias, ranking SNPs would be a natural and straightforward way to identify the most informative biomarker. In multiple testing approaches that rely on marginal models, non‐causal SNPs in LD with a causal SNP can exhibit spurious significance due to correlated genotypes, leading to biased estimates of predictive effects and distorted SNP rankings, even when family‐wise error rate or false discovery rate is controlled (Ding et al. 2018; Bush and Moore 2012). This bias arises because marginal association tests cannot distinguish between true causal effects and statistical associations induced by LD. While machine learning approaches such as LASSO and tree‐based methods are widely used for feature selection and prediction, they often yield biased or unstable estimates of predictive effects, particularly under LD, regularization, and correlated features—making them unreliable for identifying the single best predictive SNP.

In this paper, we investigate the bias that arises when estimating predictive effects of non‐causal SNPs due to LD and finite‐sample variation. We then propose a bias‐reduced estimator for the predictive effect using least squares means (LSmeans) and integrate it with the Multiple Comparisons with the Best (MCB) framework (Hsu 1996) to identify the single best predictive SNP with statistical guarantees. This approach enables simultaneous inference across all candidate SNPs while controlling the probability of incorrect selection.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the proposed methodology and theoretical justifications. Section 3 reports simulation studies evaluating the accuracy and reliability of the approach. Section 4 applies the method to pharmacogenomic data from the IMPROVE‐IT trial. Concluding remarks are given in Section 5.

Based on the constrained MCB simultaneous confidence intervals, we propose to make inferences with lower and upper bounds. As illustrated in Figure 1, the pattern of MCB confidence intervals falls into two scenarios. In the first situation, the lower bound of the sample best SNP is zero; therefore, we claim that the SNP is the causal SNP. In the second scenario, the lower bound of the sample best SNP is smaller than zero. Therefore, we decide there is not enough evidence to claim the sample best SNP is indeed superior to the others, and we make no selection for the single best predictive SNP. Instead, we select a subset of “potential” best predictive SNPs whose upper bounds are larger than zero (recall in the Introduction section, we have indicated that the exact equality is not possible as observed in real life; therefore, there is only one unique true best SNP). SNPs with upper bounds equal to zero are eliminated from consideration. The whole analysis workflow is summarized in Figure S3.

Existing Methods

We investigate the performance of our proposed method, compared to the multiple testing approach (p‐value ranking) and two machine learning approaches (Lasso and interaction trees [IT]). Specifically, the p‐value ranking method ranks all SNPs based on their genotype by treatment interaction test p‐values by testing H0:β=0. The Lasso (Tibshirani 1996) ranks all candidate SNPs based on their magnitudes of estimated interaction coefficients. The IT method (Su et al. 2009) ranks all candidate SNPs based on their variable importance. A brief introduction of these three methods is described in Supporting Information Section S6.

Simulation Study

We generated our genotype data from the 1000 Genomes phase 1 data, which is currently used as the worldwide reference for human genetic variation. The genotype data are accessible from The Center for Statistical Genetics (http://csg.sph.umich.edu/abecasis/MaCH/download). We then downloaded the SNP data for the 379 Caucasians (individuals with European ancestry) and obtained haplotype data for all the SNPs on chromosome 19 (with 387,810 SNPs). For quality control, we kept only SNPs for which the number of subjects in each of AA, Aa, and aa categories within each of the T and C arms was greater than six. This was to ensure that there was a sufficient sample size in each patient subgroup. Moreover, clumping (Marees et al. 2018) was performed to retain only one representative SNP with the strongest statistical evidence (i.e., the smallest treatment‐by‐genotype interaction p‐value from PGx genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics) in each LD region (Privé et al. 2018). Therefore, SNPs that were in LD with this one (with r2>0.5 and window size = 100 kb) were removed. After clumping, there were 5505 SNPs left.

Recall that Equation (1) models how a causal SNP contributes to the clinical outcome with its prognostic and predictive effects. One therapeutic area where treatment responses align with this model is Alzheimer's Disease (AD), where subgroups can be defined by a specific genetic marker. Responses to AD treatments are typically measured by the reduction in ADAS‐cog from baseline. The outcome data from clinical trials for this measure generally follow a normal distribution and are often modeled linearly as a function of the treatment and other predictors (e.g., the subgroup effect as defined by a SNP in our case) (Ding et al. 2018). As indicated in the introduction, we assumed the SNP rs429358 (minor allele C, MAF = 0.155) on the APOE gene was the only causal SNP in our simulation studies (therefore, it is the best predictive SNP). Patients were divided into a marker‐positive group carrying the rs429358 C allele (with CC or CT genotypes) and a marker negative group not carrying the rs429358 C allele (with TT genotype). We included four additional SNPs to create a pool of candidate best predictive SNPs together with rs429358. The best predictive SNP was selected from this candidate pool. The phenotype was generated following Equation (1) using parametric values listed in Table 1 to mimic the real situation of AD. Finally, we randomly assigned half of the patients to the treatment arm and the rest to the control arm. The above simulation procedure was repeated 2000 times for error rate calculation and 500 times for power calculation. IMPROVE‐IT PGx Data

We applied our proposed method to the IMPROVE‐IT (IMProved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytroin Efficacy International Trial) PGx genome‐wide association study. IMPROVE‐IT is a phase 3b, multicenter, double‐blind, randomized study to establish the clinical benefit and safety of Vytorin (Ezetimibe/Simvastatin tablet) versus Simvastatin monotherapy in high‐risk subjects (Cannon et al. 2015). The details of the endpoint, genotyping, genotype QC, and imputation for this GWAS analysis are introduced elsewhere (Zhang et al. 2022). After GWAS QC and SNP imputation, there were 9,407,967 variants and 6502 subjects available for analyses. The subjects were further filtered down to 5661 subjects for the GWAS analyses by excluding subjects who had a cardiovascular event prior to month 1, since a cardiovascular event affected the low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) in a manner that may not be treatment‐related. A total of 5661 European subjects were included for the analysis of the drug response, defined as LDL‐C fold‐change at 1‐month. Specifically, this was computed as logy1−logy0, where y1 is the on‐treatment LDL‐C response and y0 is the baseline LDL‐C. Other covariates were adjusted in the model, including age, gender, prior lipid‐lowering therapy, early Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) trial treatment variable (unenrolled, enrolled receiving test treatment, or enrolled receiving control treatment), high‐risk ACS diagnosis, baseline LDL‐C (logy0) and the top five principal components estimated from the GWAS data for controlling population structure.

Simulation Results: Power Comparisons

When There Is One Single Best Predictive SNP

In this section, we focus on identifying the single one best predictive SNP rs429358. The power is defined as the probability that rs429358 is identified as the single best predictive SNP. We compared the power for different combinations of the sample sizes (n = 100, 379) and the predictive effect sizes (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0) across four different methods (i.e., 3 M, p‐value ranking, Lasso, and IT). Simulation results are summarized in Figure 3A, which shows that our proposed 3M method generally outperforms all other methods. In addition, the p‐value ranking method turns out to have the second best power performance. And the two machine learning–based methods (Lasso and IT) exhibit the lowest power. A possible explanation is that both Lasso and IT estimate predictive effects of SNPs using a joint model, prioritizing the accuracy of drug response prediction over marginal effect size estimates.

When the Noise Is High

Our previous discussions are based on σ=3, a realistic value for AD. In this section, we increase σ to 6 to compare methods under a higher noise level, with respect to identifying SNP rs429358 alone (Figure 3C) and identifying at least one of SNPs rs429358 and rs4420638 (Figure 3D). Even at high noise levels, although all methods suffer reduced power, they retain a consistent pattern of relative performance. That is, our proposed 3M method is still superior to the other methods. Marginal model–based approaches (i.e., 3M, p‐value ranking) generally outperform joint model–based approaches (i.e., Lasso, IT). Furthermore, Lasso demonstrates relatively more robust performance when the predictive effect size is small (i.e., when the signal‐to‐noise ratio is low).

When the Sample Size and/or the Signal‐to‐Noise Ratio Are Large

We further conduct a sensitivity analysis to check the scenario when the power of different methods hits the upper bound (i.e., power = 1) with σ=3. Figure S5 shows that our proposed method hits the bound first (when β1=3 and n = 379), followed by the p‐value ranking approach (when β1=4 and n = 379 or β1=3 and n = 1000). Powers of Lasso and IT can also reach one when we further increase the effect size of β1 and/or the sample size.

When There Are Multiple Causal Predictive SNPs with One Dominating Others

Our theoretical derivation assumes that there is one causal marker to demonstrate the fact that a non‐causal SNP may pick up prognostic and/or predictive effects from the causal SNP due to the LD between them and/or the finite sample size, using the traditional linear regression model without any adjustment. But in the real world, multiple markers may be causal, jointly influencing the drug response. Subsequently, we conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the scenario when there are two causal SNPs. We chose rs429358 and rs7412 within the APOE gene as two causal SNPs. The rs429358 and rs7412 polymorphisms encode for the APOE ε2 and ε4 alleles, respectively. These alleles are risk factors for AD. The combination of rs429358 (CC) and rs7412 (CC) increases the risk of late‐onset AD by 12× and early‐onset AD by 61×, respectively. We further set that the predictive effect size of rs429358 is three times greater than the predictive effect size of rs7412. Power is subsequently defined as the probability of selecting rs429358 as the best predictive SNP. Figure S6 demonstrates that our proposed 3 M method continues to exhibit superior performance compared to other methods.

Application to the IMRPOVE‐IT PGx Data

Our analysis goal is to identify the best predictive SNP; thus, it would be more efficient to apply our proposed method to a group of “candidate” SNPs, instead of all the SNPs on the whole genome. In this study, the “candidate” SNPs were defined as the top 50 SNPs ranked by their Pearson correlations (i.e., between the drug response and the predictive signal) as the marginal measures (Fan and Lv 2008). In clumping, we set the window size to be 100 kb, and the correlation threshold to be 0.71, meaning that we removed SNPs that were close to each other and in LD.

The 3 M method identified rs114462013 (minor allele T, MAF = 0.015, in the gene STAG1) on chromosome 3 as the single best predictive SNP (Figure 4 and Table 2). The minimal distance between rs114462013 and the other SNPs in terms of the predictive effect and its 95% CI was 0.091 (0, 0.173). The association between LDL‐C and rs114462013 is identified by Klimentidis et al. (2020) using UK Biobank data with p‐value = 5.2e‐09. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, both rs112549081 on chromosome 2 (with the largest marginal predictive effect estimate) and rs55954432 on chromosome 13 (with the smallest p‐value) have no published results indicating the association with LDL‐C. Furthermore, although our 3M approach identified rs114462013 as the single best predictive SNP, we noticed that the predictive effect estimates of other SNPs were not smaller than 0 as expected (Table 2). A possible explanation was that the conditional marginal model was not fully adjusted. More specifically, the results indicated that there might exist multiple causal variants in the IMPROVE‐IT PGx study, under which condition the effect of LSmeans adjustment was weak because only one of multiple causal variants was used to adjust the marginal model each time.

Discussion

In this paper, we propose a framework to identify the best predictive SNP in PGx studies for personalized medicine. Compared to the existing approaches, our methods overcome their two main drawbacks: (i) the estimation of predictive effects of SNPs may be biased; (ii) the probability of incorrect inference may not be controlled. Specifically, we proposed to first estimate the predictive effect of each candidate SNP by LS means. The new predictive effect estimator can better distinguish the predictive effect from the prognostic effect of a causal SNP (i.e., without bias). We further select the best predictive SNP in the framework of MCB. We choose the sample best as the best if the MCB constrained lower bound is zero (assuming the predictive effect of the causal SNP is larger than 0); and if the lower bound is smaller than zero, we make no selection. At the same time, we eliminate SNPs that cannot be the best from consideration, based on the MCB constrained upper bounds. The probability of incorrect inferences from these two approaches is well controlled. The proposed 3M approach is generally more powerful than other existing methods, including both multiple testing and machine learning approaches. This is evidenced by our numerical studies, which suggest that our proposed procedure substantially outperforms the simple p‐value ranking method, Lasso, and IT.

Our proposed method has several limitations. First, the 3M approach relies on the assumption specified in Equation (10), which holds when the sample size is large enough. Therefore, it might be appealing to calculate the minimal sample size required in a clinical trial, which requires further efforts. Second, in this study, we assessed the predictive effect only based on the dominant genetic model to highlight our focus on distinguishing prognostic and predictive biomarkers. However, the misspecification of the genetic model may potentially lead to a reduction in power (So and Sham 2011). In other words, a SNP with the largest dominant effect may not be the SNP with the largest recessive or additive effect, whose effective size may be even larger than the dominant one. In this case, the SNP with a nondominant genetic model may provide more predictive information on patient stratification. A useful approach is to create a more robust method by considering all possible effects under different genetic models (i.e., dominant, recessive, and additive models, etc.). This remains one of our future research directions. Moreover, all single SNP detection methods tend to fail when the effective size of each individual SNP is too small. How to handle the polygenic effects of many SNPs in our methods remains another future research direction. In other words, our research focuses on the single‐SNP biomarker identification. Incorporating SNP‐by‐SNP interactions (Lim and Hastie 2015) or multi‐SNP biomarkers (Naulaerts et al. 2017) to improve the prediction accuracy of a single‐predictor model is beyond the scope of this paper. Lastly, in our derivation, we assume that the treatment assignment T and the genotype matrix X are fixed (i.e., all patients have been sampled). In the situation when they are random variables (considering the randomization paradigm of RCTs), the characteristics of the LSmeans estimator warrant further investigation. In addition, we also assume the number of SNPs (i.e., the dimensionality) p is fixed. For the high/ultra‐high dimensional case (i.e., p > n), we propose to conduct a screening step first to reduce the dimensionality p (Chu et al. 2016; Fan and Lv 2008; Zhu et al. 2011; Li et al. 2012). For example, in Chu et al. (2016), they reduced the total of 870,000 SNPs from the dbGaP study to 23 SNPs as predictors for future analysis. Ding et al. (2018) identified 22 SNPs with significant predictive effects from a total of 167,812 SNPs with only one causal SNP. In our case, our feature screening procedure is based on the Pearson correlation (between the drug response and the predictive signal) as the marginal measure. Fan and Lv (2008) showed that such a correlation‐based screening procedure possesses the sure screening property, that is, with probability tending to one, it produces a subset that contains the true underlying causal predictors.

Although we mainly demonstrate our proposed 3M method in the analysis of continuous drug response phenotypes, it can be directly applied to binary (i.e., drug‐induced adverse reactions or other drug safety responses) and survival (i.e., time‐to‐event drug responses) phenotypes by adopting the logistic and Cox regression, respectively. Theoretical derivations showing that the proposed algorithms can still distinguish prognostic and predictive effects for binary or survival responses are another future research topic we can explore. Furthermore, since our goal is to identify the best predictive SNP(s), we recommend first screening for a subset of “candidate SNPs” from millions of SNPs across the whole genome before applying our proposed method. A simple and direct approach is to select the candidate SNPs with the smallest genotype by treatment interaction or predictive p‐values. With the decreasing cost of genotyping and sequencing technologies, we envision that our proposed method would play an important role in identifying the best predictive biomarker for personalized medicine, and applying our proposed method to PGx studies may provide valuable information for drug response prediction and patient stratification, which is valuable in drug discovery and development. While this model still underlies many CDx applications, recent advances have expanded toward multi‐gene panels and tumor‐agnostic biomarkers. 3M method is implemented in the 3M R package, freely available at https://github.com/xpcui/3M.
