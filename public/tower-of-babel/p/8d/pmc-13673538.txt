Unequal economic impacts of climate change via idealized carbon dioxide removal: Insights from deep learning
Authors: Bin Tang, Jianan Wei, Yimin Liu, Bian He, Wen Bao, Yi Yang, Wenguan Wang, Wenting Hu, Anmin Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapidly reducing carbon dioxide (CO2) levels is essential to meeting the Paris Agreement’s temperature targets. Previous assessments of CO2 removal (CDR) have primarily focused on the hysteresis and invertibility of climate change itself, overlooking quantitative analysis of potential economic impacts of climate change via CDR. In this study, we first develop a powerful neural network model, EconClimNet, trained on decades of economic data from 1,554 sub-national regions worldwide and 111 climate indices derived from the fifth-generation ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis (ERA5). Compared to popular machine learning algorithms used in the Earth science community, EconClimNet achieves superior performance in capturing the intricate relationship between climate indices and economic outcomes. On this basis, we apply EconClimNet to the outputs from idealized CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down experiments from phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6). It is found that altering climate trajectories through idealized CDR can yield significant long-term economic benefits globally, with Oceania overall positioned to experience the greatest gains with an increasing trend of about 1.41 [0.73–8.76] million USD year−1. Unequal economic effects also emerge across income levels. Rich countries see more benefits (an increasing trend of about 1.56 [0.31–6.21] million USD year−1) via CDR, mainly contributed by services (an increasing trend of about 0.03 [0.00–0.23] million USD year−1). Our findings underscore the need for governments to foster the innovation, development, and prudent deployment of CDR technologies, as well as to advance global CDR strategies that prioritize vulnerable nations from a climate justice perspective.Keywords: carbon dioxide removal, climate change, deep learning

Introduction

Human-induced greenhouse gas emissions, especially carbon dioxide (CO2), have accelerated global warming at an unprecedented rate, raising serious concerns about future climate,1 ecological,2 and environmental3 crises, as well as social stability.4 To address this, the Paris Agreement calls for limiting global warming to well below 2.0°C, with an aspirational target of 1.5°C above pre-industrial (PI) levels.5 However, achieving this target remains a formidable challenge for nations across the globe.6 In this context, CO2 removal (CDR) techniques are increasingly being considered as part of the climate mitigation portfolio.7,8,9 CDR refers to the artificial extraction of CO2 from the atmosphere and its long-term storage underground, on land, or in the ocean, with the aim of reducing atmospheric CO2 concentrations and mitigating climate change.10 Terrestrial biological approaches include afforestation, improved forest management, agroforestry, soil carbon stabilization, biochar application, peatland restoration, and biomass energy with carbon capture and storage. Chemically based methods involve direct air capture and enhanced weathering, while ocean-based techniques include coastal blue carbon management (e.g., wetland restoration), ocean alkalinity enhancement, ocean fertilization, and artificial upwelling.11 Nevertheless, the implementation of these techniques has the potential to alter climate response, including both average and extreme conditions.11,12,13,14 For example, afforestation, while a recognized CDR approach, may elevate greenhouse gas emissions through associated land-use changes and thus affect climate change.15 Based on model outputs from the CDR Model Intercomparison Project, several studies have investigated the reversibility and hysteresis of global mean surface temperature,16 regional precipitation,17,18 global drought,19 monsoon onset,20 El Niño-Southern Oscillation,21 Hadley circulation,22 and tropical cyclones.23 Since climate change profoundly affects the economy,24,25,26,27 a quantitative assessment of the economic feedback from the climate responses to CDR is crucial for fully understanding the global implications of these techniques. This assessment provides a scientific basis for governments to formulate informed climate adaptation policies.

Climate change impacts economic output and growth across multiple sectors, including agriculture,28,29,30 industry,31,32 infrastructure,33,34 tourism,35 labor productivity,36,37 energy supply,38 global trade,39 and renewable energy sources.40 Recent progress in climate econometrics offers compelling evidence on how daily variations in temperature,26 precipitation,27 and their extremes25 impact sub-national economic growth. These insights help estimate the economic costs of addressing climate change under different emission scenarios through empirical approaches.24,41 Despite their usefulness, these methods face limitations in effectively describing the complex relationship between climate indices and economic output or growth. For instance, projections of future economic impacts derived from the distributed lag model framework assume that the effects of temperature and precipitation variations on the global economy conform to purely linear or quadratic functional forms. Yet, the actual relationship between these climatic variables and the global economy is likely to be far more intricate rather than being restricted to such simple functional specifications, thus leading to complex analytical pipelines and potential biases. There are also some studies documenting the economic and equity outcomes of planning for high or low CDR adoption based on integrated assessment models (IAMs).42,43,44 However, these works typically focus on the costs and implementation-associated benefits of CDR technologies rather than the climate-mediated benefits generated by CDR through its regulatory effects on climate change dynamics.42,43 Therefore, there is a pressing need for a more advanced and powerful approach to better capture these connections and offer a reliable platform for estimating the economic impacts of CDR-driven climate changes.

In the era of big data, deep learning has revolutionized numerous fields by uncovering intricate structures within large datasets45 and has been increasingly applied to atmospheric sciences in recent years.46,47,48,49,50 Compared with traditional empirical economic models,24,26,36 deep learning offers a powerful means of capturing complex, nonlinear relationships among variables, making it a promising tool for more accurately quantifying the economic impacts of climate change. For example, a previous study indicates that a more general and flexible deep learning model can effectively facilitate intelligent management, contributing to energy conservation and enhanced security in energy systems, thereby supporting the realization of net-zero emissions.51 However, there remains a gap in applying deep learning models to quantify the economic feedbacks associated with the climate effects of CDR. Early studies25,26,27 primarily investigated how selected climate indicators (e.g., annual mean temperature, total precipitation, temperature variability, and precipitation anomalies and extremes) affect economic output at the global or national scale. However, these analyses often relied on numerous prior assumptions about long-run climate-economic relationships, leading to complex analytical pipelines and potential biases. Drawing on recent advances in deep learning for climate analysis, we develop a framework to automatically learn intricate climate-economic patterns, including variable interactions, climate-economic linkages, and spatial interdependencies influencing economic output. Unlike prior climate prediction studies46,48,50 that use gridded datasets, our economic data are discrete and unordered. This characteristic motivates our use of a transformer architecture, treating regional economic data as tokens to capture global dependencies among them. In this study, EconClimNet is trained on historical global economic data across 1,554 sub-national regions and 111 climate indices for the past decades, selected based on empirical evidence from previous studies (Table S1). Our model achieves cutting-edge performance, surpassing widely adopted machine learning algorithms45,46,47,48 in the Earth science community (Figure 4). Leveraging this neural network, we aim to provide a more robust and persuasive assessment of the potential economic response to changes in temperature, precipitation, and their extremes under an idealized CDR scenario.

Materials and methods

Data

Historical daily 2-m air temperature and precipitation are obtained from the ERA5 database, spanning 1979 to 2016, with a horizontal resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°. These daily records are used to calculate yearly climate measures (see the climate metrics section), which are then aligned with annual economic data. The economic data focused on macroeconomic output per capita at the sub-national level, and population data at the sub-national level was provided by Wenz et al.52 This dataset encompasses 77 countries and a total of 1,554 sub-national regions. Data periods differ by region, ranging from 1901 to 2016. To optimize the use of demographic information, we employ 2010 census data for each region as the population baseline to calculate total economic output (total economic output = economic output per capita × population).

Our study utilizes daily 2-m air temperature and precipitation from three experiments conducted by six models as part of phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6; see Table S2). The experiments include (1) the PI control (piControl) experiment, which is designed to represent a near-equilibrium state of the climate system under conditions imposed around the year 1850, with a global mean atmospheric CO2 concentration of 284.7 ppm; (2) the 1%CO2 experiment, where the climate system continues from the piControl experiment, with CO2 levels increasing by 1% annually over 140 years, ultimately quadrupling to 1,138.8 ppm while other forcings remain fixed at 1850 levels. This phase is referred to as the ramp-up phase. (3) The 1%CO2-CDR experiment, where the climate system begins from the end of the 1%CO2 experiment, with CO2 concentrations decreasing in reverse compared to the ramp-up phase, while other forcings remain fixed at 1850 levels. This is termed the ramp-down phase. The CO2 concentration trajectories for the 1%CO2 and 1%CO2-CDR experiments are illustrated in Figure 1 (gray lines). The total duration of the 1%CO2 experiment and 1%CO2-CDR experiments in this study is 280 years, with 140 years allocated to both the ramp-up and ramp-down phases. The 1%CO2-CDR and CDR experiments exhibit a high signal-to-noise ratio, thereby facilitating the study of climate responses and impacts resulting from substantial CDR.10

Climate metrics

A total of 111 climate indices, classified into temperature-related and precipitation-related categories (Table S3), are employed as factors influencing economic output, which has been validated by previous studies.27,53,54,55,56,57 Precipitation indices include annual total precipitation, monthly precipitation deviation, wet days, annual extreme precipitation amounts, maximum consecutive precipitation over 1 to 92 days, and maximum lengths of dry and wet spells. Temperature indices include annual mean temperature, day-to-day temperature variability, and annual seasonal temperature difference.

Neural network model

We denote all studied global climate metrics for year y as My, structured as a 2D matrix of size Nr × Nv. Herein, Nr = 1,554 represents the number of distinct geographical regions considered, while Nv = 111 refers to the number of meteorological variables analyzed. The mathematical task is that given the annual climate map My as input, the algorithm is required to generate the economic outputs Ey. Our purpose is to develop a deep neural network f to solve this intricate problem in a data-driven manner.

EconClimNet is grounded in the vision transformer (ViT)59 paradigm, due to its superior ability to learn from long, incomplete sequences and model long-range dependencies among inputs. Given an input of size Nr × Nv, EconClimNet tokenizes the input into patch embeddings F of size Nr × D using a linear layer with GELU (Gaussian error linear unit) activation,60 where D is the hidden dimension of EconClimNet. To maintain permutation invariance among patches, position embeddings are added to patch embeddings to retain positional information. These position embeddings are typically calculated based on the relative position of each patch. Under Earth’s coordinate system, it is evidently feasible to map each region to an absolute position expressed as a pair of latitude and longitude.46 However, the economic connections between regions are sometimes not as tight as their geographical proximity would suggest. There exist hierarchical relationships that constitute an inter- and intra-country economic layout. Thus, we construct a learnable relative positional bias as a tree-like hierarchy to represent the country-region relationship. Specifically, country-level position embeddings across Nc distinct countries are generated using a 1-day sinusoidal-cosinusoidal positional encoding and then mapped to Nr regions via an Nc × Nr relational matrix, formulating region-level position embeddings Epos of size Nc × D.

The self-attention mechanism in ViT facilitates relational reasoning among all input patches to capture long-range dependencies, which is beneficial for weather forecasting46,48 influenced by global atmospheric dynamics and thermodynamics. For a global economic map, however, the situation is a bit different: as the intra-national regional correlations tend to be significantly stronger than those across disparate national boundaries, it is unreasonable to assume equal treatment in all regions. To explore climate-economic relationships in both global and domestic patterns, we introduce a hybrid global-domestic mechanism into the standard encoder layer of ViT, which organizes scattered regions based on political geography into multiple scales. The model architecture incorporates six encoder layers featuring a self-attention mechanism. By default, we employ a dropout rate of 0.1 throughout the model to mitigate overfitting, while the hidden dimension is set to 256, and the count of attention heads is designated as 8. The training objective is to minimize the mean square error between the predicted and actual economic growth rates. The model is implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained on a machine with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU and 24 GB of memory. Testing is carried out on the same machine. We use AdamW61,62 for optimization with a 10−4 weight decay. The model is trained for 1,000 epochs, with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 and a batch size of 4.

To mitigate overfitting, each training epoch employs random sampling of a fixed number of instances from the training dataset until exhaustion, eliminating the influence of temporal patterns. Training, validation, and test sets remain strictly disjoint to prevent information leakage. Additional anti-overfitting measures include incorporating dropout layers63 (rate = 0.1) and LN64 within every transformer layer for stochastic regularization.

Results

Climate recovery due to CDR

Major discrepancies in climate indices during CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down experiments arise from systematic biases in the direct outputs of CMIP6 models. To address this, a bias adjustment method (see materials and methods) is applied to daily precipitation and temperature data to obtain more accurate climate indices with reduced uncertainty. Based on these adjusted outputs, the responses of four representative climate indices during the CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down phases, drawn from the multi-model ensemble (MME) and six individual models, are presented in Figure 1. All four climate indices exhibit asymmetric responses and hysteresis to symmetric CO2 forcing. As CO2 concentrations rise, the MME anomalies for annual mean temperature, wet days, and maximum consecutive 2-day precipitation increase relative to PI levels (Figures 1A, 1C, and 1D), while day-to-day temperature variability decreases (Figure 1B). The extremes of these anomalies occur roughly 20 years after CO2 levels peak. Even when CO2 concentrations return to PI levels, the global mean climate remains notably warmer and wetter, with reduced temperature variability compared to PI conditions. The mechanisms governing hysteresis and reversibility in temperature, precipitation, and their extremes exhibit regional variations. For example, Indian precipitation is associated with El Niño-like sea surface temperature anomaly patterns,12 whereas extreme precipitation changes in China are linked to a southward shift of the westerly jet and alterations in vertical motion patterns.65

The recovery ratio65 is introduced to quantitatively measure the effectiveness of climate change mitigation through CDR. Figure 2 shows that recovery ratios in MME for four climate indices exceed 80% in most global regions. However, there are significant variations in recovery ratios across different climate indices and regions. For instance, annual mean temperature shows a lower recovery ratio in the northern parts of North America and central Eurasia, indicating that these regions would remain warmer even if CO2 concentrations were to return to PI levels (Figure 2A). Precipitation frequency and extreme precipitation in Antarctica exhibit relatively high irreversibility, with smaller recovery rates (Figures 2C and 2D). Results from each model (Figures S1–S4) show nearly all models exhibit spatial distributions highly consistent with the MME, indicating strong agreement. On a continental scale, day-to-day temperature variability exhibits the highest recovery potential compared to the three other climate indices (Figure 2E), suggesting it is the most promising candidate for reversal through CDR. In contrast, annual mean temperature and wet days exhibit lower recovery potential across nearly all continents, indicating that warm and wet conditions are likely to persist even if CO2 concentrations return to PI levels. Overall, none of the four representative climate indicators across most of the world is expected to return to PI levels following CDR. Compared to PI levels, global conditions remain largely warmer and wetter, with more pronounced temperature rises in North America and central Eurasia and greater precipitation over Antarctica, indicating elevated risks of negative impacts on these regions.

Economic feedback uncovered by EconClimNet

Here, we introduce EconClimNet, a novel neural network framework developed to quantify economic feedback mechanisms associated with CDR. EconClimNet (Figure 3) is built upon a transformer encoder architecture66 that is composed of a stack of encoder layers. The model establishes the relationship between annual climate indices, derived from historical temperature and precipitation, and corresponding annual economic outcomes at the sub-national scale, covering the period from 1979 to 2016. Two key mechanisms are incorporated into EconClimNet to integrate economic-geographical insights: (1) a hierarchical bias based on country-region composition is adopted to retain political geography information and (2) a hybrid global-domestic mechanism is designed to learn economic connections to climate indices in both global and domestic contexts.

Overview of EconClimNet

(A) Architecture. Climate indices are decomposed into regional patches and transformed into higher-dimensional embeddings. These embeddings are combined with political geography data to create the input tokens for EconClimNet. A stack of encoder layers with hybrid correlations captures economic dependencies at both global and domestic levels. Economic trends are then obtained by regressing the output tokens.

(B) Encoder layer with a hybrid global-domestic mechanism. Built on the standard transformer encoding pipeline, the attention mechanism is enhanced with geographic knowledge using well-designed attention masks.

We assess EconClimNet’s performance by comparing it with several machine learning algorithms, including decision tree,67 random forest,68 gradient boosting machine,69 multi-layer perceptron,70,71 and vanilla ViT48,72 (Figure 4). For a fair comparison, all models are trained on pairs of climate observations and economic data collected over a 30-year period from 1979 to 2008. Hyperparameters for each model are then optimized using a validation set spanning 2009 to 2013, and performances are evaluated on a test set from 2014 to 2016. Other popular alternatives47,62,73 fail to converge in our experimental setup and are thus excluded from this analysis. Further details can be seen in Tables S4–S7. EconClimNet consistently outperforms other methods (Figure 4), achieving significantly better scores on both sets. For example, the R2 values of EconClimNet in the test set for all sectors, agriculture, manufacturing, and services are 0.77, 0.49, 0.73, and 0.71, respectively, which are always higher than those of decision tree, random forest, gradient boosting machine, multi-layer perceptron, and vanilla ViT (Tables S2–S5). These results demonstrate EconClimNet’s superior ability to capture complex climate-economic relationships, making it a valuable tool for analyzing the economic impacts of climate variability. After training, EconClimNet is applied to the MMEs of bias-adjusted climate indices derived from idealized CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down experiments across six CMIP6 models. Note that our goal is not to provide a direct projection of economic outputs under CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down scenarios. Instead, we leverage EconClimNet to assess the global economic response to climate change driven by idealized CO2 ramp-ups and ramp-downs at the sub-national scale.

To further investigate climate-economy feedbacks, we conduct the attribution analysis (Figure 5). Results indicate that precipitation-related indices dominate the top predictors for both total and sectoral economic outputs. Specifically, maximum precipitation for 4 consecutive days (Rx4day) emerges as a key indicator for total output, agriculture, and manufacturing, while the longest duration of wet days (CWD) ranks highest for services. These findings underline the influence of short-term extreme precipitation on agriculture and manufacturing and the role of persistent precipitation in service-related activities.

During the ramp-up stage, precipitation and temperature show notable shifts in mean state, extremes, and variability (Figure 1). Concurrently, global economic output shows an overall downward trend (Figure 6A). This suggests that rising CO2 emissions will hinder long-term global economic development by contributing to climate change. Conversely, the ramp-down stage reveals a positive trend in economic output changes across almost all regions globally (Figure 6B), indicating that economic development is prompted by climate change associated with CDR efforts. This provides evidence that CDR deployment benefits long-term global economic development, which plays a key role in guiding adaptation strategies aimed at mitigating CO2 emissions.

Regional trends in economic output during ramp-up and ramp-down stages for the MME are further averaged across six continents: Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania, and South America (Figure 6C). Oceania shows the steepest negative trend during the ramp-up stage, indicating substantial economic vulnerability to rising CO2 levels. Conversely, it benefits most from CDR during the ramp-down phase, with an increase of 1.41 [0.73–8.36] million USD year−1. At the continental scale, only Asia and Europe exhibit long-term median benefits from CDR that match or exceed the economic losses experienced during the ramp-up stage. These findings suggest that while CDR is expected to contribute positively to global economic development, the magnitude of its benefits will differ across continents, with Oceania poised to gain the most.

Divergent economic feedback by income and sector

To clarify how climate indices affect economic outputs during both ramp-up and ramp-down phases, we further assess their impacts across sectors and nations, categorized by income levels—rich and poor (Figure 7). Countries are classified as rich or poor based on whether their per-capita macroeconomic output is above or below the median. During the ramp-up phase, both rich and poor nations generally experience a negative economic response, with the effect being more pronounced in rich nations, with an average trend of about −1.57 [–6.51 to −0.21] million USD year−1. Across all sectors in rich nations, economic activity declines in response to climate change induced by the CO2 increase. In contrast, manufacturing in poor nations exhibits a slight positive response, though this is outweighed by negative impacts in agriculture and the services, resulting in an overall decline in economic output. The modest resilience of manufacturing in poor nations may reflect better adaptation to extreme conditions compared to rich nations.

During the ramp-down phase, economic output improves in both rich and poor nations in response to climate change induced by CDR, with gains being more substantial in rich countries, with an increasing trend of approximately 1.56 [0.31–6.21] million USD year−1 (Figure 7B). CDR efforts yield the greatest benefits in services (about 0.03 [0.00–0.23] million USD year−1), with negligible impact on agriculture. This underscores that the economic advantages of CDR are primarily driven by benefits in the services sectors. The service sector’s disproportionate gains from CDR likely reflect both differential climate sensitivities across sectors and structural differences in national economies. Services are empirically shown to respond strongly to precipitation and tend to account for a larger share of GDP in high-income countries while remaining limited in low-income contexts.27 Additional factors, such as variation in infrastructure resilience and data availability, may compound these effects. Agriculture in rich nations shows a negative response to CDR, most likely due to a more pronounced temperature drop following the reduction in CO2 concentration compared to poor nations (Figure S5). This cooling effect may elevate the risk of extreme cold events, such as frost and cold fronts, thereby negatively affecting agricultural yields.

Discussion

Efforts to reduce global emissions are struggling to meet the temperature targets set forth by the Paris Agreement,74 emphasizing the critical need for CDR strategies. Quantifying the economic feedback from climate responses to CDR is an essential step for advanced assessment prior to their implementation. Unlike previous studies24,25 that relied on empirical economic models, we first develop a novel neural network, EconClimNet, to capture the intricate relationship between climate indices and economic outputs. Our model achieves superior performance, outperforming widely utilized machine learning algorithms in the Earth science community. Building on this, we apply EconClimNet to bias-corrected outputs from idealized CO2 ramp-up and ramp-down experiments derived from CMIP6 models. Our findings highlight that global economic development is prompted by efforts to mitigate climate change through CDR, with Oceania reaping greater overall economic benefits (with an increasing trend of about 1.41 [0.73–8.36] million USD year−1) than other continents. Inequality is also reflected in the economic feedback across income levels and sectors when CDR is implemented. Economic gains are larger in rich countries, with CDR efforts delivering greater benefits to services but having a relatively minor impact on agriculture.

Continued delays in emission reductions make it highly likely that global warming will exceed 1.5°C, and potentially even 2°C, along the current emissions trajectory.74 If efforts to bring global mean temperatures back to safe levels are only implemented after surpassing these thresholds, strong feedback within the Earth system could trigger further climate risks with substantial uncertainty.75 Given the asymmetric response and lag effects of the climate system to symmetric CO2 forcing (Figure 1), preventive CDR should be incorporated into pathway design. Our research indicates that shifting the trajectory of climate change through CDR could deliver considerable long-term economic benefits. This insight encourages governments to accelerate innovation, fast-track implementation, and maintain momentum in advancing CDR technologies and practices. Nevertheless, the practical implementation of CDR strategies must consider feasibility constraints, the cost of CDR technologies, and associated energy penalties. We acknowledge that with current technological capabilities, removing such a substantial amount of CO2 within the experimental time frame using CDR methods indeed presents considerable technical and operational challenges. However, with ongoing advances in materials science and energy technology, the global scale of CDR deployment is expected to increase by orders of magnitude and therefore may gradually approach the levels assumed in this study.76 Thus, the conclusions of this paper are not based on the premise that “current technology can achieve CDR at this scale,” nor do they represent a near-term implementation roadmap. Instead, this work provides key scientific evidence to inform the long-term innovation and policy planning of CDR technology. Besides, the framework maintains historical climate-economy linkages, suggesting that some of the observed economic improvements under mitigation may partly result from model artifacts. Addressing this limitation will require broader multi-model experimental designs beyond the current scope of CMIP6. Given the disproportionate economic gains from CDR observed in high-income versus low-income nations, global deployment strategies must explicitly embed equity considerations to prioritize support for under-resourced regions—aligning with climate justice imperatives.77 Climate finance offers a key mechanism for redistributing capital to vulnerable areas by combining public and private resources through instruments such as carbon markets, concessional lending, and fiscal transfers. These funds can support renewable energy transitions and ecosystem resilience, helping to reduce disparities in CDR outcomes. Crucially, geographically targeted deployment is essential. For example, regional hotspots with high biochar CDR potential relative to national emissions have been identified in South America, northwestern Africa, and eastern Europe, offering promising avenues for equitable investment.78 Moreover, the implementation of CDR strategies may accelerate industrial transformation—supporting the emergence of new sectors, enhancing energy security, attracting green capital, mitigating long-term climate risks such as extreme weather and resource scarcity, and ultimately promoting sustainable growth. However, these long-term benefits may be offset by short-term disruptions, including job losses in carbon-intensive industries and substantial investment requirements. These trade-offs underscore the ethical complexity of CDR deployment. Achieving global climate goals and ensuring an equitable, sustainable future therefore demands an integrated approach—combining technological innovation, sound policy design, and economic planning—while placing fairness at the center of decision-making.79,80 In addition, although EconClimNet effectively models complex, nonlinear relationships among heterogeneous variables and handles high-dimensional data and parameter uncertainty, it currently lacks strong interpretability and causal inference. Enhancing the interpretability of the model will be a key focus for future work. Finally, the reliability of the results depends on the available economic and meteorological data. More comprehensive and accessible datasets in the future would help to optimize the findings.
