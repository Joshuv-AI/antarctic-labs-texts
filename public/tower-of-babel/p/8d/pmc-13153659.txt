Trigger-Negative Takotsubo Syndrome
Authors: Kazufumi Nakamura, Kazuna Hayashi, Hironobu Toda
Published: 2026

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is increasingly recognized as a diverse clinical entity rather than a single, homogeneous disorder. Initially described as a transient and relatively benign cardiomyopathy triggered by psychological stress, subsequent research has revealed a more complex clinical spectrum, including physical stressors and serious complications.1–3 Among these unresolved issues, the recurrence of TTS remains one of the most clinically significant yet poorly understood aspects of the syndrome.

In this issue of the Journal, Nishimura et al. provide valuable observational data on the incidence and clinical characteristics of recurrent TTS.4 By analyzing 236 consecutive patients hospitalized over a 16-year period, they report a recurrence rate of 5.5%, which is generally consistent with previous registry-based studies.5 More importantly, they identified the absence of a clear trigger at the time of initial TTS as a strong and independent predictor of recurrence. This observation provides an important opportunity to reconsider how TTS is conceptualized and how recurrence risk is stratified in clinical practice.

Absence of a Trigger: A Key Clue to Recurrence

The most striking finding of this study is the strong association between recurrence and the absence of a clear trigger at the initial presentation of TTS. In the multivariate analysis, the absence of an identifiable emotional or physical trigger was associated with more than a 15-fold increased odds of recurrence. Importantly, similar findings have been reported previously, demonstrating that the absence of an identifiable trigger is independently associated with an increased risk of recurrent TTS.6 This observation challenges the traditional stress-centered view of TTS and warrants careful consideration.

In this editorial, we refer to such cases as “trigger-negative TTS”, a potentially distinct phenotype of TTS, which we define as TTS occurring without an identifiable emotional or physical trigger at the time of initial onset. This term does not imply that no trigger exists; rather, it indicates that no obvious or acute stressor can be identified through standard clinical evaluation. Importantly, this distinction may carry prognostic significance with respect to recurrence.

One possible interpretation is that trigger-negative TTS reflects an underlying vulnerability that predisposes certain patients to recurrent episodes, independent of external stressors. Such vulnerability may involve chronic autonomic dysfunction, increased myocardial sensitivity to catecholamines, impaired central autonomic regulation, or persistent microvascular dysfunction. In contrast, trigger-positive TTS, particularly the cases associated with acute physical stress such as infection or surgery, may represent a more situation-dependent and self-limited response that is less likely to recur once the precipitating factor has resolved (Table). Phenotypic Differences Beyond Triggers

The authors further demonstrate that patients in the recurrent group exhibited distinct clinical characteristics at the time of their initial TTS episode. Chest pain and dyspnea were more frequent, but ECG changes were less common, compared with patients without recurrence.

The lower prevalence of ECG abnormalities in recurrent cases may suggest differences in the pattern or extent of myocardial stunning, regional sympathetic activation, or electrical remodeling. Alternatively, it may reflect more subtle myocardial injury that nonetheless confers increased susceptibility to subsequent episodes. These hypotheses warrant further investigation using advanced imaging modalities, such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and myocardial strain analysis.

Consistency of Ballooning Patterns: Evidence of Individual Vulnerability?

Another noteworthy observation was the remarkable consistency of the left ventricular ballooning patterns between initial and recurrent TTS episodes. In 12 of 13 patients who experienced recurrence, the ballooning patterns at recurrence were highly similar to those observed during initial presentation. This finding supports the notion that TTS is not a random or stochastic phenomenon, but rather reflects patient-specific myocardial vulnerability.

The reproducibility of the ballooning patterns suggests that certain myocardial regions within individual patients may be inherently more susceptible to stress-induced dysfunction. Potential mechanisms include regional differences in sympathetic innervation, adrenergic receptor density, or microvascular reactivity.3 From a broader perspective, this observation aligns with the emerging view that TTS may involve an individualized “cardiac vulnerability map”, shaped by both biological and environmental factors.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

This study should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Its greatest contribution is to clarify the prognostic relevance of trigger status and propose a clinically intuitive framework for understanding recurrence risk. Future multicenter studies with standardized trigger assessments, longer follow-up, and mechanistic evaluations will be required to validate and further refine these findings.

Conclusions

This study provides important insights into the recurrence of TTS and highlights the heterogeneity of this disorder. The identification of trigger-negative TTS as a phenotype prone to recurrence supports a shift from a stress-only paradigm toward a more nuanced, phenotype-oriented understanding of the syndrome. Although many questions remain unanswered, this study represents a meaningful step toward improving individual risk stratification and the long-term management of patients with TTS.
