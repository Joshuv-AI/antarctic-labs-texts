Ability of detection in different resolution endoscopy for upper gastrointestinal mucosal lesions
Authors: Xiaoliang Jin, Qiujun Zhou, Bin Lyu, Chunli Zhang, Liang Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost endoscopists believe that higher resolution improves lesion detection rates. However, existing studies primarily compared the detection rates of white light endoscopy (WLE) and other imaging modalities. Our previous study demonstrated the advantages of magnifying endoscopy from general endoscopy for lesion detection, prompting further investigation into the variations in lesion detection rates across endoscopes with different resolutions.MethodsEndoscopic and corresponding pathological data from our medical unit over the past 5 years were analyzed. We excluded specific-purpose endoscopic procedures to ensure the natural randomization of the data. Baseline adjustment and risk factor analyses used multi-group propensity score matching and logistic regression.ResultsThe overall lesion detection rate was significantly higher with high-quality endoscopy (Q-endoscopy) compared to high-definition endoscopy (H-endoscopy) and high definition and quality endoscopy (HQ-endoscopy) (34.4% vs. 30.2% vs. 29.6%, P = 0.001). Similar results were observed for elevated lesions (25.7% vs. 21.0% vs. 22.9%, P = 0.001) and depressed lesions (6.6% vs. 6.2% vs. 3.6%, P < 0.001). HQ-endoscopy had a superior detection rate for superficial lesions compared to both H- and Q-endoscopies (3.0% vs. 2.8% vs. 1.8%, P = 0.041). However, there were no significant differences in neoplastic detection rate or missed neoplastic lesion rate among the three groups.ConclusionQ-endoscopy is superior in detecting non-superficial lesions, while HQ-endoscopy is better at detecting superficial lesions. However, there were no statistically significant differences in detecting or omitting neoplastic lesions among the three endoscopic examinations.Keywords: White light endoscopy, High-definition endoscopy, Upper gastrointestinal lesion, Neoplastic lesion

The upper gastrointestinal cancer primarily encompasses esophageal and gastric cancers, which pose a significant global burden. According to the Global Burden of Cancer Report, 1,089,103 new cases of gastric cancer and 604,100 new cases of esophageal cancer were reported worldwide in 2020. The report also documented 768,793 deaths attributed to gastric cancer and 544,076 deaths related to esophageal cancer [1]. The cancer burden is more pronounced in East Asia. A recent study conducted in China revealed approximately 33.08 cancer-related fatalities per 100,000 individuals [2]. Despite remarkable achievements in early detection through screening, it is imperative to emphasize the significance of endoscopic screening of upper gastrointestinal cancers.

Endoscopy plays a crucial role in screening and diagnosing upper gastrointestinal malignancies. Endoscopists assess the gastric mucosa and identify lesions based on information gathered by the endoscope. Various techniques are now available to aid in this decision-making process, including magnifying endoscopy with narrowband imaging (M-NBI), composite band imaging (CBI), and linked color imaging (LCI) [3–5]. However, white light endoscopy (WLE) remains the primary source of visual information for endoscopists.

Technically, the fundamental criterion for evaluating endoscopy is the acquisition of comprehensive mucosal information. Endoscopy assessment aims to obtain additional information from the endoscopic field of view. Our previous investigation demonstrated that magnifying endoscopy exhibits superior screening and diagnostic efficacy compared with conventional endoscopy [6]. This is due to its ability to capture more mucosal information within an identical endoscopic field of view.

During the process of acquiring mucosal information using endoscopy, variations in resolution and image quality lead to disparities in image sharpness. While many endoscopists believe that higher resolution and clearer images are more beneficial for lesion identification and diagnosis, there is insufficient clinical evidence to support this common view. In this study, we compared the efficacy of three types of endoscopes with varying resolutions and image qualities in detecting diverse morphological lesions, aiming to provide empirical support for a long-standing clinical perspective.

Materials and methods

Participants

All Endoscopic and pathological biopsy data were collected from patients who underwent gastroscopy at the Endoscopy Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University between January 2018 and December 2022. Exclusion criteria included (a) history of esophagectomy or gastrectomy, (b) age less than 18 years, (c) inability to tolerate complete gastroscopy and opting out of the study, and (d) cases where gastroscopy was performed for specific purposes, such as endoscopic resection of known lesions [6] or targeted observation in other studies [7]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (2023-KL-084-04).

Gastroscopy examination

The patients underwent gastroscopic examination using endoscopes from Olympus Corporation (Tokyo, Japan), including GIF-Q260, GIF-H260, and GIF-HQ290. According to the supplier’s manual, “Q” stands for high-quality endoscopy, “H” for high-definition endoscopy, and “HQ” for high definition and quality endoscopy.

Exclusion criterion “c” was applied to exclude cases where endoscopy was performed for a specific purpose (known lesions) to ensure endoscopes were randomly assigned by the endoscopic decontamination center. Participants fasted for at least 8 h before the procedure and received pepsinase granules (TIDE PHARMACEUTICAL CO., Beijing) 30 min before the examination to eliminate gastric mucus [8]. Experienced endoscopists conducted all examinations following standardized protocols. Photographs were taken at the throat, cardia, antrum, body, fundus, greater and lesser curvatures, and anterior and posterior walls of the stomach and duodenum.

Biopsy and pathological examination

After imaging, biopsies were obtained from visible lesions. Random biopsies were performed in the antrum if no visible lesions were present. Biopsies of the stomach corpus were conducted at the discretion of the endoscopists based on their assessment of the mucosal background.

The pathological diagnosis of each biopsy sample was made by senior pathologists at our center, who were blinded throughout the study. An independent pathologist confirmed the pathological diagnoses.

Classification and definition

The lesions were categorized into neoplastic and non-neoplastic based on pathological diagnosis. Neoplastic lesions included low-grade neoplasia (LGN), high-grade neoplasia (HGN), and cancer. Non-neoplastic lesions include hyperplastic polyps, fundic gland polyps, and mucosal morphological changes from various etiologies.

Morphological classification of the lesions was determined based on endoscopic findings during biopsy, employing a combination of the Borrmann and Paris endoscopic classifications, including elevated, superficial, depressed, and diffuse types. Superficial lesions were further classified as superficial elevated, superficial flat, and superficial depressed. The term ‘missed lesions’ was employed to describe cases where a neoplastic component was detected through random biopsies.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed data were presented as Mean ± Standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using T test and ANOVA. Skewed distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the rank sum test. Count data are reported as n (%) and analyzed using the R × C chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Multivariate logistic regression was used to examine the contribution of the dependent variable to the outcomes. Multiple-group propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in R using the PM3 package to adjust for baseline variables [9]. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The flowchart of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Participants and lesion detection rate

A total of 30,637 endoscopic examinations were included in the study: 3084 Q-, 17,100 H-, and 10,453 HQ-endoscopic examinations. In total, 8692 lesions were detected in 6342 patients, averaging 1.37 lesions per person. Specifically, 1075 lesions were detected in 718 patients who underwent Q-endoscopy (1.50 lesions per person), 4417 lesions were detected in 3316 patients who underwent H-endoscopy (1.33 lesions per person), and 3200 lesions were detected in 2308 patients who underwent HQ-endoscopy (1.39 lesions per person). Univariate analyses at baseline revealed statistically significant differences in reflux esophagitis and atrophic gastritis among the three groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to eliminate potential confounding factors. PSM analysis demonstrated that the resolution of endoscopy influenced the lesion detection rate, with Q-endoscopy exhibiting a superior overall detection rate compared to H- and HQ-endoscopies (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 34.4% vs. 30.2% vs. 29.6%, P = 0.001). Additionally, a higher proportion of lesions was detected in the upper third of the stomach with Q-endoscopy compared to H- and HQ-endoscopies (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 8.9% vs. 6.9% vs. 7.9%, P = 0.047), as well as in the lower third of the stomach (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 8.7% vs. 8.8% vs. 6.4%, P = 0.005) and duodenum (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 1 0.9% vs. 0 0.8% vs. 0 0.5%, P < 0.001).

Endoscopes with different resolutions exhibit varying capabilities for detecting diverse types of lesions. Q-endoscopy demonstrates superior detection ability compared to H- and HQ-endoscopies for both elevated (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 25.7% vs. 21.0% vs. 22.9%, P = 0.001) and depressed lesions (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 6.6% vs. 6.2% vs. 3.6%, P < 0 0.001). Conversely, for superficial lesions, HQ-endoscopy outperformed H- and Q-endoscopies (HQ vs. H vs. Q: 3.0% vs. 2.8% vs. 1.8%, P = 0.041). Detected lesion characteristics

The characteristics of the 6342 detected lesions and 2041 detected cases were analyzed after PSM (Table 2). The results revealed no significant differences in lesion size among the three groups (P = 0.119, PPSM = 0.339). However, based on different definitions of endoscopies, there were distinct characteristics regarding lesion location and type. Lesions identified by HQ-endoscopy predominantly occurred in the middle and upper regions of the stomach. In contrast, those detected by Q-endoscopy primarily appeared in the lower part of the stomach. Additionally, elevated and superficial types constituted the majority of lesions identified using HQ-endoscopy.

Neoplastic lesions

Neoplastic lesions constitute a significant component of identified lesions and carry a potential risk of cancer progression. Regarding lesion detection rate analysis, no statistically significant difference was observed among different resolution endoscopes for detecting neoplastic lesions (P = 0.690, PPSM = 0.466) (Table 1). Analysis of the proportion of neoplastic lesions among the detected lesions revealed an initial disparity that became non-significant after PSM (Table 2). Considering PSM entails sample size reduction, we conducted multivariate logistic regression for neoplastic lesion detection. The results were consistent with those obtained from PSM analysis, indicating no statistically significant association between different resolution endoscopies and the detection of neoplastic lesions (OR = 1.003, 95% CI 0.862–1.167, P = 0.968) (Table 3).

Detected neoplastic lesions

In this study, 640 neoplastic lesions were pathologically diagnosed, and 441 were detected via endoscopy. Neoplastic lesions were detected in 12 patients using Q-endoscopy, while H- and HQ-endoscopies detected them in 123 and 64 patients, respectively (Table 4). Analysis before and after PSM revealed no significant difference in the overall ability to detect neoplastic lesions among the three groups (P = 0.627). The LGN detection rate was higher with HQ-endoscopy than with H- or Q-endoscopies. No significant differences were observed in the ability to detect different types and locations of neoplastic lesions among the three groups. Before PSM, the superficial neoplastic lesion detection rate of HQ-endoscopy was higher than that of both H- and Q-endoscopies (P = 0.007); this difference became less significant after PSM (P = 0.153).

Missed neoplastic lesions

A total of 199 patients (0.7%) met the defined criteria for missed lesions, comprising 12 cases (0.4%) in the Q-endoscopy group, 123 cases (0.7%) in the H-endoscopy group, and 64 cases (0.6%) in the HQ-endoscopy group. No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups (P = 0.093). The demographic characteristics of the missed lesions did not vary between the groups.

Among the missed lesions, 182 cases were classified as LGN and 17 were classified as HGN. For HGN lesions, subsequent examinations using magnifying endoscopy and endoscopic submucosal dissection revealed superficial lesions with minimal morphological alterations on magnifying endoscopy (Table 5). A total of 182 patients with LGN were followed up until the present time, with no evidence of disease progression.

Non-neoplastic lesions

Most lesions of the upper digestive tract are non-neoplastic, including hyperplastic polyps, fundus glandular polyps, and inflammatory changes. In this study, non-neoplastic lesions accounted for 94.9% of all detected lesions. The detection rate and characteristics of these non-neoplastic lesions are presented in Table 6. Q-endoscopy exhibited a higher detection rate for raised non-neoplastic lesions and those smaller than 2 cm. There was no significant difference in the detection of superficial non-neoplastic lesions among three different resolutions (Q vs. H vs. HQ: 1.6% vs. 2.6% vs. 2.4%, P = 0.052).

Discussion

Endoscopy is an effective method for diagnosing and screening precancerous lesions and early gastric cancer [10–12]. Enhancing the detection rate of precancerous conditions in the upper gastrointestinal tract has long been a shared pursuit among endoscopists. Previous studies have summarized characteristics of neoplastic lesions observed during endoscopy [4], while endoscopic providers developed more advanced imaging modalities, such as NBI endoscopy, autofluorescence endoscopy, and blue light laser imaging [13, 14]. WLE is commonly employed during routine examinations. Identifying lesions through WLE examination holds significant importance. WLE images with higher resolution offer greater image information and aid in lesion identification, although evidence supporting this is lacking.

The quality of the endoscopic images is determined by their resolution. Resolution is the optical ability to distinguish between closely related objects or points [15]. High-resolution endoscopy enables the differentiation of objects spaced more than 10 µm apart, enhancing the discernment of details during endoscopy [16]. In continuous video imaging, resolution depends on pixel density. By utilizing charge-coupled devices (CCD) with high pixel density, a slightly magnified view of the mucosa can be obtained, facilitating a more detailed visualization of mucosal manifestations [17].

Q-endoscopy is equipped with CCD, which is advantageous for image processing. The improvement in the resolution of H-endoscopy is similar to the Q-endoscopy, with a higher image pixel (1080 px). Technically, HQ-endoscopy represents the most advanced type, employing dual-focus counting to collect mucosal information at different distances simultaneously and mitigate the blurring of edge images typical with single-focus methods. Theoretically, high-resolution endoscopy enhances the ability to detect small and flat mucosal lesions. However, our study found that lesion detection rates did not sequentially increase with Q-, H-, and HQ-endoscopies.

In contrast, Q-endoscopy showed significantly higher lesion detection rates compared to H- and HQ-endoscopies, evident for both elevated and depressed lesions. However, HQ-endoscopy exhibited a superior detection rate for superficial lesions compared to Q- and H-endoscopies. These findings highlight that the detection capabilities of endoscopy vary across resolutions and different types of lesions and locations within the gastrointestinal tract. Notably, Q-endoscopy demonstrated a higher lesion detection rate in the upper and lower parts of the stomach and duodenum. After PSM, HQ-endoscopy detected more lesions in the middle region of the stomach. The anatomic structure may be an influencing factor contributing to these differences. For instance, lesions are more likely to be missed in the posterior and anterior walls of the middle stomach body when viewed tangentially [18]. The high resolution provided by endoscopy partially compensates for the limitations of viewing angles. Additionally, this study revealed variations in lesion detection rates across different locations, specifically for non-neoplastic lesions. However, no significant difference was observed in neoplastic lesion detection rates among the three endoscopy groups.

The Paris classification categorizes superficial lesions into three subtypes: superficial elevated, superficial flat, and superficial depressed lesions. These lesions are characterized by relatively smooth mucosal surfaces without significant undulations. Minor changes in these lesions can contribute to missed diagnoses during endoscopy. A study in France revealed that 0.6% of advanced precancerous lesions (LGN and HGN) were not detected endoscopically, with 8% of LGN and 16% of HGN incorrectly classified as normal [19].

Our study found no statistically significant differences in the distribution of subtype classifications or neoplastic lesions among superficial lesions detected by endoscopy at different resolutions.

The detection of LGN and HGN lesions was a key focus of our study due to the risk of cancer development [20]. Multi-center prospective studies have demonstrated that high-definition endoscopy exhibits higher specificity (95%) than NBI endoscopy in detecting neoplastic lesions, although its sensitivity varies significantly from 29 to 74% [21, 22]. Although the European Society of Gastroenterology strongly recommends high-definition endoscopy for managing precancerous gastric lesions, more data are required. Our study found no statistically significant difference in the detection rate of neoplastic lesions among the three different endoscopic resolutions, a result confirmed by logistic regression analysis.

Simultaneously, we identified neoplastic lesions that were missed during endoscopy. There was no statistically significant difference in missed lesions among Q-, H-, and HQ-endoscopies. The missed rate of LGN lesions was higher for both H- and HQ-endoscopies compared to Q-endoscopy. Among all missed neoplastic lesions, 91.5% were classified as LGN and 8.5% as HGN. No further progression of LGN occurred until study completion. Additionally, patients with HGN underwent further magnification endoscopic examinations and endoscopic submucosal dissections.

This natural, non-interventional, real-world study effectively complements the clinical application value of randomized controlled trials. We employed multi-group PSM as a statistical method to ensure baseline stability. However, this study had certain limitations. First, including research subjects from a single center may introduce geographic bias. Second, there may be statistical concerns regarding sample loss in the PSM. Third, this was a retrospective study, some confounding biases may have interfered with the results. Implement prospective study designs are needed in future, to minimize retrospective biases and ensure more rigorous control over data collection and analysis.

Conclusion

Conventional wisdom suggests that higher resolution brings more information to the endoscope, and the ability to detect lesions improves image quality. This single-center, real-world study with realistic randomization of endoscopic assignments provides crucial insights for clinical practice and future research. It highlights that Q-endoscopy outperformed both H- and HQ-endoscopies in detecting non-superficial lesions, while HQ-endoscopy showed superiority in detecting superficial lesions. These findings suggest a reminder for us to be more vigilant regarding the possibility of missing lesions with varying morphology that may be present in endoscopies with different resolutions, potentially improving detection rate in routine gastroscopic examinations.
