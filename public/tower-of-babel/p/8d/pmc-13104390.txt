Developing a national strategy to support implementation of reablement for community-dwelling people living with dementia
Authors: Claire MC O’Connor, Sasha Houlden, Christopher J Poulos, Michele Callisaya, Angela Collins, Kylie Miskovski, Glenys Petrie, John Quinn, Natalie Taylor, Susan Kurrle, Kaarin J Anstey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia reablement is globally recommended to support people living with dementia to maximise their independence. Despite this, dementia reablement is not broadly available and there is currently no consensus around how it should be implemented. This study aimed to develop a national strategy by reaching expert consensus on the barriers, enablers, and potential implementation strategies to promote uptake of evidence-informed dementia reablement.MethodsA modified online Delphi survey process was used to gain national consensus on the determinants and implementation strategies pertaining to implementing community-based dementia reablement in Australia. Purposive sampling and snowballing were used to recruit a range of knowledge and experience-based expert interest holders, including people living with dementia, informal/family carers, allied health professionals, program managers/referrers, policy makers, experts and thought leaders. Using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), round one sought consensus around a range of determinants (i.e. barriers and enablers) to implementing reablement for community dwelling people living with dementia across four domains: (1) the system, (2) service providers, (3) family, and (4) the person living with dementia. Using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) strategies compilation, round two sought consensus on a range of implementation strategies, presented across the same four domains. Across each domain in both survey rounds, participants were invited to provide written comments to supplement their ratings. Consensus for inclusion of each item was defined as ≥ 70% agreement across experts.ResultsFifty participants completed the round one survey, and n = 35 (70%) participants completed round two. In round one, a range of determinants (barriers (n = 11) and enablers (n = 17)) for implementing community-based dementia reablement reached consensus. In round two, n = 28 implementation strategies reached consensus. Taken together, outcomes from this Delphi process highlight four key themes relating to the implementation of dementia reablement: (1) education and promotion, (2) engaging consumers and providing support to carers and people living with dementia, (3) service provider capacity to deliver reablement, and (4) funding, policy, and access to services.ConclusionsEffectively implementing dementia reablement will take a sector-wide approach that involves top-down and bottom-up support, focuses on education and promotion of reablement, and leverages policy and funding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00917-y.Keywords: Dementia, Reablement, Rehabilitation, Determinants, Implementation, Delphi

Background

Dementia prevalence is increasing, with estimates that by 2050, there will be > 152 million people living with dementia worldwide [1]. In Australia, dementia is the leading cause of death; one in 12 people aged ≥ 65 have dementia, and around two in three people with dementia live in the community [2]. While disease modifying therapies for dementia remain in an emergent state, dementia reablement (or rehabilitation) is globally recommended to support people living with dementia to maximise their independence [3–5]. Dementia reablement describes an individualised, goal oriented, multidisciplinary approach, with the aim of maintaining or improving a person’s independence and functional ability in a broad range of areas e.g. cognition, activities of daily living, physical functioning, social interactions, communication, and behaviour [6]. The terminology around this process is still debated. Internationally, the term ‘rehabilitation’ is becoming more common [3–5], whereas in Australia, current policy refers to ‘reablement’ [7]. As this paper describes a study conducted within an Australian context, the term reablement will be used henceforth.

According to Article 26 within the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with a Disability, people with a disability (including people living with dementia) should have early access to comprehensive reablement programs to maximise independence, and ongoing training should be available to health professionals [8]. Australia is a signatory on this convention, and the most recent Australian National Dementia Action Plan [7] promotes that people with dementia should have access to reablement. Despite this, dementia reablement is still not broadly available and there is currently no consensus in Australia around how evidence-informed reablement should be implemented for people with dementia living in the community. We have applied implementation science methodologies to develop a program of research to address this implementation gap [9], and the current paper reports on a key stage of this process.

A recent scoping review [10] including 50 global publications identified a range of barriers and enablers to implementing dementia reablement in community settings that were mapped to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [11]. The review also identified a range of implementation strategies to facilitate dementia reablement, the most common of which were: adapting and tailoring processes and intervention approaches during implementation; working with staff to support communication, implementation planning and intervention training to enhance fidelity; and promoting broad stakeholder engagement (i.e. across family, care support workers, interdisciplinary team members) [10]. While this scoping review provides important international insight, local context (i.e. country-specific community aged care settings) is a vital consideration to promote successful implementation [12, 13]. In a preliminary exploration of the Australian context, a clinical audit and feedback process was undertaken with a small sample (n = 4) of community aged care service providers to explore current practice and identify barriers and enablers around implementing evidence-informed dementia reablement [14]. The audit identified variability around what people with dementia receive when referred for allied health support. Some aspects of the intervention (e.g., goal setting, environmental modifications, exercise prescription) aligned closely with evidence-informed reablement recommendations [15], while other areas (e.g., provision of written support plans, involving family supporters) were less aligned. The study included feedback focus groups with allied health professionals (n = 18) that identified a range of barriers (e.g. funding limitations, limited family capacity), enablers (e.g. multidisciplinary teams with good communication, dementia and reablement education), and potential practice change targets (e.g. enhanced documentation, promoting reablement to clients) to implement evidence-informed dementia reablement [14]. While this audit and feedback study was an important step, the small sample size means the outcomes may not be nationally transferrable.

Early engagement with stakeholders is an important stage in implementation preparation [16, 17]. To understand how dementia reablement can be sustainably implemented across Australia, it is therefore necessary to consult and converge opinions from the diverse interest holders (aka 'experts') who either influence or may be impacted by reablement implementation. The Delphi survey technique is a commonly used constructivist approach to gain consensus around a specific topic [18, 19]. The use of Delphi technique to develop implementation strategies in aged and dementia care is well established [20–22]; the methodology provides a platform for hearing from the range of interest holders who may be impacted by implementation of dementia reablement. For example, as ‘end-users’ of dementia reablement, people living with dementia [23, 24] and allied health professionals [25] are both key groups who should be involved in this process. Other relevant interest holder groups may include informal carers, program managers and referrers, policy makers, experts and thought leaders. Transparency around the nature of the contributors will maximise recognition of the resulting implementation strategy as a legitimate resource to achieve widespread approval and uptake within the sector [26]. Ultimately, this study aimed to develop a national strategy by reaching expert consensus on the barriers, enablers, and potential implementation strategies to promote uptake of evidence-informed dementia reablement. The outcomes from this process will be used to develop a national implementation guide that models how to drive uptake of evidence-informed dementia reablement in real-world practice.

Methods

This study forms one part of a broader program of research that is guided by implementation frameworks. This body of work is described elsewhere [9], but in brief, involves a four-phase approach to (1) explore current clinical practice and practitioner-identified barriers and enablers to implementation [14]; (2) seek national consensus on a dementia reablement implementation strategy (the focus of the present study); (3) pilot the newly developed implementation guide; and (4) promote sustainability through implementation capacity building. This program of work is supported by a nationally representative project advisory group consisting of: living experience experts (people with dementia and family carers), allied health practitioners, program managers, policymakers, and academics.

Participants and recruitment

To enhance the epistemological rigour of the Delphi process and the likelihood of a resulting resource (i.e. a national implementation guide) that would be acceptable across the community aged care sector, we sought a wide range of knowledge and experience-based expert interest holders to inform the survey [27]. All participants were required to be over 18 years of age, living in Australia, and willing to participate in at least two rounds of Delphi survey. Other inclusion criteria for the various stakeholder groups at the time of the survey were as follows: (1a) allied health practitioners must be involved in delivering allied health services to people living with dementia in the community, and be an allied health professional (e.g. occupational therapist, physiotherapist, exercise physiologist, speech pathologist, dietitian, psychologist); (1b) allied health assistants must be involved in supporting the delivery of allied health services to people living with dementia in the community; (2) people with living experience of dementia (both people living with dementia and informal carers e.g. family and friends) must be willing and able to participate in the survey and have capacity to provide informed consent; (3) program managers and referrers must be involved in coordinating the delivery of community aged care services (e.g. receiving/managing referrals, designating programs, linking funding etc.), or referring clients with dementia to allied health services in the community; (4) policy makers must be working within the aged care sector in a role that impacts on policy at an organisational, sector, and/or government level; (5) experts and thought leaders must be academics or influencers in the field of community aged care with a track record through publications, conference presentations, and/or known profiles within the community aged care sector.

Purposive sampling and snowballing were used to facilitate recruitment for this study. A hyperlink to the Delphi survey (administered via Qualtrics) facilitated sharing across newsletters, social media, webpages, and email. Recognised experts were identified by the investigator team via a range of sources, such as scientific publications, conferences, and/or known profiles within the community aged care sector e.g. via media appearances, public communications about the sector or field of working with people living with dementia in the community. Identified experts were sent an email from the research team notifying them of the study with a participant information and consent form (PISCF) attached. A separate email generated via Qualtrics was then sent to each identified expert, which contained a link to the round one survey. In parallel with purposively recruiting recognised experts, the research was advertised broadly via a range of dementia and community aged care organisations, relevant sector-based communities of practice, and social media. For all participants, once on the survey landing page, they were requested to read the PISCF via an included link and could not proceed to the survey until they indicated that they had read the PISCF and indicated consent to participate. Only participants who participated in the first survey round were invited to round two. This study was approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (iRECS6591).

Design

A modified online Delphi survey process was used to allow for remote participation from a broad range of expert stakeholders across Australia to gain consensus around development of a nationally relevant implementation strategy [19, 28]. A Delphi process involves a series of anonymous surveys completed by experts on the topic in question. Between survey rounds, the research team reviews survey outcomes and presents overall findings back to participants [29]. As the outcomes of this work pertained specifically to delivery of reablement for people living with dementia, it was important that these living experience experts (including both people living with dementia and informal carers) were included [23, 24, 30]. Previous research has identified the need to adapt Delphi methodology to appropriately include people living with dementia [31, 32]. The format of the survey within each round was therefore tailored to maximise accessibility; all experts completed this same version of the survey. Specific strategies to maximise accessibility were informed by previous research, and included: (1) Language: accessible language was used for the survey items, including using concise, non-technical, simple and clear language, without jargon [31]. Living experience experts from our project advisory group [9] were consulted on the format and wording of the survey items prior to roll out; (2) Scoring and consensus: based on previous research that specifically looked at meaningful involvement of people with dementia in the Delphi process [31, 33–35], a 3-point Likert scale was used, and consensus was defined at ≥ 70%; (3) Support: people with dementia were offered the option of either the online survey via Qualtrics or a paper survey mailed to them. In addition, support from the research team via phone or Microsoft Teams was offered to assist with completing the survey. Round one was open 16 October – 20 November 2024 and round two was open 13 January – 26 February 2025. The study was reported according to the Guidance on Conducting and Reporting DElphi Studies (CREDES [18]; appendix 1).

Round one

A range of demographic information was collected, including age, gender, state, locality, education, stakeholder role (e.g. person living with dementia, family member/friend, allied health professional, allied health assistant, program manager/referrer, policy maker, expert/thought leader, other). Round one of the survey was developed based on underpinning work by the research team including a clinical audit and feedback process with community-based allied health professionals [14] and a scoping review that explored barriers and enablers to implementing reablement for people living with dementia in the community [10]. The project advisory group reviewed the outcomes from these underpinning works via a virtual workshop, contributing important insights to inform the first round of the survey. Using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [11], a range of potential barriers and enablers (i.e. determinants) to implementing reablement for community dwelling people living with dementia were grouped into four domains: (1) the system: community aged care in general and the interactions between services, (2) service providers: community aged care service providers and the allied health teams delivering services, (3) family: informal or family carers of people living with dementia, (4): the person living with dementia. Each individual survey item within each of these domains was presented with a topic (e.g. ‘dementia knowledge’) and an explanation (e.g. ‘Lack of dementia knowledge amongst allied health professionals and the broader team e.g. impacts the type and quality of services that are offered and delivered to people living with dementia). Consensus was sought using 3-point Likert scales around the barriers (‘not much of a challenge’, ‘a bit of a challenge’, ‘a big challenge’) and enablers (‘not very helpful’, ‘helpful’, ‘very helpful’). A free text box was provided at the end of each domain of items to allow participants to provide comments or expand on their ratings. Items from round one that reached consensus and relevant qualitative responses were mapped to the CFIR [11]. The identified barriers and enablers from round one, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) strategies compilation [36–38], the scoping review outcomes [10], and the audit and feedback data [14] were all used to develop a list of potential implementation strategies, which were then presented to participants in round two.

Round two

Participants were presented with a summary of the results from round one, and the list of potential implementation strategies, which were presented across the same four domains from round one (system, service, family, person with dementia). For each strategy, participants were asked to consider two concepts: 1) importance: how important is the strategy e.g. considering how much impact they think the strategy would have in addressing the identified barrier(s), whether there might be potential negative unintended consequences, and whether there might be potential wider positive impacts in using this strategy to address an identified aim, and 2) feasibility: how feasible is the strategy e.g. thinking about whether this strategy was something that could be realistically done, and whether it would be worth the effort for the potential outcome it could generate. As per round one, consensus was sought using separate 3-point Likert scales around the importance (‘not very important’, ‘important’, ‘very important’) and feasibility (‘not very feasible’, ‘feasible’, ‘very feasible’). A free text box was again provided at the end of each domain of items to allow participants to provide comments or expand on their ratings. For a strategy to be included, consensus was required for the item being both ‘very important’ and ‘feasible/very feasible’.

Data analysis

Participant demographics were analysed in Excel using descriptive statistics. Consensus for inclusion of each item was ≥ 70% scored as ‘a big challenge’/‘very helpful’ and < 20% scored as ‘not much of a challenge’/‘not very helpful’ (round 1) or ≥ 70% scored as ‘very important’ + ‘feasible/very feasible’ and < 20% scored as ‘not very important’ (round 2). Qualitative written responses were used to supplement understanding of outcomes and to frame the approach to presenting implementation strategies. For example, following qualitative feedback in round one, the strategies proposed for the person living with dementia category were all around upskilling teams, enhancing service delivery practices, and providing more supports to the person. Items where consensus was reached are reported below; items where consensus was not achieved are reported in appendices 2 and 3.

Results

Sixty-three individuals consented to participate in round one; of these, nine (14.3%) did not progress past the consent stage and four (6.3%) completed their demographic information but did not complete the survey. Fifty participants (79.4%) completed the round one survey; one of these participants only completed the first half but was still included. The response rate to round two was 70% (35/50); two of these participants only completed the first quarter (‘the system’), but were still included. In round one, experts participated from every state and territory across Australia, with the exception of no surveys being returned from the Northern Territory. Participants included people living with dementia (4%), family/friends of a person with dementia (8%), allied health professionals (54%; representing occupational therapy, physiotherapy, speech pathology, orthoptics, exercise therapy, social work, psychomotor therapy), expert and thought leaders (8%; including consultants, dementia practitioners); researchers and academics (24%), and a program manager or referrer (2%). • Communication with family carers should be clear, concise, and any education and information that is provided should be relevant and feasible within the context of each family and their broader community setting (CFIR Individuals: implementation deliverers; need; CFIR Inner Setting: relational connections; communications)

• Service providers should link family carers with broader community supports such as suitable services for themselves or where possible, engaging with the wider family group (CFIR Individuals: implementation team members; innovation deliverers; need; opportunity; CFIR Inner Setting: access to knowledge and information; relational connections, recipient-centeredness)

• Promote the importance of reablement (ERIC: develop educational materials; distribute educational materials; use mass media)

• To best support people living with dementia to participate in a reablement program, the way that reablement is offered and delivered should be addressed (CFIR Individuals: innovation deliverers; capability; opportunity). E.g. service providers should have skilled practitioners who can communicate the potential benefits of reablement to the person, and the person is supported to participate in the program (e.g. using dementia care navigators, accessing group-based programs for peer support, and linking in to family where possible) (CFIR Inner Setting: relational connections; communications; recipient-centeredness; available resources)

• Consider transport to support people to access services (ERIC: build a coalition; facilitation; promote network weaving)

• Allocate sufficient time (and funding for this time) to deliver reablement programs (ERIC: access new funding; fund and contract for clinical innovation; organize clinician implementation team meetings; use other payment schemes)

• Convey the importance of reablement to the person with dementia and highlight their strengths and capabilities to participate in a reablement program (ERIC: assess for readiness and identify barriers and facilitators; develop educational materials; distribute educational materials; intervene with patients/consumers to enhance uptake & adherence; involve patients/consumers and family members; tailor strategies)

• Ensure consistency amongst providers and efficient communication strategies between services such as care support workers, allied health assistants and the allied health team (ERIC: build a coalition; create a learning collaborative; create new clinical teams; revise professional roles)

• Recognise the advanced interprofessional practice with unique specialist training in dementia that care support workers and allied health assistants can have when trained in supporting dementia reablement programs (ERIC: conduct ongoing training; create a learning collaborative; create or change credentialing and/or licensure standards; create new clinical teams; revise professional roles)

Discussion

This is the first Delphi study in Australia, and indeed globally, to explore barriers and enablers to implementing evidence-informed dementia reablement and identify potential implementation strategies to promote uptake in practice. Guided by the CFIR [11], a range of barriers and enablers reached consensus across four domains: the system, service providers, family carers and supporters, and people living with dementia. The ERIC strategies compilation [36–38] was then used, guided by the same domains, and a suite of potential implementation strategies were identified as being both very important and feasible to address the barriers and enablers. Overall, outcomes from this work have highlighted four key themes around determinants and strategies relating to the implementation of dementia reablement: (1) education and promotion, (2) engaging consumers and providing support to carers and people living with dementia, (3) service provider capacity to deliver reablement, and (4) funding, policy, and access to services.

Education and promotion

One of the strongest themes that we identified during this Delphi process, across both rounds and all domains, was the need for education around both dementia and reablement, from the system (e.g. referrers, GPs, aged care services, the community in general), service providers and allied health practitioners, and people with dementia and family carers. In parallel, our outcomes indicated that reablement should be promoted to referrers, people living with dementia, and families as an important approach to supporting people with dementia. This aligns with international and Australian research that has discussed the role of negative attitudes in contributing to challenges with accessing dementia reablement, and suggested education as an important mitigation strategy [39, 40]. An international position paper on how to address implementation issues for dementia reablement has also highlighted the importance of changing attitudes around dementia and educating broadly about the potential for reablement to support the abilities of a person with dementia to engage in meaningful activities and to be engaged with their community [41]. In practice, specific dementia and reablement training has been shown to enhance knowledge and confidence in GPs and allied health professionals [42–44]. Moving forward, consistently embedding evidence-based skill development and education around dementia and reablement within allied health and healthcare degrees has potential to enhance knowledge, attitudes and practice [45–48]. By changing ‘local attitudes’ and ‘culture’, increasing ‘societal pressure’, and enhancing ‘access to knowledge and information’ (CFIR [11]) around dementia through education, there is potential to ‘increase demand’ and push to ‘mandate change’ (ERIC [37]) for reablement. Addressing both top-down and bottom-up perspectives would encourage service providers to offer reablement to clients and empower people living with dementia and families to ask for dementia reablement.

Engaging consumers and providing support to carers and people living with dementia

Providing appropriate support to the person with dementia and in parallel, their family supporters (where available) was identified in the current study as important for effective implementation of reablement programs. Strong consensus was reached around the importance of building rapport with and getting to know the person with dementia. Indeed, these strategies have been identified as critical to supporting people with dementia to participate and engage in activities [49, 50]. Access to dementia navigators reached consensus in the current study as a strategy to support reablement program access and participation. This is supported by a longitudinal randomised controlled trial (n = 456 dyads) from the United States that found care navigation models involving provision of multidisciplinary support for dementia dyads contributed to improved quality of life for the person with dementia, and for carers, higher self-efficacy and lower rates of depression [51]. Our findings around delivering dyadic programs (where appropriate) to engage and upskill carers, and providing carer supports (referrals, respite, and links with services) in parallel with the reablement program align with international dementia reablement recommendations [41]. It is important to recognise however, that some people with dementia live alone or may not have available family support. With the right support (e.g. care support workers or allied health assistants), these people still have potential to benefit from interventions such as reablement [52]. In parallel, some family carers may feel reluctant to participate in a dyadic program, for example, due to stress or time commitments [53]. However, evidence suggests that carers who do participate in dementia rehabilitation programs may benefit from social connections, access to support from health professionals, and enhanced knowledge and skills [54, 55]. Overall, effectively implementing reablement requires supporting people with dementia and family supporters (CFIR innovation recipients) to participate, and this is largely led by the service providers (CFIR innovation deliverers) [11]. In providing effective support, providers should be using ‘evaluative and iterative strategies’ to ‘engage consumers’ by ‘adapting and tailoring their approach to each individual context’ [38].

Service provider capacity to deliver reablement

Outcomes from the current Delphi process illustrate the central importance of service provider capacity to effectively implement reablement for people living with dementia. In addition to education as previously discussed, participants in our study highlighted strategies around planning for implementation such as involving leadership, structuring multidisciplinary teams, establishing communication strategies, and allocating sufficient time and resources to be able to effectively deliver reablement. These service-level implementation concepts align with those previously identified in dementia reablement recommendations [41], and in a recent aged care scoping review [56]. An Australian implementation study evaluating a multicomponent dementia program identified similar service level issues with allied health professionals commenting on factors such as training, documentation, adequate preparation time and staffing as potential sustainability barriers [53]. Reablement program adaptation may be required to promote sustainability of implementation. For example, a recent Australian study describing a post-diagnostic dyadic program reported the program was acceptable to both the dyad and program facilitators, yet the facilitators indicated that sustainability of implementation would be challenging without further adapting the intervention to incorporate into routine practice [57]. It is well recognised that implementing complex interventions into real world settings often requires adaptation [58]. The term ‘flexible fidelity’ has been used to describe the potential for adapting/changing non-essential intervention elements to fit the context, while ensuring core intervention elements are retained to maximise intervention effectiveness [59, 60]. The adaptation process should be proactive (rather than reactive) and consider how adaptations may impact on client outcomes and whether they will retain the core elements of evidence-informed reablement [15].

While much implementation research discusses the importance of champions [61–63], participants in our study discussed taking a service wide approach (i.e. cultural shift) to account for staff turnover that can lead to champions leaving and services being disrupted. Culture change has been described as an ongoing, complex process that requires both top-down and bottom-up involvement [64]. On a service provider level, a range of implementation strategies to facilitate organisational culture change are outlined in the Cochrane Effective Practice and Organisation of Care taxonomy [65]. Strategies should be tailored to the service and setting, but examples include audit and feedback and establishing a community of practice to promote a collaborative approach to reablement. In parallel with culture change, participants in our study agreed that leveraging program supports such as allied health assistants and care support workers could promote sustainable delivery of reablement programs in addition to potentially supporting program engagement and participation from people living with dementia. This aligns with previous research that has discussed the value of allied health assistants for supporting efficient allied health practice [66] and demonstrated that post-diagnostic interventions can be effectively delivered by trained but unlicensed care providers, who are supported and supervised by a multidisciplinary team [51]. Additionally, a UK study evaluating implementation of community-based cognitive rehabilitation in dementia found that involvement of occupational therapy assistants could reduce intervention costs [61]. Looking at service provider capacity to deliver reablement, our outcomes suggest the importance of ‘promoting adaptability’ of reablement and ‘tailoring to context’ (ERIC [37]) to facilitate necessary ‘work infrastructure’ changes and maximise ‘compatibility’ of reablement with the service (CFIR [11]).

Funding, policy, and access to services

Policy-backed funding programs to enhance access to services such as early screening and therapeutic programs have been shown to reduce costs for people living with dementia [67]. The concept identified in the current Delphi around leveraging policy so dementia reablement is an expectation (rather than something that is nice to have) aligns with previous work, which adds that financial incentives in parallel with policy would strengthen this facilitator [68]. In Australia there are a range of government funded health and social care support programs. Participants in the current study identified the importance of leveraging these varied funding programs to maximise reablement access for people with dementia. It should be noted that many of these programs may not necessarily be primarily intended to support dementia reablement (e.g. the National Disability Insurance Scheme or Medicare chronic disease plans). In response to this potential mis-match, flexibility around using public finances and resources across sectors and budgets was a recommendation in a recent international overview of economics and dementia [69]. This will require a system-wide approach where the value and importance of dementia reablement is understood by referrers, the assessors who assign program funding, service providers, and clients and families [69, 70]. An example of the importance of dementia knowledge and how this may impact on a person’s access to dementia reablement comes from the new Australian Support at Home program manual (version 4 [71]) that outlines the delivery of funded home support programs under the new Aged Care Act 2024 [72]. In Section 14 on the restorative care funding pathway (that could fund reablement programs), one of the 'suitability requirements for restorative care' is listed as 'participant motivation' [71]. For a person with dementia who lives alone or does not have available family support, they may need some extra assistance (e.g. via allied health assistants or care support workers) to participate in the program. If this is not explicitly laid out in the manual, there is a risk that providers assume that a person with dementia who experiences apathy or finds it difficult to remember to exercise between sessions is 'not motivated' to participate and therefore may not offer reablement. In contrast, the Australian National Dementia Action Plan [7] explicitly states that reablement intervention approaches should be offered early for people with mild cognitive impairment and dementia. Having positive policy such as this is an important step forward, but this must be backed up by public funding models and access to dementia reablement programs [39, 70].

While accessing dementia-specific allied health services was identified as a barrier in the current study, qualitative responses discussed potential to upskill the general allied health workforce to enhance accessibility. Indeed, there are around 300,000 registered allied health professionals in Australia [73] and almost 430,000 people living with dementia [2]. If all allied health professionals engaged in freely available dementia and dementia reablement training (e.g. University of Tasmania MOOC; Dementia Training Australia Introduction to Dementia Rehabilitation e-course), there is potential for about seven allied health practitioners for every 10 people with dementia who could support a reablement program. In parallel, as discussed under the ‘education and promotion’ theme, there is a need for more undergraduate education to create a skilled and confident allied health workforce [47, 74]. Outcomes from this Delphi have led to the generation of an implementation guide that was reviewed by the advisory group and is currently being piloted within the Australian community aged care context. The ultimate plan is to make this guide freely available alongside the existing suite of evidence-informed reablement in dementia resources (www.hammond.com.au/reablement) to support all service providers in preparing for and implementing dementia reablement.

Moving forward, there is a need to continue the push to change national policy around dementia to enhance access to dementia reablement. The international collaborative project Strengthening responses to dementia in developing countries (STRiDE) described a policy change model that included a three-sphered Venn diagram to illustrate when policy change may be possible [75, 76]. Applying this to the Australian context for dementia reablement policy, these three spheres could include: (1) clearly describing the issues faced by people living with dementia e.g. functional decline; (2) identifying the evidence-informed solutions e.g. dementia reablement; and (3) the current political environment e.g. the new Aged Care Act and Support at Home program [76]. By leveraging ‘policies and laws’ and ‘financing’ (CFIR [11]), there is potential to ‘change infrastructure’ and ‘develop stakeholder interrelationships’ [38] to promote broad implementation of dementia rehabilitation. The strategy consensus reached in this study was based on a broad level of thinking in terms of importance and feasibility of each ‘type’ of strategy suggested. Moving forward, the challenge will be to operationalise these strategies within the complexities of real-world community aged care settings [77]. This process may be facilitated by leveraging complementary frameworks (e.g. the Interactive System Framework [78]) and techniques (e.g. behaviour change technique [79]) to devise clear action plans to effectively implement strategies and address identified determinants (i.e. barriers and enablers) [77, 80]. In parallel, providing service teams with a range of support to implement interventions such as dementia reablement has been highlighted as an important facilitator to actioning implementation strategies. Examples include establishing project teams, providing ongoing encouragement, and fostering teamwork and relationships within and across teams [81].

Limitations

This study has some limitations which must be considered. The reliability of outcomes from a Delphi survey is dependent on the available evidence and the experts who participated in the process [82]. Despite the survey being specifically designed to support accessibility, only two people living with dementia participated. As this small number was not sufficient to allow for subgroup analysis, the data were analysed using group consensus [83]. Therefore, it is possible that the strategies identified may be skewed towards the perspective of allied health practitioners (the primary stakeholder group who participated), and future work should look more specifically at strategies from the perspective of people living with dementia. While a few survey participants identified as being from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds, none identified as being First Nations Australians. First Nations and CALD Australians living with dementia face additional barriers to accessing services and supports and have unique needs [84, 85]. An important future step will be to consult with First Nations and CALD Australians to explore the concept of dementia reablement and how implementation can be supported within these contexts. While the sample size of this Delphi may be viewed as ‘diluted’ due to the range of stakeholder groups that participated, having transparency around the nature and range of the contributors enhances the likelihood of the resulting implementation strategy being recognised as a legitimate resource to support widespread approval and uptake within the sector [27]. Finally, the three-point Likert scale may have limited the nuance in results, however, a three-point Likert scale was identified as the preferred approach in dementia-specific Delphi processes [31], and in a study investigating clinically relevant treatment goals [86]. Overall, Delphi has been demonstrated as a useful approach to identifying implementation strategies within a dementia care context [22].

Conclusion

This Delphi study identified that a range of barriers and enablers exist to implementing evidence-informed community-based dementia reablement in Australia. Specific strategies were identified to support implementation, that spanned the system (e.g. seeking policy mandates around dementia reablement, and educating community), service providers (e.g. planning for implementation, and education on dementia and reablement), family (e.g. education on the person with dementia’s abilities, and carer support), and the person with dementia (e.g. promoting reablement to people with cognitive impairment or dementia when they come onto a service, and providing support for participation in reablement programs). Overall, this work has highlighted four key themes relating to the implementation of dementia reablement: (1) education and promotion, (2) engaging consumers and providing support to carers and people living with dementia, (3) service provider capacity to deliver reablement, and (4) funding, policy, and access to services. Effectively implementing dementia reablement will take a sector-wide approach that involves top-down and bottom-up support, and leverages policy and funding to make it happen.
