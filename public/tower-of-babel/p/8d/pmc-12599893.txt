Diagnosis and Severity Rating of Parkinson’s Disease Based on Multimodal Gait Signal Analysis With GLRT and ST-CNN-Transformer Networks
Authors: Miaoxin Ji, Hongru Dong, Lina Guo, Wen LI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective: Parkinson’s disease (PD) diagnosis relies on the evaluation of motor and non-motor symptoms, with gait abnormalities serving as a key marker for early detection. Traditional clinical assessment often relies on visual gait analysis, which is a subjective process prone to bias. This study introduces a PD severity classification method that leverages gait features. Methods: A Spatial-temporal Convolutional neural network-Transformer (ST-CNN-Transformer) model for PD severity classification was established. Multimodal gait data, including foot acceleration, angular velocity, and Vertical Ground Reaction Force (VGRF), were collected in collaboration with Xiangyang First People’s Hospital, Hubei Province. Zero-velocity points (ZVPs) were detected using the Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT), and gait cycle features were extracted from inertial measurement unit data for precise segmentation. The ST-CNN-Transformer model captures spatial-temporal features and periodic correlations. Results: Evaluation on a dataset comprising 10 healthy controls and 30 PD patients yielded a classification accuracy of 98.81%, surpassing existing gait-based methods for PD severity classification. Conclusion: This study introduces a deep learning (DL) approach to automating PD severity classification by integrating ZVP and gait segmentation derived from multimodal data. The proposed model significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy. Significance: By combining DL with GLRT-based gait segmentation and multimodal gait analysis, this study proposes a robust and interpretable PD severity assessment framework that contributes to more accurate and objective clinical decision-making.

Clinical and Impact: By combining DL with GLRT-based gait segmentation and multimodal gait analysis, this study proposes a robust and interpretable PD severity assessment framework that contributes to more accurate and objective clinical decision-making

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder that impairs motor function, primarily due to the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain, leading to reduced dopamine levels in the striatum. Motor dysfunction is caused by dopamine deficiency [1]. From the European Parkinson’s survey, the prevalence of PD in industrialized countries is about 0.3% per year and continues to grow [2].

Early diagnosis of PD is crucial for subsequent treatment and can effectively slow the progression of the disease and the worsening of symptoms. Traditionally, PD diagnosis relies on clinical evaluation of movement disorders such as limb stiffness, resting tremor, gait slowness, and freezing gait [3]. Among these, the Hoehn and Yahr(H&Y) scale is a commonly used tool to assess the severity of PD [4]. The scale uses a five-stage scoring system that increases with H&Y staging from unilateral body (Stage 1) to bilateral body (Stage 2) to balance newly severely affected (Stage 3), difficulty walking independently (Stage 4), and loss of ability to walk independently (Stage 5) to quantify the patient’s dysfunction and to facilitate clinical diagnosis. To characterize the intermediate course of the disease, two additional stages were later introduced: unilaterally affected with mild balance problems (Stage 1.5) and bilaterally affected with accompanying balance problems (Stage 2.5), resulting in the widely accepted modified H&Y Scale. Despite the availability of quantitative criteria, expert-dependent assessments often introduce bias, potentially compromising diagnostic precision. In recent years, advanced methods have increasingly been employed to improve diagnostic precision, such as using a microphone to record voice signals [5], deploying electroencephalogram (EEG) sensors for brain activity analysis [6], and utilizing magnetic resonance imaging for the assessment of PD [7].

Gait analysis has emerged as a more objective and precise method for PD diagnosis due to significant variations in motor symptoms [8]. Various sensor technologies, including inertial measurement units (IMUs) for foot acceleration and angular velocity [9], pressure sensors for plantar pressure distribution [10], and computer vision for gait parameter extraction [11], have been utilized to collect gait data. These methods allow for the quantitative evaluation of gait, aiding in the classification of PD severity [12], [13]. However, the analysis of time-series gait signals presents challenges due to their inherent nonlinearity, high-dimensional properties, and motion-specific characteristics. To overcome the challenges in signal analysis, machine learning methods utilizing manually designed features have been applied [14]. For example, Balaji et al. developed a gait classification system that combines statistical analysis with spatiotemporal feature extraction. This system employs supervised learning algorithms, including decision trees (DT), support vector machines (SVM), ensemble classifiers (EC), and Bayesian classifiers (BC), achieving a classification accuracy of 99.4% [15]. While decision trees have shown considerable accuracy in PD diagnosis, manually engineered features are limited in their ability to fully capture relevant gait patterns [16]. Despite this, manually engineered features are limited in their ability to fully capture gait characteristics, driving interest in deep learning (DL) approaches for PD diagnosis [17], [18], [19].

Recent studies have applied DL models for PD diagnosis. Balaji et al. proposed a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network that analyzes Vertical Ground Reaction Force (VGRF) signals to identify PD and assess its severity [20]. Guo et al. developed a network architecture that captures spatial-temporal relationships in the data, effectively extracting discriminative features for multi-class classification [21]. However, most DL models in PD diagnosis rely on a single data modality and often fail to account for the periodicity inherent in gait signals. To overcome these limitations, a spatial-temporal convolutional network was adopted to extract salient features from multimodal inputs, complemented by an attention mechanism to highlight temporal dependencies across gait cycles.

The ST-CNN-Transformer hybrid network model was introduced to handle the complexity of gait data. A single convolutional network may fail to capture complementary features that are essential for a comprehensive representation, whereas the hybrid approach enhances feature diversity and robustness. The ST-CNN module leverages a dual-branch architecture to extract high-dimensional spatial-temporal features from multimodal inputs. To model temporal dependencies in gait sequences, a Transformer is incorporated, utilizing self-attention to capture periodic patterns and long-range temporal correlations. To handle gait variability and pathological deviations, the Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test (GLRT) detects Zero-velocity points (ZVPs), which serve as critical markers for gait cycle segmentation, improving segmentation accuracy. The GLRT was applied to IMU signals for ZVP detection, which was aligned with VGRF signals to train the model. The ST-CNN module extracts localized features, which are processed by the Transformer to learn global dependencies, facilitating accurate PD severity classification. Related Work

Trabia et al. demonstrated that SVM applied to IMU data achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 81%, though the model was unable to distinguish between different stages of PD severity [22]. In contrast, B. Vidya et al. developed a multi-class SVM classifier using VGRF and kinematic features, achieving an average accuracy of 98.65% [23]. Borzì et al. employed a parallel architecture of multi-head convolutional neural networks to detect freezing of gait in PD patients in real time, capturing spatial-temporal dependencies from gait data. However, this approach was limited to binary classification and did not assess PD severity levels [24]. Building on this, B. Vidya applied empirical mode decomposition to VGRF signals and utilized a hybrid CNN-LSTM model, attaining 98.32% accuracy in PD severity classification [25]. The gait cycle in PD patients is characterized by both intra-periodic and inter-periodic features [26]. Naimi et al. utilized VGRF signals, segmented into 100 time-step windows with 50% overlap to facilitate feature extraction. A hybrid CNN-Transformer network was proposed, embedding temporal and spatial encoders to analyze gait variations along temporal and positional dimensions. By leveraging both inter-periodic and intra-periodic gait features, the model quantified PD severity using the H&Y scale, achieving an accuracy of 88% [27]. The Transformer model effectively integrates local and global features through iterative application of the self-attention mechanism across multiple layers. Optimal window size selection remains critical to balance intra-periodic and inter-periodic feature integration, yet fixed-length windows commonly used in literature limit cross-temporal and multi-scale analyses. Sigcha et al. designed a Freezing of gait (FOG)-Transformer network based on accelerometer data. To optimize computational efficiency without sacrificing performance, they downsampled the original 200 Hz data to 40 Hz and segmented it into 3.2-second windows, enabling effective global pattern recognition. However, this approach is confined to FOG detection and does not extend to PD severity classification [28]. Zhang et al. examined the use of multimodal data—including gait acceleration, electroencephalography (EEG), electromyography (EMG), and skin conductance—to detect and predict FOG in PD. Their results demonstrated that integrating multiple signal types substantially outperforms single-modality methods, achieving an average accuracy of 93% [29]. Nonetheless, the complexity of the wearable system may reduce patient comfort and mobility, while reliance on costly EEG devices limits clinical applicability. Zhao et al. leveraged multimodal datasets to differentiate amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, and PD, attaining a classification accuracy of 98.34% through combined modeling [30]. Building on these efforts, our method utilizes pressure sensors and IMUs to capture diverse gait data. We apply the GLRT for adaptive gait cycle segmentation, feeding the segmented data into a novel diagnostic framework based on an ST-CNN-Transformer architecture.

Weighted GLRT-Based Cycle Fusion Layer

This study explores the impact of gait cycle features at various stages of PD on the classification accuracy of neural networks, emphasizing the critical role of precise gait data classification. As a solution, the proposed fusion method incorporates the GLRT for effective handling of gait variability. Accurate detection of ZVP is essential but challenged by noise and irregular gait patterns. Our algorithm adaptively identifies ZVP by combining acceleration and angular velocity data within the GLRT framework. It integrates information from both stance and swing phases, estimating ZVP via likelihood ratios of gait states to minimize detection errors. To preserve inter-period dependencies in the network input, ZVP variance is employed as a confidence measure. This confidence-guided fusion improves cycle segmentation fidelity and enhances the model’s capacity for PD severity assessment.

Feature Extraction through ST-CNN

The ST-CNN-Transformer architecture integrates spatial-temporal convolutional layers with a Transformer module, depicted in Fig. 2. Input signal matrices comprising acceleration, angular velocity, and VGRF are processed through multiple attention heads to extract rich spatial-temporal features. The Transformer encoder captures local periodic patterns and global multi-periodic dependencies for classification. While increasing the number of layers in neural networks is a common strategy to enhance performance, escalating hyperparameters such as the convolutional kernel size and channel count significantly heightens computational load [31]. To enhance the classification performance of DL models while reducing computational complexity, the ST-CNN model was introduced. This novel CNN is designed to leverage spatial-temporal information for the effective extraction of gait features in patients with PD during ambulation. In ST-CNN, temporal characteristics and spatial features of foot acceleration, angular velocity, and plantar pressure data are extracted and enhanced with spatial residuals, obviating the need for deeper, more computationally intensive networks. The ST-CNN model features a dual-branch architecture, with each branch employing a CNN structure, illustrated in Fig. 2. Unlike conventional CNNs that focus solely on one-dimensional features, ST-CNN synergistically integrates spatial and temporal information to capture richer gait characteristics. The temporal branch applies one-dimensional convolutional layers to capture local temporal patterns in gait signals, extracting deep temporal features through successive convolution and pooling operations. Meanwhile, the spatial branch treats the eight-channel input as a single-channel pseudo-image and employs two-dimensional convolutional layers to capture cross-channel interactions, extracting spatial features from sensor relationships. Features from both branches are concatenated to form complementary spatial-temporal representations before being dimensionally adjusted for input into the Transformer encoder. To align the modalities, the spatial branch’s output undergoes dimensionality reduction via global average pooling, converting it into a one-dimensional feature vector. This operation reduces computational complexity and ensures a unified multimodal feature representation, thereby enhancing classification performance.

Transformer Encoder

The Transformer encoder relies on the self-attention mechanism to capture the long-term and short-term dependencies between sequences. Unlike conventional CNNs and LSTMs, the Transformer processes all data in parallel without relying on sequential operations, enabling efficient modeling of temporal correlations. In this work, we introduce an encoder utilizing cycle fusion layers to classify gait data from individuals with PD. Recognizing significant variations in both inter-periodic and intra-periodic gait features across PD severity levels, the model aims to accurately capture these temporal dynamics for precise classification. Input data first passes through a pre-circular fusion layer that integrates gait cycle information across severity stages. The fused features then feed into the ST-CNN module to extract critical spatial and temporal representations from the time series. Subsequently, these features enter the Transformer encoder, where self-attention mechanisms model temporal dependencies by aggregating information across the entire gait sequence. Residual connections within the encoder mitigate gradient vanishing, preserving initial feature information and enhancing training stability and convergence.

The pre-cycle fusion layer consolidates multidimensional gait cycle data, enabling the encoder to simultaneously capture intra-periodic details and inter-periodic temporal relationships. Through iterative processing, the model progressively learns dependencies within and between gait cycles, yielding a comprehensive feature representation that underpins accurate PD severity classification.

Multi-Layer Perceptron

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) is employed to classify the encoder’s output in the proposed framework. Initially, the extracted features are amplified to increase nonlinearity. Then, redundant components are eliminated by compressing the output into four distinct classes.

Experimental Equipment and Dataset

Experimental Equipment

This study employed the IMU18B inertial sensor and the MD-30 piezoresistive pressure sensor to collect multimodal gait data from PD patients. Data acquisition and processing were managed by an STM32 microcontroller, while remote storage and management were facilitated through Alibaba Cloud, enabling telemedicine applications.

The IMU18B combines a high-precision triaxial MEMS gyroscope and accelerometer arranged orthogonally to measure angular velocity and linear acceleration across three perpendicular axes. Its integrated digital control circuitry performs real-time calibration for zero bias, scale factor, and misalignment, ensuring accurate inertial data. The sensor, affixed to the dorsal foot, transmits processed data via an RS422 interface.

The MD-30 pressure sensor, installed on both forefoot and hindfoot regions, detects fine plantar pressure fluctuations with high sensitivity, capturing VGRF essential for detailed gait analysis.

Data Preprocessing

During data acquisition, mean filtering was applied to attenuate transient noise arising from sensor errors or external disturbances. This method employs a sliding window to substitute each data point with the average of its neighboring values. In this study, continuous gait data were processed accordingly. Fig. 4 presents the mean-filtered acceleration, angular velocity, and VGRF signals, which clearly reveal the periodic characteristics of gait. Patients with PD exhibit distinctive intra- and inter-periodic features in their gait time series. Signal variations at a given time point are influenced not only by adjacent frames but also by long-range temporal dependencies across surrounding gait cycles. Owing to the limited size of the dataset, a data augmentation strategy was adopted, in which samples from different severity levels were partially mixed. This approach effectively increased data diversity and enhanced the generalization capability of the model. GLRT-Based Cycle Fusion

This paper employs the GLRT method for automatic gait cycle segmentation and fusion, leveraging the statistical properties of inertial sensor signals to integrate acceleration and angular velocity data. The GLRT method accurately detects ZVP in PD patients’ gait cycles while ensuring high precision, enabling precise cycle segmentation. During continuous walking, zero-velocity detection is performed using the relationship between acceleration and the angular velocity modulus, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The GLRT method exhibited perfect accuracy in detecting ZVP, facilitating robust cycle fusion. FIGURE 5.Zero-velocity points identified using GLRT in gait signals: (a) angular velocity, (b) acceleration.

Zero-velocity points identified using GLRT in gait signals: (a) angular velocity, (b) acceleration.

ZVP analysis indicates that the average gait period across PD severity levels approximates 80 time steps for cycle fusion. Weighted fusion is applied to normalize the period to 100, after which three representative cycles are selected. Two gait cycles would provide insufficient temporal information, whereas incorporating more than three cycles would unnecessarily increase computational complexity; thus, three cycles offer a suitable balance. The resulting F1 and F2 data frames comprise 240 and 300 time steps. Fig. 6 presents the time-series data for 240-frame and 300-frame segments in the dataset. Examination of consecutive time steps in the segmented frames shows that F2 contains more complete gait cycles than F1, providing the periodic encoder with richer feature correlations, which enhances the model’s training effectiveness. Multi-Class Classification

Assessment of PD severity is formulated as a multi-class classification task. To differentiate between various health states and PD severity levels, the ST-CNN-Transformer architecture is utilized. The gait dataset is segmented into TIME-period frames with 8 channels (F2), which serve as input to the ST-CNN-Transformer network. The ST-CNN component is employed for initial testing. Fig. 7 illustrates the confusion matrix for the PD severity multi-class problem. When the input data frame is F2, both classifiers can make correct judgments for healthy people. When using the ST-CNN-Transformer classifier, the model achieves 100% accuracy for Severity-2.5 and a 1.85% misclassification rate for Severity-2, owing to the period encoder’s ability to integrate local and multi-period gait features. Conclusion

This study presents a novel DL framework for classifying PD severity using the H&Y scale. The framework integrates an ST-CNN with a Transformer model, utilizing multimodal gait data from inertial and pressure sensors. By incorporating the GLRT, the method effectively addresses gait periodicity, which has been overlooked by traditional approaches, thereby enhancing feature fusion and improving training efficiency. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed ST-CNN-Transformer model outperforms existing methods in both classification accuracy and computational efficiency. Specifically, the model achieved 98.81% accuracy in PD severity classification, markedly improving the ability to differentiate between various PD stages. These findings underscore the potential of DL-based gait analysis to provide more accurate, objective, and efficient assessments of PD severity in clinical environments. Although the model performs well in controlled experimental settings, further research is needed to optimize it for real-time application and large-scale clinical validation. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset, improving model adaptability, and ensuring its reliability for widespread clinical use.
