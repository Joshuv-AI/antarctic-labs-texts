Wireless charging ferrite core design with improved material efficiency for mobile devices by enhancing outer-region flux density
Authors: Yohan Park, Syed Ahson Ali Shah, Woo-Young Cho, Hyojun Park, Hyunwoong Lee, Yun-Su Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWireless power transfer (WPT) systems integrated with machine learning (ML) techniques have emerged as promising solutions for enhancing performance and efficiency. Material efficiency, which involves achieving high performance with minimal material use, is crucial for mobile devices to enhance user satisfaction. This paper proposes a core structure design methodology for WPT systems that enhances mutual inductance (MI) by segmenting and assembling the ferrite core, thereby improving material efficiency within limited space constraints. For genetic algorithm (GA)-based optimization, an ML estimator is implemented to replace iterative finite element method simulations during the GA optimization loop, reducing the average computation time from 203s to 86μs and significantly accelerating the GA optimization process. Furthermore, the designed core structure exhibits improved uniformity of magnetic flux density by enhancing the outer-region magnetic flux density between the coils, thereby contributing to higher MI. Experimental results show that the proposed core structure achieves a 17.44% higher normalized MI compared to conventional structures, using less material through an asymmetrical configuration, which also reduces weight and cost. Furthermore, the optimized asymmetrical structure demonstrates robustness under lateral misalignment conditions (± 5 mm), consistently maintaining higher material efficiency than the full-piece reference even under spatial offsets. These findings demonstrate the potential of ML-driven approaches for developing high-performance, computationally efficient, and cost-effective WPT systems.Keywords: Ferromagnetic cores design, Genetic algorithm, Inductive power transfer, Mutual inductance, Wireless power transferSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Wireless power transfer (WPT) has attracted considerable research interest due to recent integration with artificial intelligence (AI). AI-based WPT optimization techniques can be applied across a broad range of power scales, from low-power Internet of Things (IoT) sensors1,2 to high-power electric vehicles3,4. AI methods for WPT are being utilized to optimize impedance matching and frequency tuning5, as well as magnetic or RF beamforming6, misalignment tolerance and safety7, and data-driven parameter/model identification8 to maximize efficiency and reliability under dynamic and uncertain conditions. Notably, WPT enables wireless charging for mobile devices such as cell phones9 and implantable medical devices, including pacemakers, cochlear implants, and electroceuticals10,11. For the WPT system of mobile applications, AI methods are used for matching the impedance and frequency5, parameter prediction12, and coil design13.

The material efficiency, which means reduced material usage and high charging efficiency, is a crucial specification for WPT system optimization as it directly impacts the device weight and cost, ultimately affecting user comfort and satisfaction. Creating efficient and safe WPT systems with high material efficiency remains a key challenge, particularly for AI-based WPT systems. The inductive power transfer (IPT) system is the most widely used WPT technique for small mobile devices14. In IPT systems, a ferrite core enhances the magnetic coupling between the receiving (Rx) and transmitting (Tx) coils by increasing the magnetic flux linkage. Therefore, a ferrite core is crucial for maximizing the efficiency of transferred power. However, in mobile devices, the space available for components is minimal. This necessitates optimizing all components, including the ferrite core, within strict spatial constraints. Therefore, for mobile applications, optimizing components within strict spatial constraints is critical15. To address this, we propose a ferrite core structure design method that achieves high material efficiency under limited space.

A primary challenge in ferrite core engineering is the spatial non-uniformity of the magnetic flux density distribution. Previous research has addressed this by considering alignment, geometry, and permeability of the coil and core to enhance flux uniformity16–19. In20, asymmetrical alignment of the ferrite core was analyzed as a solution to non-uniformity. The major causes of non-uniformity are magnetic saturation caused by excessive magnetic flux density and low core utilization in areas with low magnetic flux density. The alignment, geometry, and permeability of the core influence these factors. However, previous studies often did not consider constraints on space and material usage. In many cases, especially for mobile devices, every element must fit within an extremely limited space, and material usage is restricted to meet human safety guidelines21,22. To address these challenges, we propose a ferrite core structure design method that explicitly considers space and material limitations.

As comprehensively summarized in the recent review on IoT-oriented WPT systems23, achieving high material efficiency and spatial optimization is a critical bottleneck for mobile devices. Our study directly addresses this challenge by introducing a highly processable, non-conductive ferrite sheet. Furthermore, the integration of advanced algorithms in WPT design is a rapidly growing trend. For instance, recent studies have successfully employed machine learning for hybrid parameter extraction24 and evolutionary algorithms for the complex optimization of cross-frequency metasurfaces25. Illustrating our novelty: Inspired by these data-driven and algorithmic trends, our work stands out by utilizing a Gaussian Process Regressor (GPR) specifically as a surrogate model to predict mutual inductance based on spatial geometries. Combined with a Genetic Algorithm (GA), our methodology actively exploits an asymmetrical layout strategy to overcome the inherent low-permeability limitation of thin sheets. This enables an approximately 5,000-fold acceleration in design time compared to conventional FEM, proving that high efficiency can be achieved not just by upgrading the material itself, but through AI-driven asymmetrical spatial redistribution.

In this paper, our ferrite core design aims to improve not only the uniformity of magnetic flux density but also the MI of the core, which is concerned with power transmission efficiency (PTE)26. The proposed method segments and assembles the ferrite core to achieve high MI using asymmetrical structures. While previous studies have focused on symmetrical ferrite core designs to enhance MI27–29, we demonstrate that asymmetrical structures can achieve higher MI by forming a more uniform magnetic flux between Rx and Tx coils. Symmetrical cores, such as those with y-axis or xy-axis symmetry, represent a limited subset of possible designs, whereas asymmetrical cases offer broader possibilities. By investigating asymmetric core structures through simulation and experiments, we show that they can provide higher MI, improved magnetic flux density uniformity, and reduced material usage. As a result, the proposed method yields core structures with higher material efficiency, lower weight, and reduced cost factors that enhance user satisfaction and mobility in mobile devices.

For WPT system optimization, reinforcement learning (RL) and GA are widely used28–31. However, both approaches require extensive data during optimization. FEM simulation tools, such as the Ansys Maxwell solver, are commonly used for iterative analysis and automation, but their high computational burden leads to inefficiency in the design process when using RL or GA. To overcome this, we develop a machine learning (ML) model to estimate MI much faster, replacing the simulation tool in the optimization process and enabling hundreds or thousands of iterations. The developed MI estimator is integrated into the GA as an objective function to optimize the ferrite core structure.

Ferromagnetic cores design approach with genetic algorithm

Genetic algorithm for WPT system optimization

A genetic algorithm (GA) has been adapted for various electromagnetics applications because it is suitable for optimization in complex search spaces37. In this study, the GA was used to design the optimal ferrite core structure. The ML-based MI estimation model is used as the objective function of the GA to reduce computational time. The highly nonlinear and computationally expensive simulation process is simplified, requiring less computational time, when the simulation tool is replaced by the ML estimation model as the objective function.

For the GA optimization process, an initial population is randomly initialized and set as the population size, and the fitness values are calculated using the objective function F(). Then, the parent, by parent ratio among the population, is selected based on fitness values. Individuals with high fitness values are selected as elite by the elite ratio and directly passed to the next population. Subsequently, children are produced from the selected parents by mutation, making random changes to a parent with mutation probability and crossover, combining a pair of parents with crossover probability . Finally, the population with children and elites is updated, and the same procedure is continued until satisfying t < η, where t is the number of iterations and η is the maximum number of iterations, or the improvement index n is lower than the maximum number of iterations without improvement ε.

GPR surrogate model

The ML model was implemented to estimate the MI with a low computational time. We select a GPR for the estimation model38. GPR incorporates a Gaussian process kernel that maps the original feature space onto a complex feature space. The kernel enables more efficient feature training by representing its complex feature space. Kernel parameters are optimized during the training session. The GPR model is implemented and trained using the scikit-learn Python library39, and the GPR estimator is trained with the simulation results to search for the ferrite structure of the optimal MI using an optimization function of the GA. A core structure with superior performance that was not included in the training data can be obtained using this model. This approach can be considered a form of one-class anomaly detection, which detects outliers using an ML model40.

The unit cell size of 5 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm was selected to match the minimum manufacturable ferrite segmentation and the coil pitch used in our prototype. The thickness (0.5 mm) corresponds to the commercial ferrite sheets adopted in the hardware. The spot matrix size 8 × 8 × 2, corresponding to the chosen cell size, reflects a trade-off between geometric resolution, manufacturability, and computational cost, rather than an arbitrary choice, while keeping the design space dimension. The full ferrite plate is discretized into unit cells of 5 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm. Each candidate core configuration is encoded as an 8 × 8 × 2 binary spot matrix X, where the 8 × 8 grid samples the effective coil footprint in the XY‑plane and the third dimension represents the Tx and Rx ferrite layers as shown in Fig. 9a. The MIs, corresponding to spot matrix sizes of as shown in Fig. 9b, are calculated by simulation for both the training and validation data. For each candidate core structure, the binary spot matrix X is passed to a Python script that parameterizes the ferrite geometry in Ansys Maxwell. The magnetostatic solver computes the inductance matrix of the two‑coil system, and the mutual inductance is obtained. This is equivalent to , where is the flux linkage of the receiver coil due to a fixed transmitter current . The entire workflow is automated in a Python script.

Computational cost and training

In the GA optimization process, the loop should be repeated thousands of times to obtain an optimization result. Since crossover and mutation are conducted in a short time, the GA’s computation time depends on the objective function’s calculation time. As described in Table 3, the per-sample calculation time is reduced from 203 to 86 µs by using the GPR estimator, which is 2 million times faster than that with the simulation tool. This fast calculation enables the GA to repeat the loop hundreds of times to determine the optimal ferrite core structure. For the GPR estimator training, initial data generation, which is computationally intensive, approximately 56 hours ( s), is required on an Intel i7-10700F CPU and 32GB RAM workstation. However, this is a one-time offline cost. Furthermore, the total computational time for GA optimization with 5000 populations and 1000 iterations is 5 thousand times faster with the GPR estimator. The computation time required to obtain the optimization result was approximately 55 min. However, with the simulation tool, it took approximately 2.7 million minutes to obtain an optimized result. It is almost impossible to set the population size to 5000. The optimization result achieved almost the best performance using the proposed design method. This enables the GA to perform an extensive search of the design space, which would be computationally infeasible otherwise.

While a natural reduction in the mutual inductance () was observed for all structures due to the decreased flux linkage under offset conditions, the optimized D1 structure consistently exhibited the highest normalized mutual inductance () across all tested positions. Specifically, despite having 14.85% less ferrite material, the D1 structure maintained a between 43.94nH and 46.52nH/%, significantly outperforming the reference C1 structure, which ranged from 37.41nH/% to 40.39nH/%. These results demonstrate that the GA-based asymmetrical optimization does not result in over-fitting to the perfectly aligned position. Instead, by redistributing the magnetic flux toward the outer regions, the D1 structure provides superior material efficiency and stable performance even in practical mobile charging scenarios where lateral offsets frequently occur.

The optimized ferrite core structure demonstrated that the asymmetrical structure can have higher material efficiency than the symmetrical structure. Through the simulation, it is demonstrated that the proposed asymmetric core configuration yields a more uniform magnetic flux density profile by increasing the flux density in the outer region between the windings. Consequently, by using the asymmetrical structure composed of lower material, the enhanced uniformity leads to equal MI with the symmetrical structure. The asymmetrical structure should be considered when incorporating a WPT system into devices for lower cost and weight. The proposed method optimized the ferrite core structure by considering these characteristics. The proposed method can be re-applied to the optimized structure by decomposing smaller units. However, only segmentation of 8 × 8 × 2 is conducted in this paper.

Conclusion

In this paper, GA-based optimization produced an asymmetrical ferrite core structure with improved uniformity of magnetic flux density, particularly by increasing the outer-region magnetic flux density. For the optimization process, the GPR surrogate model enabled rapid calculation of MI, accelerating the computation time by a factor of approximately 5000. The proposed core structure achieves equal MI to the symmetrical core structure and exhibits 17.44% higher normalized MI than the symmetrical structure at a 5 mm distance, as well as 15.20% higher normalized MI at a 10 mm distance and 16.38 % higher normalized MI at a 15 mm distance. Although the relative permeability of ferrite core material is lower than that of rigid ferrite blocks, the proposed asymmetrical design effectively overcomes this limitation by redistributing the magnetic flux toward the outer region, successfully maximizing material efficiency within the limited design space. Crucially, the robustness of the D1 structure was validated through lateral misalignment tests; it maintained superior material efficiency (43.94–46.52nH/%) compared to the reference C1 structure (37.41–40.39nH/%) under ± 5 mm offsets. This indicates that the designed structure uses less material, resulting in reduced weight and cost. Consequently, this approach broadens the scope of optimization for commercial IPT systems by enabling asymmetrical design structures. Moreover, the optimized core structure is implemented and validated with 5 mm unit segmentation, considering manufacturability, which means it is not limited to research but can also be produced in the real world. The proposed framework is designed to be extensible. The spot-matrix encoding and GPR-accelerated GA optimization are not restricted to the 8×8×2 grid or the 5mm unit size used here; finer discretization can be adopted by regenerating the FEM dataset and retraining the surrogate model. Likewise, the method can be applied to different coil geometries (e.g., rectangular, DD) or coil-to-coil distances by updating the simulation template. For the structures studied in this paper, the simulated coupling coefficient of the optimized asymmetric core D1 (0.02345) remains within 0.4% of that of the symmetric reference core C1 (0.02337), suggesting that the gain in normalized MI is obtained without a large increase in leakage flux.
