Effects of different transcranial magnetic stimulation coil types on phosphene thresholds and their association with motor cortex excitability
Authors: Halit Fidancı, Halil Can Alaydın, Cemre Cöddü, İrem Yakıcı, Zülfikar Arlıer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhosphenes can be induced by applying transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to the visual cortex. Since multiple factors influence phosphene perception, this study aimed to examine the effects of different TMS coil types on phosphene thresholds (PTs). Additionally, the relationship between PT and motor cortex excitability was explored.MethodsIn Session 1, TMS was applied to the left visual cortex of 22 healthy individuals using a round coil and a figure-eight coil, and PT was recorded. Resting motor threshold (RMT), active motor threshold (AMT), and short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) were assessed by stimulating the left motor cortex. After 5–7 days, the visual cortex was stimulated again in Session 2 with the same healthy individuals.ResultsIn Session 1, the mean PTs obtained with the round and figure-eight coils were 39.71 ± 9.67% (n = 17) and 53.93 ± 14.26% (n = 15), respectively (p = 0.001). In Session 2, the mean PTs were 37.83 ± 11.34% and 51.53 ± 14.03% (p = 0.001) for the round and figure-eight coils, respectively. The intraclass correlation coefficients for PTs across studies were 0.832 for the round coil and 0.591 for the figure-eight coil. No significant correlation was found between PT and AMT, RMT, or SICI.ConclusionPTs were lower with the round coil than with the figure-eight coil. The reliability of PTs can be considered good for round coil and moderate for figure-eight coil. Additionally, the findings suggested that motor cortex and visual cortex excitability are different entities.

Background

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a neurophysiological technique that enables noninvasive cortical stimulation [1, 2]. Various coil types, including round, figure-eight, and double-cone coils, are commonly used in TMS research. These coils differ in their stimulation properties and are primarily employed to assess motor cortex excitability and corticospinal tract function [1, 2]. Key neurophysiological measures reflecting motor cortex excitability include motor thresholds or paired-pulse TMS outcomes, while TMS-derived parameters such as central motor conduction time provide insights into corticospinal tract physiology [1–3].

Beyond the motor cortex, TMS can also be applied to other brain regions [4–7]. Phosphenes can be elicited by stimulating the visual cortex with TMS, and the minimum TMS intensity required to elicit phosphenes is defined as the phosphene threshold (PT) [4, 5, 7]. PT is widely regarded as a marker of visual cortex excitability, with studies reporting lower PT values in migraine patients compared to controls, suggesting altered excitability in migraine [8, 9]. The potential association of PT with excitability in other brain regions has also been questioned. To explore this, researchers have investigated the relationship between PT and motor thresholds. While some studies have found no association, others suggest a possible link [10–14].

While motor thresholds can be influenced by factors such as drowsiness, medication use, and muscle contraction, PT is similarly affected by variables including individual characteristics, some diseases like migraine, time for adaptation to darkness, ambient light, and whether the eyes are open or closed [1, 2, 8, 9, 12, 13, 15–17]. Understanding the factors influencing PT is crucial, as PT serves as a marker of visual cortex excitability and offers insights into the underlying physiology and cortical inhibition/facilitation balance. Since different coil types may exert varying effects on the brain, PTs obtained with different coils may also differ [1, 2, 16]. Moreover, the reliability of PTs may vary depending on the coil type, as PTs can also be influenced by factors related to learning or adaption [4, 5, 7]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether coil type influences PT. In the light of previous inconsistent findings regarding the association between PT and motor thresholds, a secondary objective of this study was to further explore their relationship through the assessment of short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) and motor thresholds.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This study was conducted in the Clinical Neurophysiology Laboratory of Adana City Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences. The study included 22 right-handed healthy individuals (15 males, 7 females) over the age of 18. The mean age of the healthy individuals was 31.64 ± 5.92 years (range: 25–45). The mean height, weight, and body mass index were 174.18 ± 9.00 cm (range: 156–190), 78.27 ± 18.78 kg (range: 54–117), and 25.50 ± 4.22 kg/m² (range: 19.72–33.95), respectively. Participants were required to have a normal neurological examination, normal visual acuity or normal corrected visual acuity, and no history of cerebrovascular disease, ocular pathology, cerebral lesions, neurodegenerative disorders, migraine, psychiatric conditions, or the use of psychiatric medications such as antidepressants. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Adana City Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (approval number: 9/305, date: 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design

In the first session (Session 1), TMS with round and figure-eight coils was applied to determine PT, resting motor threshold (RMT), active motor threshold (AMT), and SICI values. The order of coil application was randomized. Initially, TMS was administered to the visual cortex to elicit phosphenes. After a 10-minute interval, TMS with the first coil selected was applied to the motor cortex to determine RMT and AMT. SICI measurements were performed exclusively using the figure-eight coil. Following a 30-minute break, TMS was repeated with the alternate coil type. The second session (Session 2) involved TMS application to the visual cortex. There was a 5- to 7-day interval between the two studies, and the order of coil application in Session 2 was also randomized. Figure 1 depicts the experimental session design and workflow.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

TMS was delivered using a Magstim 2002 device for single-pulse protocols and a Magstim BiStim2 device for paired-pulse protocols (Magstim Co. Ltd, Whitland, Dyfed, Wales, United Kingdom). Motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were recorded with a Cadwell Sierra Summit electromyography (EMG) unit (Cadwell Laboratories, Kennewick, Washington, USA). TMS was applied with a 90 mm diameter round coil (Magstim TMS coil, electrical field at 20 mm from the coil surface: 174 V/m, peak magnetic field at 0 mm: 2.42 T; pulse width: 106µs) and a 70 mm diameter figure-eight coil (Magstim TMS coil, electrical field at 20 mm from the coil surface: 245 V/m, peak magnetic field at 0 mm: 2.11 T; pulse width: 86µs). Monophasic TMS pulses were delivered using both the round coil and the figure-eight coil for PT and motor thresholds. TMS-related side effects were documented, and participants were questioned regarding any discomfort experienced during the procedure.

Phosphene threshold

Before the sessions, healthy individuals were shown drawings depicting potential phosphene appearances [16]. Single-pulse TMS was applied while healthy individuals were seated. They were kept in a dark room for at least five minutes, with their eyes covered by a sleep eye mask. TMS single-pulses were delivered every 5 s at a fixed interval, with no variation throughout the procedure. A phosphene was considered valid if it appeared contralateral to the stimulus. Participants classified phosphenes as central if perceived in the center of the visual field, and as peripheral if perceived in the outer visual field [16]. Phosphenes should be perceived in at least 5 out of 10 stimulations with both the round coil and the figure-eight coil. Phosphene threshold (PT) was determined using the Modified Binary Search (MOBS) method [18, 19]. Initially, TMS was applied 2–7 cm above the inion, and moved 1–3 cm laterally depending on the phosphene perception [9, 13, 16, 17]. The study began with a TMS intensity set at 51% of the maximum stimulator output, with lower and upper limits defined as 0% and 100%, respectively. The TMS intensity at which a phosphene was perceived was assigned as the upper limit, while the intensity at which no phosphene was observed was assigned as the lower limit. The midpoint between these values was selected as the next TMS intensity. For example, if a participant did not perceive a phosphene at 51% but did at 76%, the next intensity was set at 64%. The minimum TMS intensity required to elicit a phosphene using this method was recorded as PT. At the conclusion of the study, participants were asked to describe their phosphene experience.

Motor thresholds and short-interval intracortical inhibition

TMS was applied to the left motor cortex as recommended [1, 2]. The low- and high-frequency filters were set to 20 Hz and 10 kHz, respectively. Participants kept their eyes open during TMS. MEPs were recorded from the right abductor pollicis brevis muscles using surface electrodes. MEP amplitude was measured from peak to peak. RMT and AMT were assessed during the rest of the right upper limb in a healthy individual and during approximately 20% maximal voluntary contraction of the hand muscles, respectively. The presence of muscle contraction was carefully checked on the monitor. RMT and AMT were defined as the minimum TMS intensity at which at least 5 out of 10 MEPs exceeded amplitudes of 50 µV and 200 µV, respectively. TMS intensity was initially set at 35% of maximum stimulator output and adjusted in 5% increments or decrements based on the target MEP amplitude. Once the TMS intensity eliciting appropriate MEPs was identified, it was reduced by 1% to determine the minimum TMS intensity.

The testing stimulus was set at 110% of RMT, whereas the conditioning stimulus was set at 70% of RMT for SICI study. During the SICI study, the right upper extremity of the healthy individual was at rest. Conditioning and testing stimuli were delivered at interstimulus intervals of 1ms, 3ms, and 5ms. For each condition, 10 MEPs were recorded. SICI was defined as the percentage reduction in mean MEP amplitude following paired stimulation compared to the mean MEP amplitude elicited by the testing stimulus alone.

Statistical analysis

Numerical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, and range, while categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage. The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was used to compare dependent variables. Effect sizes for Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests were computed using the formula r = Z / √N. In Session 1 and Session 2, PT values were evaluated for reliability using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). An ICC < 0.5, 0.5 < ICC < 0.75, 0.75 < ICC < 0.9, and ICC > 0.9 were interpreted as poor, moderate, good, and excellent reliability, respectively [20]. Spearman’s correlation was used for correlation analysis. The strength of the correlation was classified according to the correlation coefficient (r) as follows: r ≥ 0.9 / r≤-0.9: Very strong; 0.7 ≤ r < 0.9 / -0.9 < r≤-0.7: Strong; 0.4 ≤ r < 0.7 /-0.7 < r≤-0.4: Moderate; r < 0.4 / r>-0.4: Weak [21]. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0.

Results

The same healthy individuals participated in both Session 1 and Session 2. No adverse effects were noted following TMS application. None of the participants experienced discomfort sufficient to warrant discontinuation of the procedure. The RMT, AMT, and PT values measured using the round and figure-eight coils in Session 1 and Session 2 are presented in Table 1. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests comparing PTs between the round and figure-eight coils demonstrated large effect sizes, with r = 0.851 in Session 1 and r = 0.816 in Session 2. The number of healthy individuals exhibiting phosphene with the round coil in Session 1 and Session 2 were 17 (77.3%) and 18 (81.8%), respectively. The number of healthy individuals exhibiting phosphene with the figure-eight coil in Session 1 and Session 2 were 15 (68.2%) and 17 (77.3%), respectively. Phosphene was absent in 5 (22.7%) and 4 (18.2%) healthy individuals in Session 1 and Session 2, despite stimulation with both the round and figure-eight coils. Five healthy individuals experienced central phosphenes with the round coil, but peripheral phosphenes with the figure-eight coil in Session 1. The remaining healthy individuals experienced peripheral phosphenes with both the round coil and the figure-eight coil. One healthy individual who described central phosphenes with the round coil in Session 1 described peripheral phosphenes in Session 2.

Discussion

Given the physiological differences between round and figure-eight coils, this study aimed to compare the PTs obtained with these coil types. The PT measured with the round coil was lower than that obtained with the figure-eight coil. Additionally, the reliability of PT obtained with both coil types was found to be adequate. Despite ongoing debate, no correlation was observed between PT and motor cortex excitability [10–14].

TMS may contribute to the assessment of both visual and motor cortex excitability [1–5]. Several studies have investigated PT changes in various conditions, including migraine [8, 9, 17, 22]. It has been reported that PT is lower in individuals with migraine compared to controls [8, 9, 17]. In addition to pathological conditions, multiple factors such as ambient light and whether the individual’s eyes are open or closed can influence phosphene perception [1, 2, 8, 9, 12–15]. Stimulation parameters, including frequency and intensity, also play a role [23, 24]. In this study, PT values obtained with the round coil were lower than those obtained with the figure-eight coil in both Session 1 and Session 2. This finding may be attributed to the distinct physiological effects of different coil types on the cortex. TMS induces an electrical field in the brain, with characteristics such as penetration depth and focality being important considerations. Different coil designs generate magnetic fields of varying strength and configuration. For instance, the round coil produces a more powerful and widespread electrical field, while the figure-eight coil produces a more intense and focal electrical field. Additionally, coils differ in pulse width, which may affect the temporal characteristics of the stimulation. These coil-specific properties clearly support the findings of the current study. Although PT findings are expected to vary depending on coil characteristics, this study underscores the significance of this difference by highlighting its potential impact on diagnostic and therapeutic applications of TMS. The round coil appears to be more advantageous for eliciting phosphenes; however, the figure-eight coil may be preferred if more focal stimulation is desired. In a study comparing an iron-core coil with a figure-eight coil, phosphenes were elicited in all six participants using single-pulse stimulation with the iron-core coil, whereas phosphenes were reported in 8 out of 14 participants with the figure-eight coil [23]. The differences in PTs found in other studies may be related to coil type, supporting the notion that coil characteristics can influence PT [16, 25]. These findings suggest that phosphenes are more easily elicited using the round coil than the figure-eight coil. If the purpose of TMS is simply to demonstrate excitability of the visual cortex, as in migraine, the round coil may offer a practical advantage. However, when the goal is to stimulate a more focal region of visual cortex for diagnostic or therapeutic use, the figure-eight coil would likely be the more suitable option. In the application of treatment methods such as repetitive TMS targeting visual cortex, it is essential to recognize that PTs and stimulation distributions differ between round and figure-eight coils. These distinctions may lead to varying effects on visual cortex excitability.

Lower PT values have been observed with round coils ranging in diameter from 9 to 13.5 cm in both this study and previous research [9, 17, 24]. This may be explained by the round coil’s ability to stimulate a larger area of the visual cortex, including regions that generate more phosphenes. Conversely, the figure-eight coil’s more localized stimulation could contribute to the observed differences. Indeed, phosphene rates as high as 90% have been reported in some studies using the round coil [17]. However, in the present study, phosphene rates were approximately 77% and 68% for the round and figure-eight coils, respectively, in Session 1, and 82% and 77% in Session 2. The failure to reach a 90% phosphene rate with the round coil in this study may be due to methodological differences between studies [17, 23]. Notably, the round coil’s diameter in this study was 9 cm, whereas previous studies used coils ranging from 9.5 to 13 cm [9, 17, 24].

Although the exact source of phosphenes and the precise location of phosphene-generating neurons remains unclear, converging evidence from functional magnetic resonance imaging studies and other research suggests that the primary visual cortex (V1) is considered by many to be the most likely region, with additional contributions potentially arising from extrastriate areas, the distal portions of the optic radiation, and geniculostriate fibers [4, 7, 26]. This is further supported by evidence that phosphenes can be elicited through stimulation of areas outside V1 [4, 7, 26]. Even if phosphene-generating neurons are not directly targeted, they may become activated indirectly through their connections with stimulated regions or via the spread of excitatory effects induced by TMS within the stimulated area. The round coil’s ability to activate a larger number of phosphene-generating neurons may lead to temporal and spatial summation, contributing to cortical integration, thereby lowering the activation threshold and facilitating neuronal firing [27–29]. In contrast, at a similar stimulation intensity, the figure-eight coil stimulates fewer phosphene-generating neurons, potentially failing to reach the critical number required to induce phosphenes, thereby necessitating higher TMS intensity to surpass the threshold. This could also explain why phosphenes appear brighter with high stimulation intensity [9, 30]. As all these findings show, phosphenes can be elicited with both coil types under appropriate conditions and stimulation parameters. However, the figure-eight coil’s more localized stimulation area may make it more challenging to identify phosphene-generating regions, which could be a limitation for phosphene studies using this coil.

Studies have reported that PT reliability is generally good [4, 10–12, 24]. Similarly, the present study found that PT reliability was moderate with the figure-eight coil and good with the round coil. The correlation between PTs obtained in Session 1 and Session 2 was strong with the TMS round coil and good with the TMS figure-eight coil, supporting the reliability of PT. The slightly higher reliability observed with the round coil may be due to the previously mentioned difficulty in stimulating phosphene-generating neurons with the figure-eight coil. However, it should be noted that the round coil can also stimulate muscles, which may interfere with phosphene perception. In this present study, peripheral phosphenes were elicited with the figure-eight coil, whereas in some cases, central phosphenes were observed with the round coil. These findings may be attributed to differences in the cortical areas affected by the round and figure-eight coils [31, 32].

The inability to elicit phosphenes in some individuals may be attributed to individual variations in cortical topography, cortical morphology, or skull thickness [7]. In a small subset of healthy participants, phosphenes were not observed in Session 1 but were successfully elicited in Session 2. This finding suggests that phosphene perception may involve a learned component [4]. In contrast, none of the participants who perceived phosphenes in Session 1 failed to perceive them in Session 2. However, despite the learned nature of phosphene perception, the stability of PT across both studies in most individuals suggests that a specific threshold must be surpassed to activate phosphene-generating neurons. Notably, in participants who did not perceive phosphenes in Session 1 but did so in Session 2, PT values were not particularly low, ranging from approximately 60–70%.

PT was determined using the MOBS method, which was selected for its ease of application and its low likelihood of yielding false positives or negatives, given the careful selection of participants according to the study’s inclusion criteria [18, 19]. Nevertheless, future research employing alternative methods to assess PT with different coil types may yield novel insights. Moreover, only healthy individuals were included in the present study. Further investigations examining the impact of various coil types on PT in patient populations with conditions known to influence PT, such as migraine, may enhance our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and the mechanisms of phosphene generation in these disorders [8, 9, 17]. Should future studies demonstrate that phosphenes can be elicited significantly with the round coil but not with the figure-eight coil, this could suggest the presence of a localized abnormality within a small region of the visual cortex in the disease under investigation; nevertheless, future studies are required to confirm this hypothesis.

Unlike PT, motor thresholds obtained with the round coil and figure-eight coil did not differ. This raises the question of why motor thresholds with the round coil were not lower than those with the figure-eight coil, as seen with PT, given that the round coil stimulates a wider area. We believe this discrepancy can be explained by the distinct morphology and physiology of the motor and visual cortices. The absence of a correlation between motor thresholds and PT in this study, along with the finding that PT was influenced by coil type while motor thresholds remained unchanged, suggests fundamental differences between the motor and visual cortices [10–12]. Several studies have explored the correlation between PT and motor thresholds, yet failed to identify significant associations [10–12]. In contrast, another study and a meta-analysis have suggested a potential relationship between these measures [13, 14]. These inconsistencies in the literature may be explained by methodological variations across studies. This study extended previous research by not only investigating the relationship between PT and motor thresholds but also examining the correlation between PT and SICI. The lack of a significant association between these measures further supports the idea that motor and visual cortex excitability differ.

This study has some limitations. It was specifically designed to assess the reliability of PT across different coil types, and motor thresholds and SICI were not evaluated in Session 2. Additionally, the study did not include older participants, which may be considered a limitation. Although comprehensive studies investigating the relationship between age and phosphene perception are lacking, PT may be affected by the age-related decline in perceptual sensitivity. In this regard, it should be considered that age and individual characteristics may influence the size or function of visual cortex [31–33]. Future research involving patient populations as well as older adults may provide valuable insights into both the pathophysiology of these conditions and the physiological mechanisms underlying phosphene perception. Finally, although participants did not report any discomfort during the TMS sessions, the lack of a specific questionnaire addressing discomfort or pain constitutes a potential limitation.

Conclusion

This study showed that PT differs between coil types. It also found that PT reliability was good with the round coil but moderate with the figure-eight coil. These findings may be explained by the fact that the round coil produces a stronger stimulus and affects a wider area of the visual cortex compared to the figure eight coil, thereby facilitating easier neuronal firing through mechanisms such as temporal and spatial summation. Thus, the round coil offers an advantage for general phosphene elicitation, whereas the figure-eight coil provides a better option for achieving more precise and focal stimulation. Additionally, the results indicated no relationship between PT and motor cortex excitability.
