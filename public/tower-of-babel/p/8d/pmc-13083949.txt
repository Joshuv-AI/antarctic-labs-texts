Improving non-expert performance in musculoskeletal MRI protocoling through a large language model
Authors: Sungwon Lee, Hyeondeok Choi, Keum San Chun, Seungeun Lee, Hyemin Park, Tuan Dinh Le, Pritam Mukherjee, Jungwoo Lee, Soy Chang, So-Yeon Lee, Joon-Yong Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMRI protocoling, the process of selecting appropriate sequences, is especially complex in musculoskeletal (MSK) cases. This study evaluates whether a large language model (LLM) can effectively assist non-experts in MRI protocoling. We collected two retrospective datasets of musculoskeletal (MSK) MRI orders: (1) a prompt-development dataset (Jan–Dec 2023; 538 cases) and (2) a testing dataset (Jan–Jun 2024; 107 cases). A prompt was refined on GPT-4o via the web-based ChatGPT interface to use patient information and complete an MRI protocol worksheet. For the testing dataset, 6 non-experts (three radiology residents [RD] and three radiographers [RG]) completed the worksheet with and without LLM-assistance. All worksheets were scored using a clinical pass system, categorizing responses as “excellent (4 points),” “acceptable (3 points),” “insufficient (2 points),” or “failure (1 point)” based on potential outcomes. We compared clinical pass scores between LLM-assisted and human-only worksheets using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a mixed linear model. Fleiss’ kappa across five iterations was measured for stochasticity. LLM-assisted worksheets had higher average clinical pass scores than human-only worksheets for both RD (3.42 vs. 3.18, p < 0.001) and RG (3.06 vs. 2.9, p < 0.001). LLM-assistance decreased the proportion of “insufficient” and “failure” cases, potentially decreasing MRI re-checks, with an average reduction of 12.2% (13 out of 107) for RD and 8% (8.7 out of 107) for RG. This improvement was consistent across professions (p = 0.43). LLM outputs showed strong reliability across five iterations (κ = 0.66). Introduction

“MRI protocoling” is the process of selecting appropriate sequences for an upcoming MRI exam to ensure the targeted anatomy and abnormalities are properly scanned1. While the American College of Radiology provides appropriateness criteria for imaging recommendations2, these serve only as broad guidelines, such as specifying image modality type (e.g., radiography, CT, MRI) and contrast use without detailing protocol adjustments.

MRI protocoling for musculoskeletal (MSK) imaging is unique, requiring more complex decision-making than protocoling for other parts : tailoring the field of view (FOV), predicting artifacts from metallic implants or body geometry, and handling MSK-specific sequences3. MSK imaging encompasses a wide anatomical and pathological spectrum with its own protocol variations. As a result, institutions often manage hundreds of distinct MRI protocols. Moreover, these protocols are diversified through institution-specific feedback loops between MSK radiologists and referring clinicians, rather than being standardized. At our institution, MSK radiologists dedicate at least one hour daily to MRI protocoling, and similar time commitments have been reported at other institutions1. As workloads increase and specialist shortages persist, this time-intensive task is increasingly delegated to non-experts. However, errors in protocoling can lead to repeated scans, causing patient inconvenience and workflow disruptions4. Therefore, there is a growing interest in using artificial intelligence to assist non-experts in MSK MR protocoling when direct specialist input is unavailable5,6.

Natural language processing and machine learning have previously been used to identify routine protocols7 and contrast enhancement needs8. Large language models (LLMs) have recently shown progress in determining the need for imaging9 and selecting the appropriate modality, body region, and contrast phase for head, chest, and body scans10. However, MSK MRI protocoling is significantly more complicated. Our institution alone has over 100 unique MSK MRI protocols, each requiring detailed customization of FOV, metal artifact reduction sequences (MARS), and fat saturation (FS) methods. Unlike prior studies, this complexity cannot be addressed with simple zero-shot prompts. Furthermore, real-world medical data contain typos, uncommon acronyms, and multilingual elements, further limiting the use of a general LLM prompt.

Recent LLMs trained on extensive datasets can handle longer multilingual prompts, which are features that may support our complex task11. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of a commercially available LLM in assisting non-experts with MSK MRI protocoling using a customizable institution-specific prompt.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (Approval No. KC24RISI0528). The need for informed consent was waived by the committee due to its retrospective nature.

Data collection

Two retrospective datasets were used to refine and test the prompt (Table 1). For the prompt-development dataset, 12,982 consecutive MSK MRI orders were collected from Jan to Dec 2023. This included 96 unique MRI study names (e.g., “MRI Ankle Left Enhance”), 121 protocol types (e.g., “Routine protocol”), ordered from 29 departments (e.g., “Orthopedic”). A “protocol” represented a specific combination of MRI sequences and parameters. Each study name was associated with one to six protocol types, totaling 260 protocols (Example in Fig. S1). To create a balanced dataset for prompt tuning, we randomly selected one MRI study name per protocol per ordering department, resulting in a down-sampled dataset of 538 MRI orders. For the testing dataset, 5,158 MSK MRI orders from Jan to Jun 2024 were down-sampled to 107, preserving 82 study names and 107 protocols (study flow diagram in Fig. 1). For each MRI order, we extracted patient information from the electronic medical record (EMR) and picture archiving and communication system (PACS), including study name, clinician’s comments, relevant radiology reports, and recent medical reports (Table S4). All data were compiled and anonymized by a research assistant with two years of clinical experience. Data access through EMR and PACS systems was conducted from August 15 to 20, 2024. After this period, the authors no longer had access to any information that could identify individual participants.

Reference standard MRI protocoling

An MSK radiologist with 15 years of experience (Joon-Yong Jung) reviewed the patient information and created reference protocol worksheets (Table S4) including: recommended protocol name, FOV, MARS use, target location, skin marker use, T2-weighted fat suppression (T2F) orientation, FS methods, and coil type. A second MSK radiologist with six years of experience (Seungeun Lee) reviewed the reference protocol worksheets, and any disagreement was resolved by consensus.

The radiologist (J) also developed a clinical pass scoring system, which categorized the potential outcomes of the MRI protocoling worksheet as: Failure (requires complete MRI re-do), Insufficient (requires partial MRI re-check), Acceptable (minor adjustments needed but generally adequate), and Excellent (matches the reference standard) (Table S5).

Prompt engineering for MRI protocol generation

All data processed through the LLM, including prompts, inputs, and outputs, were originally written in English. GPT-4o (OpenAI, trained on publicly available web data up to October 2023)12 was used to develop and test the prompt. The model was accessed via the web-based ChatGPT interface. No parametric adaptation (e.g., fine-tuning or weight modification) was performed. Temperature and other hyperparameters were not user-adjustable in the web interface and therefore, were not controlled. All outputs were generated under the platform’s default settings. This approach was intentionally chosen to reflect a real-world clinical environment, in which radiologists, clinicians, or radiographers would access a chatbot through a standard internet interface rather than via application programming interface (API) integration or local model deployment.

To prevent data leakage between the prompt-development and testing phases, continuous training was avoided. Prompt refinement was performed from August 23 to September 5, 2024, and testing was conducted from September 11 to 23, 2024. Each query was processed in a new, independent chat session, with intersession memory disabled to prevent contextual carryover (example of LLM output in Fig. S2).

Using the prompt-development dataset, we refined a prompt that outputs the protocoling worksheet based on patient information. The prompt engineering approach was done through the following stages. First, we tested a single, one-shot “tree-of-thought” style prompt with numbered branching instructions (e.g., “If some condition, go to #”). However, this format resulted in unstable adherence to branching logic and inconsistent outputs. Next, we implemented a simple three-step pipeline (key information extraction → protocol selection → comment generation) that separated information extraction from rule-based protocol mapping. While conceptually cleaner, this approach proved fragile and frequently failed when extraction was incomplete or inconsistent. We then evaluated a ChatGPT “Create GPT” configuration that embedded the protocol table and abbreviations as internal knowledge (RAG-style). Although this improved the retrieval of protocol options, rule compliance remained variable. The final prompt integrated the strengths of these prior attempts by: (i) explicitly constraining the task with fixed inputs; (ii) providing domain knowledge inline (abbreviations and recommendation table); (iii) decomposing the problem into ordered subtasks (reason → protocol → FOV → hardware/MARS → standardized comments → brief rationale); and (iv) encoding explicit decision rules and exceptions (priority rules, tie-breakers, and “do-not-use” conditions) within a strict output template. Together, these refinements improved determinism, reduced hallucinations, and increased consistency across cases. The entire prompt engineering process took less than 2 weeks. During testing, five consecutive outputs were generated from the same prompt for each case to assess response variability (stochasticity)13. One of the five outputs was randomly selected and provided in the corresponding LLM-assisted session by non-experts. If the model declined to provide a response (e.g., “I am not a medical doctor…”), an additional query was made.

Evaluation of protocoling performance and worksheet scoring

Three radiology residents (RD1, RD2, RD3, with 0–1 year of radiology experience) and three radiographers (RG1, RG2, RG3, with 0–1 year of MRI experience) were recruited to represent non-experts in MSK MRI protocoling. Prior to the evaluation, participants completed a two-hour pre-training course led by an expert MSK radiologist (J) and received an instruction sheet containing the same information used in the prompt (Tables S7 and S8).

The non-experts reviewed the testing dataset in two sessions with a 6-months washout period. Each participant reviewed 107 cases, divided into two batches (split method in Table S9). In the first session, participants completed one batch independently without LLM, and the other with LLM-assistance. In the second session, the order of LLM-assistance was reversed (“Cross over evaluation” in Fig. 1).

Two MSK radiologists (Sungwon Lee, So-Yeon Lee) independently reviewed worksheets generated by the non-experts (human-only and LLM-assistance), and the five LLM outputs. For the worksheets generated by non-experts, both radiologists were blinded to whether they were completed with or without LLM assistance. Each worksheet component was marked correct or incorrect based on the reference standard, and an overall clinical pass score was assigned. After the independent evaluation, cases with discrepant marking and clinical pass score were reviewed jointly, and a consensus was made.

Statistical Analysis

To visualize changes in clinical pass scores between human-only and LLM-assisted worksheets, a Sankey plot was generated (networkD3, R 4.3.3). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test (stats, R 4.3.3) was used to assess statistically significant (p < 0.05) clinical pass score differences between human-only and LLM-assisted conditions. A mixed linear model (statsmodels.formula.api, Python 3.8) was used to compare the impact of LLM-assistance between different professions (RD vs. RG) and to account for individual variability Although the clinical pass score is ordinal (1–4), the model assumes that adjacent categories represent approximately equal clinical increments, corresponding to progressively increasing adequacy of protocol quality.

The difference in clinical pass scores between human-only and LLM-assisted conditions was categorized as “Improved” (score increase or correction of an error with LLM-assistance), “Worsened” (score decrease or new error with LLM-assistance), or “Same” (no change). These categories were analyzed for all participants and visualized using a stacked bar plot (ggplot2, R 4.3.3).

To evaluate the influence of LLM performance on human performance, the median score of the five LLM outputs was calculated and compared to the participant’s performance change using a grouped bar plot (ggplot2, R 4.3.3). Additionally, non-experts completed questionnaires assessing the perceived level of assistance provided by the LLM (Fig. S3).

To evaluate the reliability of human scoring, interrater agreement between the two MSK radiologists for the clinical pass score was calculated using weighted kappa.

To evaluate factors potentially influencing LLM performance, correlations between prompt length and median clinical pass scores (across five LLM iterations) were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Differences in LLM clinical pass scores across ordering departments and body parts were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Differences between contrast-enhanced and non-enhanced MRI examinations were assessed using the chi-square test.

To assess stochasticity, Fleiss’ weighted kappa (14) (irr, boot, R 4.3.3) was calculated for the clinical pass scores and each worksheet component across the five LLM iterations.

Results

Dataset

The prompt-development dataset included 538 MRI orders (538 patients), while the testing dataset comprised 107 MRI orders (107 patients). Table 1 provides detailed demographic and clinical information for both datasets. The prompts used in the testing dataset had a median length of 17,356 tokens (interquartile range, 17,126–17,606).

Impact of LLM-assistance on clinical pass scores

Compared to human-only worksheets, LLM-assisted worksheets had significantly higher average clinical pass scores in RD (3.18 vs. 3.42, Wilcoxon p < 0.001) and RG (2.9 vs. 3.06, Wilcoxon p < 0.001). Inter-rater agreement between the two MSK radiologists for the clinical pass score was almost perfect (weighted κ = 0.86, 95% confidence interval 0.85–0.88), supporting the reliability of the scoring system.

With LLM-assistance, the proportion of cases rated as “Excellent” increased by an average of 12.1% (13 out of 107) for RD and 6.8% (7.3 out of 107) for RG (Fig. 2). Conversely, the proportion of cases rated as “Failure” and “Insufficient” (which are cases with potential MRI re-check), decreased by an average of 12.2% (13 out of 107) for RD and 8% (8.7 out of 107) for RG.

Impact of LLM-assistance on specific worksheet components

The improvement pattern due to LLM-assistance was consistent across all worksheet components and professions (see Fig. S4). A questionnaire (Fig. S3, Question 1) revealed that non-experts found the “Additional comment section” (containing target location, marker, T2F orientation, FS method, and coil type) to be the most helpful feature. The “Reasoning and explanations” provided by the LLM were rated as the second most beneficial.

Regarding the frequency of recalling LLM-provided answers from previous cases during human-only evaluation sessions (Fig. S3, Question 4), non-experts reported doing so on average 20.1% of the time, with 10.3% for RD and 29.8% for RG.

Influence of LLM-only performance level on human performance

Non-expert performance outcomes were generally stable across LLM-only clinical pass scores (Fig. 3, Table S10). With LLM-assistance, human scores improved when the LLM-only score was “Excellent” by 16.9%, “Acceptable” by 24.5%, “Insufficient” by 11.5%, and “Failure” by 16.7%. The human scores worsened when the LLM-only score was “Excellent” by 2.2%, “Acceptable” by 3.9%, “Insufficient” by 7.7%, and “Failure” by 4.2%.

Additionally, non-experts reported feeling more influenced when the LLM’s responses aligned with their initial answers compared to when they disagreed (likely to incorporate LLM’s response to non-expert’s initial response: mean 90% vs. 75.8%) (Fig. S3, Questions 2 and 3).

Incorrect LLM outputs

The most frequent incorrect recommendations involved fat-saturation (FS) technique (18/107 cases) and target location specification (16/107 cases) (Table S11). The majority of incorrect outputs resulted from minor misinterpretations of the provided clinical information, which were typically identifiable in the reasoning sections of the LLM responses. Representative examples of these errors are presented in Figures S5 to S12. Notably, there was one case of true confabulation (1/107), in which the LLM falsely altered the provided data (Fig. S13). In this instance, for a leg MRI ordered to evaluate a femur fracture, the LLM incorrectly recommended a MARS protocol for “potential internal fixation in the femur,” despite no such surgical history being indicated. Notably, all non-expert evaluators correctly identified this error and disregarded the recommendation.

LLM performance was not significantly influenced by prompt length or MRI-related clinical characteristics. There was no correlation between prompt length and median clinical pass scores (Spearman p = 0.445). Additionally, clinical pass scores did not differ significantly across ordering departments or body parts (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.312 and p = 0.189, respectively), nor between contrast-enhanced and non-enhanced MRI examinations (chi-square p = 0.391) (Fig. S14). These results suggest that the LLM’s clinical performance remained stable regardless of input length or MRI examination type.

Consistency analysis of LLM outputs

The LLM’s clinical pass scores were substantially consistent across five iterations (κ = 0.66, confidence intervals 0.57–0.744, Table 4). In 55.1% of cases (59/107), the LLM consistently achieved an “Excellent” rating across all five iterations. A combined “Excellent” or “Acceptable” rating was observed in 70.1% of cases (75/107) across all iterations. When considering at least four out of five iterations, “Excellent” scores were achieved in 62.6% of cases (67/107), and “Excellent” or “Acceptable” scores in 77.6% of cases (83/107). The consistency for individual worksheet components ranged from 83.2% (FS) to 98.1% (MARS) (Table S12).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that LLM assistance improves non-expert performance in MRI protocol selection. Worksheets completed with LLM assistance achieved significantly higher average clinical pass scores than human-only worksheets for both residents (RD: 3.42 vs. 3.18, p < 0.001) and radiographers (RG: 3.06 vs. 2.90, p < 0.001). Although the absolute score increases were modest, the clinically meaningful finding was the reduction in “Failure” and “Insufficient” ratings-categories associated with potential partial or complete MRI re-checks. With LLM assistance, these at-risk categories decreased by 12.2% in residents and 8% in radiographers. Operationally, in a clinical setting processing approximately 40 MSK MRI orders per day, this reduction corresponds to roughly 3–5 fewer at-risk protocols daily, potentially improving workflow efficiency and reducing patient inconvenience.

Prior work has demonstrated that protocol selection can be supported by task-specific deep-learning models for MSK MRI protocol determination and natural language processing–based tools for predicting intravenous contrast requirement in MSK MRI examinations7,8. More recently, several studies have explored the use of LLMs for MRI protocoling tasks. Çamur et al. evaluated multiple LLMs for MRI acquisition protocol recommendations based on a national imaging guideline for MRI and CT across body regions, demonstrating that LLMs can generate protocol suggestions in response to both case-based and open-ended questions14. In MSK MRI protocoling, Hallinan et al. reported that LLM-alone performance was comparable to, or slightly worse than that of first-year residents (78.4% vs. 89.2%–90.0% overall) in spine MRI15. However, that study assessed only three protocol components (region of spine, contrast use, and instrumentation detection). The same group subsequently expanded this approach to broader MSK MRI protocoling using an institutional LLM, reporting protocoling accuracy of 95.8%, which was significantly higher than that of non–MSK-trained radiologists (88.2–88.6%)16. While these studies provide important evidence supporting the feasibility of LLMs in protocoling tasks, they primarily focused on LLM-alone performance using accuracy-based metrics. In contrast, our study specifically focused on the impact of LLM-assistance on human decision-making across 260 MSK protocol types, each with eight tailored components. Moreover, we employed a clinically grounded outcome measure-the clinical pass score-which reflects real-world consequences. This design emphasizes the operational impact of LLM assistance in practical settings where LLMs are more likely to augment, rather than replace, human protocoling.

Because the web-based interface does not provide access to decoding parameters (e.g., temperature or top-p), generation behavior could not be directly controlled. To account for stochastic variability under these default settings, we evaluated output consistency using Fleiss’ kappa across five independent outputs per case. Although no universal standard exists for assessing LLM reproducibility, this approach provides a practical estimate of functional consistency. Given that both multiple iterations and four ordinal outcome categories tend to lower kappa values, the observed κ = 0.66 likely underestimates practical consistency. Notably, more than 95% of protocol components demonstrated 4/5 or 5/5 agreement, indicating strong real-world reliability despite algorithmic variability.

In human-in-the-loop clinical workflows, the effectiveness of an LLM is determined not only by the model’s standalone accuracy but also by how users interpret, and incorporate its recommendations. A key finding of our study is that LLM performance did not significantly worsen non-expert performance. Across all cases, performance worsened in only 3.7% of evaluations, while 17.5% improved and 78.8% remained unchanged. This suggests that non-experts were able, in many instances, to maintain or improve their performance despite imperfect AI outputs. However, even in cases where the LLM performance was rated as “Failure” or “Insufficient” cases, approximately 80% of participant decisions remained aligned with the LLM output, suggesting the presence of cognitive biases. Notably, radiographers demonstrated higher reliance rates compared with residents, which may reflect differences in clinical and contextual understanding. Questionnaire responses also supported that substantial influence when LLM responses differed from participants’ initial judgments (mean influence score 75.8/100), suggesting automation bias17. Furthermore, reinforcement was stronger when LLM responses aligned with initial judgments (mean 90/100), consistent with confirmation bias18. These findings indicate that such cognitive bias may influence decision-making during LLM-assisted protocoling. However, survey responses suggest that explanation-based outputs may help mitigate blind reliance on LLM recommendations. The explanations or reasoning provided by the LLM supported decision-making and enabled participants to identify and correct errors, such as a case of LLM confabulation (Fig. S13). Therefore, strengthening explanation-based prompting may help reduce blind acceptance of LLM outputs.

Although not our initial aim, we observed that the LLM-generated summaries of the “reason for MRI” were often more structured and comprehensive than the original clinician comments. We propose that these summaries could be integrated into the MRI reading stage, providing radiologists with a more structured and comprehensive clinical context compared to the original clinician comments. This integration could improve communication and diagnostic accuracy, ultimately benefiting clinicians, radiologists, and patients.

The institution-specific prompt was developed through iterative refinement over less than two weeks and remained stable throughout one year of data collection without major modification. Although this required upfront effort, the observed improvement in non-expert performance and reduction in at-risk protocols suggest that the initial time investment may be balanced by downstream efficiency gains, including fewer protocol corrections and reduced need for repeat imaging.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not use an API-based setup or a fine-tuned, standalone LLM. The model was accessed through the web interface, in which generation parameters are neither user-accessible nor disclosed. We intentionally used this easily accessible interface to reflect real-world usage by non-expert users at the time of the study. However, commercial web-based LLM systems have increasingly incorporated hidden system prompts and additional software layers that cannot be controlled by users and may interact with institution-specific prompts. In addition, the model may drift over time as part of platform updates. Therefore, strict reproducibility of outputs cannot be guaranteed. Future work would be strengthened by API-based implementations that allow clear documentation of model identifiers and stable control of generation settings.

Second, we did not employ retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)19 to dynamically access external knowledge bases. Despite attempts to split the query and information using RAG, we encountered multiple errors in correctly retrieving data with the built-in RAG system. This could be explained by retrieval failure in larger knowledge bases due to information dilution of the traditional RAG20,21, Instead, we embedded static information, such as an abbreviation table and protocol recommendation list, directly within the prompt context. While this approach potentially limits the adaptability of LLM to new or evolving MRI protocols, it provides a more controlled environment for rule adherence, contributing to consistent and reliable outputs.

Third, the prompt was not tested on an external dataset, which may limit generalizability. The protocol recommendation table, abbreviation list, and reference standards were developed based on institution-specific MSK MRI practices. Implementation in other institutions would require adaptation of the prompt structure, protocol tables, and evaluation criteria. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating feasibility within a single institutional context rather than establishing generalizable performance across diverse clinical environments.

Fourth, errors in MRI protocoling do not all carry equal clinical risk. Our clinical pass score was designed to reflect real-world clinical consequences in terms of scan adequacy. However, it does not explicitly weight different error types by their potential diagnostic or safety impact. Future work could refine the scoring systems to better capture risk asymmetry.

Lastly, although only anonymized patient information was used and available privacy options were applied, reliance on a commercial web-based LLM involves external data processing. This limitation also constrained deeper clinical integration, as direct automated access to non-anonymized EMR data was not feasible under current institutional governance and privacy requirements. As a result, this study adopted manual data selection and processing rather than an automated pipeline, which may limit scalability. For clinical deployment, privacy-preserving on-premise deployment using open-weight models (e.g., Llama or Gemma/MedGemma) may represent a more attainable approach by secure internal data processing and automated integration with hospital systems.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that LLM assistance can improve non-expert performance in institution-specific MSK MRI protocoling. This finding suggests that LLM-based protocol support may help reduce suboptimal protocol recommendations that could lead to partial or complete MRI re-checks, particularly in settings where MSK radiologist input is limited.
