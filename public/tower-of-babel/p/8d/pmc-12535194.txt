Passion, Perfectionism, and Sports Commitment as Predictors of Exercise Addiction
Authors: Bhavya Chabbra, Adél Bartos, István Soós, Roberto Ruíz‐Barquín, Ricardo de la Vega, Attila Szabo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExercise addiction is studied along with behavioral addictions but has no diagnostic criteria mainly because dysfunctional cases have incomparable etiologies, and research is based on a presumed risk assessment that may never turn into dysfunction. The scalar measurements of the risk of exercise addiction (REA) share substantial variance with passion, perfectionism, and sports commitment. However, the extent to which their subdomains jointly determine the REA is unknown. Consequently, this study aimed to fill this gap and then evaluated possible group differences (gender, competition status, and individual vs. team sports), intending to emphasize the need to control these covariates of REA to avoid false interpretations based on simple group comparisons. An international sample (mean age 31.65 (± SD = 14.48) of 1003 regular exercisers (46.86% males)) completed validated instruments to assess the REA, its predictors, and exercise characteristics. Although all measures showed medium to strong correlations with the REA, a bootstrapped hierarchical regression yielded six predictors (exercise volume and intensity, harmonious and obsessive passion, rigid perfectionism, and constrained commitment), sharing 42.7% of the variance with the REA. Three bootstrapped univariate tests yielded gender, competition status, and sports‐form differences in REA when the predictors were not included in the model as covariates. However, all group differences vanished when the predictors were controlled. Our results suggest a conceptual unclarity between what is currently conceptualized as REA and its covariates. More of the latter could entirely obscure the impact of REA. Hence, research should develop more specific tools to assess exercise addiction as a potential dysfunction.Keywords: behavioral addiction, competition, exercise dependence, gender, team

Introduction

Exercise addiction is characterized by uncontrolled self‐harming and obligatory exercise or sports training and manifests the typical symptoms of addictions (Juwono and Szabo 2020). For unknown reasons, exercise addiction is a widely studied topic within psychology and sports sciences based on over 1000 scholarly publications located five years ago (Szabo and Kovacsik 2019). Nevertheless, Juwono and Szabo (2020) found fewer than a dozen clinically problematic cases in academic literature, which does not underpin the necessity of extensive research in this area. To address this imbalance, Szabo and Demetrovics (2022) stressed that the voluminous publications, emerging primarily from cross‐sectional research, only assess a “risk” of exercise addiction based on numerical cutoff points, which have no clinical diagnostic value. Indeed, the high prevalence rates of exercise addiction (i.e., > 10%) in some sports (Di Lodovico et al. 2019) would mean that one in 10 athletes suffers from clinical dysfunction if the research assessment tools had diagnostic capabilities.

However, a maladaptive exercise involving pain and suffering exists based on reports and testimonials located on the internet. Juwono and Szabo (2020) searched the World Wide Web until they located 100 cases exhibiting self‐harm at either social, psychological, or physical levels and manifesting the typical symptoms in the components model of addiction (Griffiths 2005). The authors provided functional links to each case at the time of publication. However, only a few or none of these cases would surface in survey‐type research on exercise addiction, and no details about the etiology of the dysfunction would emerge through cross‐sectional research. Accordingly, Szabo and Demetrovics (2022) have identified a gap between clinical practice and academic research, one focusing on treatment and the other on theory. Only close collaboration between the two, based on clinical interviews following up the cases identified as high risk in research, could be fruitful in bridging this gap between clinicians and researchers (Szabo and Demetrovics 2022) and pave the road for the necessary clinical evidence for including exercise addiction in medical reference manuals (Sicilia et al. 2023).

Recent research has increasingly emphasized the role of psychological risk factors in the development and persistence of exercise addiction. Gori et al. (2021) found that alexithymia indirectly influences exercise addiction through body image concerns. However, high self‐esteem served as a protective factor, reducing the effect of alexithymia on body dissatisfaction. Similarly, Sicilia et al. (2018) found that obsessive passion positively mediated the relationship between both self‐determined and nonself‐determined forms of motivation and exercise addiction, whereas harmonious passion negatively mediated this relationship. These findings suggest a complex interaction between psychological factors and passion in the development of problematic exercise behavior. Together, these studies suggest that there is a need for a better understanding of the psychological profiles that predispose individuals to exercise addiction. Consequently, this study aims to explore the relationships between passion, perfectionism, and sports commitment, providing further insights into the possible underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon.

This study is timely because it addresses a conceptual problem inherent in the current assessment tools of the risk of exercise addiction (REA), which share a significant portion of the variance with several other measures, such as passion or perfectionism (Çakın et al. 2021; de la Vega et al. 2020). For example, Szabo (2018) found that a female bodybuilder claiming to be addicted to exercise scored high on two measures of REA without any sign of dysfunction. However, she also reported high passion and commitment to exercise. Further, de la Vega et al. (2020) found that the REA was inversely related to the changes in exercise volume during the COVID‐19 pandemic. However, after controlling passion and perfectionism, this relationship vanished. Furthermore, Szabo and Kovacsik (2019) reported that high‐ and low‐volume exercise groups differed in REA, but the difference disappeared after controlling for passion. Still, passion differentiated between the two groups.

Indeed, passion seems to overlap with the REA to various extents. Testing a Hungarian sample, Kovacsik, Griffiths, et al. (2018) reported that exercise intensity, obsessive passion (OP), and harmonious passion (HP) shared 38% of the variance with REA, with OP being the most influential, accounting for almost 32%. In another Hungarian study, the relationship between passion and REA emerged as a function of the sports context. For example, OP and HP explained 25% of the variance in the REA in team sports, whereas the figure was double (50%) in individual sports (Kovacsik, Soós, et al. 2018). In another study involving nine, mostly Spanish‐speaking nations, OP and HP predicted 29.8% after controlling. for age, gender, and exercise volume (Szabo et al. 2022). In this study, OP and HP were both moderately associated with REA. In contrast, Lichtenstein et al. (2020) found that OP predicted 48% of the variance, whereas HP only 1% in the REA, in a large sample of Danish fitness attendees. Furthermore, de la Vega et al. (2016) reported that OP predicted 37% of the variance in Spanish athletes. However, their dedication to sport was also a significant predictor, accounting for 25% of the variance in the REA. In this work, HP was unrelated to the REA.

As described above, several reports in the literature have demonstrated that passion, primarily obsessive and, to a lesser extent, harmonious passion, share significant variances with the REA. In contrast, only a few investigations have tested the impact of dedication to sports or commitment to exercise on the REA scores. The shortage of inquiries in this direction might be related to the position of scholars conjecturing that commitment to exercise and exercise addiction are distinct (Landolfi 2012; Sachs 1981; Szabo and Demetrovics 2022). The addicted exerciser is, in fact, “highly committed” to exercise due to the obligatory nature of the exercise, which controls the affected person. Indeed, long ago, Farrell and Thompson (1998) have shown that commitment to exercise predicts the REA, a finding that agrees with de la Vega et al. (2016). It is possible that instruments assessing REA may be “contaminated” by the subjective interpretations merely conveying high commitment. As Szabo et al. (2015) illustrated, salience, which is assessed (Based on the Exercise Addiction Inventory (Terry et al. 2004)) on a 1 to 5 Likert scale in response to the statement “Exercise is the most important thing in my life” in the components model (Griffiths 2005), can be interpreted by addicted exercisers and ambitious athletes differently, for example, “I cannot manage my life without exercise” or alternately “I want to become the best or to achieve my personal best” (p. 307). Although the former interpretation reflects dependence on (obligatory) exercise, the other mirrors high dedication and an inner desire to reach a personal mastery goal.

About a quarter of a century ago, Leedy (2000) studied 239 American runners and found that high commitment to running was positively related to mental health but not to the negative aspects of exercise addiction. In contrast, Hyun and Yoo (2015), such as Farrell and Thompson (1998), reported that exercise commitment was significantly related to the exercise addiction scores of 151 Korean weight‐training individuals. Furthermore, a recent study with 134 Iranian elite athletes found that sports commitment explained 29% of the variance in REA (Ghabel Damirchi et al. 2020). Therefore, sufficient empirical evidence justifies examining the commonalities of sports commitment with the REA.

de la Vega et al. (2020) suggested that the REA should be studied in conjunction with controlling for passion and perfectionism. Indeed, perfectionism is consistently associated with the REA. For example, Çakın et al. (2021) reviewed 22 studies. They found that 21 reported positive moderate associations between perfectionism and RAE, with effect sizes (r) ranging from 0.14 up to 0.50. Another systematic literature review of 22 studies (González‐Hernández et al. 2021) supported the positive connection between perfectionism and REA. Consequently, numerous studies supported the relationship of passion and perfectionism to REA, and at least three studies yielded robust evidence for also controlling sports commitment. Additionally, exercise intensity (Kovacsik, Griffiths, et al. 2018), competition status (de la Vega et al. 2016; Juwono et al. 2021), and the form of sport (team or individual; Kovacsik, Soós, et al. 2018) may also affect the REA and, hence, they should also be included in the model.

The current study aimed to determine the extent to which two forms of passion, three forms of perfectionism, and 12 forms of sports commitment contribute to the variance in REA. We postulated that various components (subdomains, i.e., OP and HP) of the three independent variables would result in different predictive powers. Identifying the most specific aspects or subdomains of passion, perfectionism, and sports commitment that contribute to variance in REA, while controlling for exercise contexts, volume, and intensity could help future work isolate REA more effectively that reflects possible dysfunction from passionate and healthy exercise. Indeed, controlling the measures that share substantial variance with the REA might generate different results and conclusions when examining the REA. This dilemma was demonstrated by Szabo and Kovacsik (2019), showing that after controlling for passion, low‐ and high‐exercise volume groups no longer differed in REA whereas they differed in the control measures. Consequently, estimating the specific correlates of the REA could help research better untangle problematic exercise patterns from committed and passionate exercise behaviors.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through the online research platform SurveyCircle (SurveyCircle 2023) and on social media platforms, including Facebook, LinkedIn, Instagram, Reddit, and Twitter, targeting exercise‐related forums. Eligibility criteria were age 18 or over, regular (at least twice a week [Makizako et al. 2021]) exercise or sports participation for the past year, and fluency in English. We received 1401 responses from 60 countries but had to remove 398 for not satisfying the eligibility criteria or for not completing more than 20% of the survey questions. Hence, the final sample comprised 1003 respondents (470 males and 533 females). Nearly half (n = 501) were from India, 149 from the US, 86 from Hungary, 64 from the UK, 31 from Canada, and the rest from 55 other countries. They practiced 107 different sports or exercises, of which the most common were badminton, cricket, soccer, running, swimming, and volleyball. Although we did not differentiate between sports and exercise per se, we controlled for competitive and noncompetitive exercise status. Participants' mean age was 31.65 (± SD = 14.48) years; males 34.92 (± SD = 15.61) years and females 28.77 (± SD = 12.73) years. Participants reported exercising an average of 7.35 h (± SD = 6.35) per week. About half of the sample (n = 511) was engaged in competitive sports. Most (n = 584) reported training at moderate intensity, 190 at low intensity, and 229 at high intensity. Finally, slightly more than half of the sample (n = 520) declared team sports as a primary form of exercise.

Measures

Demographic questions were used to assess participants' age, gender, residence, and exercise characteristics, including hours and occasions of weekly training, perceived usual training intensity (low, medium, and high), type of the primary sport or exercise, competitive status, and form of the primary sport or exercise (individual and team).

Revised Exercise Addiction Inventory (EAI‐R)

To measure the risk of exercise addiction (REA), we employed the revised Exercise Addiction Inventory (EAI‐R; Szabo et al. 2019). This tool has six statements, each assessing a symptom of addiction on a six‐point agreement–disagreement Likert scale, based on the components model of addiction (salience, tolerance, withdrawal, mood modification, conflict, and relapse; Griffiths 2005). A person scoring 29 or above out of 36 maximum is classified as being at risk of exercise addiction. A sample item of the EAI‐R is as follows: “Exercise is the most important thing in my life.” The EAI‐R's reported internal reliability (Cronbach's alpha [α]) was 0.90 based on a predominantly male (243/277) sample (Szabo et al. 2019). In this multinational large‐sample study, the α was lower (0.70) and slightly higher (0.72) for males than for females (0.68).

The Passion Scale (PS)

We used the revised Passion Scale (PS; Marsh et al. 2013) to assess harmonious and obsessive passion (HP and OP, respectively) with two six‐item subscales, each rated on a seven‐point agreement–disagreement Likert scale. An example of a sample item for HP is “This activity is in harmony with the other activities in my life,” and OP is “If I could, I would only do my activity.” In this context, “activity” was replaced with sport or exercise to refer to exercise behavior specifically. The internal reliability reported for the OP subscale of the Passion Scale was (α) 0.86, whereas that of the HP subscale was 0.83 (Marsh et al. 2013). This study's values were 0.88 for both OP and HP subscales.

The Big Three Perfectionism Scale—Short Form (BTPS‐SF)

The Big Three Perfectionism Scale—Short Form (BTPS) assessed perfectionism (Feher et al. 2020). It consists of 16 items gauging three dimensions of perfectionism: (1) rigid perfectionism (RP), (2) self‐critical perfectionism (SP), and (3) narcissistic perfectionism (NP). The items are rated on a six‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to agree strongly. A sample item is “I have a strong need to be perfect.” The internal reliability of the BTPS‐SF in the current work ranged from α 0.90 to 0.91 for the three subscales, and it was 0.93 for the whole scale. These values match or exceed the value range of 0.78–0.90 recently reported by Neshat et al. (2023).

Sport Commitment Questionnaire‐2 (SCQ‐2)

We used the Sport Commitment Questionnaire‐2 (SCQ‐2; Scanlan et al. 2016) to assess the sources and types of sport commitment. The scale has 58 items, with 12 subscales, rated on a 5‐point agreement–disagreement Likert scale. A sample item is “Playing this sport is fun.” The scale has good psychometric properties. In this study, the internal reliabilities of the subscales were as follows: 0.87 (enthusiastic commitment), 0.84 (constrained commitment), 0.88 (sport enjoyment), 0.77 (valuable opportunities), 0.83 (other priorities), 0.84 (personal investments loss), 0.81 (personal investments quantity), 0.82 (social constraints), 0.80 (social‐support emotional), 0.83 (social‐support informational), 0.87 (desire to excel mastery achievement), and 0.85 (desire to excel‐social achievement). Finally, the Cronbach's α of the whole scale was 0.96.

Procedure

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Research Review Ethics Board (Certificate No.: 2022/308) at ELTE Eötvös Loránd University. Data collection was anonymous using the Qualtrics online research tool (https://www.qualtrics.com; Qualtrics 2013). It lasted from February to August of 2023. After reading an informed consent form, online consent for participation was given by typing “YES” in capital letters in a designated text box, allowing participants to proceed to the survey questions. Subsequently, the data file was scrutinized for completeness (an 80% or higher completion rate was the criterion for retention and inclusion in the statistical analyses). The other eligibility criteria and time taken to complete the survey were also checked.

Data Analyses

The raw data were downloaded into an SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) file from the research platform. Then, we performed statistical analyses using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp 2022, v. 29) software. The assumption of normality was violated in the current data. However, based on a test of the Central Limit Theorem, this should not be an issue with sample sizes greater than 50 (Islam (2018). Still, we resorted to the bootstrapping method, which is robust in guarding against such violations (Johnston and Faulkner 2021; Kelley 2005). We calculated Pearson's correlations to determine whether measures of passion, perfectionism, and sports commitment are related to the risk of exercise addiction (REA). Their results (i.e., the r values) were interpreted as effect sizes, and we calculated their 95% confidence intervals. Although Likert‐scale data are ordinal, if the scale points equal or exceed five, they can be treated as continuous to gain more robust results than nonparametric correlations (Sullivan and Artino 2013).

Next, we employed a multiple linear regression at an exploratory level to identify which correlated measures predict the REA. In this analysis, we controlled categorical measures such as gender, age, weekly hours of exercise, exercise intensity, the context of sport (competitive or noncompetitive), and form of sport (individual or team‐based). Subsequently, the statistically significant predictors from the multivariate linear regression were subjected to a bootstrapped multiple hierarchical regression (despite sports commitment is expected to be related to the risk of exercise addiction, we had to explore which of its 12 subdomains emerged as predictors of the REA before ordering them into hierarchical regression). Thus, the multivariate linear regression was an “a priory” exploratory test before constructing the hierarchical model to determine the extent to which each statistically significant predictor contributed to the shared variance of the model (Woltman et al. 2012).

Finally, we calculated three univariate tests comparing gender, form of sport (individual vs. team), and competition status to demonstrate how controlling for covariates, or alternately ignoring them, changes the results and interpretation of this research. We chose to look at these groups because several earlier works have claimed that these groups differ in REA (e.g., Costa et al. 2013; de la Vega et al. 2016; Kovacsik, Soós, et al. 2018; Smith and Hale 2004; Szabo et al. 2013). Nevertheless, past studies did not control passion, perfectionism, commitment, or their subdomains. With these tests, we aimed to illustrate that researchers must consider that several covariates can affect group comparisons on the REA; ignoring them yields false results.

Linear Regression

First, we tested the assumption of collinearity, indicating that multicollinearity was not an issue in the data (i.e., the tolerance was > 0.20, ranging between 0.226 and 0.805, whereas the variance inflation factor [VIF] was < 5.00, ranging between 1.242 and 4.419 for all the predictors). The data also met the assumption of independent errors based on the Durbin–Watson value (2.003). Finally, the data met the assumption of nonzero variances (for all predictors: [s 2] > 0.00). The multiple regression with 23 predictors was statistically significant (F (23, 844) = 29.668) and p < 0.001) and yielded an adjusted R 2 = 0.432. As illustrated in Table 2, the multiple linear regression yielded 10 statistically significant REA predictors, with standardized beta values between 0.071 and 0.265. Apart from two inverse (negative) predictors (valued opportunities and emotional social support), all were positive. Only exercise intensity predicted the REA among the control measures. Gender, age, competition status, and the form of exercise (individual or team) were not statistically significant.

Hierarchical Regression

Again, multicollinearity was not a concern in the data (i.e., the tolerance was > 0.20, between 0.446 and 0.834, whereas the VIF was < 5.000, between 1.200 and 2.243 for all the predictors). The data also met the assumption of independent errors based on the Durbin–Watson test, which yielded an almost ideal value (2.014; Table 3). Finally, the data met the assumption of nonzero variances (all predictors: s 2 > 0.000). The statistically significant predictors from the multiple linear regression were entered in the following order in the hierarchical regression (see also Table 3, Model 9.): Blocks 1 and 2: exercise characteristics (weekly hours of exercise and exercise intensity), Blocks 3 and 4: passion (HP and OP), Block 5: perfectionism (rigid perfectionism), Block 6: constrained commitment, Block 7: valued opportunities, Block 8: other priorities, and Block 9: social support (informative and emotional). This calculation resulted in eight statistically significant models out of nine, but model 6 (Table 3) was the last meaningful and acceptable one (adjusted R 2 = 0.427 and F [6, 861] = 108.81, p < 0.001) since in the remaining models the R 2 change was negligible being less than 1% (1.00%, see Table 3). The coefficients are presented in Table 4, whereas the bootstrapped coefficients are illustrated in Table 5. Passion (HP and OP, 22.1%) and exercise characteristics (intensity and weekly hours, 17.1%) added the most (39.2%) to the variance explained by the model. Rigid perfectionism and constrained commitment only added 3.8% to the variance (see Table 4).

Discussion

Based on a relatively sizable international sample, this cross‐sectional study aimed to identify the joint effect of those predictors of the REA, which emerged as separate predictors in the past, primarily in one‐nation studies. We followed the recent recommendations of de la Vega et al. (2020), echoed by Weinstein and Szabo (2023), to examine passion and perfectionism in conjunction with the REA. Furthermore, we expanded it by including 12 factors of commitment to sport or exercise as additional potential predictors based on novel research evidence (Ghabel Damirchi et al. 2020). Together with two forms of passion and three forms of perfectionism, we examined the relationship of 17 variables to the REA. They showed a medium to strong positive correlation with the REA (Table 1). Although the relationships with both passion and perfectionism were expected, the association with the 12 components of sports commitment was exploratory because, to our knowledge, no research has explored the connection between sports commitment and the REA using the SCQ‐2 (Scanlan et al. 2016).

Therefore, we entered all measures in an exploratory multivariate linear regression model that yielded 10 statistically significant predictors, two from among the control measures (exercise volume and intensity) and eight from among the independent measures: passion (harmonious and obsessive passion), perfectionism (rigid perfectionism), and sports commitment (constrained commitment, valued opportunities, other priorities, social support emotional, and social supports informational) jointly predicting 43% of the variance in the REA. The control measures, such as gender, age, competition status, and form of exercise (individual or team), were not statistically significant.

Subsequently, we examined the importance of the contribution (percent of variance) of each predictor using a bootstrapped hierarchical multiple regression and found eight significant models, of which model 6 was the most meaningful with six predictors: exercise characteristics (volume and intensity), passion (OP and HP), perfectionism (rigid perfectionism), and sports commitments (constrained commitment). This model predicted 42.7% of the variance in the REA, with passion and exercise characteristics accounting for most of the variance. Concerning passion, these results contrast with earlier research (i.e., de la Vega et al. 2016; Kovacsik, Griffiths, et al. 2018; Kovacsik, Soós, et al. 2018; Lichtenstein et al. 2020) showing that OP is a stronger predictor of REA than HP. However, these studies were conducted in developed Western nations (Denmark, Hungary, and Spain). In contrast, the current results come closer to the findings of de la Vega et al. (2020), stemming from eight Spanish‐speaking nations in which the effect size of the direct relationship between HP and REA (r = 0.420) was comparable to the current one (r = 0.488). However, de la Vega et al. (2020) did not examine HP's contribution to REA in a multivariate model as we did in the current work, disclosing that HP contributed 11.4% whereas OP 10.7% of the variance to the model (Table 3). The only explanation we can offer for HP's higher than OP's (or almost equal) contribution to REA—in contrast to past research—is that a large proportion of the sample was from a nonwestern collectivist nation (India). Therefore, cultural differences in passion linked to sports and exercise (Szabo et al. 2018) may mediate the relationship between passion and the REA. Future investigations should use more balanced, culturally representative samples to evaluate cultural factors' mediating roles in the passion and REA relationship.

Our results concerning the connection between perfectionism and REA agree with past research (Çakın et al. 2021; González‐Hernández et al. 2021). The direct correlation of three forms of perfectionism resulted in moderate effect sizes of 0.330 (rigid perfectionism), 0.244 (self‐critical perfectionism), and 0.313 (narcissistic perfectionism). These values fit the range (r = 0.15 to 0.50) Çakın et al. (2021) reported. However, only rigid perfectionism emerged as a statistically significant predictor based on the multiple linear regression (Table 2). Still, in the hierarchical regression, its contribution to the most acceptable model (model 6, Table 3) was only 1.9%. Constrained commitment contributed the same amount of variance to the REA. This figure (1.9%) is much smaller than the 29% shared variance between sports commitment and REA reported by Ghabel Damirchi et al. (2020).

This finding probably illustrates that the relationship between commitment and REA is substantially weaker if factors, such as gender, age, exercise characteristics (i.e., volume and intensity), passion, and perfectionism, are controlled. Indeed, exercise characteristics and passion (HP and OP) accounted for most of the variance in the current sample after controlling for several factors. These results justify recent suggestions that the REA should be studied with control for passion (de la Vega et al. 2020; Weinstein and Szabo 2023). However, they tune down our conjecture concerning the predictive power of perfectionism and commitment.

One significant question that stems from the current results is to what extent the various covariates of the REA modify research results. For example, would the results be the same if we measured and included perceived stress (Pinto et al. 2019), extroversion and narcissism (Cook et al. 2018), or metabolic features of the exercise (Pálfi et al. 2021) in the model? The answer is probably “not” because controlling for the covariates modifies the variance with other predictors. This fact is illustrated by the group comparisons performed in the current study. Gender, competition status, and form of sport (individual vs. team) differences emerged when the groups were compared on their REA scores. However, there were no differences when the predictors of the REA were controlled in the statistical calculations.

So, what is the message of this comparison? The answer is simple: Group comparison studies (i.e., Basoglu 2018) of REA scores will likely yield false results unless one controls the “known” covariates. However, we do not know how many covariates REA has under various circumstances (i.e., the form of exercise and competition status) and the combination of these circumstances with different assessment tools. Thus, it is probably too idealistic to control all potential covariates of REA under all possible circumstances. Consequently, REA research seems to follow a never‐ending path (as suggested by the over 1000 peer‐reviewed papers in contrast to about a dozen clinically significant cases) for scholars in the field as its predictors vary in the context of many factors, including sample type and size, sport or exercise characteristics, and personality factors, such as perfectionism, passion, commitment, assessment tools (Weinstein and Szabo 2023), and several other possible factors, not scrutinized in the current study and not measurable in future research either because of their breadth jeopardizing both participation in research and answers' reliability.

Exercise addiction is unique to the individual as purported by the clinical interactional model (Dinardi et al. 2021; Egorov and Szabo 2013). This model involves inductive reasoning built on the unique dysfunctional cases of exercise addiction. Still, research uses primarily deductive logic based on past research. Although this approach could yield a better understanding of the risk of exercise addiction, that risk may never turn into dysfunction. It is not surprising, then, that exercise addiction is not included in the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association 2013) or other clinical reference manuals. Furthermore, as our results suggest, the REA can be predicted by various factors under various circumstances, so the point of studying it decreases validity with the increasing number of incongruent publications. A comprehensive evaluation of research on the REA calls for the reconsideration of the study of exercise addiction per se in contrast to a probably unproductive poor index, which is the risk factor.

Limitations

The study's cross‐sectional nature and the testing of volunteers is a severe general limitation in psychology; this study is no exception. Despite setting participation criteria, we had no control over those criteria in this online anonymous study. The diverse sports or exercises and national representations render the sample unique and restrict the generalizability of the results. Further, differentiating between sports and exercise merely on competition status is not enough as recreational and professional athletes may both compete. It is argued that researching the REA in the latter group may be futile (Juwono et al. 2021). Furthermore, we looked at differences in team and individual exercises based on past research, but a new position paper argues that addiction in a team context is highly unlikely (Griffiths et al. 2023). Finally, there are several other measures assessing our target‐dependent measures that may yield different results, but we choose the EAI‐R and BTPS‐SF for brevity and the SCQ‐2 for its 12 different subdomains.

Conclusions

This study is based on an international sample, slightly more than half from Asia, exercising twice weekly over the past year. The risk of exercise addiction (REA) was predicted primarily by harmonious and obsessive passion and exercise volume and intensity. Rigid perfectionism and constrained commitment amounted to less than 2% of the variance in the REA. The findings agree in part with past research results demonstrating that perfectionism and commitment to sport are predictors of the REA. However, in the current study, controlling gender, age, exercise characteristics, and passion, their predictive power was almost negligible. The current findings also disagree with past works in the context of harmonious passion, which emerged as the most important predictor of the REA, which we attribute to possible cultural differences that should be tested in future research. The study also demonstrates that unless one controls the established predictors of the REA, statistically significant group differences may emerge between the two genders, competing and noncompeting athletes and individual and team exercisers. These group differences vanish if the predictors are controlled in the model. Despite the unlikely contribution of the nomothetic REA research to clinical diagnostic criteria for exercise addiction, its study should consider that passion and perfectionism, albeit with a small contribution in the current study, are covariates of the REA when making group comparisons based on questionnaire scores.

These findings have practical implications for both researchers and practitioners. When assessing the REA, it is essential to consider underlying factors, such as passion, perfectionism, and sport commitment, to avoid misinterpreting high involvement in exercise as pathological. Therefore, intervention and prevention efforts should address not only the quantity of exercise but also the psychological profiles associated with elevated REA.
