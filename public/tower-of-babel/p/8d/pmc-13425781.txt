Positional difference in deep femoral artery during intramedullary nailing for proximal femoral fractures: a within-subject comparative study
Authors: Yuuki Mitani, Kazuhiro Ikeda, Takamasa Kudo, Sho Kohyama, Tomokazu Yoshioka, Takaji Yanai, Yuichi Yoshii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the positional changes in the deep femoral artery (DFA) during intramedullary nail surgery. We hypothesized that the femur and DFA may be closer due to the medial compression of the thigh on a traction table during the surgery.MethodsThis within-subject comparative study included 20 patients with femoral trochanteric fracture (five males, 15 females; mean age: 83 ± 10.2) who underwent intramedullary nailing. Procedures were performed by five orthopedic surgeons. We performed computed tomography (CT) scans on the healthy thigh in the neutral and surgical limb positions. In the surgical limb position, we performed CT scans 2 weeks postoperatively in the lateral recumbent position with a simulated groin post-compression. Axial images 135 mm distal to the greater trochanter were reconstructed to evaluate the position of the DFA relative to the femur and the transverse locking screw axis. The distance from the medial femur edge to the DFA, medial soft tissue thickness, and the angle between the screw and the DFA were measured. Statistical analyses were performed to compare these parameters between the two positions.ResultsThe femur-DFA distance significantly decreased from 19.4 ± 3.5 mm in the neutral position to 12.5 ± 3.6 mm in the surgical position (P < 0.001). Medial soft tissue thickness also significantly reduced from 44.7 ± 15.0 mm to 31.0 ± 12.1 mm (P < 0.001). The two positions had no significant difference in the DFA angle (neutral: -23.1 ± 11.1°, surgical: -20.0 ± 10.6°; P = 0.17). The DFA was consistently positioned posterior to the screw insertion axis.ConclusionsThe surgical position during intramedullary nail insertion significantly reduces the femur–DFA distance and compresses soft tissues, which may increase the risk of DFA injury. Therefore, maintaining the hip joint in a neutral position during transverse locking screw insertion should be considered. Extra caution is warranted for taller patients and males owing to anatomical factors. Future studies should validate these findings and optimize surgical techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09975-8.Keywords: Deep femoral artery, Proximal femoral fracture, Intramedullary nail, Complication

Background

The incidence of proximal femoral fractures has increased globally due to an increase in the older adult population and lifestyle changes [1, 2]. This has significantly increased the number of unstable proximal femoral fractures, including intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric fractures [3]. For unstable femoral fractures, internal fixation is necessary for early recovery, as rigid internal fixation promotes lower limb mobility and reduces complications [4, 5]. Internal fixation can be performed using an intramedullary nail. In intramedullary nail surgery, deep femoral artery (DFA) injury is a rare but serious complication that can significantly affect a patient’s prognosis [6–8]. This injury occurs in 0.2–0.5% of internal fixation procedures for proximal femur fractures and can be caused by reduction or drilling using instruments during proximal femoral nailing [9–11]. These injuries can lead to severe complications, including hemorrhagic shock, pseudoaneurysm, hematoma, and anemia. Therefore, avoiding DFA injury is crucial during surgeries for proximal femoral fractures.

Previous studies have investigated the high-risk vertical range and axial angle of DFA injuries using computed tomography (CT) angiography. The high-risk range is reportedly 50–80 mm distal to the lesser trochanter, and the high-risk angle is 6.6°–12.8° from the lateral femoral cortex [12]. Another report stated that the risk range is 110–120 mm for women and 120–130 mm for men, as measured from the tip of the greater trochanter [13]. These reports indicate that positions should be considered when placing intramedullary nail transverse locking screws. However, these reports did not consider the changes in the position of the femoral artery due to the patient’s position at the time of surgery. During the actual surgery, a traction table with the unstable femur is used, and the hip is positioned in adduction and internal rotation. This may cause a change in the anatomical position of the DFA. Furthermore, to our knowledge, measures to prevent vascular damage in the surgical position have not yet been established.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the position of the femoral artery on the healthy side under normal conditions and when placed on a traction table. The position of the DFA with and without a traction table was verified by simulating the intraoperative limb position to examine whether there were changes in its position. We hypothesized that when placed on a traction table, the positions of the femur and artery may be closer, owing to the medial compression of the thigh.

Methods

This within-subject comparative study (level III evidence) was approved by our institutional review board (approval no. KC-H32; May 11, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study purpose and risk mitigation measures.

This study evaluated the femur–DFA distance on CT in both the neutral position and the simulated intraoperative position. Eligible patients were those who underwent intramedullary nailing for unilateral femoral trochanteric fractures at our institution in Noda, Chiba, Japan, between May 15, 2023, and January 26, 2024. Procedures were performed by five orthopedic surgeons using a standardized operative protocol. We excluded patients with (1) a fracture or prior surgery of the contralateral hip or femur, (2) occlusive disease of the DFA or a history of vascular intervention, or (3) bilateral proximal femoral fractures. A total of 20 patients were included (5 males and 15 females; mean age, 83 ± 10.2 years [range, 54–100 years]).

The femoral trochanteric fracture was diagnosed based on the history, characteristic clinical symptoms, and radiographic examination. Notably, all patients underwent radiography and CT scans to evaluate their fracture conditions. At our hospital, CT is obtained preoperatively to assess the 3D fracture pattern and postoperatively to confirm the reduction type (intramedullary or extramedullary). These scans are part of our clinical routine and did not result in additional tests or radiation exposure for this study.

Image analysis

To investigate the DFA positions, we performed plain CT scans with the healthy thigh in two different positions: the neutral (preoperatively) and operative limb positions. Two weeks postoperatively, we performed CT scans in the operative limb position when the wound had healed in the lateral recumbent position with the affected side down. We performed bilateral CT scans for the pre- and post-operative assessments of the fracture site.

The medial thigh was compressed using a band below the knee across a simulated groin post, and the hip was positioned in adduction and internal rotation (Fig. 1). The simulated groin post was a 12 cm diameter cylinder made by covering a paper core with a vinyl chloride mat so that metal artifacts could not occur. The size of this post mimics the groin post used at the hospital (MIZUHO, Japan). Plain CT scans were performed using a tube setting of 120 kV and 500 mAS, with a section thickness of 0.5 mm and a pixel size of 512 × 512 with AquilionONE (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan). CT images were acquired from the level of the iliac crest around the distal 1/3 of the femur.

We reconstructed CT images to investigate the positions of the femur and arteries. Fig. 2 shows the definitions of the insertion direction of the intramedullary nail and transverse locking screw. The insertion direction of the intramedullary nail in the coronal plane was defined as passing through the center of the medullary cavity from the top of the greater trochanter. However, that of the transverse locking screw was defined as parallel to the femoral neck axis in the axial plane. Based on the position of the transverse locking screw hole of the intramedullary nail (InterTAN; Smith & Nephew, Watford, UK), we evaluated the axial images reconstructed 135 mm distal to the tip of the greater trochanter. The DFA and deep femoral vein were identified on axial images at the same level. The center of the circle that touched the outer perimeter of the artery and vein without distinguishing between them was defined as the center of the DFA. In this study, only the main trunk of the DFA was evaluated; perforating branches were not included in the analysis.

Figure 3 shows the measurement parameters. We measured the distance from the medial edge of the femur to the DFA, the thickness of the medial soft tissue, and the angle between the transverse locking screw and the DFA. The DFA angle was defined as the angle between insertion direction of the transverse locking screw and line connecting the screw insertion site on the lateral side of the femur and the DFA. The position of the DFA relative to the screw insertion axis was considered positive if it was anterior and negative if it was posterior. These measurements were performed in the neutral and simulated surgery positions. Two orthopedic surgeons served as raters: rater A (7 years of experience) and rater B (13 years of experience). Each parameter was measured twice by each rater, independently, with an interval of at least 1 month between the first and second measurements. For the primary analysis, we used the first measurement performed by the rater A. The overall study flow is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the datasets. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Three measurements were taken with the same rater A for the first and second measurements and a different rater B for the third measurement. To examine the variability among the three measurements, we evaluated the intra- and inter-rater reliabilities using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). The results of the first measurement were analyzed. The distance from the medial edge of the femur to the DFA (femur-to-DFA distance), the thickness of the medial soft tissue (soft tissue thickness), and the angle between the transverse locking screw and the DFA (DFA angle) were compared between the neutral and simulated surgery positions. A paired t-test was used to evaluate differences in normally distributed data. However, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences in non-normally distributed data.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate correlations between body characteristics and measurement parameters. Correlations were interpreted as weak (0.1 ≤ |r| < 0.4), moderate (0.4 ≤ |r| < 0.7), or strong (0.7 ≤ |r|). For the power analysis, G*Power 3.1.9.7 was used to calculate the necessary sample size for a one-tailed paired t-test for the primary outcome (femur–DFA distance) between the neutral and simulated surgical positions. Because we hypothesized a priori that the surgical position would shorten the femur–DFA distance, a one-tailed test was applied. In the absence of published data for this specific metric, we assumed a conservative moderate-to-large standardized effect size (Cohen’s dz) of 0.6 based on conventional criteria. With an α error of 0.05 and a power of 0.8, the required sample size was 19 participants. Therefore, we planned to enroll 20 participants.

All statistical analyses other than the power analysis were performed using Bell Curve for Excel software (SSRI Co., Ltd., Shinjuku, Japan).

Discussion

The most significant findings of this study were that both the femur–DFA distance and the medial soft-tissue thickness were significantly reduced in the simulated intraoperative position compared with the neutral position. These findings suggest that limb positioning during intramedullary nailing may increase susceptibility to DFA injury by reducing the safety margin between the femur and the vessel and by compressing intervening soft tissues. This reduced clearance may be particularly relevant during drilling and transverse locking screw insertion.

In addition, the DFA was typically located posterior to the transverse locking screw insertion axis, and body habitus appeared to influence the spatial relationship between the DFA and the femur. These observations provide further context for intraoperative risk, particularly when the drilling trajectory deviates posteriorly and in larger patients. Because the DFA often lies posterior to the transverse locking screw insertion axis, caution is warranted when the drilling trajectory deviates posteriorly; this may be especially relevant in patients with relatively low femoral anteversion. Although the DFA angle did not differ significantly between positions, the finite diameters of both the drill and the vessel mean that a reduced femur–DFA distance may still increase the likelihood of contact in the simulated intraoperative position. Moreover, the DFA angle tended to decrease with increasing height, suggesting that the point where the DFA crosses the medial aspect of the femur may be relatively more distal in taller patients, thereby narrowing the safety margin at the level of distal locking. A previous study similarly reported a higher risk of DFA injury in relatively taller men than in women, attributed to a more distal crossing of the DFA [13]. Taken together, these findings support heightened vigilance when drilling for transverse locking, particularly in taller patients and males.

Anatomically, the DFA arises from the posterior–lateral aspect of the femoral artery and shows substantial variation in the level of branching relative to the inguinal ligament [14]. After branching, it courses posteriorly and typically runs closer to the femur than the femoral artery, passing between the pectineus and adductor longus muscles [15, 16]. Prior anatomical work has described a femur-to-artery distance in the range of approximately 11–34 mm, with the shortest distance occurring around 30% of femoral length [17]. Although we did not evaluate the entire femur, our measurement level corresponded approximately to 30–40% of femoral length, and our measured distances were comparable to those previously reported. These anatomical features help explain why the DFA is susceptible to injury during procedures around the proximal femur, and our results further indicate that simulated intraoperative positioning may substantially reduce the femur–DFA distance.

From a practical standpoint, maintaining the hip in a neutral position during creation of the transverse locking screw hole should be considered when feasible. In addition, because the femur–DFA distance in the neutral position could be as short as approximately 15 mm, avoiding advancement of the drill more than 15 mm beyond the far cortex may help reduce the likelihood of inadvertent vascular contact. This study has some limitations. First, this study investigated vascular runs on images, and we did not examine the clinical effects of limb positioning. Further clinical studies are needed to clarify whether returning to the neutral position reduces the incidence of vascular injury and whether it affects the quality of fracture reduction. Second, we performed postoperative CT imaging to mimic the surgical position, which may differ from the actual intraoperative position. Conversely, obtaining a CT scan in the actual intraoperative position was not feasible. These factors may have influenced the reproducibility of the vascular run. Third, our analysis was based on the transverse distal locking screw axis of the intramedullary nail used in this study. In clinical practice, however, the orientation of the distal locking screw may vary depending on the implant design or intraoperative insertion technique, including oblique directions. This limitation should be considered when interpreting our results. In addition, variations exist in terms of the traction table and the groin post, various degrees of hip adduction and internal rotation and amount of traction, but it is difficult to consider all of these items. However, based on these findings, we hope to evaluate the actual risks in clinical practice in the future. Finally, it is possible that the body size of the population in this study is not common. However, in Japan (Asia), it is the standard physique for patients with femoral trochanteric fractures [19, 20]. Since this study suggests the importance of the patient positioning, it is worth examining the validity of this idea in other ethnicities.

In conclusion, the simulated intraoperative position was associated with a shorter femur–DFA distance and reduced medial soft-tissue thickness on CT, suggesting a narrower safety margin during transverse locking. Maintaining a neutral hip position and careful control of drill depth may help reduce susceptibility to DFA injury. Because these findings are based on a simulated position, they should be interpreted with caution.
