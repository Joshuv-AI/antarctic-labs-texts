The lower boundary of workplace mistreatment: Do small slights matter?
Authors: Michal Hodor, Liat Eldor, Peter Cappelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceManagers strongly influence how employees behave and perform. While research has focused on serious mistreatment like harassment, much less is known about how minor, everyday slights affect workers. Using data from a national retail chain, we study a subtle form of mistreatment—when a manager fails to deliver a birthday gift and card on time—and find that even this small lapse increases employee absences and reduces working hours. Emotional responses to feeling overlooked may help explain these outcomes. Our results show that even modest failures in employee recognition can meaningfully harm productivity, underscoring the importance of everyday interpersonal interactions in the workplace.

The study of behavior toward subordinates and its consequences spans multiple disciplines. In psychology, the focus is often on the mechanisms through which managerial actions shape employee reactions (1, 2). The more recent literature in economics arguably began with the basic finding of considerable variance in employee performance across managers, demonstrating that “better managers matter.” Bertrand and Schoar (2003) found that chief executive officer (CEO) quality was associated with better organizational performance and linked this to observable traits such as holding a Master of Business Administration (MBA) degree. Lazear, Shaw, and Stanton (2015) found that better managers appear to contribute to stronger team performance, although the mechanisms remain unclear. Adhvaryu, Nyshadham, and Tamayo (2023) extended this work by examining specific managerial traits such as attention and control, while Hoffman and Tadelis (2021) came closest to studying supervisor behavior directly by focusing on employee perceptions of their managers’ interpersonal abilities. The study by Bender et al. (26) exemplifies a broader strand of the literature on general management practices, including monitoring, goal setting, and the use of incentives.

In framing managerial behavior, psychology often uses the concept of incivility to describe a broad range of negative actions, from serious misconduct that violates the law to everyday interpersonal interactions. This literature primarily focuses on identifying the mechanisms through which such behaviors exert their effects, with less emphasis on measuring empirically the effects themselves (For a recent review and discussion of response mechanisms, see ref. 3). Empirical evidence has generally focused on the most harmful practices, particularly supervisor abuse that borders on illegality (2, 4–7). Other studies have examined various severe forms of interpersonal incivility, such as expressions of contempt (8), bullying (3, 9, 10), and overt rudeness (11–13). The behaviors examined in these studies typically involve overt hostility and intentional provocation. However, there is little, if any, evidence on more routine or modest mistreatment in the workplace and its possible effects on employees. This leaves an important open question: What are the boundary conditions of our knowledge about the consequences of workplace mistreatment? Put differently, how minor can an act of mistreatment be and still affect employees’ performance?

We define mild mistreatment as the failure to treat employees with the basic respect they are due, as defined by social norms and by prevailing customs within a given context. The scarcity of research in this area may stem from the intangible nature of such behaviors, which makes them difficult to document and measure. Examples of such slights include eye-rolling, ignoring emails, dismissive body language, interrupting, and talking over others.

Another possible reason for the lack of studies examining the lower boundary of managerial behavior that can harm employee performance is the unique nature of the employment relationship. Rooted in common law, it is a relationship in which the supervisor holds significant formal authority over subordinate employees. The nature of the supervisor’s job allows—and may even require—changing employee schedules without much notice, override their decisions, assign difficult tasks, criticize their performance, behavior, or attitudes, and discipline them for noncompliance (14). Any of these actions would likely be perceived as inappropriate or even abusive in other contexts. In a work setting, for example, employees may not view being asked to stop what they are doing to assist a supervisor as insulting, though they might perceive it as such outside the workplace. Nevertheless, 78% of employees across various cultures report experiencing or witnessing what they describe as insults in the workplace on a daily basis (15).

A further challenge in examining how modest a workplace slight can be and still negatively affect employee performance—especially outside of laboratory settings—is the difficulty of identifying a suitable, observable example and reliably linking it to work performance. A limitation of much of the incivility literature is that it typically relies on subjective definitions of mistreatment, often through self-reports in which employees indicate when they felt insulted or mistreated by a supervisor. This approach raises well-known concerns about selection and the reliability of self-reported data. Even more difficult to address are endogeneity concerns—most notably, the possibility that poor employee performance may trigger slights or insults from supervisors. This dynamic may unfold over time, with mistreatment increasing as work performance deteriorates, or subsiding as performance improves.

We are fortunate to identify a context that allows us to address these challenges. It comes from a major retail chain, where there is a standard practice to recognize employees on their birthdays. Each employee receives a small gift card, along with a greeting card, personally delivered on the day of their birthday. This recognition is a formal policy codified in company rules, and employees are aware of and expect this recognition. In some cases, however, the presentation occurs after the employee’s actual birthday. Crucially, the company digitally records the exact delivery date, meaning that delays are not self-reported. The slight in question, then, is the manager’s failure to acknowledge an employee’s birthday on the expected day. While the offense is modest, it violates widely recognized social norms.

There are several reasons to believe that this situation qualifies as a slight or a very modest form of perceived workplace mistreatment. First, the incident is relatively minor. Second, social norms emphasize the importance of recognizing birthdays on the exact date. Etiquette guides still highlight the significance of timely birthday acknowledgments as a matter of good manners (e.g., ref. 16). Third, the practice of missing the exact date is common enough that the five largest greeting card companies each offer dedicated sections for “belated birthday” cards.* These cards typically convey an apology in a light-hearted tone, reinforcing the idea that such an oversight is widely regarded as a modest faux pas.

We leverage the experience of not receiving a birthday greeting card and gift on time from a manager as a proxy for perceived mistreatment and examine employee performance in the period following the birthday. The value of this modest form of mistreatment lies in its dual nature: It is widely recognized as inappropriate, yet often dismissed as “not a big deal,” making it a clear example of very modest mistreatment. From a research design perspective, the context is particularly useful because evidence indicates that the timing of greeting card and gift delivery is exogenous to employee performance. Birthday gifts and cards are prepared and distributed to store managers by the retail chain, who are then responsible for delivering them to employees. This setup presumably reduces the likelihood that employees interpret any delay as unavoidable. Yet, as the data show, delays still occur. Regardless of the cause (discussed below), what matters for our purposes is how employees perceive the event and how they respond to it.

Importantly, for this setting to be a credible example of mild mistreatment, employees must perceive the delay as such. We tested this using a survey of a representative sample of retail employees with similar occupations and education levels to those in our study, all drawn from the same country as our study sample. Seventy-four percent reported they would feel hurt or insulted if their birthday gift were not delivered on time. Respondents also noted that they would likely interpret the delay as either forgetfulness, or a sign that other priorities took precedence—yet neither explanation diminished the extent to which they perceived the missed delivery as a slight.†

Store managers and human resource professionals noted in informal conversations that when birthday gifts and cards are delivered late, it is accidental. We validate this assumption in several ways. First, we examine the relationship between store operational periods and delays in gift delivery and find evidence consistent with the claim that delays are more frequent during periods of high organizational and operational load. Second, we also corroborate the claims of managers and human resource professionals using a survey of a representative sample from these groups drawn from the same country as our study sample. We found that 100% of managers reported they would not delay delivering birthday gifts to employees they viewed as low-performing. Managers also consistently stated that their personal relationship with an employee would not influence the timing of birthday gift delivery. Among human resource professionals, 91.43% indicated it is unlikely that such delays reflect employee performance.

We might be concerned about reverse causation if poor performers were disproportionately likely to receive their birthday gifts late. Yet, our data suggest this is not the case. We show that delays are not correlated with observable employee attributes, such as demographics, professional characteristics, and past performance, nor with managerial characteristics, workplace environment measures, or store-level characteristics. This evidence supports the crucial claim that although delays result from managerial actions, they are not intentionally targeted at specific employees but instead occur sporadically due to organizational or logistical disruptions. Moreover, the company operates a recognition program through which managers nominate employees for appraisal rewards-special acknowledgments given for exceptional performance. We found that employees who received such rewards were just as likely to experience delayed birthday gift and card delivery as those who were not recognized.

We use a dynamic difference-in-differences (DiD) model to isolate the net effect of the mistreatment event. Our econometric model exploits information on employees’ birth dates and the exact date of birthday gift delivery to form two groups: a control group consisting of employees who received their gift within a five-day window around their birthday,‡ and a treatment group consisting of employees who received their gift outside the five-day window.§ We incorporate these groups into models with store and birthday month fixed effects, thereby capturing time-invariant characteristics specific to each store or to the month-day birthday timing. We also estimate a model with individual fixed effects to control for time-invariant individual characteristics and mitigate concerns about unobserved individual heterogeneity.

The DiD approach allows us to examine the effects of the birthday slight on two outcome variables that represent employees’ labor supply: working hours and sickness absence. Variations in recorded working hours reflect employees arriving late, leaving early, or taking extended breaks. Sickness absence—a common form of paid absence—refers to paid days off that employees may take, providing insight into the extensive margin of labor supply. We leverage these monthly cumulative variations to analyze how this perceived modest mistreatment affects employees’ effort and attendance.

Importantly, our econometric approach also assesses the validity of the identification strategy by testing the parallel trends assumption, that working hours and absence patterns in the treatment and control groups follow similar trajectories in the months prior to the birthday. This helps address endogeneity concerns, such as the possibility that not giving the birthday gift on time was used to penalize poor performance or that employees might anticipate receiving the gift late. Reassuringly, all model specifications confirm the absence of any pretreatment trends, supporting the credibility of our approach.

Our main findings reveal that not handing the birthday gift and card on time leads to a 50% increase in employees’ monthly sickness absence and a reduction of more than two working hours per month. These effects remain consistent across various model specifications, underscoring the robustness of the impact and demonstrating that even a perceived mild mistreatment can significantly impair employee performance. We also find that following the receipt of a delayed gift, employee performance improves and eventually returns to premistreatment levels.¶ In Results, we discuss potential mechanisms driving this outcome.#

We then explore potential mechanisms through which perceived workplace mistreatment may induce changes in employee labor supply. Our findings indicate that the reduction in labor supply is primarily driven by middle-level managerial employees within the store. This is consistent with evidence suggesting that higher-status employees have greater expectations for a strong relationship with their managers, leading to a stronger emotional reaction when they perceive mistreatment or insult (17, 18).

The birthday gift accompanying the birthday card is a cash gift card. Employees who do not receive their cash gift card on time may respond not only to the perceived slight but also to diminished time value of money, especially for lower-paid employees, for whom timely access to funds can have a greater impact. We examine this issue by exploiting variation in gift values, which depend on employee tenure. If financial concern drove the response, we would expect those receiving higher-value gifts to reduce their performance more. We find little evidence supporting this financial mechanism explanation. Instead, employees with lower tenure, who receive gifts worth approximately 2% of their monthly pay, exhibit a stronger negative reaction. This suggests that not receiving the birthday gift and card on time is perceived primarily as a form of mistreatment rather than a financial loss.

Our study begins to map out the lower boundary of negative effects stemming from managerial behavior and contributes to several literatures. First, it adds to an emerging body of work in economics that mainly examines severe forms of mistreatment in the workplace, including sexual and physical harassment (19), assault and violence (6), and threatening behavior (7), along with their adverse outcomes and economic consequences. Despite survey evidence indicating that mild mistreatment is widespread in the workplace (15, 20) and may harm employee well-being and disrupt firm performance, our study seeks to quantify its causal effects.

Second, we identify a similar gap in the psychology and management literatures, where there is no empirical research on mild mistreatment in the workplace. Existing studies focus on abusive supervision (e.g., refs. 4, 21, and 22), aggression (e.g., ref. 21), and bullying (e.g., ref. 9). Moreover, these studies often face methodological limitations in establishing causation, as they typically rely on cross-sectional, self-reported surveys and outcomes—often with the same source reporting both the mistreatment and its perceived effects—and they frequently lack objective performance measures (e.g., ref. 20). While these studies have found negative effects on job satisfaction and organizational commitment (20, 23), as well as on perceptions of justice (24), the literature lacks empirical evidence on how perceived mild mistreatment affects employee performance. As we discuss below, our study addresses many of these methodological challenges.

Third, our paper also contributes to the literature on management quality. To date, this literature has focused primarily on task-oriented practices at the firm level, such as monitoring and controlling employees, personnel management practices, and decentralization (25–29). We provide causal evidence at the level of the immediate supervisor, showing how perceived mistreatment by a manager affects employee performance. This highlights the importance of people-oriented practices, such as personal consideration and interpersonal recognition, which are distinct from more widely studied operational practices.

Fourth, uncovering the consequences of mild mistreatment is practically important for firms and managers, as the estimated global cost of all forms of workplace mistreatment in terms of sickness-related absence and productivity loss ranges from $691.70 billion to $1.97 trillion annually (15). While these figures encompass all types of mistreatment, they underscore the broader stakes involved. Notably, these estimates exclude additional organizational burdens such as increased burnout, higher turnover, and reduced employee engagement.

Settings & Data

Background.

Our data were collected from a national retail chain comprising 252 stores (30).‖ The stores are located in high-traffic areas such as central malls and downtown districts and offer a broad range of wellness products, including health care items, cosmetics, cleaning supplies, and pharmaceuticals. All stores maintain a uniform appearance, carry the same products, and follow standardized operating procedures. They share a consistent organizational structure consisting of a store manager, department managers (i.e., middle-level managers), and front-line service employees. Employee qualifications and training are similar across all retail chain stores. Front-line employees handle customer service, inventory management, and other routine retail tasks. All marketing, strategy, and human resource policies are set by headquarters and implemented uniformly across stores. Store managers are responsible for overall store operations and employee productivity, subject to these standardized arrangements.

Employees’ Birthday Gift.

Every employee who has completed at least one year of service is eligible to receive a birthday gift and a greeting card, which the company requires the store manager to physically hand-deliver. This initiative is mandated and funded by the company’s headquarters.**

Birthdays are widely regarded as meaningful personal milestones, and it has become common practice for companies to acknowledge employees’ birthdays on the actual day itself. Receiving a birthday gift and greeting card adds special significance to the occasion, distinguishing it from other forms of communication or compensation, and symbolizes the recognition of the employee’s “special day.” The practice of having the store manager hand-deliver the gift is intended to foster a more personal and meaningful interaction, strengthening the employee–manager relationship by demonstrating attention and care for personal milestones.

In practice, the data show that store managers do not always hand-deliver an employee’s birthday gift on the actual birthday. We use the delay in gift delivery as a proxy for a very modest slight or mistreatment event. The data indicate that managers who do not deliver birthday gifts on time do not do so intentionally, though employees nonetheless perceive not receiving the gift and card on time as a form of mistreatment; both aspects are discussed in detail below.

Our dataset includes precise information on both the employee’s date of birth and the actual date the birthday gift was presented by the store manager. Drawing on this unique data, we can identify whether there was a delay and how long it lasted. Unlike many measures of the employee–manager relationship, which are typically subjective and self-reported (e.g., from employee surveys), the timing of gift delivery offers an objective and quantifiable measure of mild workplace mistreatment.

Fig. 1 displays the distribution of the time differences between an employee’s birthday and the actual date the gift was presented.†† The figure shows that the largest proportion of employees, over 42%, received their gift within five days of their birthday. It also reveals variability in gift delivery timing both before and after the employee’s birthday. Receiving a card early appears to carry no negative connotation and may even signal attentiveness aimed at avoiding delays. However, receiving the birthday gift and card late, we argue, indicates at best an oversight and at worst a perception that other tasks took priority or that the manager did not care enough to remember, each of which carries a negative connotation.

Data Description.

We obtain comprehensive data from 2018 to 2019 that includes individual-level demographic information such as age, gender, and birthdate. Additionally, we have extensive organizational and occupational data, including employee tenure at both the store and organizational levels, managerial positions, and employment status, which enables us to track job terminations. At the store level, our dataset includes information on store operating duration and the number of employees per store. We also collect detailed data on store managers, including their organizational and store-specific tenure. Furthermore, we obtain an annual measure of “transactional empowering leadership” style, assessed by store employees through surveys conducted and provided by the company. (The transactional empowering leadership measure assesses the degree to which store managers exhibit behaviors that empower and guide employees to perform their tasks effectively. For further details, see SI Appendix, sections G and H).

During the two-year period, we observed a total of 8,722 eligible employees entitled to receive birthday gifts. Of these, 3,057 employees worked for the company in both 2018 and 2019, while the remaining employees were employed either in 2018 (37%) or in 2019 (63%). Outcome Variables.

We measure labor supply using two metrics: working hours and sickness absence. These administrative data are obtained from the retail chain’s accounting department. By examining both the extensive margin (absence frequency) and the intensive margin (working hours) of employees’ labor supply, we provide comprehensive and quantifiable evidence on the impact of workplace mistreatment on employee outcomes.

Working hours.

Monthly working hours, as shown in Table 1, vary substantially, ranging from 2 to 214 h. This wide range reflects the company’s diverse work arrangements, including full-time, part-time, and nonstandard schedules. For the working hours analysis, we restrict the sample to full-time employees. These employees follow a predetermined schedule of 8-h shifts, set weekly by the store manager, typically exceeding 140 h per month—closely aligning with the sample median. (Part-time employees follow irregular schedules: They may work full 8-h shifts in some weeks and fewer or no hours in others. Nonstandard arrangements include contract employees, who typically work about two hours per month and visit stores infrequently for technical or consulting tasks. These groups are excluded from the analysis because variation in their working hours reflects schedule volatility rather than mistreatment. SI Appendix, Fig. B.1 illustrates the distribution of working hours across all employee types).

While employees are expected to follow their assigned hours, they have some autonomy in managing their time. This flexibility can result in minor forms of shirking, such as arriving slightly late, leaving early, or taking extended breaks. Over time, these small deviations accumulate and contribute to variation in monthly hours. (Because the data from the chain are aggregated at the monthly level, we cannot provide direct empirical evidence for this claim. However, both employees and chain management attribute the observed variation to these behaviors). Restricting the working hours analysis to full-time employees allows us to capture variation driven by employee choices rather than scheduling inconsistencies. Specifically, the stable and routine schedules of full-time workers allow us to identify the effect of the minor mistreatment of failing to receive the birthday gift and card on time, on actual working hours.

The working hours sample includes 939 full-time employees in the treatment group and 2,406 in the control group, observed over two years. Employee attendance and working hours are important indicators of productivity and operational efficiency (e.g., refs. 31–33). Actual working hours, in particular, serve as a measure of the extent of employee effort. Scholars use this metric to gain insights into employee satisfaction with the work environment (34, 35), as well as to understand workplace dynamics, including perceptions of fairness and their impact on behavior (36, 37).

Sickness absence.

Our sickness absence outcome variable measures the number of paid sick days employees take each month. Calling in sick is discretionary, based on employees’ assessment of their health and reasons for not coming to work. The company follows a common policy whereby employees receive 50% of their salary for the first three days of absence in a month without needing a doctor’s note—that is, they are not required to justify their absence. From the fourth day onward, employees receive 100% of their salary if their absence is medically approved. (To mitigate potential bias from chronic or severe health conditions, we exclude employees with monthly sickness absence above the 99th percentile (more than 7.56 d per month). Employees working only about 2 h per month are also excluded from this analysis). Overall, our sickness absence sample includes 1,158 employees in the treatment group and 2,921 in the control group, observed over two years.

Our sickness absence measure builds on the literature showing that employees perceiving inappropriate treatment at work tend to take more sick days than those perceiving respectful treatment (38–41). The common explanation is that such absences allow employees to escape unpleasant work environments.

Empirical Setting

Identification.

In this section, we aim to validate our identification assumption by first examining why store managers sometimes fail to deliver birthday gifts and cards to employees on time.

We first gathered insights from informal conversations with store managers to better understand the reasons behind variation in the timing of birthday gift delivery. We also spoke with human resource professionals regarding the gift delivery practice and the expected timing for store managers to present the gifts. Both groups consistently indicated that delays primarily arise from understandable challenges, such as managers being preoccupied with operational matters, prioritizing urgent profit-oriented tasks, managing other responsibilities, or occasionally forgetting. SI Appendix, section C provides survey evidence from managers and HR professionals that supports these findings and complements the informal conversations. All surveys conducted in this study are preregistered and received approval from the Tel Aviv University Ethics Committee (IRB #0010446-1). Participants were informed of the study’s purpose and the voluntary nature of participation on the first page of the survey. Consent was given at the beginning of the survey, and participants could withdraw at any time. While the surveys are from the same country as the study sample, they may not include workers employed by the company under study. To ensure reliable interpretation of respondents’ perspectives on the mistreatment event, the gift card practice was described in broadly comparable terms to those used by other firms in the country.

The reasons for delayed gift delivery described in our conversations and surveys align with prior research showing that managers often deprioritize employee-related duties when faced with immediate operational and profit-driven pressures (42). Importantly, although these explanations suggest that “other things were more important,” there is no indication that managers intend to signal disregard. Indeed, in the surveys store managers and HR professionals emphasized that intentionally delaying birthday gifts or cards as a form of punishment would be inappropriate.

We explore possible differences in the sources of these delays between the treatment and control groups. First, we conduct balance tests on observable characteristics to examine whether demographic and occupational differences exist between the two groups. Table 2 compares these group characteristics by displaying average values separately for each group, along with the differences between averages and the results of a t test. The table shows that the groups are similar across most demographic and professional characteristics, including turnover and performance variables. This evidence supports the assertion that store managers did not selectively target delays toward employees with specific characteristics.

For example, quitting and dismissals, measures typically associated with extreme conflict, tend to peak within the first six months of these relationships. Moreover, as shown in SI Appendix, section D, the negative response to mistreatment is primarily driven by employees working with less-tenured managers, further mitigating this concern). The tenure discrepancy leads to variation in the average value of birthday gifts between the treatment and control groups, as the gift’s value depends on employees’ length of service.

A concern raised by Fig. 2 is that managers’ heavy workloads and increased demands could lead to other negative behaviors, potentially causing employees to reduce working hours and increase sick days. To address potential biases related to timing, we incorporate fixed effects for birth month along with store fixed effects in our model. This approach isolates variation in lateness within specific birth months. Importantly, the model including both store and birth-month fixed effects produces estimates similar to those from a model with employee fixed effects, alleviating this concern.

A primary endogeneity concern in our analysis is reverse causation, where poor performance leads managers to delay giving birthday gifts. A related, conceptual concern is that employees might anticipate such delays and respond by reducing their labor supply. To address these concerns, our identification assumption posits that, absent mistreatment, outcome measures for both treatment and control groups would follow similar trajectories—i.e., parallel trends—before the birthday slight. We present evidence supporting this assumption below, thereby mitigating this concern (SI Appendix, section H explores the relationship between managerial quality dimensions and perceived mistreatment, showing that stronger responses occur in stores with above-median fairness climate and transformational leadership, mitigating concerns that delayed gifts reflect interpersonal conflict in manager–employee relationships).

Another concern arises from the employee side: Employees may take leave or reduce their working hours around their birthday, which could result in both delayed gift delivery (if they are not present to receive it) and reduced working hours. Our definition of on-time delivery—as within a five-day window around the birthday—helps address this concern by design. Additionally, as shown below, sickness absence and working hours both move in the same direction, reducing the likelihood that absence alone drives the results. Finally, to directly address this issue, we analyze vacation day data as an outcome and find no relationship between gift delay and the use or cashing in of vacation days. (These results are available upon request).

Model Specifications.

We use a dynamic difference-in-differences (DiD) estimation procedure to estimate the temporal effects of workplace mistreatment. Using a monthly panel dataset, we define a relative time variable τ, where τ=0 corresponds to the employee’s birthday month. Months preceding the birthday serve as leads, while subsequent months serve as lags, with the month immediately before the birthday, τ=−1, designated as the reference period. Based on this structure, we create dummy variables Diτ equal to 1 if employee i is τ months away from their birthday month, τ=0. This approach yields a relative event-time framework aligned across individuals, enabling consistent analysis of outcomes before and after the birthday regardless of when birthdays fall within the calendar year. (This is not a staggered adoption model because time is defined relative to a shared event (the birthday), rather than calendar time. Although birthdays occur in different months, our framework aligns all individuals at time 0 (the birthday month), enabling consistent comparisons across leads and lags. Treatment status is fixed for each individual: Employees who did not receive their birthday gift and card on time are classified as treated (Ti=1), while those who did are controls (Ti=0). This classification remains constant over time, and we do not exploit variation in treatment timing; hence, the control group composition remains stable. For robustness, we modified our settings and also estimated a staggered adoption model using the Sun and Abraham (2021) estimator. The results are nearly identical and are available upon request).

The econometric model incorporates these dummy variables into a linear specification with fixed effects to explain the working hours and sickness absence of employee i in store s during month t of year j, as follows:[1]yistj=βTi+∑τ≠−1;τ=−4τ=4δτDiτ+∑τ≠−1;τ=−4τ=4μτDiτTi+θ′Xistj+αs+λj+ζt+ϕi+εistj,

where Ti is an indicator variable equal to 1 if the employee received the birthday gift late (i.e., belongs to the treatment group) and 0 otherwise (i.e., belongs to the control group). The coefficients μτ are the key parameters of interest, capturing the causal effect of mistreatment on employee outcomes. They measure the difference between the treatment and control groups for each month τ relative to the month before the birthday.

αs and λj represent store and year fixed effects, respectively, capturing unobserved factors specific to each store and year. ζt is a month fixed effect that accounts for any bias related to the specific month in which a birthday occurs, and ϕi is an individual fixed effect controlling for all unobserved personal characteristics. These fixed effects help address biases arising from unobserved heterogeneity across stores, individuals, and birthday timing. While Eq. 1 includes all these fixed effects, in practice, employee fixed effects are not estimated simultaneously with store and birth-month fixed effects due to multicollinearity.

θ is a vector of coefficients for control variables, and Xistj includes store-level information that varies across years, such as the store manager’s transactional empowering leadership score and managerial tenure at the store, factors that potentially affect the timing of birthday gift delivery. In models without store fixed effects, Xistj includes observed store characteristics like store age, store size (number of employees), and physical location (e.g., neighborhood, downtown, mall, or outside the city). In models without employee fixed effects, Xistj contains employee characteristics, including gender, age, a dummy variable indicating managerial status (1 if managerial, 0 otherwise), organizational tenure, and a categorical variable capturing employment status in year j (employed, voluntarily left, or laid off). (Because the monetary value of the birthday gift is determined by the employee’s tenure at the organization, these variables are highly correlated and cannot be included as controls simultaneously. When the birthday gift’s monetary value is included as a control (and tenure is omitted), the results remain consistent. In Section 4.2, we conduct an in-depth analysis related to the monetary value of the gift). The error term εistj captures unobserved factors and is clustered at the store level.

The validity of our identification assumption is tested by examining parallel trends in the months leading up to the delay in gift delivery. Specifically, this assumption requires that the estimated pretreatment coefficients be centered around zero, i.e., μ^τ=0 for τ<0, implying that delays occur as if randomly. Our results reveal no differential trends between the treatment and control groups before the birthday month, suggesting that employees were likely surprised by the delay and that poor performers were not targeted, thereby supporting the validity of our empirical strategy. (A permutation test shows that the distribution of placebo effects is centered around zero across all event times, providing support for the robustness of our results. Furthermore, a formal test for differential pretrends between treated and control groups finds no statistically significant divergence. Detailed results are available upon request).

The observed decrease in monthly working hours can be viewed as a cumulative form of “revenge.” Employees who experienced mistreatment by their manager may adopt absenteeism behaviors such as arriving late, leaving early, or taking longer breaks. These minor instances of lateness or early departure, when repeated throughout the month, cumulatively result in a reduction in employees’ monthly working hours.

This reduction in working hours negatively impacts employee productivity, potentially harming the company’s overall profitability and operational efficiency. Direct costs include resources spent on finding replacement employees, while indirect consequences may arise from decreased service quality, lower sales, and reduced customer satisfaction due to overworked staff, further compounding the financial impact.

An important empirical question arises regarding the long-term effect of perceived mild mistreatment from not receiving the birthday gift and card on time after they are eventually delivered. One argument posits that belated delivery serves an apology (44) and should restore employee labor supply to its premistreatment state. Another possibility is that employees react in the short term but eventually move on, accommodating the minor incident. However, the experience may damage the employee–manager relationship enough to leave lingering resentment—especially if combined with other minor slights—leading to persistent declines in performance despite the eventual receipt of the gift.

The estimates in Table 3 and Figure 3A provide insights into this question by examining the working hours after the gift is received (and the mistreatment is perhaps resolved). The analysis for periods when all employees have received their gifts (τ>1) indicates that the slight event did not lead to a lasting decline in working hours. Specifically, while working hours drop significantly immediately following the mistreatment, they return to predelay levels by τ=2, τ=3, and τ=4.

The observation that employees’ working hours return to premistreatment levels suggests that store managers can restore the manager–employee relationship by promptly addressing issues like these and improving their interactions with employees. Whether this recovery reflects active managerial effort or simply employees adjusting to the mistreatment and moving on remains an open question. Ideally, we would test this by examining employees who received their gift more than a month after their birthday to see if their working hours remained lower during the waiting period. However, since most employees received their gift within a month (Fig. 1), the small number of such cases limits our statistical power and indicates the need for further research.

Nonetheless, the significant consequences of even mild mistreatment on employee effort—and, by extension, firm outcomes—highlight that even minor, short-lived, or ultimately forgiven events can have meaningful implications for firms. (SI Appendix, Table A.8 examines the relationship between perceived minor mistreatment and voluntary and involuntary turnover, showing that such mistreatment does not lead to increased turnover).

Suggested Mechanisms

In this section, we explore potential mechanisms driving the effect, specifically examining whether the results are due to an emotional response to the perceived workplace mistreatment or if employees are merely reacting to the loss of anticipated monetary income in the form of the birthday gift.

Emotional Response.

An emotional response to not receiving the birthday gift and greeting card on time can encompass several aspects. It may involve feelings of insult and raise concerns about fairness and justice (45), potentially creating a sense of inequity. The delay may also induce stress, which can lead to a decline in employee labor supply.

We analyze the reactions to not receiving the birthday gift and greeting card on time among both managerial and nonmanagerial employees. In this context, a managerial position refers to a middle-level manager within a store who supervises front-line employees while reporting to the store manager. Research shows that breaches of the psychological contract are particularly damaging to trust and job satisfaction among higher-level employees (17), who may hold greater expectations of fair treatment. Building on this, we use professional position as a proxy for the manager–employee relationship to explore the emotional response mechanism. If emotional response drives the observed outcomes, we expect a larger reduction in labor supply among managerial employees, who are more likely to perceive mistreatment due to their elevated expectations for respectful treatment, than nonmanagerial employees.

The findings presented in Fig. 4A, B, E, and F, support the emotional response mechanism. The figure shows that employees in managerial roles experience a notable decrease in working hours and an increase in sickness absence due to not receiving the birthday gift and card on time, with effects that are more than double those observed in the overall sample. In contrast, nonmanagerial employees exhibit significantly smaller changes in both outcomes following the mistreatment event. These results are consistent with meta-analytic evidence showing that employees in managerial roles are more sensitive to breaches involving emotional aspects such as fairness, consideration, and recognition (18). Put differently, they likely expect more from executive management, as they tend to identify more closely with the organization and its leadership than front-line employees do.

Financial Reaction.

Although the birthday gift is primarily intended to strengthen the interpersonal relationship between managers and employees, it also carries monetary value. We examine whether the negative effect of not receiving the gift on time is driven mainly by the perceived loss of anticipated financial resources.

Fig. 4C, D, G, and H, suggests that employees who received higher gift values were not more likely to show a decline in labor supply. In fact, the increase in sickness absence (G) is significantly greater among those who received lower-value gifts. The effect on working hours appears for employees with both higher and lower gift values, though it is somewhat stronger among those receiving higher-value gifts. This evidence suggests that monetary value alone is not driving the response to the delay. (One might have expected that longer-tenured employees (i.e., those receiving higher-value gifts) would exhibit a larger increase in absence, since they have accrued more sick leave and may find it easier to be absent. However, Fig. 4 shows this is not the case. Moreover, working hours are consistently scheduled by the store manager for all employees, regardless of tenure, mitigating potential bias. Because gift value maps one-to-one with employee tenure, this analysis effectively captures tenure effects. A separate analysis by tenure is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. B.3).

Alternative Mechanisms.

Other mechanisms may influence employees’ responses to not receiving the birthday gift and card on time. For example, such perceived mistreatment could erode trust in management, leading to decreased motivation and engagement, and ultimately affecting labor supply. Additionally, not receiving the birthday gift on time could create uncertainty and anxiety about future rewards and recognition, contributing to reduced morale and performance. While our current dataset does not allow for a direct assessment of these alternative mechanisms, they represent important venues for future research.

Supplemental Analyses

Timing of Birthday within the Month.

Ideally, we would use within-month daily variation in working hours and sickness absence to analyze responses to the mistreatment event. However, since the available data are aggregated at the monthly level, such granularity is not possible. To address this limitation as effectively as we can, we exploit variation in the timing of employee birthdays. Specifically, we compare responses between employees whose birthdays fall early in the month (before the 15th) and those with birthdays later in the month (after the 15th). We expect stronger reactions during the birthday month (τ=0) for early birthdays, whereas for later birthdays, the effect should be more pronounced in the following month (τ=1).

The results, shown in Fig. 5, support this hypothesis for working hours (A and B). For sickness absence, the increase is more pronounced in the month following the birthday for all employees (E and F). This pattern may reflect the greater effort and administrative requirements involved in taking sick leave, compared to adjusting working hours, which can be done more easily and immediately.

Month of Birth.

Fig. 2C indicates that a disproportionate share of individuals born in January did not receive their birthday gift on time (76%). One might be concerned that our results are merely a mechanical effect driven by something unique happening in January. To address this, we excluded individuals born in January and conducted the analysis on those born between February and December. The results, presented in Fig. 5 C and G, alleviate this concern. They show that the response in working hours remains strong and significant for individuals born outside of January, particularly in the birthday month. For sickness absence, the estimated effects are similar in magnitude to those observed in the full sample.

We further examined the analysis excluding December-born employees, who experience delays at a significantly lower rate (23%). The estimates shown in Fig. 5 D and H, indicate that the effects remain consistent both in magnitude and significance, confirming that the results are not driven by variation in these specific birth months.

Variation in Gift-Giving Timing Across Years.

As employee–manager dynamics evolve over time, past experiences with gift-giving timing likely shape employees’ expectations and influence whether they perceive not receiving the card on time as mistreatment. Specifically, employees who received their gift on time in 2018 probably developed expectations for timely delivery in 2019, making any delay that year more likely to be perceived as mistreatment and potentially eliciting a stronger response. In contrast, those who experienced a delay in 2018 may have adjusted their expectations, resulting in a diminished response.

To test this, we focus on employees observed in both years (38% of the sample) and redefine the treatment and control groups. (Among employees observed in both years, 56% received the gift on time in both years, 34% experienced a delay in one year, and 10% experienced delays in both years). The treatment group includes employees who received the gift on time in 2018 but experienced a delay in 2019, while the control group consists of those who received the gift on time in both years.

Conclusions

This paper examines the impact of perceived mild workplace mistreatment on individual employee labor supply. Using unique data from a retail chain that provides employees with birthday gifts, we measure workplace mistreatment by identifying cases in which birthday gifts and greeting cards were not received on time. We use this context to provide evidence of the link between minor mistreatment and employee performance.

Our findings reveal that perceived workplace mistreatment significantly affects labor supply, resulting in a substantial reduction in working hours, totaling more than two hours monthly, and a notable increase in employees’ sickness absence of over half a day per month. This addresses an important boundary condition gap in both economics and psychology: Even common mistreatments, often considered minor, can have significant effects when occurring between managers and employees. Given the widespread prevalence of mild mistreatment in workplaces (20), our results carry important implications across industries and organizations, highlighting that even minor mistreatment may produce far-reaching negative consequences for individual and organizational performance outcomes. Organizations should therefore actively monitor, address, and reduce incidents of mild mistreatment, recognizing their costs not only to employee performance but also to overall organizational effectiveness.

While addressing mild mistreatment may be challenging for organizations due to its subtle nature, our findings suggest that organizations should remain vigilant and avoid dismissing such behaviors as harmless. Taking workplace mistreatment seriously—even subtle instances—sends a clear message to employees and managers that any form of uncivil behavior will not be tolerated. Organizations should also implement preventive measures, including establishing clear channels for reporting mistreatment, ensuring appropriate responses when concerns are raised, and investing in targeted interventions.

Our findings show that the negative effects of the delay dissipate once employees receive their birthday gift, with employees’ performance outcomes returning to baseline levels. This suggests that the relationship can be repaired through timely managerial acknowledgment and resolution or, alternatively, that employees eventually move on and adapt to the minor incident. In either case, our results underscore the critical role of the manager–employee relationship in shaping employee performance and highlight the fragile nature of these dynamics, contributing to the broader literature on factors influencing employee–manager relationships and firm management practices (26, 46).

In terms of future research, our findings highlight the critical role of managerial interpersonal skills in shaping employee performance. The minor mistreatment caused by a late birthday gift exemplifies common day-to-day situations in which these skills are tested and required, especially in managers’ ability to meet employees’ personal expectations. By exploring this dynamic, we also contribute to the literature on management quality, demonstrating how managerial interpersonal skills directly influence employee performance.

Our results suggest that interpersonal skills and managing mistreatment should not be the sole responsibility of individual managers. Organizational factors, such as workplace demands and managerial workload, also play a crucial role and thus should be addressed at the organizational level to balance profit-driven objectives with people-management responsibilities. Moreover, emphasizing interpersonal skills in managerial job descriptions and selection processes is essential, as this may help mitigate mild mistreatment often caused by insufficient attention to the importance of the manager–employee relationship. Future research should therefore focus on identifying effective interventions to reduce mild mistreatment and enhance managers’ awareness and interpersonal skills to improve manager–employee relations.
