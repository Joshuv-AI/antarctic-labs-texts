Ice-cold beer–triggered swallow syncope with reproducible supra-Hisian AV block documented by intracardiac recordings
Authors: Daisuke Sato, Kumi Uchimura, Takashi Katashima, Yumie Matsui, Masahiro Yoshinaga, Masahiro Karakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSwallow syncope is a rare situational syncope caused by an exaggerated vagal reflex and may result in bradyarrhythmia or atrioventricular block.MethodsA 63-year-old man with recurrent syncope triggered by beer ingestion underwent cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, ambulatory electrocardiographic, and electrophysiological evaluation, including provocation testing.ResultsOnly ice-cold beer reproducibly induced transient AH block. Introduction

Swallow syncope is an uncommon form of situational syncope characterized by transient loss of consciousness triggered by swallowing [1]. It is considered a neurally mediated reflex disorder caused by exaggerated afferent stimulation from the pharynx or esophagus, leading to increased parasympathetic outflow and subsequent bradyarrhythmia or hypotension [2], [3]. Reported electrocardiographic manifestations include sinus arrest, sinoatrial block, and atrioventricular (AV) block [1], [4], [5]. Although swallow syncope has been described previously, detailed electrophysiological confirmation of the level of block during targeted provocation remains uncommon [4], [5]. We report a rare case in which only ice-cold beer reproducibly induced transient AH block, providing mechanistic insight into a highly specific trigger of vagally mediated swallow syncope.

Methods

A 63-year-old man was evaluated for recurrent syncope occurring immediately after beer ingestion. Diagnostic evaluation included physical examination, 12-lead electrocardiography, inpatient telemetry, transthoracic echocardiography, head and chest computed tomography, head-up tilt testing, exercise treadmill testing, right and left heart catheterization, coronary angiography with acetylcholine provocation, and barium esophagography. Ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring was performed to capture spontaneous events.

Because swallow syncope was suspected, provocation testing was conducted under continuous monitoring using routine meals, hot coffee, effervescent agents, cold carbonated soda, cold sake, room-temperature beer, and ice-cold beer freshly taken from a refrigerator at 4 °C. Electrophysiological study with intracardiac recordings was performed during provocation to localize the level of conduction block.

Results

The patient had experienced intermittent presyncope for approximately 10 years, initially about once yearly, with progression to monthly episodes during the preceding year. Symptoms consisted of a choking sensation in the pharynx followed by visual dimming or syncope immediately after drinking beer. He was admitted after a witnessed syncopal episode associated with facial trauma.

Baseline examination was unremarkable. Sinus rhythm was present on 12-lead electrocardiography, and several days of inpatient telemetry showed no spontaneous arrhythmia. Echocardiography, computed tomography, head-up tilt testing, treadmill testing, right and left heart catheterization, and coronary angiography revealed no structural or ischemic abnormality. Acetylcholine provocation was negative for coronary spasm. Barium esophagography showed no diverticulum, mucosal lesion, spasm, or achalasia.

During monitored provocation testing, routine meals, hot coffee, effervescent agents, cold carbonated soda (4 °C), cold sake (4 °C), and room-temperature beer (24 °C) did not reproduce symptoms or AV block. In contrast, ingestion of ice-cold beer (4 °C) reproducibly induced transient AV block due to AH block, lasting approximately 4 s during electrophysiological evaluation (Fig. 1). Disopyramide was initiated, but repeat provocation during treatment again induced high-grade AV block, indicating lack of efficacy (Fig. 2A). Because of recurrent syncope with traumatic injury and documented bradyarrhythmia, permanent pacemaker implantation was performed. After implantation, repeat provocation with ice-cold beer induced AV block with appropriate ventricular pacing and complete absence of symptoms (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

This case highlights three clinically important points. First, the trigger was extraordinarily specific: only ice-cold beer reproduced symptoms, whereas other cold beverages and room-temperature beer did not. This suggests that temperature alone was insufficient and that additional factors related to beer ingestion, possibly including alcohol content or its interaction with esophageal stimulation, may have contributed to reflex activation [6].

Second, targeted provocation combined with intracardiac recordings clarified the mechanism. The documented AH block localized the disturbance to the atrioventricular nodal level and strongly supported a functional vagally mediated process rather than intrinsic His-Purkinje disease. Although swallow syncope is a recognized entity, detailed electrophysiological confirmation of the level of block during symptom reproduction remains uncommon [4], [5]. Thus, the novelty of the present case lies not in the existence of swallow syncope itself, but in the highly specific trigger, the systematic negative provocation results with related beverages, and the intracardiac documentation of transient supra-Hisian AV block during provocation.

Third, permanent pacing was effective when pharmacological therapy failed. In this patient, disopyramide did not prevent recurrence, whereas pacemaker implantation abolished symptoms despite repeat exposure to the trigger. These findings support pacing as a reasonable therapeutic option in patients with recurrent swallow syncope accompanied by documented bradyarrhythmia or AV block [3], [4], [5].

An additional contemporary treatment consideration is cardioneuroablation (CNA). In selected patients with reflex syncope and extrinsic vagally mediated AV block, CNA has emerged as a potential alternative to cardiac pacing [7]. In particular, functional supra-Hisian or AH-level block with reproducible vagal provocation may represent a mechanistically suitable substrate for CNA [7], [8]. Moreover, successful CNA has been reported in swallow syncope with functional AV block [9], [10]. Therefore, CNA could be considered a potential therapeutic option in similar patients.

Nevertheless, in the present case, recurrent syncope had already resulted in traumatic injury, and a treatment with established immediate reliability was preferred. For this reason, permanent pacemaker implantation was selected rather than CNA. Accordingly, this case not only demonstrates a highly specific trigger of swallow syncope, but also highlights the value of mechanism-oriented provocation testing in guiding contemporary therapeutic decision-making.
