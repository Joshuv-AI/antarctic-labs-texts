From Collection to Collaboration: Building Ethical Frameworks for Genetic Research
Authors: Devin A. Madden, Emerson Dusic, Isabella E. Rios, Kimberly Zayhowski, L Jude Tantay, Nasa Sinnott-Armstrong, Rodrigo Aguayo-Romero, Sima Rabinowitz, Michelle A. Ramos, Kellan Baker, Sari L. Reisner, Carol R. Horowitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse (TNGD) communities are not routinely included as partners in the research that will impact them. The expansion of genomic and gender identity data collection has allowed researchers to explore transgender identity genomics research (TIGR), or associations between genomics and gender identity. We used a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach to explore TNGD perspectives on TIGR in order to advance ethical practices, minimize harms, and maximize potential benefits. An Executive Stakeholder Board of TNGD community members, advocates, clinicians, and ethics, legal, genomics, and health equity scholars co-developed recruitment strategies, data collection tools, analytic strategies, and a dissemination plan which informed qualitative interviews with 31 U.S.-based TNGD adults. The mean age was 34 years (range = 19–73). Twelve participants were nonbinary, 12 were trans women, and 7 were trans men. The majority (61%) were people of color. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we conceptualized four themes related to ethical TIGR research practices: 1) Are we represented? 2) Are we protected? 3) Are we benefiting? 4) Are we understood? Findings underscore the importance of conducting any TIGR or genomics research with and not on TNGD communities. Ethical research benefits from sustained partnership, transparency, and reflexivity, particularly amidst ideologically motivated threats to inclusive science. Centering TNGD voices in, and recommendations for, genomics strengthens both scientific rigor and community trust. The incongruity of attempting to interrupt a CBPR study focused on ethical research practices in TIGR underscores the contradiction between stated federal commitments to research integrity and the actions taken.

Introduction

The existence of large biobanks with genomic, clinical, and sociodemographic data can propel critical research.1,2 However, such research is not without risks or ethical concerns,3 raising questions about privacy protections, scope of informed consent, dissemination of sensitive results,3–6 and failing to distinguish between biological and social influences on genetics.7

The rise of biobanks has been accompanied by increasing calls to enhance racial, ethnic, and cultural diversity in genomic datasets, with the goal of improving understanding and mitigating health disparities.1,7,8 More recently, there has been growing recognition of the importance of including transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse (TNGD) individuals in studies, and particular care has been place on examination of how gender- and sex-related data are collected and used.9–13 Consequently, an expanding body of data on TNGD individuals may now be available for genomics research.

While these data have the potential to advance understanding of health risks, disparities, and inclusion in scientific innovation for TNGD populations,14–16 they also present distinct ethical and social risks that are amplified by a legacy of eugenics and ongoing experiences of discrimination faced by TNGD individuals.9,11,17,18 Introduced in the late 1800s, theories of eugenics endorsed genetic practices (e.g., selective breeding) that were deeply embedded in scientific racism and gained momentum throughout the twentieth century.19 Today, many scientists continue to grapple with the global impact of eugenics on marginalized populations while cautioning that eugenics practices still exist.19–21 For example, many argue that the racist eugenics attitudes that were foundational to nationalist immigration laws in the 1920s continue to prevail in more modern border control efforts and detention practices.20,22,23 Scientists and scholars also worry that assisted reproductive technologies allowing for trait selection are being utilized in ableist ways.19–21 Additionally, TNGD people have been subjected to “passive eugenics” practices through sexual and reproductive health policies, practices, and norms that neglect to consider TNGD people and affect both their ability to receive care and the care they receive.24 TNGD identities have long been framed by a pathologizing medical model that relies on diagnostic language such as gender identity disorder (removed after the Diagnostics and Statistics Manual of Mental Disorders [DMS] IV), gender dysphoria (DSM V), and, more recently, gender incongruence (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-11).25–27 Even affirming providers have to work within this model, and such diagnostic guidance may influence how clinicians’ make decisions when providing or restricting access to gender affirming healthcare.25–27 This pathologization model, coupled with heightened experiences of discrimination when seeking medical care,28 apprehension over limitations to data privacy protection measures as TNGD people are facing heightened discrimination,11 and arguments about “passive eugenics”24 can help explain the increased medical distrust among TNGD people, including in the context of genomics research processes.29

These concerns are particularly pertinent to Transgender Identity Genomic Research (TIGR): the investigation of “trans-genes” or potential associations between genes, biology, and transgender identities. Exploration of genetic underpinnings of gender identity began decades ago.30,31 While one study found TNGD participants more likely to hold positive than negative views on TIGR, the researchers still cautioned against moving too quickly,32 and qualitative interviews with TNGD participants on the topic support such prudence.17 Those concerned with eugenics note, large genomics datasets make such inquiries more feasible and expands the types of research,19 and consequently, the pool of researchers who can study potential associations.16,20,25 Such research is often conducted without the meaningful input or oversight of TNGD communities with the lived experience of harms noted above, and there is limited guidance to help researchers maximize the benefit and minimize the harms of their work.17,25,32–34

Using a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach grounded in applying critical pedagogy to public health, researchers can collaborate closely with community members who contribute their lived experience as integral partners in the research process rather than as peripheral advisors.35–37 Although CBPR is used with increasing frequency in both TNGD health34,38,39 and genomics research,17,40,41 we have an unrealized opportunity to ensure that TNGD communities play a pivotal role in guiding TIGR. This will allow TNGD communities to incorporate their varied and nuanced perspectives, halt the ongoing pathologization and discrimination against TNGD individuals, and provide guidelines for researchers so that findings are more helpful than hurtful.17,25,32

Our team embarked on The Transforming Genomics: Maximizing Community Input and Impact Study (5R01HG013145–03) to better understand TNGD peoples’ perspectives and develop ethical guidelines for TIGR.42 Our research team and Executive Stakeholder Board (Board), a majority of whom identify as TNGD and/or queer, co-led every phase of study design, implementation, evaluation, and dissemination. Research included interviews with TNGD people from across the US; surveys with a nationwide sample of TNGD individuals; and listening sessions with clinicians, genomic data experts, IRB experts, and ethical and legal scholars. Based on this research, we plan to host a summit with TNGD leaders, genomics researchers, ethical scholars, and other key constituents to build actionable guidelines for researchers and funders who may pursue TIGR.

In March 2025, we received notice of termination from the National Institutes of Health stating that “Research programs based on gender identity are often unscientific, have little identifiable return on investment, and do nothing to enhance the health of many Americans. Many such studies ignore, rather than seriously examine, biological realities.” Grant funding was reinstated in August 2025 after a federal judge ruled the terminations unlawful.43 In this manuscript, we seek to uphold the commitments made to participants by disseminating key findings on how researchers, if conducting TIGR, can ethically design studies. We consider these results in the context of the federal government’s attempted interruption of our science, highlighting the incongruity of disrupting a federally funded CBPR project exploring ethical research practices in participatory work. We reflect on what the current political and social context and the results of our study may teach us about the importance of CBPR approaches in future research with TNGD populations.

Methods

The majority of individuals on our investigative team and Board identify as TNGD and/or queer, and include clinicians, TNGD advocates, and ethics, genomics, and health equity scholars. The team and Board represent diversity across geography, race/ethnicity, age, gender identity, sexual orientation, and prior engagement in scientific research, and have extensive networks in diverse TNGD communities. The principal research analysts on this paper are queer and TNGD, trained in qualitative methods, and experienced in CBPR grounded in critical pedagogical theory35,37 and reflexive thematic analysis (RTA).44

Recruitment and sampling

Eligibility/inclusion included self-report of identifying as transgender, nonbinary, and/or gender diverse, 18–80 years of age, currently residing in the U.S., and being fluent in English or Spanish. The Board chartered an interdisciplinary workgroup to design and develop all recruitment materials, including customized flyers which were shared widely through their networks (Figure 1). Interested individuals completed a brief demographic survey on a HIPAA-compliant REDCap platform45 to confirm eligibility. We sought to recruit 30 participants based on guidance around theoretical saturation,46 and to pursue qualitative richness through information power,47 enriched by a maximum variation sampling approach, which facilitated a demographically and geographically diverse sample and a plurality of perspectives.48 The research team followed up with selected participants, shared a research information sheet, and scheduled interviews. The study was determined exempt by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai’s and University of Michigan’s Program for the Protection of Human Subjects’ Institutional Review Board.

Data collection and analysis

In collaboration with a Data Workgroup established by the Board, the study team developed, piloted, and revised an interview guide informed by prior research and Board member insights. The guide included topics related to perceived risks and benefits of TIGR; acceptability of TIGR; who should be leading this work; barriers to TNGD inclusion in genetics/genomics research; and recommendations for researchers. Two authors (ED, IR) conducted interviews via a HIPAA compliant Zoom platform from June through September 2024, with each interview lasting approximately 60 min. Participants completed a survey capturing sociodemographics and previous genomics research experience. All interviews were professionally recorded, redacted, and uploaded to Atlas.ti49 for coding and analysis. The analytic sample included all interviews, including two pilot interviews.

Three authors (ED, IR, DM) utilized Reflexive Thematic Analysis,44,50 modified to support a community-driven analytic process. Analysts independently coded the first three transcripts and then compared codes and memos; discussed differences and built shared understanding of each other’s perspectives; generated inductive codes; and iteratively refined the codebook. Once consensus was reached, the analysts divided the remaining transcripts for individual coding.

The research question for this paper, developed after the analysts completed the intensive coding process, was: What do TNGD participants deem important for researchers engaged in TIGR to consider about their research practice? Two analysts (DM, ED) conceptualized preliminary themes which the team refined, finalized, and articulated, considering how responses not only elucidated their perspectives on ethical research practices, but also the contexts and ideas driving these perspectives. We hosted an online session with study participants to share a summary of interview findings, and participants offered their reflections.7,11,51

Results

Table 1 displays interviewees’ demographic characteristics (n = 31). The mean age was 34 years; 12 were nonbinary, 12 trans women, and 7 trans men; 61% were people of color. The study team conceptualized four themes on how to ethically conduct TIGR research: 1) Are we at the table? 2) Are we protected? 3) Are we benefitting? and 4) Are we understood? To protect the privacy of participants, we have not included sociodemographic information with quotes from the interviews, and instead of IDs, assigned participants numbers (e.g., P1, P2, P3…) for quotes we share. Five participants joined the online session we hosted after the analysis and supported the results. Their reflections helped inform the discussion.

Theme 1: Are we represented?

Regardless of perceptions of if TIGR should occur, which varied across the sample, many also emphasized the importance of TNGD people being meaningfully engaged at every stage of the research process, including leading or co-leading studies. One participant explained, “… I think that’s probably the biggest one. You know … if it’s for us, by us. (P1)” Some participants acknowledged that while TNGD leadership may not always be possible, researchers should always work with TNGD people, have foundational knowledge about TNGD identities, and remain open to learning. As one participant noted, “I can’t expect every person to have knowledge of the way gender is constructed. But if people would understand that trans means different things to different people and everyone’s going to have a different relationship to transness, that would be lovely. (P3)” Participants also described the importance of consulting community members in developing culturally resonant and responsive study instruments, reviewing findings, establishing advisory boards with transgender scientists and community members, and co-developing dissemination strategies that maximize community impact. They cautioned against tokenism or relying on a single individual to speak for the diverse TNGD community. Others suggested enhancing transparency by including the gender identities and demographics of the research team, not just of participants. One participant, calling forward notions of positionality and reflexivity in research papers, suggested: “The diversity in the research team, I think, should be transparent … (P11)”

Participants expressed the view that meaningful engagement and representation of TNGD people throughout the research process fosters greater trust, in turn yielding improved data quality: “Trans people will always be more open to someone who is like them before they open up to someone who is not like them. (P14)” Participants highlighted that this engagement ensures that results have real-life application and are responsive to TNGD communities’ needs and wants, ultimately minimizing harms and maximizing potential benefits.

Theme 2. Are we protected?

Transparency in research purpose, data use, and privacy protections emerged as critical concerns for many participants. Some said they would feel reassured if data were to be de-identified and aggregated and privacy safeguards implemented, especially for research using large biobanks aggregating large volumes of data. Others wanted stronger commitments and attention to transparency and privacy, the importance of government-issued Certificates of Confidentiality, and clear assurance from researchers “… that giving a sample is not going to come back and bite you in the butt. (P15)”

Participants expressed varying views when asked about how and why data on gender identity should be collected, analyzed, and shared. Many articulated a desire to better understand the purpose and scope of the research, with mixed opinions about storing genetic data alongside sex and gender data, especially in large biobanks that might later use samples or data for unspecified purposes. Some were concerned that large-scale collection of gender identity data would result in a loss of anonymity and heightened risk for TNGD people; others would be comfortable sharing only TNGD-related data for specific, well-defined analysis. For some participants, these concerns were intensified by distrust of large, for-profit databanks that might sell or misuse their data. Even those who trusted biobanks’ current processes and supported TIGR research had reservations about how privacy might be maintained, citing the rapid growth of Artificial Intelligence and its potential to integrate biobank data in unforeseen ways and be “ultimately identifiable to me in ways I might not anticipate now. (P21)”

Several participants discussed that transparency should extend to who funds the research; the purpose of the research; who has access to data; if principal investigators are appropriately “vetting (P12)” those who have access to data; and how data will be used. To support transparency and ensure consenting processed are ethical and comprehensible, participants urged researchers to provide information about the research in accessible ways. This includes avoiding complicated consent forms with overly technical or legal jargon that may confuse people. One participant recommended using visual tools, such as short presentations, videos, or infographics, to explain what data are being collected and used, and how privacy is protected.

Privacy concerns were especially salient for participants of color, who described amplified risks of identification and underscored that for TNGD individuals, especially those with intersecting marginalized identities, privacy and transparency are essential conditions for safety and trust in research. One participant said:

I do not think there is a safe or reliable way to de-identify a trans person of color. … If you then localize it to an area, that brings it down to… potentially even like five to 10 people in your area. And then if you add age, sex assigned at birth, and… any other health variables, that’s done. They know who you are.

Theme 3. Are we benefitting?

Participants discussed the value of centering TNGD participants’ needs and priorities in research, asking researchers to “… make sure that what they’re doing is seen as a goal by the community they’re studying. (P13)” Several recommended that researchers link their studies with complementary initiatives that are responsive to participants’ direct needs. For example, some participants encouraged researchers to consider the larger social context, suggesting that projects could also help address unmet basic needs related to housing, food, and healthcare. Some participants acknowledged that this may not be feasible, but said, “You can do a little something. (P29)” One participant suggested that research could create professional opportunities to build skills for TNGD people, encouraging cisgender researchers particularly to mentor and hire TNGD individuals: “Whether or not I think this [TIGR research] is going to happen or should happen, it’s going to happen. And I would like to see trans people get a job out of it. (P21)” Ultimately, one participant, reflecting on prior research experiences, cautioned: “We can’t just make them [participants] feel like numbers. We can’t just make them feel like pincushions. … They have lots of things going on in their personal lives before they think about doing tests and all … (P14)”

Other participants highlighted the importance of ensuring that TNGD participants retain agency over the data they contribute and benefit from its use as a form of giving back to the community “… so [that] it’s not just like extracting but also giving back that information to you. (P4)” Another interviewee urged researchers to “[…] “…collaborate with community activists so that there is a social tool that will complement the data. (P5)”

Participants expressed that researchers should contribute to the communities from which they learn through both traditional methods (e.g., incentives) and more meaningful community-centered forms of reciprocity. Participants agreed that fair compensation was critical (“No free labor [P16]”) and offered suggestions, including small gestures of respect and support on behalf of the research team such as making themselves “available when they’re [participants are] available (P24)” and flexibility around scheduling.

Theme 4. Are we understood?

The participants clearly articulated reasons for needing research to be representative, protective of their identities, and mutually beneficial that they felt were important for researchers to understand. Participants often referenced the historic and ongoing distrust toward the scientific community with continued mistreatment, pathologization, and political targeting of TNGD people in the 2024 political climate (when the interviews took place) as the context for their requests in how the science is approached. They described how their experiences of disrespect and power imbalances in healthcare transfer to research. One participant characterized this as a “fraught relationship,” saying, “we really need the medical establishment to give us the help that we need and the medicine we need but the experience … isn’t always pleasant, and is not always collaborative and not always… dignified. (P24)” Others explicitly connected genetics research with the historical abuse of marginalized groups, noting how science has been used to “… exterminate said peoples or enslave said peoples. (P2)” Historical medical exploitation of Black people by the scientific community was perceived as a central barrier to engagement in research, with some participants reminding us that the history is not that distant.

To build trust in TIGR, many stressed that researchers should explicitly name past harms. One participant suggested “acknowledging that a lot of genetic research has a history with eugenics (P16)” and reassuring participants that researchers are taking specific measures to avoid repeating this history. Participants recognizing that repairing trust in the face of such history would take time.: “… That is a historical trauma that [we] aren’t going to get over in one or two generations.(P3)” Participants also expressed that this distrust is further heightened by the current climate of visible and increasing transphobia, and by the fear that data could be used “against the community, (P5)” especially in how research findings are communicated. One participant commented, “…it’s [history] only made me more wary of genetic research as far as the ways that eugenic ideologies have been used, are currently being used and are likely to be used under…particular governments… (P23)”. Another urged researchers to “… learn from our history and don’t ever try and divorce science from the current political moment” while also reflecting on intersectional experiences that give them pause about sharing data, noting “… living in this over-criminalization movement, over-criminalizing Black people, trans people for just existing, over-criminalizing anything and everything. (P29)”

Some participants noted that the current environment ultimately shaped what they believed should or should not be studied and that “any data collection should really, really be justified. (P1)” Another explained, “I’m not really worried about data. I’m worried about policy, (P25)” pointing to instances where states have misused people’s data from gender marker changes.

Participants acknowledged that TNGD community members’ views on whether and how TIGR research should be conducted, or what information should be collected, will differ based on the political landscape of the state people live in, and their degree of being open about their TNGD identities (“outness”). Some fear that research participation could expose them to others, including their family, stating “…I’m not really out to everyone, and I wish that I was in a place where I felt that I could, but I’m not. (P10)” Others were less concerned about de-identification because they were very public about their identity and therefore likely to experience discrimination either way. One participant summarized the issue of the de-identification of data and trust, “I think if you ask ten people you get ten different answers. It has to do with everything from comfort level, anxiety level, knowledge level, context basis–the whole nine yards. (P12)”

Discussion

We sought to engage TNGD adults in community-based participatory research to interrogate their views on the conduct of TIGR. The interviews yielded insights into how researchers could ethically conduct TIGR. Participants’ recommendations for researchers deserve thoughtful consideration by study teams in the context of their projects. Participants emphasized the importance of considering who is represented (Theme 1) by such research through leadership or active engagement across all phases of research; urged researchers to protect participants (Theme 2) by maintaining participant privacy and ensuring transparency about how data are shared and used; and encouraged traditional and novel forms of reciprocity to ensure communities benefit (Theme 3). Participants recommendations reflected a recognition of historical and ongoing discrimination, pathologization, and harms by the scientific community inflicted on marginalized communities,7,17,18,52 as well as an increasingly hostile sociopolitical climate.51,53 These findings echo previous research on the importance of community engagement in genomics research,2 including TNGD engagement,,17,25,32,33 while offering new perspectives on ethical TIGR practices, including how research is conducted, used, and shared. Additionally, it is important to observe how participants’ recommendations align with work that Indigenous scholars in North America have led to explore Indigenous populations’ attitudes on genetic research; Indigenous scholars have been at the forefront of building alternative frameworks for conducting genomic research that could serve as models for guidelines in TIGR research.52,54

Grounding a CBPR approach in critical pedagogical theory requires acknowledging that the research is continuously being informed and influenced by historical and contemporary power dynamics as embedded in social and political contexts.55 Therefore, it is important to note that these interviews were conducted in 2024 during a period when several states were enacting increasingly hostile policies toward TNGD populations including bills restricting bathroom use, healthcare bans, limits on legal gender markers, and restrictions on inclusive educational curricula.56 Prior research exploring the impacts of hostile federal administrations suggests that such policies and practices shape TNGD communities’ perspectives of safety and wellbeing and are associated with higher stress, anxiety, and hypervigilance.57,58 Our results may be influenced by this climate, and participants expressed these concerns in some cases explicitly anticipating future harm. After the interviews, the federal government issued executive orders purporting to redefine sex and gender, actions that explicitly extended federal authority while constraining TNGD individuals’ autonomy.59 Interviewees’ responses might differ in the current context. Participants’ perspectives, for example, on data privacy and protection may have evolved in response to emerging threats and policy changes. As emphasized in Theme 4, historical and social contexts profoundly shape perspectives; participants’ outlooks may evolve as these contexts shift, underscoring the need for continuous engagement with TNGD communities rather than relying on static assumptions.

Trust in the research process was a key concern for many participants who questioned whether researchers always act with good intentions. CBPR practitioners understand the reasons for this distrust in marginalized communities. CBPR is explicitly aimed at restoring trust and building and sustaining meaningful relationships with communities.37 Disrupting CBPR projects mid-cycle risks undermining the very relationships that CBPR seeks to build and could lead to the belief that researchers are conducting “helicopter research,” engaging communities briefly without sustained commitment or a sincere interest in having their research benefit the community.59–61 When funding for CBPR research is suddenly withdrawn, sustaining partnerships, maintaining transparency, seeing projects through to completion, or upholding commitments made to participants become difficult with far-reaching implications. Just as past misuse of research data continues to impact how participants think about research (Theme 4), terminating or disrupting science designed to benefit TNGD people may have lasting consequences for how TNGD communities view research and whether they choose to participate in research in the future.

Halting scientific research because it is no longer deemed a government priority signals profound disrespect for the communities such research is intended to serve, particularly those already marginalized. Selective disruption of research based on ideology rather than scientific merit (as evidenced by this study being rigorously reviewed and funded in a very competitive environment) has significant ethical, scientific, and public health implications. The federal judge who reinstated the award ruled that the NIH’s termination of grants was unlawful and “demonstrates an unmistakable pattern of discrimination.”43

The current sociopolitical context offers an opportunity to reexamine and strengthen CBPR practices across disciplines, particularly in fields such as genomics where community engaged approaches are less common and well developed than in other disciplines. The unstable funding climate (litigation regarding terminated/reinstated grants is ongoing) has resulted in significant uncertainty about the administration’s shifting research priorities. As a result, it will be especially important to engage populations and communities in exploring issues of safety and trust that may dissuade research participation and to prioritize research that addresses community interests and concerns.

Many of the themes that emerged in the current study—understanding the purpose of the research and the intentions of investigators; clarity about how data is collected, stored, interpreted, shared, and disseminated; transparency and trust—are relevant for nearly every type of research, in any context, and are not exclusive to TIGR or to research engaging TNGD communities. Nonetheless, the current sociopolitical climate appears to require heightened awareness of, and attention to, these rigorous and fundamental core tenets of scientific research.

Conclusions

At a time when research interest in TIGR appears to be growing, our research finds that TNGD participants view ethical engagement in TIGR as contingent on specific practices, including meaningful community leadership or co-leadership, maintaining transparency and privacy protections, and giving back to communities. These findings should be understood in the context of a rapidly evolving sociopolitical climate that has become increasingly hostile toward TNGD people. The disruption of the government funded research presented here represents just one example of how political ideology and the resulting actions can threaten scientific integrity and undermine the trust essential to community-engaged research, potentially shaping how TNGD communities view, participate in, and benefit from science for years to come.
