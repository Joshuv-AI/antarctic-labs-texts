Estimating prevalence and predictors of glucose-lowering overtreatment among older adults with type 2 diabetes in long-term care and community settings: a machine learning–based cohort study
Authors: Greg Carney, Sean Burnett, Anshula Ambasta, Wade Thompson, Linda Lapp, Colin Dormuth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the prevalence of potential overtreatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) among older adults and to develop and compare predictive models to identify patient and physician characteristics associated with overtreatment.DesignPopulation-based retrospective cohort study with predictive modelling.SettingA province-wide, publicly funded healthcare system in British Columbia, Canada, using linked administrative health claims data from 2016 to 2023.ParticipantsResidents of long-term care facilities over age 65, and community-dwelling individuals over age 75, with a diagnosis of T2DM and a glycated haemoglobin (A1C) laboratory value ≤7.0%. Participants were required to have ≥365 days of continuous provincial health insurance coverage prior to their index A1C test. Patients receiving palliative care and those with missing physician information were excluded.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPotential overtreatment of T2DM, defined a priori as overlapping prescriptions for ≥2 glucose-lowering medications or ≥1 insulin or sulfonylurea dispensing within 90 days after the index A1C test.Model performance outcomes included discrimination (area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value). Performance metrics were calculated with 95% CIs using a 25% temporally distinct test dataset (2021–2023). No changes were made to outcome definitions after protocol development.ResultsAmong 133 773 patients with an A1C≤7.0%, 38 074 (28.5%) were classified as overtreated. These patients had a mean age of 79.6 years, were 47% female, and had a median A1C of 6.4%. The gradient boost model was the best performing model overall, using a combination of expert-selected variables and data-driven variables, achieving an AUC of 0.87, sensitivity of 0.81 and negative predictive value of 0.89. The top predictors of overtreatment included use of blood glucose test strips, A1C test volume, polypharmacy, specialist involvement and measures of diabetes severity.ConclusionsOvertreatment of T2DM was prevalent among older adults in our cohort. Machine learning algorithms that integrate clinical expertise with data-driven variable selection performed the best in predicting T2DM overtreatment. We identified several patient and physician characteristics as key contributors that may inform future clinical practice and quality improvement initiatives, although external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Older patients with diabetes are increasing due to the ageing population in Canada.1 Overtreatment refers to use of medication where harms may outweigh benefits.2 Overtreatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) can be detrimental in older adults due to the risk of severe hypoglycaemia and other adverse outcomes.3 Observational studies of older people with T2DM and elevated clinical complexity have found an association between tight blood glucose control and increased risk of falls, severe hypoglycaemia, emergency department visits, hospital admissions and all-cause mortality.48

Evidence of benefit from aggressive glucose lowering in diabetes declines with age, while the risks increase.9 A common definition of overtreatment in diabetes is use of sulfonylureas or insulin among people with a glycated haemoglobin (A1C) <7%.10 Deintensification of therapy in frail and older patients has been incorporated into some prescribing guidelines,11 yet intensive use of blood glucose-lowering treatments in patients with controlled A1C≤7% continues.1215 Predictive modelling of health outcomes is a key tool for identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from treatment modification or targeted quality improvement interventions.1618 With advances in big data availability and computer processing capabilities, machine learning algorithms have evolved rapidly in recent years.19 Machine learning algorithms capable of evaluating large amounts of complex healthcare data have shown promise in many tasks, such as risk stratification, diagnosis and disease and treatment prediction.2022 This study seeks to assess the prevalence of T2DM overtreatment in older patients in British Columbia. It also aims to identify key predictors of overtreatment and explore the added benefits of integrating data-driven predictor selection with variable selection made by clinical experts. The findings of this study have important implications for the management of T2DM among older adults, with the potential to better identify patients at high risk of harm and guide future quality improvement initiatives.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted a retrospective, predictive analysis to identify factors associated with overtreatment of T2DM using administrative health data obtained between 2001 and 2023 from the British Columbia Ministry of Health. The database includes information on patient and physician demographics, medications dispensed from community pharmacies, outpatient physician visits, emergency room and hospital admissions, and laboratory test results. Data are available for all residents of the province, excluding federally insured patients (military veterans, Royal Canadian Mounted Police) and beneficiaries of the First Nations Health Benefits Program. Our research is presented in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data and TRIPOD-AI guidelines.23 24

The cohort was composed of patients with a haemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) laboratory test with a value ≤7.0% who were aged 75 or older, or permanent residents of a long-term care facility aged 65 or older, during the study period of 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023. The date of the first eligible HbA1c test was defined as the Index Date. Patients were required to be permanent residents of BC, with 365 days of continuous medical insurance coverage prior to their Index Date, and were required to have an inpatient or outpatient diagnosis of T2DM (International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD-9): 250.x, ICD-10: E11.x) within 2 years prior to their Index Date. Patients were excluded if they were in palliative care.

Overtreatment definition

The primary outcome of our study was potential overtreatment of T2DM. Patients were defined as overtreated if they had overlapping prescriptions for two or more diabetes medications, or one insulin dispensing, at any time in the 90-day period after their Index Date. Prescription overlap was determined using the medication dispensing date plus the number of days’ supply dispensed, assuming full compliance with the dispensed medication. Diabetes medications were identified according to Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes and included alpha glucosidase inhibitors (A10BF), biguanides (A10BA), combinations of oral diabetes medications (A10BD), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (A10BH), glucagon-like peptide-1 analogues (A10BJ), insulins and analogues for injection (A10AB-A10AE), meglitinides (A10BX), sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (A10BK), sulfonylureas (A10BB) and thiazolidinediones (A10BG). We calculated the prevalence of overtreatment by dividing the number of patients who met the overtreatment criteria within the 90-day post-Index Date period by the total number of eligible patients in the study cohort, expressed as a proportion over the study period.

Feature selection

We extracted 343 variables which included patient and physician characteristics, chronic conditions, and the most prevalent medications, primary hospital admission diagnosis, physician diagnosis, emergency room visit diagnosis and laboratory tests. We assessed medication use and diagnostic variables in the 1-year period prior to Index Date. Chronic conditions were assessed from 1 April 2001 until Index Date. Physician characteristics were assigned based on the physician that requested the index HbA1c laboratory test. We used a data-driven approach to implicitly identify covariates with the most predictive potential; outpatient and inpatient diagnosis codes, procedure codes and laboratory tests were selected based on the 50 most prevalent among all BC residents aged 65 or older during the study period. In addition, commonly used predictors were used, such as Charlson Comorbidity Index score, practitioner age and patient age. We screened for collinearity using a pairwise correlation matrix and dropped a predictor from the pair or group if the correlation was greater than 0.90.25 26 Based on this list of variables, we carried out three experiments to develop our prediction model.

In our first experiment, a team of clinical experts (general internal medicine physician, pharmacist, clinical pharmacologist and pharmacoepidemiologist) generated a list of features (31 variables) believed to be most predictive of T2DM overtreatment in our study population (online supplemental file 1). These features included clinical, demographic and healthcare utilisation characteristics. Using this list of features, we developed a multivariable logistic regression model, which was considered as our baseline model.

In our second experiment, we used all variables that we extracted that passed the collinearity test (ie, that had correlation <0.90, 304 variables) in five supervised machine learning algorithms: logistic regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, random forest, gradient boosting machine (GBM) and naive Bayes.

In our third and final experiment, we used the combination of variables that were included in our first and second experiment (324 variables) in the same five machine learning algorithms as in experiment 2.

The logistic regression model developed in experiment one was used as the baseline comparator to the machine learning models developed in the other experiments. This is to understand whether the additional variables and algorithmic complexity that are capable of identifying potentially novel or non-obvious relationships translate into meaningful improvements in predictive performance for overtreatment.

Machine learning model development and testing

The five machine learning algorithms were chosen based on their variety of identifying variable relationships in the data, as well as their ability to handle the dataset dimensions in our three experiments.2730 All models were developed using R package ‘caret’ V.7.0-1, and ‘‘RandomForest’ V.4.7-1.2 (for random forest) and ‘e1071’ V.1.7-16 (for naive Bayes).3133

Our dataset was randomly divided into a training and testing dataset, using a 75%/25% split. The models were developed, using 10-fold cross-validation on the training set and then validated, using the left-out testing set. Furthermore, where appropriate, grid search was utilised to find the most optimal hyperparameters for the models. For the final models, those with the highest area under the curve (AUC) were favoured. This was chosen to avoid the ‘accuracy paradox’.34 The hyperparameters following grid search were as follows: alpha=1, lambda=0.001 for LASSO, number of trees=600, interaction depth=10, shrinkage=0.01, number of minimum observations in node=10 for gradient boosting and mtry=20, ntree=250, nodesize=10 for random forest.

Performance was measured for all models on both training and testing data, where the best model was chosen based on cross-validation, and for the testing set to understand how the model performs on a left-out dataset. We assessed the model performance based on discrimination and calibration. For discrimination, we extracted AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV). The measures were calculated at the optimal cut-off point based on Youden index, where sensitivity and specificity are maximised for each model. The best performing model was determined based on the highest sensitivity and NPV when applied to the test set. This is to make sure that the model has the best ability to recognise patients who are overtreated while keeping the level of false negatives low. We look at calibration visually and compare the mean and SD of predicted probabilities to outcome prevalence to make sure that the model is applicable to the test set and is not over- or underfitted.35 Finally, the top contributing variables for each model are presented based on measures appropriate for each model, which are: model coefficients for logistic regression and LASSO, mean Gini Index for random forest, and permutation importance for naive Bayes and GBM.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. Our research group has extensively included various interest holders, including patients, in ongoing work related to overtreatment of type 2 diabetes in older adults.36 37 Furthermore, authors of this study include clinicians who would be the direct users of the developed models from this study. Future dissemination of findings will focus on informing healthcare providers, policymakers and health system users to improve quality of care.

Results

Baseline characteristics

We identified 136,954 older people with T2DM and an HbA1c laboratory value ≤7.0% during our study period. We excluded 422 patients for having less than 365 days of continuous health insurance coverage, 904 patients in palliative care, 20 patients who died on the Index Date, and 1835 patients with missing physician information on the Index A1c test, leaving 1 33 773 patients in our study cohort. Potential overtreatment of T2DM was identified in 38 074 (28.5%) of patients in the study cohort (figure 1). The study cohort had a mean age of 79.6 years, was 47% female and had a median A1C of 6.4 (IQR 6.0%–6.8%). Overtreated patients had a mean age of 79.4 years, were 43% female, and had a median A1C of 6.6% (IQR 6.4%–6.9%). Baseline characteristics for the study cohort are shown in table 1. The training dataset contained 100.635 subjects, among whom 26 726 (26.6%) were overtreated, while the test dataset had 33 138 patients, among whom 11 348 (34.2%) were overtreated.

Model performance

Measures of models’ discriminative ability on left-out test data are shown in table 2. Overall, the models developed with combined expert-selected and data-driven variables achieved the best discriminative measures. Of these models, logistic regression achieved the highest performance in terms of AUC (0.879, 95% CI 0.875 to 0.883). GBM, however, achieved the highest sensitivity of 0.809 (95% CI 0.801 to 0.816) and NPV of 0.885 (95% CI 0.881 to 0.889). This indicates that GBM is the best model for recognising the patients who are overtreated while also having the highest probability of predicting the negative results correctly. It is worth noting that the logistic regression model was a close second (sensitivity of 0.803 and NPV of 0.885). The random forest model had the highest specificity and PPV of 0.807 (95% CI 0.802 to 0.813) and 0.672 (95% CI 0.663 to 0.681), respectively.

The logistic regression prediction model developed with expert-selected variables only had a comparably high performance in all measures. For clinical applicability, a simple model with a considerably smaller number of variables (31) would be considerably easier to implement—the fewer variables the model requires, the more user-friendly the model becomes by being quicker to gather the data and to calculate.

As shown in figure 2, all models, except for naive Bayes, appear to have a similar discriminative performance in terms of AUC. The possible reason for naive Bayes performing worse is that our dataset has a relatively large number of variables, and in real-world clinical data, most health conditions are dependent on one another. Naive Bayes assumes that all predictors are conditionally independent given the outcome. In contrast, the other models can handle correlated predictors reasonably well, and random forest and GBM explicitly exploit interactions and correlations.

Predictive features

For the GBM model, which had the best performance, the most important variables included the use of blood glucose test strips (relative influence=37.6), duration of diabetes (relative influence=24.6) and number of medications in the prior 180 days (a measure of polypharmacy) (relative influence=15.1). Figure 4 shows the top 10 variables for each model and their respective importance ranking per model. Overall, the most common top 10 variables for models included the use of blood glucose test strips (used by 5 models), chronic hypoglycaemia (5 models), endocrinology visit 2 years before index date (5 models). Notably, the most important variables in the expert-selected logistic regression model are commonly present as top variables in most other models, but often not in those with data-driven variable selection.

Discussion

Using a large administrative health claims database, we identified the prevalence of overtreatment among older adults with T2DM and used ML algorithms to identify patient and physician characteristics associated with overtreatment. We found using a GBM algorithm incorporating a combination of data-driven and clinical expert-selected features had the best performance in terms of sensitivity and NPV. The ML models efficiently processed a large volume of variables; however, a simpler approach using logistic regression and expert-selected variables had comparably high results. This indicates that using more complex and computationally expensive models does not translate into meaningful improvements in predictive performance for overtreatment. The simpler and more transparent model, like logistic regression, may be preferable in a clinical setting where interpretability, ease of implementation and trust in model outputs are critical for decision-making. Expert-derived features that aggregated events over time—such as medication counts in the prior 180 days and the number of A1C tests in the prior 2 years—enhanced the model’s performance. This study is novel because it represents, to our knowledge, the first model specifically developed to predict overtreatment in T2DM, limiting direct comparison with other modelling approaches.

The top predictors of overtreatment identified in our study offer valuable insights into clinical practice patterns and potential areas for intervention. The most predictive feature—use of blood glucose test strips—likely reflects a heightened intensity of glycaemic monitoring, which is not unexpected as patients receiving insulin or sulfonylureas often require more frequent glucose monitoring. This finding, along with the observed association between overtreatment and higher frequency of A1c testing, may reflect long-standing treatment and monitoring routines that persist over time, particularly in patients with longer duration of diabetes. Our finding that increased medication use, an indicator of polypharmacy, predicts overtreatment may reflect greater clinical complexity and heightened risk of adverse events in this vulnerable population. The association between overtreatment and chronic hypoglycaemia, retinopathy and endocrine specialist involvement underscores the challenge of balancing disease management with patient safety in complex cases.2 38 Our results are consistent with prior studies that have linked more intensive testing with increased risk of over-diagnosis and over-treatment, suggesting that frequent monitoring may inadvertently reinforce continuation of intensive therapy.39 40 These findings highlight the need for clinical decision support tools that incorporate individualised risk assessments to support safe and appropriate pharmacotherapy deintensification.

The prevalence of overtreatment in our study was estimated to be 30%. The prevalence of potential glycaemic overtreatment in other studies has varied widely, from 16% to 74% depending on the definitions used in each study.41 42 Our high prevalence findings of overtreatment are consistent with other research that has shown the existence of barriers to deintensification in the context of overtreatment, such as a lack of knowledge around the benefits of deprescribing from the patient’s perspective, and poor communication and fragmentation of care from the perspective of healthcare professionals.43 44

Our findings identified a significant gap between evidence-based recommendations and real-world diabetes management in older adults. The high prevalence of overtreated patients highlights a disconnect between clinical guidelines and real-world prescribing practices. This reinforces the need for more rigorous implementation of deintensification initiatives in clinical care, particularly among older adults where the risks of hypoglycaemia often outweigh the benefits of tight glycaemic control. The superior performance of models that combine expert clinical insight with data-driven predictor selection suggests that integrating machine learning into clinical decision support systems could enhance identification of older adults who are overtreated. These results support leveraging predictive analytics to identify candidates for deintensification in routine clinical settings. Our findings are important because the predictors can be used to identify individual patients who might benefit from deintensification, or inform wider quality improvement initiatives to improve diabetes management in older adults.

Limitations

Our study used the British Columbia provincial health claims database, incorporating linking patients records on medications dispensed, medical diagnosis, hospital admissions and laboratory tests. Family history, genetic data, diet and lifestyle are important factors that are not documented in the datasets, but could be important factors in T2DM treatment decisions. The study was potentially affected by the loss of follow-up of patients, unrecorded disease status or unrecorded medication use due to its nature as a retrospective data analysis. Additionally, our outcome definition was broad and may yield higher prevalence estimates than definitions restricted to insulin or sulfonylurea use. Another limitation is the use of a proxy definition of overtreatment, which may not necessarily be associated with overtreatment negative outcomes.

In accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis, updated to include Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD-AI) guidance, model performance was assessed using internal validation with a large, independent hold-out test set, providing a reliable estimate of in-sample discrimination. However, the models were not externally validated in a separate healthcare system or jurisdiction. As a result, transportability of model performance to settings with different populations, prescribing practices or data structures remains uncertain. External validation and assessment of calibration and clinical utility will be required before clinical implementation. Finally, model performance may be influenced by feature engineering choices and hyperparameter tuning, and further optimisation could improve predictive accuracy.

Conclusions

Overtreatment of T2DM is highly prevalent among older adults in BC. We developed machine learning models to identify determinants of overtreatment. Machine learning algorithms incorporating a combination of data-driven features plus clinical expert selected features had superior performance over data-driven predictor models without clinical expert selection and over expert-only defined models. Top determinants of overtreatment included recent use of blood glucose test strips, polypharmacy, diabetes severity and diabetes-related complications. These findings have important implications for medication management optimisation policies and quality improvement initiatives, such as tailoring interventions to improve the implementation of glucose lowering therapy deintensification in older adults, although external validation is required before clinical implementation.
