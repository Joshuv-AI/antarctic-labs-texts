Feasibility & clinical utility of Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) and interventions in older adults with prostate cancer on Androgen Deprivation Therapy (ADT); a retrospective service evaluation
Authors: K Balachandran, S Lightbody, M Brennan, R Evans, L Fleure, D Harari, C Welch, D Josephs, D Enting, T Kalsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAndrogen Deprivation Therapy (ADT) is often a component of treatment for localised and advanced prostate cancer but carries multiple potential side-effects. We evaluated the feasibility of a clinical pathway delivering comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) for older men receiving ADT for prostate cancer, and its utility in targeting patients with multiple other long-term conditions.MethodsThis retrospective service evaluation included consecutive new patients aged 65 + with prostate cancer of any stage, commencing ADT in a dedicated uro-oncology clinic in a 6-month period in 2021. The clinical referral pathway to a specialist geriatric oncology clinic for CGA was available for any of these patients who the nurses delivering the ADT initiation clinic considered would benefit. Patient characteristics were compared and for those referred to the pathway, outcome measures included feasibility, effectiveness in targeting medically complex patients, and clinical utility including ADT-specific assessments and other CGA interventions.ResultsOverall, 84 patients were seen in the ADT clinic during the evaluation period with a mean age of 73.4 years (SD 6.6). 45% were referred for CGA and of these, 100% received CGA. There were a greater number of patients with 4 + long-term conditions in the group referred for CGA (76.3%) vs. those who were not (34.8%). ADT toxicity screening (non-radiological) was performed in over 80% of the 38 patients referred for CGA, and DEXA scanning was completed in 55.3%. For the 38 patients who received CGA, 188 interventions unrelated to ADT were also implemented. Interventions were varied and tailored to a range of long-term conditions, which may not have been identified outside CGA.ConclusionsThis service evaluation confirms the feasibility and utility of CGA performed in a specialist geriatric oncology clinic for older men commencing ADT for prostate cancer. To fully explore the impact of CGA on clinical outcomes (cancer-related and otherwise) for older men starting ADT, prospective studies are needed.Keywords: Comprehensive geriatric assessment, Prostate cancer, Androgen deprivation therapy

Background

Prostate Cancer is the most common cancer in males in the UK and the fourth most common cancer worldwide [1, 2]. In the UK, 1 in 8 men will get prostate cancer in their lifetime, with over 52,000 new diagnoses and over 12,000 deaths annually [1, 3]. Treatment depends on the stage of prostate cancer which can be classified as localised (low, intermediate or high-risk), locally advanced or metastatic. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) can be a component of treatment in many of these stages either:

as palliative treatment for men with either de novo or relapsed advanced prostate cancer (as monotherapy or in combination with a novel anti-androgen +/- cytotoxic chemotherapy).

as palliative treatment for men with either de novo or relapsed advanced prostate cancer (as monotherapy or in combination with a novel anti-androgen +/- cytotoxic chemotherapy).

in combination with radiotherapeutic treatment for patients with intermediate or high risk localised prostate cancer (as part of a radical treatment strategy).

in combination with radiotherapeutic treatment for patients with intermediate or high risk localised prostate cancer (as part of a radical treatment strategy).

or for some patients with localised prostate cancer who are not able or willing to undergo radical treatment.

or for some patients with localised prostate cancer who are not able or willing to undergo radical treatment.

ADT can be well-tolerated but has myriad potential toxicities including fatigue, hot flushes, reduced bone density and associated pathological fractures, sarcopenia, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, weight gain, cardiovascular disease, change in mood, cognitive impairment, erectile dysfunction, and loss of libido [4, 5]. ADT is sometimes given intermittently to reduce side effects; this was shown to have no difference in overall survival compared to continuous ADT in patients with relapsing disease after radical radiotherapy (RT) [6].

Like most malignancies, prostate cancer is a disease of ageing, with over 60% of new diagnoses being made in 65 + year-olds [7]. As life expectancy continues to increase, the number of prostate cancer diagnoses will grow further [8]. Inequalities in healthcare access have been described, with patients 80 + years nearly 6 times more likely to be diagnosed with metastatic disease than patients < 60 years (OR = 5.75, 95%CI 5.44 to 6.09) [9]. The development of the field of Geriatric Oncology has highlighted the unique needs of older patients with cancer, which, until relatively recently, have often gone unrecognised and unmet. Cancer treatment decision-making requires careful consideration of other long-term conditions, medications, baseline function, and the social situation of the individual patient to create a tailored management plan. This can be achieved through comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA); a multicomponent, clinical process that facilitates first the identification of medical, functional and social needs, and then development of a management plan to address these. CGA enables identification of unknown vulnerabilities as well as provide opportunity to mitigate against anticipated toxicities. Although initially validated in a number of non-cancer settings [10–13], evidence is accumulating to support the use of CGA in improving chemotherapy tolerance, outcomes, and quality of life in older patients with cancer [14–18]. However, the application of CGA in the context of older men with prostate cancer receiving ADT has received little focus thus far.

At our central London hospital, around 700 patients are started on ADT annually in the urology and oncology departments combined. Any of these patients over the age of 65 years (or 55 years with other significant complex long-term conditions e.g. diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) are eligible for referral to a geriatric-oncology liaison team (GOLD - comprising a geriatrician, clinical nurse specialist, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, and other allied health professionals as required) who will undertake CGA. GOLD was initially set up to optimise older people for intensive non-surgical cancer treatments including chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy. However, as the service embedded, it became clear that older men on ADT without systemic therapy, may also benefit from a CGA approach.

Aims

We evaluate feasibility and clinical utility in targeting patients with multiple other long-term conditions (i.e. the medically complex) who are likely to benefit from CGA, through a clinical pathway delivering CGA with interventions for older men receiving ADT for prostate cancer.

Methods

This retrospective service evaluation reviewed outcomes related to the implementation of the service pathway for men receiving ADT. The evaluation was undertaken at a large tertiary referral centre in London, UK, which provides cancer care to local patients and those living across south-east England. Patients were commenced on ADT in a nurse-led uro-oncology clinic dedicated to the delivery of hormone therapy alone (i.e. without concurrent systemic therapy). All patients attending the nurse-led ADT initiation clinic had a letter sent to the general practitioner giving them information on the ongoing prescription of ADT and its specific health effects with the expectation that primary care managed these health effects.

The referral pathway to GOLD was made available for men age 65+ (or 55 + with multiple long-term conditions, though these younger patients were not included in this study) with prostate cancer (any stage). The nurses delivering the ADT clinic could refer any man they clinically judged would benefit from CGA evaluation/intervention, referrals were not limited or guided by a GA screening tool. Those referred for CGA were reviewed by the GOLD team based in a dedicated outpatient area for older adults. The GOLD service is a geriatrician-led multi-disciplinary Geriatric Oncology service. Previous work focussing on the positive impact of GOLD-delivered CGA on chemotherapy tolerance has been previously described by Kalsi et al. in 2015 [14]. Full CGA was delivered bespoke to the stressor, which in this evaluation was ADT. CGA therefore additionally included a specific risk assessment of ADT to the patients’ overall health with targeted interventions. The period evaluated was 6th April − 28th September 2021.

Consecutive new patients age 65 + attending the nurse-led ADT initiation clinic were identified using hospital data analytics by clinic code. Patient level data was extracted from the hospital electronic patient record systems (both general and oncology specific) as well as from GP records (using electronic shared care records). Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described with descriptive statistics. χ2 (or Fisher’s Exact test when the expected count was < 5) was used to compare groups or unpaired T-test for comparison of means using SPSS version 26.

Results

In total, 84 patients were seen in the ADT initiation clinic during the review period, with a mean age of 73.4 years +/- 6.6. Of these 84 patients, 45.2% (38/84) were referred to GOLD for CGA (Fig. 1). There was no statistical difference in age, ethnicity, performance status, Gleason score, presenting PSA or plan for radiotherapy between either the group who were referred for CGA or the group who were not, suggesting that the groups were well matched in these characteristics. There was no statistical difference between the documented ethnicities of the two groups, although 16/46 who did not have CGA had no ethnicity recorded, compared to 7/38 of those who had CGA.

Other CGA interventions

In addition to screening for ADT-related health issues, a total of 188 other interventions were conducted for the 38 patients referred for CGA. Further to this, 100% (38/38) of patients were given a telephone follow-up consultation with a geriatric-oncology nurse at a set time interval decided at the initial CGA appointment (including the patient who received remote CGA), providing opportunity to assess outstanding areas of need and ensure interventions had been completed as planned.

Interventions spanned a range of domains with multiple possible interventions within each one. For example, 31.6% (12/38) who received CGA had interventions for bladder symptoms or sexual dysfunction, which may have included pelvic floor exercises, education on fluid management, providing a card for access to public toilets, referral to community incontinence services, referral to erectile dysfunction clinic and instigation of medication for lower urinary tract symptoms, as well as several others. Interventions were often multi-disciplinary, for example falls management (received by 52.6% (20/38)) may have involved physiotherapy for strength and balance and home and finances interventions (provided for 13.2% (5/38)) may have included occupational therapy referral for assessment of equipment and needs. Discussion

Here we have reviewed the role of CGA in older patients with prostate cancer treated with ADT at a London tertiary referral oncology centre. Although CGA has been evaluated in many non-oncology settings and, increasingly, in patients receiving chemotherapy, few studies have examined its role in patients receiving ADT.

The geriatric oncology liaison department were referred just under half of the patients 65 + years who were commenced on ADT during the six-month evaluation period (38/84), and had capacity to assess 100% of them, confirming the feasibility of this approach at the study site. The pathway was also effective in identifying and referring patients with the most long-term conditions who were more likely to derive benefit from CGA. CGA also appears to have clinical utility in this setting, with high completion rates of screening for health factors that may be affected by ADT in those referred to Geriatric Oncology. In addition, a further 188 interventions were implemented for the 38 patients who received CGA. These interventions were varied and targeted at investigating or managing a wide range of long-term conditions/wider issues, which may not have been identified outside CGA.

To our knowledge, this evaluation is the first to examine the feasibility and clinical utility of CGA in patients undergoing ADT for prostate cancer. In addition, we evaluated the effectiveness of the pathway in selecting patients with greater numbers of long-term conditions who are likely to benefit from CGA. The CGA group contained significantly more patients with ≥4 other long-term conditions, suggesting the patients referred to Geriatric Oncology were arguably the more medically complex and were appropriately identified and referred. Obtaining data on ‘reason for referral’ would provide clarity and insight into exactly how patients were considered and selected by the nurse-led uro-oncology team as needing CGA, and whether this difference is solely due to pre-existing disease or whether improved detection through CGA is contributory. It would also be valuable to assess the impact of more systematic frailty screening (with frailty screening tools such as the Rockwood Chronic Frailty Scale or G8 [19, 20]) on referrals. Exploring in more depth the value added by Geriatric Oncology, through studying how CGA interventions change outcomes, may also aid in identifying which patients are most likely to benefit from the referral pathway.

This evaluation was designed to provide proof of concept and act as a descriptive cohort of the wider applications of a CGA approach in cancer care. Studies to evaluate feasibility outside this setting as well as effectiveness on clinical outcomes are warranted. This evaluation was not powered to show differences in characteristics or outcomes between cohorts. Strengths of the evaluation include the broad demographic of the patients studied (from both city and suburban backgrounds) and the inclusion of a comparison group who did not receive CGA. The comparison group were acknowledged as likely different but served as a comparator to whether referral processes were effective in identifying the more vulnerable. Indeed, there were differences in the key long-term conditions being more prevalent in those referred to GOLD, whilst other baseline demographics and cancer ‘characteristics’ were similar. Ethnicity documentation rates were low for both groups and there was no statistical difference between them. Improving reporting rates is important with regards equitable access to clinical care and to evaluate whether the population seen in GOLD are reflective of the overall patient cohort.

A limitation of the study was that data collectors and analysers were unblinded and, along with the authors, work in the service evaluated which may add potential bias to the results. Longer term outcome data was also not collected, which may have provided additional insights into the impact of CGA in this cohort of patients. Another consideration is that data on a frailty score (such as the Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale, CFS) as a baseline characteristic was not collected; arguably this has little impact on the results as this service evaluation aimed to assess feasibility and utility. However, assessing the numbers of patients commencing ADT who have had CFS screening, and whether this added value to the identification of those in need of CGA would be valuable. Recent guidance from the Joint Collegiate Council for Oncology recommends frailty assessment as a key performance indicator in older people with cancer [21].

This evaluation was conducted in a well-established service in a tertiary hospital with a specialist and independent older persons’ outpatient area. Therefore, caution should be applied in assuming transferability of the results to other settings. Specific risk assessments for medical factors impacted upon by ADT were undertaken as part of CGA, as key issues of relevance to this patient population, which closely align to domains frequently addressed within CGA. This systematic ADT risk assessment alone without CGA may also be of value to longer term outcomes and could be considered as an intervention to embed within prostate cancer clinics themselves. Future studies are warranted to evaluate whether ADT health effect assessments alone are implementable within oncology ADT services without CGA. However, this evaluation also identified benefit from CGA beyond the ADT risk assessment in a variety of areas including falls, wider health as well as functional support needs amongst others. Studies in the chemotherapy setting have also delivered CGA with different service models (Geriatrician vs. management algorithms etc.) and have proven CGA is effective in different models of service delivery. Future studies to compare different models of delivery would be helpful to identify models that may be most cost effective as well as clinically useful and impact on patient outcomes. The potential toxicities of ADT are well documented; patients given ADT have increased risks of developing diabetes mellitus (adjusted HR = 1.44, p < 0.001) [22], ischaemic heart disease (OR = 2.07, p < 0.01) [23, 24], and stroke (HR = 3.32, CI 95% 1.14–9.67, p = 0.028) [25–27]. Assessment of cardiovascular risk factors before ADT is started enables primary and secondary prevention of cardiac disease, mitigating ADT-associated morbidity and mortality [28, 29]. Similarly, bone mass decreases with age, and the spectrum of osteopenia, osteoporosis and increased fracture risk are also established ADT toxicities, making older men with prostate cancer particularly vulnerable to them [30, 31]. GOLD needed to arrange bone health interventions in almost one third of patients included, suggesting that the holistic approach of CGA to be a helpful safety net for this area of care.

Our data clearly shows that the majority of patients on ADT who received CGA were screened for ADT-related toxicities (with blood pressure, ECG, HbA1c, lipid, Vitamin D, calcium, and DEXA testing) (Table 4). For patients who are not able to access CGA (including those treated in centres without geriatric oncology departments or who do not meet specific referral criteria), alternative systems need to be in place to ensure these men are adequately screened and treated for the longer-term effects of ADT which may impact on non-cancer related morbidity and mortality. Indeed, a specialist CGA may not be required for management of these common medical issues. A ‘passport’ given to patients at the outset of ADT with a guide to which tests are required at which timepoints would assist all healthcare professionals involved in the care of these men in knowing what screening is required and would also empower patients to be involved in their own care. This ‘passport’ is under development and will be piloted in our centre in due course. Prospective studies are needed to both evaluate this and fully elucidate the impact of CGA as well as structured risk assessments on clinical outcomes for older men on ADT, both cancer-related and otherwise.

Conclusions

ADT has wide-ranging potential toxicities but despite CGA being validated in other settings, its role has not been explored in this patient cohort. This retrospective service evaluation confirms the feasibility of performing CGA through a dedicated clinical pathway for older men commencing ADT for prostate cancer. It also confirms the clinical utility of this approach in targeting patients with more long-term conditions, addressing ADT-specific health risks and facilitating broader, CGA-driven interventions. Prospective studies are needed to assess the impact of both CGA (in a variety of settings, including geriatrician-led and management algorithm-directed) and more focused ADT-specific monitoring on cancer-related and other clinical outcomes for this population.
