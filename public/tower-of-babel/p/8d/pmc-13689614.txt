Garsorasib (D-1553) in Combination With Pembrolizumab and Chemotherapy for First-Line Metastatic NSCLC With KRAS G12C Mutation: Results of a Phase 1b Study
Authors: Dong Yan, Chaoying Liu, Xingya Li, Melissa Moore, Ruihua Xu, Rachel E Sanborn, Shirish M Gadgeel, Michael Millward, Zhe Shi, Yaolin Wang, Kathryn Stazzone, Michael J Chisamore, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGarsorasib (D-1553) is an orally administered selective KRAS G12C inhibitor. We report results from group 1 of the phase 1b D1553-101 study assessing garsorasib plus pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced or metastatic NSCLC.MethodsEligible participants had locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC with KRAS G12C mutation and were candidates for pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy. Participants received garsorasib 600 mg once daily or twice daily combined with pembrolizumab 200 mg once every 3 weeks plus 4 to 6 cycles of cisplatin or carboplatin and pemetrexed (nonsquamous histology) or carboplatin and paclitaxel (squamous histology). The primary end points were safety and the incidence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs).ResultsAs of February 24, 2024, 17 participants received garsorasib 600 mg once daily (n = 10) or twice daily (n = 7) combined with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy. Overall, 94% of participants experienced grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events (AEs), and 88% had a dose reduction or interruption due to treatment-related AEs. One participant in the twice-daily group experienced a DLT of grade 2 diarrhea; no other DLTs occurred. Among all participants, the objective response rate was 59%, and the median progression-free survival was 7.4 months.ConclusionIn participants with KRAS G12C–mutated locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC, despite an encouraging signal of efficacy, the incidence of treatment-related AEs with garsorasib plus pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy was high, and participants frequently had dose modifications or interruptions due to treatment-related AEs. Based on these findings, the study did not proceed to phase 2.ClinicalTrials.gov identifierNCT04585035.Keywords: Garsorasib, KRAS G12C mutation, Immunotherapy, Phase 1b, Non–small cell lung cancer

Introduction

KRAS is one of the most frequently mutated oncogenes in human cancers.1,2 Several different KRAS mutations have been identified, with the KRAS G12C mutation being the most prevalent in patients with NSCLC, occurring in 9% to 14% of all cases. The mutation has been associated with poor prognosis in patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC.1,3, 4, 5 Two selective, irreversible, small-molecule KRAS G12C inhibitors (adagrasib and sotorasib) have demonstrated antitumor activity in patients with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC that progressed on or after receiving platinum-based chemotherapy and/or anti–programmed cell death protein 1 (anti–PD-1) or anti–programmed death-ligand 1 (anti–PD-L1) therapy6,7 and have been approved in this setting in several countries.8, 9, 10, 11

Garsorasib (D-1553) is an orally administered, potent, selective, irreversible covalent inhibitor of KRAS G12C developed for the treatment of patients with KRAS G12C–mutated solid tumors.12 In a phase 1 dose-escalation and dose-expansion study, garsorasib demonstrated a manageable safety profile and antitumor activity in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC refractory to standard therapy or intolerant to it.13 The safety and efficacy of garsorasib in this population were further confirmed in a pivotal open-label, single-arm, phase 2 study14 that led to the regulatory approval of garsorasib in China for the treatment of adult patients with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC who had received at least one line of prior systemic therapy.

Pembrolizumab (an anti–PD-1 antibody) plus platinum-based chemotherapy is a standard-of-care first-line therapy for patients with previously untreated advanced or metastatic NSCLC15 based on results from randomized trials revealing that such combinations improve overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared with chemotherapy alone.16, 17, 18 Notably, pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy is recommended as a first-line treatment option for advanced NSCLC with KRAS mutations because the combination regimen improves OS and PFS outcomes irrespective of KRAS mutation status.15,19 Targeted therapy is a preferred treatment option for advanced NSCLC with actionable genomic alterations, such as EGFR and ALK. At present, KRAS G12C inhibitors have been primarily used in the second- or later-line settings for advanced NSCLC with KRAS mutations, and their long-term efficacy is often limited by intrinsic and acquired resistance.15,20 To delay drug resistance and improve clinical outcomes of KRAS G12C inhibitors, combination therapeutic approaches with standard therapies are being explored. Preclinical evidence suggests that KRAS G12C inhibitors may induce remodeling of the KRAS-driven immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and increase cytotoxic T-cell tumor infiltration, thereby augmenting antitumor activity when combined with a PD-(L)1 inhibitor.21,22 In addition, encouraging antitumor activity was found in early-phase clinical trials of a KRAS G12C inhibitor combined with chemotherapy in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC.23,24

In addition to combining KRAS G12C inhibitors with standard therapies for KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC, other combination therapeutic approaches targeting receptor tyrosine kinases (e.g., EGFR, HER2, or MET inhibitors), the upstream receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathway (e.g., SHP2 inhibitors), the downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway (e.g., MEK inhibitors), and parallel oncogenic pathways (e.g., PI3K, AKT, or mTOR inhibitors) are also ongoing to overcome drug resistance, thereby improving treatment outcomes.20

The D1553-101 study is a phase 1/2, open-label, multicohort study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04585035) evaluating the safety, pharmacokinetics, and antitumor activity of garsorasib as a single agent or in combination with immunotherapy and chemotherapy or targeted therapy in participants with advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring a KRAS G12C mutation. The phase 1a part of the study evaluated dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) with ascending doses of garsorasib (150–1200 mg once daily and 400–800 mg twice daily) in participants with advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring a KRAS G12C mutation.25 No DLTs were observed, and the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was not reached; based on safety, pharmacokinetic, and efficacy data, garsorasib 600 mg twice daily was chosen for further investigation.25 Here, we report findings from group 1 of the phase 1b portion of the study that evaluated garsorasib combined with pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

Participants were eligible to participate in group 1 of phase 1b if they were 18 years of age or older and had histologically or cytologically confirmed locally advanced unresectable or metastatic NSCLC with no EGFR or ALK aberrations, had a documented KRAS G12C mutation in a tumor or blood sample, and were candidates for first-line treatment with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy. KRAS G12C mutation status was tested locally. Additional inclusion criteria were measurable or evaluable disease per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1; an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1; and adequate hematologic, hepatic, renal, and coagulation function. Key exclusion criteria included prior therapy with any KRAS G12C inhibitor; prior anticancer systemic therapy for locally advanced or metastatic disease; prior therapy with an anti–PD-1, anti–PD-L1, or anti–PD-L2 antibody or any agent directed to another stimulatory or co-inhibitory T-cell receptor that was discontinued because of grade 3 or higher immune-related adverse events (AEs); anticancer therapy up to 14 days or 3 half-lives before the first dose of the study drug; chronic systemic steroids or immunosuppressive therapy up to 7 days before study treatment; an active autoimmune disease requiring systemic treatment within up to 2 years; unstable or progressive central nervous system metastases and/or carcinomatous meningitis; clinically significant cardiovascular disease; current pneumonitis/interstitial lung disease or a history of noninfectious pneumonitis requiring systemic steroids; impaired gastrointestinal function; or any other illness that might confound study results. Participants with a history of brain metastases were eligible if they were clinically stable.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice and was approved by the appropriate institutional review boards and regulatory agencies. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolling.

Study Design

Participants enrolled in phase 1b group 1 received oral garsorasib 600 mg once daily or twice daily on an empty stomach plus intravenous pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks and cisplatin 75 mg/m2 or carboplatin area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) 5 mg/m2/min plus pemetrexed 500 mg/m2 (nonsquamous histology) or carboplatin AUC 6 mg/m2/min plus paclitaxel 200 mg/m2 (squamous histology) on day 1 of every 21-day cycle for 4 to 6 cycles. After completion of platinum-based chemotherapy, pembrolizumab treatment was to continue for up to 35 cycles; pembrolizumab plus pemetrexed maintenance treatment was also allowed for nonsquamous histology. Study treatment continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, participant withdrawal, or completion of planned dosing.

In the event of treatment-related toxicities, garsorasib dosing could be interrupted or modified in accordance with the predefined dose reduction guidelines contained in the study protocol. In the event of treatment-related toxicities, pembrolizumab dosing could also be interrupted or discontinued, with or without supportive care, in accordance with the predefined dose modification and toxicity guidelines contained in the study protocol. Once treatment-related toxicities, including grade 3 or 4 events, had improved, part or all of the combination regimen could be resumed according to the investigator’s judgment.

End Points

The primary end points were the type, incidence, severity, attribution, and timing of AEs and the incidence of DLTs. Secondary end points included pharmacokinetic parameters of garsorasib, objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), and duration of response (DOR) per RECIST version 1.1 by investigator assessment. ORR was defined as the proportion of participants with a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR). DCR was defined as the proportion of participants with a best overall response of CR, PR, or stable disease. PFS was defined as the time from the first administration of study drug to documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. DOR was defined as the time from documentation of objective response (CR or PR) to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first.

Assessments

AEs were assessed throughout the study period and for 30 days after the last dose of the study treatment and were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Participants were monitored for DLTs during cycle 1 (21 d). DLTs were defined as grade 3 or higher hematologic or nonhematologic toxicity (with certain exceptions) and any treatment-related toxicity that caused study drug discontinuation or prolonged treatment interruption (details are provided in Supplementary Table 1).

Blood samples for evaluation of garsorasib pharmacokinetics were collected pre-dose on days 1, 2, and 14 of cycle 1 and day 1 of cycle 3 and were collected at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 10 hours post-dose on days 1 and 14 of cycle 1 and on day 1 of cycle 3. Plasma concentrations of garsorasib were determined using a validated liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry method, and pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using noncompartmental analysis with WinNonlin version 8.4 (Certara USA, Inc., Radnor, NJ).

Radiographic tumor assessment was performed by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at screening and every 2 cycles for the first 8 cycles and then every 3 cycles until disease progression, the start of new anticancer treatment, participant withdrawal, death, or the end of the study. Confirmed tumor responses were assessed per RECIST version 1.1 by the investigators.

Statistical Analysis

The planned sample size for phase 1b group 1 was approximately 13 participants, with the assumption that garsorasib 600 mg once-daily and twice-daily groups would be evaluated with up to 10 participants allocated to the higher dose level.

Safety and efficacy analyses were conducted for all participants who received at least one dose of the study drug. Pharmacokinetic analyses were conducted for all participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one plasma concentration value. All analyses were descriptive. The ORR, DCR, and their 95% CIs based on the Clopper-Pearson method were calculated. The DOR and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Results

Participants and Treatment

Between February 7, 2022, and September 21, 2023, 17 participants at eight global sites were enrolled in group 1 and received garsorasib 600 mg once daily (n = 10) or twice daily (n = 7) in combination with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy. As of the data cutoff (February 29, 2024), seven participants in the 600 mg once-daily group and six in the 600 mg twice-daily group had discontinued the treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1). The median time from the start of treatment to data cutoff was 7.5 (range, 0.3–13.6) months. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. All participants had adenocarcinoma histology, and 88% had stage IV disease. Furthermore, 24% of the participants had undergone prior surgery (e.g., lung lobectomy or segmentectomy) and 24% had received prior radiation therapy (e.g., bone palliative radiation) for local disease and palliative or symptomatic treatment; 12% had received prior systemic anticancer therapy (anti–PD-[L]1 therapy for nonmetastatic disease and adjuvant chemotherapy for nonmetastatic disease, n = 1 each).

Safety

One participant in the twice-daily dosing group experienced a DLT of grade 2 diarrhea that began on day 17; the event improved to grade 1 on day 21 and resolved on day 36. No other DLTs occurred, and the MTD was not reached.

All participants experienced at least one treatment-related AE. Grade 3 or 4 treatment-related AEs occurred in 16 (94%) of 17 participants; none were grade 5. The most frequently reported treatment-related AEs in the garsorasib 600 mg once-daily and twice-daily groups were increased alanine aminotransferase (100% and 57%, respectively), increased aspartate aminotransferase (100% and 57%), anemia (100% and 43%), vomiting (70% and 57%), diarrhea (70% and 43%), nausea (40% and 86%), decreased neutrophil count (90% and 14%), decreased platelet count (70% and 43%), and decreased white blood cell count (60% and 43%). Furthermore, 15 participants (88%) had dose reduction or interruption of garsorasib or pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy due to treatment-related AEs. Three participants (18%) discontinued study treatment early: one participant discontinued all study treatment because of grade 3 Clostridium difficile colitis; one participant discontinued pembrolizumab only because of grade 3 diarrhea; and one participant discontinued carboplatin only because of grade 3 increased alanine aminotransferase, with the other treatments ongoing (Table 2).

Pharmacokinetics

Following once- or twice-daily administrations of garsorasib 600 mg on day 1 of cycle 1, the maximum plasma concentration of garsorasib (Cmax) was reached at a median time (tmax) of 2.1 hours postdose. The geometric mean Cmax and AUC from time 0 to the last observation (AUC0–t) of garsorasib were 8040 ng/mL and 35,445 h·ng/mL, respectively, in the 600 mg once-daily group and 10,739 ng/mL and 44,342 h·ng/mL, respectively, in the 600 mg twice-daily group. In both dosing groups, the mean half-life (t1/2) of garsorasib on days 1 and 14 of cycle 1 was similar to that observed on day 1 of cycle 3 and ranged from 2.2 to 3.4 hours.

Antitumor Activity

Of the 17 participants enrolled in phase 1b group 1, none had a CR and 10 had a PR, with an ORR of 59% (95% CI: 33%–82%); of the remaining participants, four had stable disease, two had progressive disease, and one was not evaluable for response. In the garsorasib 600 mg once-daily group, seven of 10 participants had an objective response (ORR: 70% [95% CI: 35%–93%]). In the garsorasib 600 mg twice-daily group, three of seven participants had an objective response (ORR: 43% [95% CI: 10%–82%]). Median DOR (95% CI) was not reached (NR; 2.7 mo–NR) among all responders, NR (2.7 mo–NR) among those in the 600 mg once-daily group, and 6.2 months (2.7 mo–NR) among those in the 600 mg twice-daily group (Table 3). The DCR was 82% in all participants, 90% in the once-daily group, and 71% in the twice-daily group (Table 3). Among the 15 participants with at least one postbaseline tumor assessment, 87% had reductions from baseline in target lesion size (Fig. 1A). Time on study treatment and treatment response of individual participants are found in Figure 1B. Median PFS (95% CI) was 7.4 months (4.0 mo–NR) in all participants (Fig. 1C), NR (1.4 mo–NR) in the once-daily group, and 4.0 months (1.6 mo–NR) in the twice-daily group.

Discussion

The phase 1b combination and dose-escalation group 1 of the D1553-101 study evaluated the safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of garsorasib 600 mg once daily or twice daily combined with pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated advanced or metastatic NSCLC. Although only one DLT was observed (in the garsorasib 600 mg twice-daily group) and no unexpected AEs were observed based on the known safety profiles of garsorasib, pembrolizumab, and chemotherapy,13,16,17,26 the overall toxicity of the combination was greater than expected. In this study, 94% of the participants experienced grade 3 or 4 treatment-related AEs, and 88% had a dose reduction or interruption in garsorasib treatment or pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy due to treatment-related AEs. The incidence of grade 3 or higher AEs in this study was greater than that previously reported with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy (approximately 70%) in randomized studies in participants with previously untreated metastatic NSCLC.16,17 Previous clinical studies of KRAS G12C inhibitors plus immunotherapy have revealed that certain combinations may increase toxicity, especially hepatotoxicity; however, available data are limited, and the specific mechanism of increased toxicity is yet to be elucidated.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Overall, although the incidence of certain treatment-related AEs (e.g., increased alanine aminotransferase, increased aspartate aminotransferase, anemia) appeared to be higher with garsorasib 600 mg once-daily dosing, the incidence of treatment-related AEs of any grade and of grade 3 or 4 was similar in the once-daily and twice-daily groups. In addition, the small sample size in the study limits the ability to evaluate the influence of dose frequency on the safety profile of garsorasib.

Pharmacokinetic profiles of garsorasib were similar when administered as a single agent or combined with pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy. In the phase 1a part of the study, the median tmax was 1 to 4 hours with garsorasib single-agent administration.25 In addition, the Cmax and AUC of garsorasib increased in a dose-dependent manner with dose cohorts of 150 to 600 mg once daily, but the changes did not increase in a dose-proportional manner with dose cohorts of 600 to 1200 mg once daily and 600 to 800 mg twice daily.25 In phase 1b group 1, the Cmax and AUC0–t of garsorasib after once- or twice-daily administration of garsorasib 600 mg were similar to those observed in the phase 1a part of the study. The pharmacokinetic profiles of garsorasib were similar between once- and twice-daily dosing.

Across all participants in phase 1b group 1, the ORR was 59% and the DCR was 82%. In addition, the median PFS was 7.4 months (range, 4.0 mo–NR). The observed ORR in this study was numerically higher than that observed with first-line pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy in a subgroup of 26 participants with KRAS G12C–mutated NSCLC in the phase 3 KEYNOTE-189 trial (50%), although the median PFS was numerically longer with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy (11.3 mo).19 However, such comparisons should be evaluated cautiously given the potential for differences in study designs and differences in participant demographics and disease characteristics between the study populations, which may contribute to differences in treatment outcomes.

There is limited evidence on the combination of a KRAS G12C inhibitor and an anti–PD-(L)1 inhibitor with or without chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC with a KRAS G12C mutation.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 In the phase 1b, dose-exploration part of the CodeBreaK 100/101 study in participants with KRAS G12C–mutated NSCLC who were naive to KRAS G12C inhibitor therapy, the incidence of grade 3 or 4 treatment-related AEs was 60% with concurrent sotorasib and atezolizumab (n = 10) and 79% with concurrent sotorasib and pembrolizumab (n = 19), with more than 50% of participants discontinuing treatment because of treatment-related AEs.27 Moreover, only modest antitumor activity was observed, with ORRs of 20% and 32%, respectively. In the phase 2 KRYSTAL-7 study of adagrasib combined with pembrolizumab in participants with previously untreated, KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC (n = 148), grade 3, 4, and 5 treatment-related AEs occurred in 55%, 9%, and 1% of participants, respectively, and 4% discontinued treatment because of treatment-related AEs.28 The combination therapy demonstrated antitumor activity in participants with PD-L1 tumor proportion scores (TPS) 50% or higher with an ORR of 63%.28,33 In a separate, phase 1b, dose-expansion study of olomorasib plus pembrolizumab in participants with previously untreated KRAS G12C–mutated advanced NSCLC (n = 50), the incidence of grade 3 or higher treatment-related AEs was 54%, with an ORR of 74%.30 In the study, the safety and efficacy of olomorasib combined with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy (n = 21) were also evaluated, and the incidence of grade 3 or higher treatment-related AEs was 43%, with an ORR of 50%.29 Similarly, in a phase 1, dose-escalation study of MK-1084, an investigational, next-generation, selective KRAS G12C inhibitor, plus pembrolizumab in participants with previously untreated, KRAS G12C–mutated metastatic NSCLC and PD-L1 TPS 1% or higher (n = 72), the incidence of grade 3 or higher treatment-related AEs was 29%, with an ORR of 77%.31 In addition, among participants with previously untreated metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC (n = 42), the incidence of grade 3 or higher treatment-related AEs was 60% and the ORR was 53% with the combination therapy of MK-1084 plus pembrolizumab and chemotherapy. Together, these findings suggest that there may be additive antitumor activity when KRAS G12C inhibitors are combined with anti–PD-(L)1 therapy with or without chemotherapy in patients with previously untreated, KRAS G12C–mutated metastatic NSCLC; however, the safety profile of this combination therapy remains to be established, and the long-term efficacy is unknown. Different strategies, including dosing adjustments, treatment sequencing, and patient selection, can be explored to optimize the clinical efficacy and tolerability of the combination therapy. More phase 2 or 3 studies are needed to draw more conclusive findings.

Limitations of this study include a small sample size and the lack of a comparator group. In addition, PD-L1 expression data were not available for most participants in this study; consequently, we were unable to evaluate whether there was an association between efficacy and PD-L1 expression with this combination. Furthermore, co-occurring genomic alterations with KRAS mutations, such as TP53, KEAP1, and STK11 mutations, which may be associated with treatment sensitivity or resistance to KRAS inhibitors, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy in patients with NSCLC,34,35 were not collected in this study. Collecting such information on biomarker status in future studies may help identify the patient population that is most likely to benefit from KRAS G12C inhibitor plus anti–PD-(L)1 inhibitor and chemotherapy treatment.

In summary, the incidence of treatment-related AEs with garsorasib combined with pembrolizumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy was high, and participants had frequent dose modifications or interruptions because of treatment-related AEs. Based on these findings, the study did not proceed to the phase 2 expansion for this participant population, and combination therapy with garsorasib, pembrolizumab, and platinum-based chemotherapy was discontinued.
