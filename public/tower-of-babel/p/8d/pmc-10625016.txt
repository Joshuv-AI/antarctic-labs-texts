Addressing sexually transmitted infections in Europe: challenges and strategies
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Europe
Published: 2023

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are a major public health problem, not only due to their immediate impact on sexual and reproductive health, but also because of associated morbidity and mortality. Despite increased awareness and advances in treatment and health care, in the WHO European Region a 49% increase in new HIV diagnoses was observed between 2010 and 2019, and an all-time high in reported numbers of bacterial STIs in 2019. The COVID-19 pandemic and the war in Ukraine have further jeopardised progress towards achieving the WHO's Global Health Sector Strategies targets to end the HIV, viral hepatitis, and STIs epidemics in Europe by 2030.

To bring Europe closer to achieving these goals, it is crucial to first discern the intricacies of the STIs challenges prevailing in Europe. This comprehension will facilitate the formulation of efficacious strategies for prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies in response to the increasing incidence of STIs. To this end, The Lancet Regional Health—Europe convened a group of experts in the field to provide an overview of the current landscape of emerging STIs, including newly recognised STIs (e.g., mpox and Shigella) and re-emerging STIs that were previously uncommon in Europe (e.g., lymphogranuloma venereum and hepatitis A virus), while identifying general and specific challenges to tackling STIs. The experts also offered recommendations for priority actions and concrete solutions for effective management of STIs.

This series on sexually transmitted infections adopts a multifaceted approach, covering key aspects of STIs in four papers: the current epidemiology in Europe, ongoing prevention strategies, approaches to managing asymptomatic infections, and state-of-the-art treatments.

Epidemiological data from 49 countries highlight significant variations in bacterial STIs reporting across countries in the WHO European Region, whereby notification rates per 100,000 population were higher in northern and western Europe countries than other regions. Additionally, between 2012 and 2021, there was an increase in the notification rate of Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and syphilis cases in countries within the European Economic Area (EEA), while a decrease was observed in non-EEA countries. Considering the important regional variations in STIs reporting across the European Region, efforts should be made to improve and harmonise epidemiological surveillance systems and reporting practices and ensure equal access to testing and screening.

These regional variations in reporting, together with health-care disparities and resource shortages, hamper prevention strategy efforts in Europe. Sustaining health education and condom promotion is crucial, alongside other interventions. While pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV has been introduced in 38 of 53 countries in the region, use remains low due to costs, limited licensing and low adherence. Cabotegravir, a long-acting injectable, has the potential to revolutionize HIV PrEP by replacing daily pills with just six annual injections, enhancing adherence, reducing risks, and expanding access to underserved areas and populations. Vaccination is another approach for STIs prevention. Current investigations are delving into innovative strategies, like repurposing existing vaccines (e.g., meningococcal for N. gonorrhoeae and vaccinia virus for mpox) and utilizing mRNA technology for HIV vaccines to expedite their availability.

Rising trends in STIs incidence have drawn attention to screening for asymptomatic STIs in the general population. This area is controversial due to the discrepancies in the overall benefit-harm balance of screening for each pathogen. For instance, screening for asymptomatic N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis infections is associated with psychosocial consequences related to diagnosis and higher antibiotic consumption, with a potential increase in antimicrobial resistance and disruption of the microbiome, with no clear benefit in reducing symptomatic infections and complications. However, for asymptomatic HIV and syphilis, there is clear evidence that early detection allows for the initiation of life-saving treatment while preventing further transmission. These discrepancies have fuelled the idea of a pathogen-specific approach to STIs screening, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach, that considers risks, benefits, cost-effectiveness, diagnostic tools, national health systems and population preferences.

Numerous challenges are associated with the therapeutic management of bacterial STIs in Europe, such as increasing antimicrobial resistance, drug availability affected by shortages and access issues, and slow progress in drug discovery. There is need for development of new approaches to reduce antimicrobial resistance. These might include utilising molecular assays for rapid identification of drug-resistant pathogens, implementing contact tracing, reducing empirical treatment in asymptomatic contacts, and even repurposing older antibiotics for the treatment of STIs.

The findings of the four Series papers provide valuable insights into the challenges faced by the region in eliminating STIs. Key actions that need immediate prioritisation include harmonising data collection across countries, ensuring access to novel prevention strategies and accurate screening measures, and the development of new drugs and vaccines. The timely execution of these essential actions is crucial to expedite efforts against HIV, viral hepatitis, and STIs epidemics in Europe, especially with the 2030 deadline rapidly approaching.
