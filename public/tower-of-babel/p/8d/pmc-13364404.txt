Prediction of Factors Influencing the Incidence of Diabetic Foot Ulcers Using Classical Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches: A Systematic Review
Authors: Alireza Jafarkhani, Mehdi Jafari Oori, Masoud Arabfard, Kiavash Hushmandi, Mohammad Pourebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcers are among the most challenging complications of diabetes. Diabetes heightens patients' risk of severe complications, including amputations and death, while also driving up healthcare system costs. Given the significance of this problem, the present study conducted a systematic review of studies that used Classical Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches to identify factors influencing the development of diabetic foot ulcers in individuals with diabetes. A thorough literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, covering their inception through 7 September 2025. This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMAs) guidelines. Data were analysed narratively using content analysis. Eligible studies used predictive methods to identify factors associated with the development of diabetic foot ulcers. A total of 4396 articles were screened, and 66 studies were selected for full‐text review after application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The review of these studies identified 95 factors associated with predicting diabetic foot ulcers, among which neuropathy, diabetes duration, age, body mass index and peripheral vascular disease were the most frequently reported. In addition, among the predictive models used in the studies, logistic and Cox regression models were the most useful for predicting factors associated with diabetic foot ulcers. This study identifies key predictive factors for diabetic foot ulcers, enabling healthcare systems to target high‐risk patients through early screening. Proactive identification of vulnerable diabetic patients can prevent severe complications, such as amputation.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, diabetic foot, machine learning, systematic review

Introduction

Diabetes is regarded as one of humanity's significant challenges in the 21st century. It is a metabolic disorder characterised by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from insufficient insulin production or the body's inability to effectively utilise insulin. According to global statistics, approximately 1 in 10 people is affected by diabetes. Moreover, it is projected that by the end of 2045, about 11% of the world's total population will be living with diabetes [1, 2, 3].

This disease can cause a wide range of complications, including cardiovascular issues, kidney problems, visual impairments and diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) [4]. DFU is one of the most serious and concerning complications of diabetes. These ulcers often develop from various causes such as neuropathy, ischemia or infections, and they tend to heal slowly. If left untreated, these ulcers can result in gangrene or severe infections. In such cases, limb amputation often becomes necessary [5, 6]. In Europe, more than 250 000 people with DFUs undergo amputation each year, and the death rate among these patients during the first 2 years after amputation averages around 40% [7].

DFUs can impose a heavy burden on patients, impacting their quality of life and raising concerns about the risk of future amputation. The economic costs associated with DFUs are also significant, including hospitalisation, surgery, wound care and rehabilitation. These issues can greatly increase mortality rates among people with diabetes. The widespread occurrence of these ulcers presents a dual challenge for healthcare providers: the need to provide both preventive care and advanced treatment for affected patients. Preventive strategies, especially those utilising modern techniques such as machine learning (ML), can help decrease the incidence and complications of DFUs, improve patients' quality of life and alleviate the financial pressure on healthcare systems [8].

Artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly advanced computer science and numerous practical applications over the past four decades [9]. ML, a subset of AI, is increasingly employed across various fields to analyse large‐scale data [10]. ML uses existing data to predict future outcomes when exposed to new data [11]. Essentially, ML learns from prior datasets, and after training with mathematical models, it can forecast outcomes based on sample data [12]. ML applications are broad, ranging from credit card fraud detection to online advertising. It develops computational systems that continually learn from data, create algorithms and make predictions—all without predefined assumptions—resulting in highly adaptable systems with minimal bias [13].

Today, ML is used across many areas of healthcare. This area of AI has excellent potential for diabetes‐related healthcare [14]. Modern ML models can forecast various diabetes issues, from hypoglycemia events [15] to DFU progression [16]. Many models have been created for these tasks. For example, multivariate logistic regression, a type of ML, is one of the most common methods for finding predictive risk factors of complications. In contrast, artificial neural networks, unlike multivariate logistic regression, are nonlinear and more adaptable, enabling them to detect complex nonlinear patterns—leading to better predictive accuracy [17].

These models can help healthcare professionals by predicting the risk of DFU progression and related complications. With the increasing prevalence of diabetes and associated ulcers, such predictive tools can play a significant role in lowering costs and saving time [18]. In fact, ML, as an emerging branch of AI, has demonstrated substantial potential in disease diagnosis, prognosis and management. Compared with conventional linear models, it offers advantages such as reduced human error, lower costs and greater adaptability, leading to improved accuracy and reliability in clinical decision‐making. Increasing evidence supports its effectiveness in enhancing healthcare outcomes [19]. In the context of diabetes management, ML techniques have been integrated with wearable technologies to support patient self‐management. These systems enable functions such as blood glucose monitoring and prediction, carbohydrate intake estimation, medication adherence tracking, physical activity monitoring, early detection of adverse events (e.g., hypo‐ and hyperglycemia) and automated insulin dose adjustment. Predictive models developed using algorithms such as Naive Bayes have shown moderate accuracy in identifying diabetes‐related outcomes [19, 20, 21].

In the present systematic review, we draw a clear distinction between classical statistical models and modern ML algorithms. Classical statistical models, including linear regression, logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression, have long been the cornerstone of medical and epidemiological research. These methods primarily focus on inference, hypothesis testing, effect estimation and interpretability, often relying on specific distributional assumptions. In contrast, modern ML techniques, such as RF, SVM, boosting methods (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM) and NN, prioritise predictive accuracy and generalisation, typically without rigid parametric assumptions and are better suited to handling high‐dimensional data, complex interactions and nonlinear patterns.

Classical statistical models have traditionally been used to forecast the development of DFUs. Techniques like logistic regression are useful for pinpointing risk factors and generating interpretable outcomes based on routine clinical data. Nonetheless, modern ML methods can detect more intricate and nonlinear relationships, making it crucial to differentiate between these approaches in the context of DFU prediction research [22, 23].

Given the importance of identifying predictive factors for the occurrence of DFUs and their role in preventing severe outcomes, such as amputation, this systematic review synthesises evidence from studies that have applied Classical Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches to identify these factors. Specifically, the review seeks to systematically extract, categorise and critically compare clinical, demographic and disease‐related predictors identified by various Predictive models.

Materials and Methods

This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines outlined by Moher et al. [24], ensuring thorough identification and inclusion of relevant studies. Also, this review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261414293).

Search Strategy

This systematic review examined the Web of Science, Scopus and PubMed databases for pertinent studies published from database inception through 7 September 2025. The search employed a combination of keywords and MeSH terms related to ML/classical statistics and DFUs. Full details of the search terms appear in Table S1–S3.

Selection Criteria

Articles were included if they explicitly aimed to predict clinical factors—excluding imaging and genetic factors—related to the development of DFUs. Classical Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches. For this review, we differentiated between traditional statistical prediction models and modern ML algorithms. Traditional models such as linear regression, logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression were analysed separately from ML techniques such as random forests, support vector machines, boosting algorithms and neural networks. Studies were included if they employed either classical statistical models or modern ML approaches to identify predictors of DFU development, provided their primary aim was prediction rather than purely inferential modeling.

Study Selection

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9, a reference management software, for organisation and duplicate removal. The screening process was conducted independently by two reviewers (A.J., K.H. and M.P.), who assessed titles and abstracts against predefined eligibility criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and evaluated by the same reviewers. The research team reached agreement on which studies to include, consulting the supervisors (M.J. and M.A.) to resolve any disputes. We removed articles that failed to meet inclusion standards, noting the specific rationales in our records. From the selected studies, we extracted key data methodically and entered them into Microsoft Excel.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by two researchers using a predefined Excel form. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the researchers and, when necessary, in consultation with the study supervisor. The extracted information included the study title, the first author's name, publication year, country, journal, data source, sample size, model category (classical statistical model versus modern ML algorithm), predictive factors, data type and study design (retrospective or prospective).

Risk of Bias

The risk of bias in the included articles was evaluated by two independent reviewers using the PROBAST (Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool). It evaluates the risk of bias and applicability of diagnostic and prognostic prediction model studies. Developed by a steering group through expert Delphi consultation and piloting, it covers four domains—participants, predictors, outcome and analysis—using 20 signalling questions. PROBAST provides a structured and transparent method to identify biases that may distort model performance in studies developing, validating or updating individualised prediction models. Each study was categorised as high, low, moderate and unclear risk of bias [25, 26].

Data Analysis

Because of significant variability in study designs, outcome measures, performance metrics and reporting methods, a quantitative meta‐analysis was not suitable. Instead, findings were summarised narratively and grouped by analytical approach, distinguishing traditional statistical models from modern ML algorithms, as well as by reported outcomes and key performance indicators.

Results

Results of the Literature Search

A comprehensive search was conducted across three databases—PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science—covering from inception to 7 September 2025. The initial search yielded 4840 records. After manually and software‐based removal of duplicates, 4396 articles entered the title and abstract review stage. Finally, 86 articles advanced to the full‐text reading stage. After reviewing these articles, 66 articles were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Source of Data and Sample Size

Approximately 70% of the studies (n = 46) in our research area were prospective, with the remaining 30% (n = 26) retrospective. In most cases, the researchers in the studies we reviewed identified hospitals as the source of their data collection; the sources of data collection in the remaining studies are detailed in Table 1. The minimum data volume in the studies on DFUs is 54, while the maximum is 63 464. Overall, the average sample size across the reviewed studies is about 11 000 people. Additionally, throughout all studies, the collected data were clinical, so there was no difference in this regard.

Distribution of Studies by Model Type

In the studies we reviewed, 13 predictive models were employed. Classical statistical methods mainly used logistic regression, with Cox and linear regression also being common. For modern ML techniques, Random Forest was the most frequently applied (Figure 4).

Discussion

Currently, with the rising number of diabetic patients, discussing the prediction of complications from this disease has become even more crucial. One such complication is a DFU. This complication incurs high costs for both patients and the healthcare system. The advent of technologies like AI and ML can play a vital role in reducing these costs by predicting this complication. Preventing amputation through timely prediction could represent a significant achievement for the healthcare sector. Therefore, this study was conducted to identify all potential factors that could predict DFUs. By reviewing relevant articles and applying specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, the authors identified 95 potential predictors of DFUs from studies that used ML and classical statistical methods, ranging from older techniques such as logistic regression to newer approaches such as random forests to forecast this condition. Among these, neuropathy, duration of diabetes, age, body mass index, and peripheral vascular disease stand out as particularly important, according to the authors of the reviewed studies.

Research shows that about 85% of DFUs are linked to neuropathy. When a patient develops neuropathy, metabolic or inflammatory processes damage sensory, motor, and autonomic nerves. This damage causes wounds to become chronic due to tissue breakdown and impaired pain signalling. As a result, patients often ignore minor wounds because of reduced sensitivity and pain transmission, leading to serious consequences like callus formation. These issues are especially significant today, as at least one in four people with diabetes develops a DFU [92, 93]. The results of the 2016 study by Volmer‐Thole and Ralf Lobmann also support our findings. In this review study, which focused on neuropathy and diabetic foot syndrome, they concluded that, along with age and a history of previous DFUs, neuropathy is also a key risk factor for developing DFUs. They also believe that peripheral vascular disease contributes to 15% of cases, while neuropathy accounts for 50% of the causes of DFUs. The remaining 35% of cases involve a combination of neuropathic and angiopathic [94].

It appears that the longer someone has diabetes, the more vulnerable the tissues become, leading to damage to nerves and tissues. These issues can slow the healing of DFUs and lead to complications such as retinopathy and nephropathy. Piran et al. (2024) concluded that having diabetes for more than 10 years is a significant risk factor for developing DFUs [23].

Increasing age is often considered a risk factor for disease. Specifically, as people age, they tend to develop comorbidities that make organs and tissues more vulnerable. Ageing can also prolong the duration of a person's diabetes, along with the associated problems mentioned earlier. In this context, the study by Yao et al. in 2024 found that most patients with DFUs are aged 50–59, while the majority of deaths from this condition occur in individuals aged 70–79 [95].

Obesity, often associated with Type 2 diabetes, increases the risk of DFU through higher plantar pressures and atherosclerosis‐induced lower limb ischemia. Elevated BMI contributes to insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction, speeding up macrovascular complications regardless of glycemia [23, 36].

Peripheral vascular disease in diabetic patients impairs blood flow to the lower extremities, delaying wound healing and increasing tissue necrosis due to ischemia. Atherosclerosis‐induced blockages decrease nutrient and oxygen delivery, raising the risk of ulceration even from minor trauma [36].

Biomechanical loading plays a key role in the development of DFUs and should be considered alongside neuropathy and vascular impairment. In individuals with diabetic peripheral neuropathy, altered gait, reduced joint mobility, prolonged weight‐bearing and increased plantar pressure can cause repeated tissue strain, which may progress to pre‐ulcerative lesions and ultimately ulceration. Evidence from systematic reviews indicates that lower barefoot plantar pressure, together with consistent use of appropriate footwear and offloading devices, is associated with a reduced risk of ulcer formation. Cumulative plantar tissue stress has also been linked to ulcer outcomes. These findings support the clinical use of pressure‐relieving interventions, including therapeutic footwear, custom‐made insoles, walkers and non‐removable offloading devices, to redistribute pressure and reduce tissue damage. From a ML standpoint, incorporating biomechanical variables such as plantar pressure, gait characteristics and walking speed may improve risk prediction and help identify patients who would benefit from individualised offloading and preventive care [96, 97, 98, 99, 100, 101].

While logistic regression predominates in the reviewed DFU studies, emerging ML methods, such as support vector machines and random forests, offer superior predictive accuracy. For instance, Nanda et al. and recent ensembles achieved AUCs of 0.88–0.98, enhancing risk stratification beyond traditional models. There was substantial variation in the data sources and sample characteristics of the included studies. Among the 66 studies, most were hospital‐based, while others used registry data, cohort datasets, outpatient clinics, community samples or large administrative databases such as national health records and electronic health records. The sample sizes also varied widely, ranging from 54 to 391 790 participants, with an overall average of about 11 000, indicating marked differences in study scale and statistical power. This heterogeneity is important because electronic health record‐based studies typically reflect real‐world clinical noise, missingness and variability in documentation, whereas cohort studies and registry‐based studies often use more standardised data collection procedures and more structured follow‐up. As a result, the predictive performance reported across studies is not directly comparable, since the same algorithm may behave differently depending on the quality, completeness and structure of the underlying data.

The definition and ascertainment of DFU outcomes varied across studies, likely influencing the reported predictive accuracy. The included studies did not uniformly define DFU development and the outcome was sometimes identified through diagnostic coding, clinician confirmation, wound‐care records, registry documentation or follow‐up assessment. Such differences in outcome ascertainment can substantially affect model performance: studies relying mainly on ICD codes may overestimate incidence because of miscoding or imprecise classification, whereas studies requiring expert verification may underestimate case counts if some managed cases are not captured in the formal record. In turn, more stringent outcome definitions often produce lower but more clinically credible performance estimates, while broader definitions may inflate apparent accuracy. Therefore, the variation in how DFU was defined and recorded should be considered a major source of heterogeneity when interpreting the pooled findings.

Also, the handling of missing data was reported inconsistently, which may have introduced selection bias in some studies. In several studies, incomplete records were excluded, whereas in others, it was unclear whether missing values were imputed, modelled separately or simply omitted from the analysis. This is important because excluding patients with incomplete documentation may disproportionately remove individuals with more complex disease, poorer access to care or more irregular follow‐up, all of whom may be at higher risk of DFUs. Consequently, complete‐case analysis can lead to overly optimistic estimates of model performance by training and testing on a more homogeneous, better‐documented subset of patients. Greater transparency in reporting missing‐data methods, together with the use of appropriate imputation strategies, would improve the robustness and reproducibility of future predictive studies.

The choice of ML algorithms and their tuning strategies varied considerably across the included studies. Classical models such as logistic regression and Cox regression were widely used, whereas more modern methods, including random forests, support vector machines, boosting approaches and neural networks, were less common and often applied in studies with different sample sizes and data structures. This matters because algorithmic performance is strongly influenced by sample size, feature dimensionality and parameter tuning; modern learning methods generally require larger datasets to achieve stable, reproducible results, whereas classical models may perform adequately in smaller cohorts. Therefore, direct comparison of algorithm performance across studies is misleading unless sample size, variable selection and tuning procedures are taken into account. The observed variability in performance metrics likely reflects not only model choice but also these methodological differences, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation of comparative claims.

Taken together, these methodological differences do not weaken the overall conclusion that ML holds promise for predicting DFUs. However, they do explain much of the heterogeneity in reported sensitivity, specificity and other performance measures across studies. For this reason, the aggregated findings of this review should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive, especially given the lack of subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Future research should aim for more standardised outcome definitions, clearer reporting of missing‐data handling and more consistent evaluation frameworks so that predictive models can be compared more reliably across settings.

This study, by identifying potentially essential factors for predicting DFUs, makes a significant advance in this field of research. Understanding these factors can significantly help the healthcare system in recognising diabetic patients at risk of developing foot ulcers. With further research, it may be possible to prevent this condition in diabetics through early screening. Such progress could be achievable thanks to the increasing capabilities of AI and modern ML algorithms.

This systematic review uniquely focuses on ML‐ and Classic Statistics‐identified predictors of DFU development. However, it has limitations; one is the exclusion of model‐validation studies that primarily assess statistical performance rather than clinical applicability. Another limitation was the exclusion of studies on DFU recurrence predictors, as including them risked confounding different profiles: occurrence factors versus recurrence factors. Another limitation of our study, as mentioned earlier, was the lack of subgroup and sensitivity analyses due to the considerable heterogeneity in the methodologies of the included studies, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies could stratify these for more comprehensive insights. Future research should broaden the scope of inclusion and integrate deep learning with traditional methods to enhance accuracy.

Conclusion

This systematic review compiles critical predictive factors for DFUs from ML and classical statistical analyses across 66 studies, empowering healthcare systems to swiftly identify and screen diabetic patients at elevated risk. Early detection through these factors allows for timely interventions that safeguard patients from devastating complications, such as lower‐limb amputations, which impose substantial morbidity and mortality burdens. Furthermore, healthcare providers can leverage these extracted factors—such as neuropathy, diabetes duration, age and peripheral vascular disease—to develop evidence‐based screening protocols, potentially mitigating the escalating prevalence of diabetes complications and alleviating systemic healthcare costs.
