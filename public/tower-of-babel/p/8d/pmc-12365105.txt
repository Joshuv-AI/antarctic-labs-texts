Comparing owner reported and genetic breed identification reveals high concordance in a large cohort from the Dog Aging Project
Authors: C L Sexton, C Reichel, A Keyser, M D Dunbar, Dog Aging Project Consortium, A Ruple
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreed is a ubiquitous classifier for both companion and working dogs, with profound implications for perceived and/or anticipated behavior, cognition, physiology, athleticism, and “temperament”. Owners, breeders, shelters and others are frequently asked to report on the known or suspected breed(s) of their dogs in various scenarios affecting dog and human wellbeing. However, there is a paucity of research on breed reporting accuracy. Using Dog Aging Project data, we compare genetic breed results with owner-reported ancestry for 5673 dogs. We find that 80% of dog owners identified their dog’s breed as matching the genetic reports. Among those few who reported disagreement with the genetic results, the majority indicated having dogs belonging to a breed not included in the reference panel. Additional causes for the 6% of samples reported as disagreeable include a stricter owner definition of match for single-breed dogs (e.g. 99.9% vs 100%) and genetic results showing a mix of two closely-related breeds for an owner-reported single-breed dog. Our findings indicate that people are usually aware of their dog’s genetic ancestry, which is important for the validity of using breed as a covariate in population-level studies, and for managing life history outcomes for dogs relative to breed-related traits and biases.Subject terms: Animal breeding, Coevolution, Genotype, Human behaviour

Introduction

Most domestic dogs alive today are free-roaming and of mixed genetic ancestry1,2. However, much of what is known about dog ancestry, broadly, comes from dogs whose lineages are dominated by a single breed, or so-called “purebred” animals3–8. Some of the earliest domestication of dogs is known to have occurred in East Asia. In China, this produced more than 50 indigenous dog breeds and in Japan at least 11, many of which are known today as “ancient,” such as Guangdong Sharpei dogs, Thin dogs, Tibetan Mastiff, Akita, and Shiba9,10. Beyond Asian lineages, other geographically distinct so-called ancient breeds include the Basenji, an ancient African breed, Arctic spitz-type breeds (e.g. the Alaskan Malamute and Siberian Husky), and Middle Eastern sight hounds (Afghan and Saluki)3. Additional evidence of selective dog breeding and pedigree records date to as early as 8000 years ago, when ancient hunter-gatherers maintained populations of dogs of differing size classifications, conceivably bred to excel at particular tasks11.

More recently and especially in Western cultures, the Victorian era brought an explosion of interest in modern breeding for “conformation” and among fanciers12. Over the last 150 years, breeding dogs for desired physical and behavioral characteristics—for both working and companion animals—has continued. Today, the breed-registering body American Kennel Club (AKC) recognizes 200 distinct breeds, and the United Kennel Club (UKC) recognizes more than 300 distinct breeds.

The degree to which specific dog breeds experience popularity in the context of the human socio-cultural environment varies over time13–15. Beyond single-breeds whose popularity waxes and wanes, “mutts” are often preferred by some for their so-called mixed-breed vigor, including both real and perceived health benefits of a lower inbreeding coefficient16–19. Today, many people interested in having a canine companion also forgo breeders and adopt from shelters, where a high percentage of dogs are typically mixed-breed20–22.

While popularity of specific breeds and breed mixes ebbs and flows, some (e.g., golden retrievers, Labrador retrievers) have maintained long-held appeal and representation in the companion dog population in the United States15. Such groups of modern dog breeds have been viewed as ideal sub-populations to study from a genetic perspective, primarily due to the sheer population size, low genetic diversity, high degree of linkage disequilibrium and the availability of their breeding and clinical records23. Indeed, these groups offer insight into specific heritable traits and markers of disease12,24,25, along with points of interest in the genome26,27, which is of particular import to veterinary diagnoses and treatment. However, even among breeds with wide-scale popularity, limited access to large numbers of dogs for genetic study has historically resulted in unequal distribution of information regarding breed genetics and global dog ancestry28.

Additionally, from a social justice perspective, discrimination of dogs has historically been and is still used as a proxy for discrimination of targeted peoples, such as via breed bans and housing restrictions on animals29–31. A common byproduct of necessary efforts to reduce such inequities is intentional and/or unintentional misidentification of breeds and breed mixes in both clinical and shelter records32–34. These inaccuracies in reporting are problematic both in terms of delivering appropriate care to the animals and in furthering our understanding of breed-linked genetics.

One potential solution to both unequally distributed and inaccurate dog lineage data that has already shown promise is the increasing availability of direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic testing. DTC tests for dogs became available to dog owners in 2009, concurrent with the rise in popularity of adopting mixed-breed/shelter dogs (at least in the US) and owner demand for more knowledge about their canine companions’ ancestry35. These tests rely on a defined set of genetic markers to establish a dog’s unique breed profile. Therefore, different tests vary in the level of detail they offer in terms of percentages and complete combinations of breeds that may be present in a given dog’s genetic code.

In addition to identifying certain or likely breed(s), DTC genetic tests can also be useful in predicting specific or likely combinations of a dog’s physical traits, such as size, weight, ear and tail shape, and coat color and texture. Some tests can show whether or not a dog might be at risk of developing specific genetically-linked health conditions, or might have a genetic variant that has been correlated with a condition in the past36,37. In both cases, predictions and potential risk factors are only as accurate as the existing genotyping technologies will allow; accuracy that depends in part on the number and kinds of dogs in the test company’s database.

In recent years, as more people have taken advantage of these dog genetic sampling products, and sequencing technology has advanced, our understanding of the underlying genetic basis for breed differences has improved. Genome-wide association studies have enabled us to associate particular genetic variants with breed-specific phenotypic traits37–42, and phylogenetic analyses have increased our understanding of the genetic relationships between breeds25,43–46.

However, there are still major gaps in our understanding of the accuracy of breed reporting from dog genetic tests applied in both consumer-focused and research capacities. In an effort to fill this gap, we used data from the Dog Aging Project (DAP), a US-wide long-term longitudinal study looking at how biology, environment and lifestyle influence aging in companion dogs47. Data from the DAP provide a unique opportunity to examine how well genetic test results align with dog owners’ understanding of their dog’s ancestry. Using DAP data, we compare genetic breed test results derived from low-pass whole genome sequencing with owner-reported responses regarding breed identification for 5673 dogs. The aim of our study is to determine the extent to which genetic testing results align with the owner-reported known or suspected breed(s). In particular, we are interested in leveraging genetic ancestry results to estimate and characterize breed reporting accuracy among dog owners.

Results

Owner and dog demographics

Among the 5,673 dog owners who completed the Genomic Report Feedback Survey (see Supplementary information), 49% were age 55 or older at the time of completing the DAP Health and Life Experiences Survey (HLES) (see Methods). Dog owners who participated are largely from a white, moderately high-income, educated population – the majority (50%) reported completing a Bachelor’s degree or higher; reported an annual income of $120,000 or higher (42%); and 95% self-identified as White (Table 1).

Among dogs who were sampled and had a post-sampling survey completed, roughly equal numbers were reported by owners to be single-breed or mixed-breed (51% and 49%, respectively). Most people reported breed(s) for their dogs in the HLES that matched the genetic sequencing results for breed ancestry for at least one of two possible breeds indicated (Table 2). In 86% of single-breed dogs (N = 2483/2873) the owner-reported breed matched the genetic panel call (alignment score = 1). In 46% of mixed-breed dogs (N = 1284) both owner-reported breeds were present in the ancestry call (alignment score = 1). In another 40% of mixed-breed dogs (N = 1116/2800), at least one of two owner-reported primary breeds was indicated by our panel (alignment score = 0.5). In only 14% of all dogs (790 single-and mixed-breed dogs), no owner-reported breed(s) were present in the corresponding genetic panel call (alignment score = 0) (Table 2).

Owner perception of genetic report vs. previous understanding of breed ancestry (response to feedback survey)

Among the 5,673 participants who completed the Genomic Report Feedback Survey, 80% (N = 4,527) reported “Yes” they agreed with the breed(s) called by the genetic panel for their dog (Table 3), whether or not the breed(s) they reported on HLES aligned with the genetic test result. Among this majority of participants who responded in the affirmative, 55% (N = 2,490) were owners of reportedly single-breed dogs, and 46% (N = 2,037) had mixed-breed dogs. An additional 14% (N = 810) of owners reported they were “Not Sure” if the genetic report got their dog’s breed ancestry right (195 of whom reported having a single-breed dog and 615 with a mixed-breed dog). The remaining 6% of dog owners (N = 336) reported “No” they did not believe the genetic report got their dog’s breed ancestry right, whether or not the breed(s) they reported on HLES aligned with the genetic test result. Owners of single-breed dogs represented 56% (N = 188) of the 336 “No” responses, while owners of mixed-breed dogs represented 44% (N = 148) of them. Among all feedback survey participants, neither age groups of owners (χ2(12, N = 5,673) = 14.83, p = 0.25) nor geographic locations of owners (χ2(6, N = 5,673) = 8.31, p = 0.22) affected whether they did or did not report disagreement.

Owners who report agreement with genetic report

For the 2,490 respondents with single-breed dogs who reported that they agreed with the genetic test results, the genetic test identified the owner-indicated “correct” breed as the top or only breed in 95% (N = 2,356) of cases. The median number of breeds with frequency > 1% representation in the profile identified by the genetic test for single-breed dogs was 1, with a mean of 4.48. In the majority of cases (N = 1,844; 64%), only a single dog breed was identified at a frequency > 1% representation in the profile for single-breed dogs (Fig. 1A).

For the 2,037 respondents with mixed-breed dogs who reported that they agreed with the genetic test results, our genetic test identified the owner-indicated “correct” breed(s) as the top first or second breed in 1546 cases (83%) (Fig. 1B). The median number of breeds with frequency > 1% in the genetic profile for mixed-breed dogs was 13, with a mean of 12.67. For these mixed-breed dogs, the number of dogs with only a single breed identified at 1% or greater was 53 (2%), indicating that the mixed-breed dogs in our study have a much more heterogeneous genetic ancestry than the purebred dogs. The higher number of breeds represented at smaller frequencies in mixed-breed dogs likely also contributes to the higher uncertainty in terms of owner perception of the genetic panel’s accuracy.

Dog owners who report disagreement with genetic report

For the 336 survey respondents who disagreed with their dogs’ breed assignments, we asked, “How did we get it wrong?”. A total of 153 (45%) owners responded, “My dog is purebred, but you got the breed wrong”, while 94 (28%) responded, “My dog is mixed breed, but you got the breed wrong”. Ninety-four of the owners (26%) chose “This just doesn’t seem right” as their response. Two individuals (0.6%) disagreed with the identification of a single breed for their dog (Table 4). Several used the free response/additional information area to expand (see Table 5).

Analyses of cases of owner disagreement

Single-breed specific results

Of the 188 single-breed dog owners who disagreed with the genetic report, 152 (81%) are cases in which the owner-reported breed is not detected by our panel/not present in the reference set, resulting in the algorithm making a “best guess” based on closely-related breeds. In these cases, some breeds had more than one instance of reported disagreement, resulting in 149 undetectable breeds in our panel. There were three cases wherein owners selected “Non-AKC breed” when indicating primary breed in the HLES. In these cases, owners wrote in a breed that we do not detect and do not ask for, including Alaskan Klee Kai, Basset Fauve de Bretagne, and Maremma.

For the remaining reported instances of single-breed dog owner disagreement where breeds were detectable by the genetic panel, only two cases were clear error (e.g., sample swap or contamination). We found that the other 36 were a result of an owner’s low confidence in the results (for unclear reasons) and/or misinterpretation of the results by the owners. These included 34 cases in which the primary breed reported by the owner was called as the highest percentage breed represented in the dog’s panel or was second to a “close relative” breed, and one case in which a “close relative” breed was called as the highest percentage, with the owner-reported breed still present in the profile. In all but one of the cases, the owner-reported breed was present in the panel; and, in this case, the owner’s breed assumption was based on information from the animal shelter.

Mixed-breed specific results

Of the 148 cases of disagreement reported by mixed-breed dog owners, 106 (72%) were cases when at least one of two primary owner-reported breeds were present in the sampling results (agreement score = 0.5; see Table 2). Of these 106, 84 (79%) were cases where individuals still reported that “my dog is a mixed breed dog, but you got the breeds wrong,” even when at least one reported breed was present in the call.

Additionally, in 44 (30%) of the cases of reported disagreement for mixed-breed dogs, owners indicated in free-response that they were simply surprised by the breed mix reported based on conjecture/visual and behavioral characteristics (Table 5); 35 (24%) were cases when neither of two breeds reported by owners as suspected primary breeds were included in the genetic profiling call (agreement score = 0); and seven (5%) were cases where the owner did not report any specific suspected or known breed.

Reported disagreement frequency by owner-reported breed group

In the 36 single-breed dogs where owners reported that they disagreed with the genetic profile breed call for reasons relating to low confidence or misinterpretation of genetic results and the breed was present in the panel, owner-reported breeds belonging to the Herding group appeared with highest frequency (33%), followed by Terriers (19%), and Sporting dogs (19%).

In mixed-breed dogs where owners responded in the Genomic Report Feedback Survey with either disagreement reason 3 (breeds wrong) or disagreement reason 4 (this doesn’t seem right) and the breed(s) were present in the panel, reported breeds belonging to the Herding group appeared with highest frequency (21%) among breed representation as either reported breed 1 or reported breed 2, followed by Sporting dogs (18%), and Working dogs (16%).

Comparatively, the top three most frequently counted breed groups among all dogs in the dataset (based on owner-reported breed) were: Sporting (25%), Herding (20%), and Toy/Working, both at 11% (Fig. 2).

Dog owners reported their dog’s primary breed(s). Dog breed group was determined by reported breed. Owners of both single-breed dogs and mixed-breed dogs who disagreed with DAP genetic test results most commonly reported a breed within the Herding group. However, the most commonly reported breeds across all dogs in the sample belonged to the Sporting group.

Reported disagreement according to source of reported dog breed information

One-third of (49) mixed-breed dog owners who reported disagreement noted more than one source of information for their dog’s suspected breed(s). Of the 99 owners of mixed-breed dogs who reported disagreement and only one source of information about their dog’s breed(s), 29% received information from an animal shelter, 9% from a breeder, 0% by meeting the father, 4% by meeting the mother, 2% from a previous owner, 25% from a genetic test, and 30% from another source (e.g. veterinarian, best guess based on phenotype, rescue organization) (Fig. 3).

Owners of mixed-breed dogs who reported they disagreed with the genetic breed profile had received information about their dog’s suspected breed(s) from a variety of sources. The most common breed information sources were shelters and “best guesses” of veterinarians and rescue organizations.

152 out of 188 (81%) of owners of single-breed or “purebred” dogs who reported disagreement indicated that they received information about their dog’s breed from a breeder. Sixteen (8.5%) said they received information from an animal shelter. (Other sources noted in free-response included “pedigree papers,” breed-specific rescue, and owner-surrender.)

Breed popularity

Owner-reported breeds

Four out of five of the current most popular breeds in the U.S. per the AKC50 aligned with the five most popular owner-reported breeds in our dataset. The top five breeds in the U.S. as of 2022 per the AKC are French bulldog, Labrador retriever, Golden retriever, German shepherd dog, and Poodle. The top five owner-reported breeds in our dataset (indicated as a single breed or either of two suspected mixed-breed options) were: Labrador retriever (11%), Poodle (6%), Golden retriever (6%), German shepherd dog (5%), and Border collie (4%) (Fig. 4).

The most common breeds among owner-reports aligned more closely with the most popularly ranked breeds in the U.S. than with the breeds determined at highest frequency by the genetic panel.

Genetic sequencing breeds

The DAP genetic panel is able to identify 114 distinct breeds (Supplementary Table S1). For 110 of these breeds, we identified at least one individual in the dataset whose ancestry included that breed at 1% of the genetic breed makeup or greater. The five breeds that appeared most frequently in the ancestry of dogs in our dataset were American pitbull terrier, Labrador retriever, golden retriever, German shepherd dog, and dachshund.

Discussion

Just as parent-reported data are critical to pediatric medicine, veterinary clinicians and researchers are often dependent on owner-reported data in assessing dogs’ health, and this information could likewise influence additional quality of life factors for both people and their companion dogs beyond the clinical setting51. The validity of research and the efficacy of practitioners’ work relies on the accuracy of these data. Our finding that the majority of dog owners in our study population seem to have an accurate if not always complete picture of their dog’s ancestry is encouraging in this regard.

In comparing owner-reported dog breeds with the results of our genetic testing, the majority (80%) of owners of both single-breed and mixed-breed dogs agreed that our test results aligned with the breed(s) they knew or suspected to be included in their dog’s ancestry. In the case of single-breed dogs, the majority of reported disagreement was due to the breed not being present in our genetic reference panel. In these instances, the breed called by the panel was most often a close relative of the owner-reported breed, suggesting that the percentage of time the owner’s assessment of ancestry is correct is even higher than 80%. Further, among owners of mixed-breed dogs who reported disagreement, 72% had at least one of two primary indicated breeds included in the panel. This likewise suggests that these owners reliably assign at least one breed in the mix more often than is indicated by the agreement rate, but that they have a stricter definition of a “mismatch.”

Although reported disagreement between owner-reported breed and genetic profiling calls represented only a small percentage of our study population (6%), evaluating these cases is important for both (a) informing real-world scenarios where incorrect breed identification and/or breed biases could be harmful or detrimental; and (b) improving our understanding of potential limitations to our methods for genetic analyses. Similarly, we found 137 cases where the owner of a single-breed dog reported the genetic breed profile as being in agreement with their previous understanding of their dog’s breed, but where the genetic results did not identify the reported breed as the highest percentage. This may have been a result of respondent error, but likewise warrants further investigation. While sampling error, either on the part of owner (i.e., submitting a sample from the wrong dog in a multi-dog household) or in the lab (i.e., sample swap) could account for some cases of discrepancy, it is likely that most instances of disagreement are influenced by other factors.

Interestingly but not surprisingly, our results suggest the possibility that breed popularity may influence whether or not an owner perceives the reported ancestry of their dog as in agreement with their previous perception of “correct” breed identification. For example, while American pit bull terrier showed up with highest frequency in the genetic panel, that breed was not included in the top five most popular breeds in the U.S., nor in the top-five owner-reported breeds. (Here especially we hypothesize the discrepancy may be related to societal biases against certain breeds/breed groups, as we discuss below). Likewise, owners of both single-breed and mixed-breed dogs who reported disagreement with the genetic profile were most likely to have a dog with a reported breed belonging to the Herding group, even though the most commonly reported breeds belonged to the Sporting group. This may be attributable to the distinct phenotypic characteristics that people commonly associate with Herding dogs.

We further found that the source of information about a dog’s ancestry may shape owner perceptions. The majority of mixed-breed dog breeds that were reported discordant with our genetic profiling were those for which owners indicated that they had received the breed information from shelters and rescue groups, or by best guess based on visual characteristics. This is not surprising as Gunter et al.52 show that while shelter workers match at least one breed at a 67% success rate in comparing visual identification to genetic test results, agreement falls to just 10% when asked to identify more than one breed. Furthermore, shelters and rescue groups are often under-funded and under-resourced as they face myriad challenges in rehoming millions of animals53,54. Thus it is possible that this high rate of misidentification could be a response to cultural and societal biases related to perceived behavioral and/or physical characteristics of specific breeds33,34. That is, even if a non-owner caretaker is aware of the dog’s ancestry, they may highlight a particular breed/breed mix over another to make the dog seem more adoptable to prospective owners55.

Misidentification could also be linked to ill-founded biases and legal policies that extend beyond the animal and target specific groups of people (e.g., certain breed bans have been reported to discriminate against individuals applying for housing opportunities29). For example, if a dog has both retriever and pit bull ancestry but is identified as a “lab mix” rather than a “pit bull mix” it would not be subject to laws restricting access to housing for those who own so-called “bully breeds”30,31,56,57.

In cases of dogs reported to be of a single breed, most survey-reported cases of disagreement between owner-reported breed and genetically sequenced ancestry were the result of lack of consensus regarding the genetic definition of single-breed or “purebred.” The term “purebred” is problematic for many reasons, not the least of which is that it is understood by most people to essentially mean one singular source of ancestral inheritance, or, one line of descendants all equally represented genetically. In the case of dogs, for some breeds, like those of ancient origin we describe at the beginning of this paper, ancestral lineage can be clearly defined and detected. For others, however, several factors such as geographic admixture, number of discrete lineages, and relative time since the breed was first recognized all contribute to the genetic profile that might designate an individual as “purebred”.

For example, in recent years, deliberate cross-breeding of poodles (both standard size and miniature) with secondary “purebreds,” such as retrievers, Labradors, Bernese mountain dogs, cocker spaniels and others, has resulted in increased popularity of hybrid “doodle” dogs. We found that in some cases, owners in our study referred to these dual-breed dogs as “purebred” and thus indicated that the genetic profile call of a 50/50 percent mix (or thereabouts) was “inaccurate.” But instead of a profiling error, these are cases of misunderstanding on the part of the owner.

In another instance, an owner reported disagreement with the genetic breed results because our panel indicated that their dog, which they identified as a “purebred” Norfolk terrier, was 88% Norfolk terrier and 12% Norwich terrier. In this case, familiarity with lineage history is an important factor in understanding why these results were not truly misaligned. The Norfolk terrier was recognized as a breed distinct from the Norwich terrier only as recently as 1977 per the Canadian Kennel Club and 1979 by the AKC58. Thus, it would not be surprising nor in error to find a notable percentage of the “parent” breed in this dog’s profile. Furthermore, 88% is still above the 85% threshold for assigning single-breed status24. However, dog owner participants were not provided with a definition or standard for interpreting panel-called percentages, which might otherwise have helped reduce instances of reported discordance.

Beyond the potential for owners to misinterpret results, absent reference markers are one of the true limitations currently inherent in our genetic breed assignment algorithm. We found that the majority of reported disagreement in single-breed dogs (90%) occurred because the owner-reported breed was presented as a breed option in the HLES but not present in the genetic panel. Cases such as these do beg the question as to how precise we can or should expect our technology to be when it comes to parsing closely-related breeds. Some larger, commercially-oriented DTC breed ancestry products are supported by more comprehensive algorithms with data from additional breeds and lineages, which likely contributes to the high specificity with which they identify distinct breeds59. Our data, on the other hand, are available in the public domain, and unlike the data used by many privately-owned DTC testing companies, are not proprietary. Because of this, quality control is of utmost importance, and results from this study show that even though our panel may not be “complete,” we are appropriately identifying ancestry in individual dogs for the breeds that are included at least 95% of the time. A future goal of DAP research will be to expand the breed panel.

Quality control and accuracy in the realm of genetic testing is not only important from a scientific perspective. As we have seen with DTC genetic testing in humans, results from such tests have real and impactful bearing on individuals’ lives and on society at large60–65. The same is true when it comes to interpreting companion animal ancestry. Understanding and addressing potential limitations of genetic profiling from an algorithmic perspective is relevant to how we use information about dogs’ ancestry to inform veterinary care. But our results also indicate that understanding where and how people obtain information regarding their dog’s breed can likewise have implications for dogs’ overall wellbeing.

Conclusions

Our results have important implications for the validity of using dog breed as a covariate in population-level studies, as well as managing health, longevity, and life history outcomes for dogs relative to breed-related traits and biases.

Dog breed ancestry calls determined by the DAP genetic panel were highly concordant with owner-reported breed(s). Out of 5,673 participants who completed the Genomic Report Feedback Survey, only ~ 6% of dog owners reported that genetic results disagreed with their answer for the primary dog breed, whether single-breed or mixed-breed. And, in 51% of cases where owners indicated disagreement between the genetic breed call and previously reported known or suspected breed, the breed is not currently detected by DAP’s panel (76% of single-breed cases of disagreement, 19% of mixed-breed cases). Of the remaining 49% of reported disagreement cases, most were the result of misinterpretation of breed percentages and/or high possibility of owner reporting inaccurate breed(s) based on “best-guess” information.

The high concordance between the genetic panel results and what dog owners reported as their dogs’ breed(s) not only confirms DTC genetic testing as a valuable tool for identifying breed-related factors that could influence dogs’ quality of life, but also suggests that dog owners have very good awareness of their dog’s general ancestry. Nevertheless, evidence of breed misidentification and/or misinterpretation of genetic panel results among the cases of reported disagreements underscores the importance of genetic counseling, outreach, and education for the public and veterinary professionals and animal care workers. Collaborations with housing authorities, veterinary associations, and animal welfare organizations are needed to address potential misuse or misinterpretation of breed data, which could lead to discrimination or negative impacts on dogs and their owners.

Methods

Data collection

Data were collected as part of The Dog Aging Project (DAP) initiative, an open data project conducted in the population of companion dogs in the United States. These data are available to the general public at dogagingproject.org/open_data_access. Dogs included in this analysis represent a subset of companion dogs enrolled in the DAP, who were deemed eligible for inclusion in various genetic studies47. To enroll a dog, the owner completes a short nomination survey on the DAP website (www.dogagingproject.org) and sets up a secure personal research portal. This portal utilizes the REDCap electronic data capture system66 to collect survey information from the owner throughout the duration of their participation in the DAP. The owner is led through an informed process (see Supplement). Once consent is obtained, owners must complete the Health and Life Experience Survey (HLES), which collects extensive information about the home environment, activity and behavioral habits of the dog, and the dog’s health history.

As part of HLES, owners are asked if their dog is purebred or mixed breed, and they are presented with a list of 279 breed choices (Supplementary Table S1) derived from the AKC recognized breeds. Owners are asked to select their dog’s known or suspected breed(s). It is important to note that while owners are instructed to report known breed information, in many cases, especially for mixed-breed dogs from shelters or rescues, the breed information provided to owners may have been inaccurate, incomplete, or based on visual assumptions. Owners who report a mixed-breed dog are instructed to select the top two breeds that they believe their dog’s ancestry includes. Upon completion of HLES, the dog is enrolled in the largest study cohort, the DAP Pack. Owners are asked to complete the Annual Follow-up Survey (AFUS) annually as well as other surveys and activities throughout the year.

Owner-reported breed data used in this study, as well as demographic characteristics regarding dogs and their owners and households, were sourced from the baseline HLES. Life stage for dogs enrolled in DAP is determined by the 2019 AAHA Canine Life Stage Guidelines48 in conjunction with mortality data, as previously described67. Dog size as reported by owners is binned according to 10 kg increments from < 10 kg to ≥ 40 kg, which are further described by breed estimated size as AKC and non-AKC dogs of toy/small, medium, standard, large, and giant. For this analysis breed size was further condensed to small (< 25 kg) and large (> 25 kg).

Following completion of HLES, owners who upload their dog’s veterinary electronic medical records (VEMRs), who allow DAP to contact their primary care veterinarian directly, and who express interest in participating in genetic testing become eligible for inclusion in one of the DAP study subcohorts nested within the Pack. These subcohorts facilitate more in-depth studies. Dogs are invited to participate based on the specific needs of each study cohort. Genetic testing occurs in many of these cohorts, including Foundation, Precision, the Brain Health Study, and a limited number of dogs in the Pack. Owners must complete an additional informed consent process in order to participate in genetic testing. The University of Washington IRB deemed that recruitment of dog owners for the Dog Aging Project, and the administration and content of the DAP Health and Life Experience Survey (HLES), are human subjects research that qualifies for Category 2 exempt status (IRB ID no. 5988, effective 10/30/2018). All study-related procedures involving privately owned dogs were approved by the Texas A&M University IACUC, under AUP 2021-0316 CAM (effective 12/14/2021).

When invited to participate in a subcohort, owners complete the consent process and elect to participate in genetic testing, a DNA Kit is pre-assigned to the dog and mailed to the participant’s address by our kit fulfillment partner (GBF, Inc., High Point, NC). Once received, the participant follows the provided written and video instructions to sample the dog using a Performagene swab (DNA Genotek, Ottawa Canada) and returns the DNA Kit to our sequencing partner (Neogen/GeneSeek, Lincoln, NB) in a prepaid mailer (see Supplementary information] for low-pass DNA sequencing.

Once samples are analyzed, owners receive a report with their dog’s genetic test results, including information about detected breed(s), sex, and likely physical characteristics (see Genomic Report Example in Supplement). Upon receiving and reviewing these results, owners are asked to complete a feedback survey, indicating their perceived agreement with the breed(s)/traits identified (see Genomic Report Feedback Survey in Supplement).

Genetic breed profiles and feedback survey data included in this study were sourced from those DAP participants who had elected to participate in genetic testing. A total of 7,627 dogs (52% single-breed and 48% mixed-breed; 51% male and 49% female) have been sequenced and have genomic data available. Of these, 5,673 dog owners completed the genomic report feedback survey. We included only those owners who completed this post-testing survey in our analyses. Dogs with feedback survey information were 51% single-breed and 49% mixed-breed; 50% male and 49% female. (See Table 1 for additional population demographic data for those who completed the survey and are included in analyses.)

Sequencing methods

Upon receipt of samples, the DAP utilized a low-pass whole genome sequencing and imputation approach to establish ancestry. Sequencing reads were aligned to the CanFam3.1 reference genome assembly (NCBI accession GCF_000000145.2). Genotype imputation was performed using LOIMPUTE and the commercial dog low-pass v2.0 [0.1x–6x] imputation panel of reference haplotypes (Gencove), consisting of 34,191,821 single nucleotide polymorphisms and 11,943,064 insertions/deletions and representing 540 dogs of known breed ancestry distributed among ~ 133 breeds, 28 dogs of mixed breed ancestry, 12 dogs of unknown ancestry, 62 worldwide indigenous or village dogs, 33 wolves, and 1 coyote (Gencove).

Publicly available genotype data from 109 modern breeds with at least 4 dogs per breed, 3 regional village dog populations (4 Nigerian village dogs, 5 Vietnamese village dogs, 55 Chinese village dogs), and 2 wolf populations (19 North American wolves and 25 Eurasian wolves) were used to infer ancestry for each dog (Supplementary Table S2)68. PLINK2 was used to identify ancestry-informative markers. 4,267,732 biallelic single nucleotide polymorphisms with < 10% missing genotypes were selected, and Wright’s F-statistics were calculated using Hudson method for (1) each dog breed versus all other single-breed dogs; (2) all village dogs versus all other single-breed dogs; (3) each regional village dog population; (4) all wolves versus all other dogs; (5) North American wolves versus Eurasian wolves. The DAP selected 1,569,037 SNPs with FST > 0.5 across all comparisons, and performed LD-based pruning in 250 kb windows for r2 > 0.2 to extract 115,427 markers for global ancestry inference.

Genotype data for the biallelic SNPs from query samples were merged with genotype data from reference samples. Global ancestry inference was performed using ADMIXTURE (version 1.3.0) in supervised mode (random seed: 43) to infer ancestry from these populations. Only admixture proportions over 1% for each dog were reported.

Descriptive statistics

We used R (version 4.4.0) with custom scripts written in Python to aggregate and parse data. In addition to genetic sampling results, owner-reported breed data, and Genomic Report Feedback Survey data, we also included HLES survey responses pertaining to owner demographics including geographic location, education level, ethnicity and household income in our reporting.

For the purposes of our analyses, we define a “single-breed” dog as an individual with at least 85% genetic representation of a single identified breed24. In the owner surveys, single-breed dogs were referred to as “purebred.” We likewise define “mixed-breed” as any dog with two or more breeds represented, with no single breed comprising more than 85% of the profile. We further define breeds as “close relatives” if they are in the same clade, described by Parker et al.69.

We first examined dog breed(s) from DAP genetic testing results in comparison to the breed(s) owners reported via HLES, to see how well the genetic profile and the reported breeds aligned. Individual dogs’ results were given a score of 0, 0.5, or 1, depending on the alignment between the owner-reported breed(s) and the breed(s) called. For single-breed dogs for which the owner report matched the call, a score of 1 was assigned. For mixed-breed dogs, owners were directed to indicate two primary breeds in HLES. If both of these breeds were present in the call, the dog was assigned a score of 1. If one of the two owner-reported breeds was present in the call, the dog received a score of 0.5. For any dog, if the singular reported breed (single-breed) was not present or neither reported breeds (mixed-breed) were present in the call, the dog was assigned a score of 0.

We next examined how “accurate” the owners perceived the genetic testing results to be, based on their responses to the Genomic Report Feedback Survey. For the subset of individuals who reported that they did not agree with the genetic testing results regarding their dog’s breed(s), we further analyzed the reasons for disagreement in both reportedly single-breed and mixed-breed dogs. We define disagreement as a case wherein the suspected or known dog breed(s) according to the owner is reported by that owner in the Genomic Report Feedback Survey as inconsistent with the genetic test’s breed profile. Owners reported disagreement in one of four response options on the survey: 1 = “My dog isn’t a purebred dog.”; 2 = “My dog is a purebred dog, but you got the breed wrong.”; 3 = “My dog is a mixed-breed dog, but you got the breeds wrong.”; or 4 = “This just doesn’t seem right.”

Finally, we explored several other factors that we hypothesized may have an impact on breed and agreement reporting, such as the dog/owner’s geographic location, age of owner, dog breed group, source of breed information, and cultural breed popularity. To evaluate whether owner age or geographic location was related to reported agreement vs. disagreement, we ran a chi-square test using JASP (v. 0.19.1). To evaluate breed groups, individual breeds were binned according to AKC group designation; e.g., Herding, Toy, Sporting, Non-sporting, Hound, Working, and Terrier, as well as Foundation Stock Service breeds70. To evaluate reported breed popularity within our population, we tabulated the total counts of each reported breed represented (in either single-breed or one of two breeds in mixed breed dogs) and compared to the AKC’s top-ranked breeds in the U.S. as of 202250.
