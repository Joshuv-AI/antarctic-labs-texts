Hepatic Amyloid Light-Chain Amyloidosis Incidentally Diagnosed via Endoscopic Ultrasound-Guided Liver Biopsy
Authors: Yuta Tsujiguchi, Tsuyoshi Suda, Tatsuya Imi, Miyabi Miura, Kiichiro Kaji, Kuniaki Arai, Kenichi Harada, Shuichi Terasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatic amyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis is characterized by amyloid protein deposition in the liver, requiring biopsy for diagnosis. We report a 71-year-old man with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinemia presenting with hepatomegaly and elevated enzymes. Imaging revealed no masses. The patient was diagnosed with hepatic AL amyloidosis through repeat endoscopic ultrasound-guided liver biopsy (EUS-LB), following an initial inconclusive result. A postprocedural hepatic hematoma resolved within 2 months. To the best of our knowledge, cases of hepatic AL amyloidosis diagnosed through EUS-LB are scarce. This case illustrates the diagnostic utility of EUS-LB in hepatic AL amyloidosis and highlights the need for careful interpretation of postprocedural findings.

INTRODUCTION

Amyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis is characterized by the deposition of amyloid proteins in multiple organs, including the heart, kidneys, nerves, gastrointestinal tract, and liver.1 With an estimated incidence of 1 case per 100,000 person-years, prompt diagnosis can be challenging due to its variable clinical presentation. Hepatic involvement often manifests as hepatomegaly and liver dysfunction; definitive diagnosis requires pathological confirmation.2,3

We present the case of a patient with unexplained hepatomegaly and elevated hepatobiliary enzymes, identified during outpatient follow-up for lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, also known as Waldenström macroglobulinemia (LPL/WM). An incidental diagnosis of hepatic AL amyloidosis was established through endoscopic ultrasound-guided liver biopsy (EUS-LB).

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old man with LPL/WM was referred to our gastroenterology department due to abnormal liver function tests and hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasonography performed during hematology follow-up. The patient did not report symptoms such as general fatigue, weight loss, or anorexia. On physical examination, no abdominal tenderness, jaundice, or peripheral edema were found. Laboratory findings showed normal aspartate aminotransferase (33 IU/L; reference range, 5–35 IU/L), mildly elevated alanine aminotransferase (54 IU/L; reference range, 0–30 IU/L) and total bilirubin (1.7 mg/dL; reference range, 0.3–1.1 mg/dL), and markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase (547 IU/L; reference range, 38–113 IU/L) and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (496 IU/L; reference range, 5–40 IU/L) levels. Serologic testing results were negative for hepatitis virus markers, antinuclear antibodies (including antismooth muscle antibodies and antimitochondrial antibodies), and tumor markers (carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, alpha-fetoprotein, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor). Macroglobulinemia with elevated immunoglobulin M levels, along with an elevated κ/λ free light chain ratio, were found. Urinalysis was negative for substantial proteinuria.

Abdominal ultrasonography confirmed hepatomegaly. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated hepatomegaly but no space-occupying lesions (SOLs) (Figure 1). Positron emission tomography/computed tomography showed no abnormal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake. Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging revealed no definitive SOLs; however, heterogeneous hepatic enhancement was noted during the delayed and hepatocellular phases (Figure 2). No biliary dilatation was observed on any imaging modality. Given the patient's known LPL/WM, hepatic lymphoma involvement was considered in the differential diagnosis, as well as cholestatic liver enzyme abnormalities, alcohol-related liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, and metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease.

Owing to needle phobia, the patient underwent EUS-LB instead of a percutaneous liver biopsy. During the procedure, tissue acquisition was performed with a single pass targeting the left hepatic lobe, using a 19-gauge EZ Shot 3 Plus (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) needle with a slow-pull technique. A single pass of EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration was performed targeting the left hepatic lobe using a 22-gauge EZ Shot 3 (Olympus Corporation) needle with 10-mL negative-pressure suction. However, owing to an insufficient sample, this initial biopsy was deemed nonconclusive.

A repeat EUS-LB was performed on another day, executed with 2 passes of EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy targeting the left hepatic lobe and segment 1 using 19-gauge Acquire (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) and TopGain (Medico's Hirata Inc., Osaka, Japan) needles. Tissue was acquired with 10 mL negative pressure suction and the slow-pull technique, providing adequate sampling (Figure 3), defined as the acquisition of sufficient liver parenchyma to allow Congo red staining and immunohistochemical analysis. Pathological examination with hematoxylin-eosin staining and immunohistochemistry revealed preserved hepatic architecture without lymphomatous infiltration and diffuse amyloid-like deposits within the hepatic sinusoids, which tested positive for Congo red staining. The Congo red-positivity persisted after permanganate treatment (Figure 4), confirming the diagnosis of AL amyloidosis.

After the initial biopsy, a 10-mm homogeneous hyperechoic SOL was recognized on imaging, consistent with a postprocedural hematoma (Figure 3). Serial ultrasound monitoring demonstrated gradual recovery of the SOL, with a complete resolution 2 months postbiopsy. Throughout this period, the patient did not develop any clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain, and no anemia or hemodynamic instability was observed. According to the adverse event definitions proposed by Cotton et al, this finding does not meet the criteria for a clinically significant hemorrhagic adverse event.4

The patient was initiated on melphalan-dexamethasone therapy for AL amyloidosis, resulting in reduced free light-chain serum levels and modest improvement in liver function tests during follow-up. Up to this day, the patient remains under outpatient follow-up.

DISCUSSION

The reported incidence of amyloidosis complicating LPL/WM is 7.5%.5 Histopathological confirmation remains essential for AL amyloidosis diagnosis, requiring demonstration of Congo red-positive amorphous deposits with characteristic apple-green birefringence under polarized microscopy.2,6 While biopsy of affected organs (e.g., the kidney, liver, and heart) is the diagnostic gold standard, it carries an inherent invasiveness.7 In patients with amyloidosis, liver biopsy has historically raised concern for bleeding due to amyloid-related vascular fragility and potential coagulation abnormalities.8

A key aspect of this case was the incidental diagnosis of hepatic AL amyloidosis achieved through EUS-LB. Although percutaneous liver biopsy has historically been used for diagnosing amyloidosis despite bleeding risks, diagnosing AL amyloidosis through EUS-LB is, to the best of our knowledge, exceptionally rare.8–10 However, there are reports of amyloidosis diagnosed through EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration of celiac lymph nodes, peripancreatic cysts, or gastric wall tissue.11–13 Compared with a percutaneous approach, EUS-LB offers technical advantages such as real-time visualization with Doppler guidance, a shorter needle path, and a more controlled needle trajectory, which may reduce bleeding risk.14,15

EUS-LB provides comparable diagnostic yield to conventional biopsy methods while offering several advantages: superior portal tract acquisition, longer specimen length, reduced postprocedural pain, and faster recovery.14 EUS-LB is suggested to have less contraindications and adverse events; however, definitive comparative evidence remains limited.15 These findings position EUS-LB as a viable alternative when, in selected clinical scenarios, percutaneous biopsy cannot be performed.

In conclusion, this case illustrates a rarely described application of EUS-LB for diagnosing hepatic AL amyloidosis. EUS-LB is considered to have a favorable safety profile comparable with that of percutaneous biopsy, serving as a potential alternative in selected patients. However, our experience highlights that postprocedural imaging findings suggestive of bleeding can occur, even when not clinically significant. Further studies are warranted to better define EUS-LB's safety profile and optimal indications when evaluating hepatic amyloidosis and other conditions in which conventional biopsy is contraindicated.
