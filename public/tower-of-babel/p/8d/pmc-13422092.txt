Evaluating the impact of South–North Joint Malaria Control Project on spatiotemporal transmission dynamics in South Korea using dynamic mode decomposition
Authors: Haram Seo, Byul Nim Kim, Arsen Abdulali, Sunmi Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria re-emerged on the Korean Peninsula after elimination in 1979, driven by cross-border mosquito incursions and imported infections. Despite ongoing control efforts, transmission persists near the demilitarized zone (DMZ). Major national programs, including the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011) and the Korean 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan (2019–2023), aimed to interrupt transmission and achieve elimination.MethodsWe applied the compatible-window dynamic mode decomposition (CwDMD) framework to malaria incidence data from 2001 to 2024 across 17 provinces and 37 subregions. This data-driven approach identified dominant spatiotemporal transmission modes and assessed structural changes in regional synchrony and hotspot connectivity before and after interventions.ResultThe South–North Joint Control Project (2008–2011) achieved a 63% reduction in national incidence between 2007 and 2011 and disrupted synchronization among border and metropolitan regions, indicating a shift in transmission structure. In contrast, the 5-Year Re-Elimination Plan (2019–2023) was associated with an approximately 49% decrease in incidence from 2018 to its lowest level in 2021; however, incidence subsequently increased by about 30% by 2023 relative to the pre-implementation level in 2018, accompanied by partial re-emergence of regional synchrony.ConclusionSustained malaria control requires not only reducing incidence but also weakening temporal and spatial coupling among high-risk regions. The CwDMD framework offers a robust tool for evaluating the impact of control measures and guiding adaptive, region-specific strategies toward malaria elimination in South Korea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05941-w.

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most serious global public health problems and is caused by parasites of the genus Plasmodium. Transmitted to humans by the bite of infected female mosquitoes, malaria continues to pose a persistent threat in many regions of the world. In Korea, the dominant etiological agent, Plasmodium vivax, was first identified by Hasegawa in 1913 and subsequently became endemic on the Korean Peninsula [1, 2]. In 1959, with the support of the World Health Organization (WHO) Western Pacific Regional Office, the Korean National Malaria Eradication Service was established, initiating systematic transmission control. As a result, malaria incidence sharply declined for several decades, and in 1979 the country was officially declared malaria-free by the WHO [3, 4]. However, in 1993, malaria re-emerged in a soldier stationed near the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) in Paju [5–7]. This re-emergence was characterized by both long-latency (6–18 months) and short-latency (<30<30 days) infections [8], reflecting the ability of P. vivax hypnozoites to persist in the liver and survive through the mosquito-free winter season [9, 10].

Following re-emergence, the number of cases peaked at 4183 in 2000 [11], but declined to 863 in 2004 after the strengthening of control policies. Thereafter, incidence fluctuated, showing repeated increases and decreases, and has remained relatively stable since 2011 [12–15]. Approximately 95.3% of all cases occurred between April and October, the peak mosquito season, suggesting a strong link between transmission and climatic factors [16–18]. Between 1993 and 2024, a total of 37,576 cases were reported, with the majority concentrated in areas adjacent to the DMZ in northern Gyeonggi and Gangwon provinces. Malaria was primarily reported in border regions including Incheon, Gyeonggi, Seoul, and Gangwon, where geographic proximity to North Korea has played a substantial role in shaping transmission dynamics [19–21]. In the early stages of re-emergence, more than 90% of cases occurred among Korean and U.S. military personnel stationed near the DMZ, but this proportion declined to around 40% by 2007, while the proportion of civilian cases in surrounding communities gradually increased [12, 13, 22, 23]. Chemoprophylaxis with chloroquine during the transmission season and primaquine after the season was implemented as a preventive measure, effectively reducing P. vivax incidence in both military and civilian populations.

Thus, malaria transmission in Korea has unfolded under the complex influence of climatic conditions and the unique geopolitical context of proximity to North Korea [24]. However, previous studies have primarily focused on incidence curves or single-region analyses, which limited the ability to capture broader spatiotemporal dynamics such as regional synchrony and asynchrony or changes in transmission structure [25]. Understanding these dynamics and identifying whether regional synchrony exists are crucial for characterizing transmission patterns, pinpointing high-risk areas, and informing effective control strategies [26].

To address these limitations, the present study introduces a data-driven approach, dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) [27, 28]. In particular, we applied the compatible-window dynamic mode decomposition (CwDMD) framework, recently used in the spatiotemporal analysis of COVID-19 [29, 30], to malaria transmission in Korea. CwDMD enables the adjustment of temporal and spatial windows to capture phase differences among regions and to quantify transmission intensity and identify hotspots through magnitude analysis. This approach allows for a refined assessment of regional synchrony and transmission patterns, while also highlighting high-risk regions that may require targeted interventions.

Accordingly, this study aims to quantitatively analyze the spatiotemporal patterns of malaria transmission in Korea using CwDMD and to determine the extent of synchrony among regional outbreaks. In particular, we compare the periods before and after the South–North Korea Joint Malaria Control Project to reveal dynamic structural features of transmission that are not readily observable through conventional statistical methods. We further analyze the periods before and after the implementation of the Korea 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan to comprehensively evaluate the effects of major public health policies. Based on phase and magnitude analyses, we identify high-risk regions where transmission intensity is concentrated. The findings are expected to contribute to the efficient allocation of public health resources and the identification of regions requiring proactive intervention. Moreover, this study provides a novel framework for interpreting malaria transmission within Korea’s unique geographic and epidemiological context. In particular, considering the divided political situation of the Korean Peninsula and the potential southward expansion of transmission under climate change, our analysis offers valuable insights not only for military and civilian populations in border areas but also for informing future malaria control policies in southern regions.

Spatiotemporal malaria data

South Korea is divided into first-tier administrative divisions, enabling a detailed regional analysis of malaria dynamics.

However, Fig. 2 reveals a clear spatial mismatch: while P. vivax cases—accounting for the vast majority of malaria incidence in Korea—are heavily concentrated in northern Gyeonggi and Gangwon provinces adjacent to the DMZ, both the proportion of rice paddies and mosquito density (TI, obtained from [33]) are substantially higher in southern inland regions where malaria incidence is rare. This discrepancy, which is further detailed through the regional statistical breakdowns provided in Additional file 1 (see Additional file 1: Tables S3 and S4), indicates that malaria transmission in Korea cannot be explained solely by vector abundance or habitat availability. Rather, the unique geopolitical context of the border region, together with climatic and seasonal constraints on vector activity, likely interacts in a complex manner to shape observed transmission patterns. This context provides an important ecological and epidemiological backdrop for interpreting the spatiotemporal structures identified in the present analysis.

The seasonal nature of malaria transmission in South Korea is evident, with over 90% of cases occurring between April and October. The highest incidence is recorded during the summer months, particularly between June and September, when high temperatures and humidity create optimal conditions for mosquito breeding and parasite transmission [34]. Over the years, a shift in malaria transmission patterns has been observed. Initially, malaria cases were predominantly reported in northern Gyeonggi and Gangwon, but Seoul and Incheon have also recorded a significant increase in malaria cases over time.

As the endemic areas have expanded, the importance of systematic management to suppress infection sources has grown significantly. In fact, since the reemergence of malaria in 1993, the Republic of Korea Ministry of National Defense (MND) has mandated the use of chemoprophylaxis (hydroxychloroquine and primaquine) for military units in high-risk areas near the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) since 1997 to prevent the spillover of malaria to the civilian community through personnel in border regions [36]. Along with these proactive military prevention policies, applying standardized treatment guidelines to diagnosed patients is a key element in maintaining the comprehensive national malaria management system.

Regarding military service history, civilians with no prior service accounted for 1818 cases (64.3%), while active-duty personnel and veterans represented 738 (26.1%) and 250 (8.8%) of the patients, respectively. These observations strongly indicate that malaria transmission in South Korea is influenced by a combination of spatial clustering, seasonality, and cross-border transmission dynamics. Malaria cases are consistently concentrated in specific border regions, such as Gyeonggi, Incheon, Seoul, and Gangwon, making localized surveillance programs particularly important in these areas [36]. In addition, malaria transmission shows a clear seasonal pattern, necessitating preemptive vector control interventions such as larviciding and the distribution of insecticide-treated nets before the peak transmission period [37]. A strong correlation has also been observed between malaria incidence and proximity to North Korea, highlighting the urgent need for inter-Korean cooperation in malaria prevention, including joint mosquito control programs, data-sharing initiatives, and public health partnerships [38, 39]. Given the overlapping spatiotemporal patterns, a spatiotemporal analytical approach is essential for comprehensively capturing the dynamics of malaria transmission and for developing region-specific intervention strategies.

The CwDMD framework was applied to selected windows corresponding to the years 2007, 2011, 2018, and 2023, generating discrete DMD modes and eigenvalues for each period. Although South Korea consists of 17 administrative regions, it was divided into 18 regions for this analysis by further subdividing Gyeonggi Province into northern and southern parts, as it contains multiple hotspots and exhibits distinct epidemiological characteristics between the two areas. The 18 regions include Northern Gyeonggi, Southern Gyeonggi, Incheon, Seoul, Gangwon, Chungbuk, Chungnam, Daejeon, Sejong, Gyeongbuk, Gyeongnam, Daegu, Ulsan, Busan, Jeonbuk, Jeonnam, Gwangju, and Jeju. To further capture spatiotemporal patterns, the DMZ-adjacent regions of Gyeonggi, Incheon, Seoul, and Gangwon were subdivided into 37 subregions based on their geographical associations. Incheon was divided into Ganghwa and the remaining area (Southern Incheon), while Gangwon was further subdivided into Northern (Cheorwon, Hwacheon, Goseong, Inje, Yanggu, Sokcho), Central (Chuncheon, Hongcheon, Yangyang, Gangneung, Pyeongchang, Hoengseong), and Southern Gangwon (Wonju, Yeongwol, Jeongseon, Donghae, Taebaek, Samcheok). The results of these analyses are presented in the following section “Results”. For each window, three principal categories of significant DMD modes were identified—oscillatory, growth, and decay modes—which were subsequently used to analyze the phase and magnitude structures.

Analytical framework for spatiotemporal model characterization

We employed three complementary visual representations—phase, magnitude, and ridgeline plots—to provide a comprehensive account of the outcomes derived from the CwDMD analysis. Each of these visual tools was designed to highlight a distinct but interrelated aspect of malaria transmission dynamics. Specifically, the Phase representation illustrates temporal differences in the timing of incidence peaks across regions, thereby allowing assessment of synchrony or asynchrony in epidemic progression. In parallel, the Magnitude representation quantifies the relative contribution of each region to a dominant mode, providing insight into the spatial burden of transmission and enabling identification of high-impact regions. Taken together, these measures allow simultaneous evaluation of temporal alignment across regions and the relative weight of each region in shaping overall transmission patterns.

To further enhance the interpretability of regional phase differences, we employed a hierarchical clustering procedure on the row vectors of the Phase matrix. This process began by computing all pairwise Euclidean distances between regional time-series profiles. This reordering ensures that regions with similar temporal dynamics are placed adjacently in the Phase plot, thereby improving visual clarity and interpretability without compromising the underlying hierarchical relationships.

In addition to Phase and Magnitude analyses, we used Ridgeline plots to visualize the temporal distribution of regional malaria incidence. Unlike simple histograms or line charts, Ridgeline plots capture both broad seasonality and finer distributional shifts, offering a clearer comparative view of epidemic patterns [43]. Weekly counts were expanded to case-level samples and smoothed via kernel density estimation (KDE), highlighting periods of concentrated incidence while reducing noise. For comparability, regions were ordered by epidemiological relevance (e.g., DMZ proximity, historical burden), and curve colors were mapped to mean distance from the DMZ to jointly encode temporal and spatial information. Plots were generated in Python with joypy, using tuned overlap, transparency, bandwidth, and color scaling for readability (Figs. 5, 6). Together with phase ordering and magnitude weighting, these KDE-based Ridgeline plots provide a concise, robust lens on CwDMD-derived spatiotemporal transmission modes.

Overall, this analytical framework combines clustering-based phase ordering, magnitude-based weighting, and KDE-derived Ridgeline visualization to deliver a comprehensive characterization of spatiotemporal transmission modes. By integrating these complementary approaches, the framework not only improves the interpretability and reproducibility of the CwDMD results but also provides a structured means to disentangle complex spatiotemporal patterns of malaria transmission. Importantly, the ability to jointly evaluate temporal synchrony, spatial heterogeneity, and distance-dependent effects within a unified framework offers a level of granularity that is not attainable through conventional visualization techniques. As such, this framework establishes a rigorous methodological foundation for subsequent epidemiological analyses, facilitates cross-study comparability, and enhances the translational value of CwDMD findings for informing targeted public health interventions.

South-North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011)

Since its reemergence in South Korea in 1993, malaria incidence increased from a single reported case to nearly 4000 cases by 2000. During much of this period, North Korea consistently reported higher numbers of confirmed cases than South Korea, underscoring the strong influence of cross-border dynamics on domestic [44]. A substantial proportion of South Korean cases were concentrated in regions adjacent to the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), particularly northern Gyeonggi, northern Gangwon, and Incheon. In both North and South Korea, malaria has been almost exclusively caused by P. vivax, the predominant species in temperate climates, while Plasmodium falciparum has been reported only in a few imported cases [45].

Although national enhanced control measures implemented in 2001 reduced the annual case count to 863 by 2004, incidence resurged thereafter, reaching 2227 cases in 2007. As shown in Fig. 4, malaria cases were concentrated in Ganghwa, Paju, Yeoncheon, and Cheorwon in 2001, but by 2007 the transmission focus had shifted toward western high-risk areas, including Ganghwa, Gimpo, Incheon, Paju, and Goyang. After the implementation of the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project in 2011, the overall number of cases decreased, with a decline observed in western regions such as Ganghwa, while eastern regions like Hwacheon showed an increase. In the most recent year, 2024, Paju recorded the highest number of malaria cases. Historically, Goyang, Paju, and Incheon were rural areas with relatively small populations, limiting opportunities for host–vector contact. However, with the rapid development of new towns, population density and human–mosquito contact increased substantially, which likely contributed to the observed rise in malaria cases [46]. This resurgence, driven by both cross-border incursions and domestic factors such as urbanization, underscored the urgent need for coordinated large-scale interventions.

In response to this resurgence, the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project was launched in 2008, primarily targeting DMZ-adjacent areas. As the number of transmission cases increased in regions geographically closer to North Korea, these collaborative interventions—including the cross-border distribution of diagnostic kits, insecticides, and disinfection vehicles—were implemented and led to a sharp reduction to 1052 cases in 2008, representing a 52.8% decrease compared with the previous year [13]. This outcome highlights the substantial effectiveness of coordinated inter-Korean initiatives in suppressing malaria transmission.

Despite these achievements, malaria transmission in South Korea remains highly complex, shaped by the strong seasonality of mosquito activity and the geopolitical division of the Korean Peninsula. Climatic, geographical, and policy-related factors interact to continually reshape incidence patterns, complicating long-term control efforts and underscoring the necessity of integrated, multi-faceted strategies. In this context, the Compatible-window Dynamic Mode Decomposition (CwDMD) framework provides a powerful data-driven approach for disentangling spatiotemporal transmission structures. Beyond identifying evolving hotspots, CwDMD enables rigorous evaluation of intervention effectiveness in high-risk regions, thereby offering actionable insights for adaptive malaria control.

Relative to Paju, the estimated phase differences were 0 weeks for Goyang, approximately 1 week for Southern Incheon, and about 2 weeks for Gimpo and Seoul. This narrow range of lags shows that peak incidence across major northern and metropolitan regions occurred within a 2-week window, reflecting a strong degree of temporal synchronization. Such synchrony suggests that transmission in 2007 was tightly coupled across the border–metropolitan network, with epidemics rising and peaking almost simultaneously across multiple high-risk areas.

Figure 5c provides ridgeline plots of regional case densities, scaled by distance to the DMZ. Contrary to expectations, DMZ proximity was not consistently associated with earlier epidemic peaks. For example, Pyeongtaek, southern Gangwon, Osan, Icheon, and Anseong—despite being relatively distant from the DMZ—displayed earlier peaks than some closer areas. These discrepancies likely stem from two factors. First, these regions reported relatively few cases, making their epidemic curves more sensitive to stochastic variability. Second, the dual-latency phenotype of P. vivax hypnozoites (long latency of 6–18 months and short latency of <30<30 days) means that some cases may have originated from infections acquired in previous seasons or from imported sources, thereby disrupting the expected spatial gradient from the DMZ.

Taken together, the 2007 analysis demonstrates three key points. First, DMZ-adjacent and metropolitan regions bore the highest transmission burden, as shown by their elevated magnitudes and case counts. Second, phase differences across major regions remained within 2 weeks, reflecting strong synchrony and indicating that epidemics were highly synchronized across northern Gyeonggi, northern Gangwon, Incheon, and Seoul. Third, while magnitude patterns aligned closely with DMZ proximity, phase patterns did not, highlighting the importance of considering both spatial burden and temporal dynamics when interpreting malaria transmission. Overall, these findings identify DMZ-adjacent areas as the structural epicenter of malaria transmission in South Korea and provide empirical justification for prioritizing northern provinces in the subsequent South–North Joint Malaria Control Project initiatives.

Post-Implementation

From 2008 to 2011, the South Korean government, in collaboration with North Korea, implemented joint malaria control programs targeting areas adjacent to the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and was able to substantially reduce malaria incidence, attracting attention as an effective cross-border strategy [47]. Reported cases decreased from 2227 in 2007 to 826 in 2011, representing a 62.9% reduction, and continued to decline to 542 in 2012 (a further 34.4% reduction) and 445 in 2013 (17.9%). These figures suggest that the joint control measures were highly effective in suppressing malaria burden. However, long-term elimination requires more than reducing incidence at the source; it also depends on interrupting inter-regional transmission pathways and altering the structural dynamics of spread.

To evaluate whether the intervention produced such structural changes, we compared spatiotemporal transmission patterns before and after implementation using the CwDMD framework. The 2007 analysis indicated that peaks in major northern and metropolitan regions occurred within 2 weeks of each other, demonstrating strong synchronization across high-incidence areas. By contrast, the 2011 analysis allows us to assess whether this synchrony weakened following the joint program.

As in the pre-implementation analysis, the study area was subdivided into 37 regions spanning Gyeonggi, Seoul, Incheon, and Gangwon, and malaria case data from April to October (29 weeks, the active mosquito season) were analyzed, yielding a spatiotemporal resolution of 37 × 2937×29. Phase analysis (Fig. 6b) revealed that Southern Incheon, Northern Gangwon, Yeoncheon, and Seoul exhibited successive phase differences of approximately 1 week each, while Goyang showed a phase difference of about 2 weeks relative to Seoul. Unlike in 2007, when most regions peaked within 2 weeks of one another, these results demonstrate substantially greater divergence in regional phase structures after implementation. This phase separation indicates that while incidence declined overall, the temporal coupling among regions was disrupted.

Korean 5-year malaria re-elimination plan (2018–2023)

Following a decade of relative stability after the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011), sporadic resurgences of P. vivax cases began to reappear along the northern border and adjoining metropolitan regions. These observations prompted the launch of the Korean 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan (2019–2023), which aimed to consolidate previous gains and achieve full elimination. To establish a baseline for evaluating the plan’s effectiveness, we first analyzed data from 2018, the year immediately preceding policy implementation. The eigenspectrum and reconstruction results for this period confirm the accuracy of the CwDMD fitting (see Additional file 1: Figs. S3 and S6). A detailed visualization of the 2018 CwDMD analysis—including magnitude, phase, and ridgeline representations—is presented in Additional file 1: Fig. S8. As in the preceding analysis, a spatiotemporal resolution of 37 × 2937×29 was employed.

The dominant mode in 2018 was identified as a Decay Mode, indicating that inter-regional transmission connectivity gradually weakened and that the overall transmission network became increasingly fragmented. Magnitude values were highest in Paju, followed by Southern Incheon, Goyang, Yeoncheon, and Ganghwa. For comparative clarity, regions were arranged relative to Paju, which exhibited the largest magnitude. Phase analysis revealed that Paju led Goyang by approximately 2 weeks, Southern Incheon and Ganghwa by 12 weeks, and Yeoncheon by 14 weeks. Taken together, these findings indicate that, although overall malaria incidence had stabilized following the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011), temporal coherence among regions continued to weaken by 2018. This progressive desynchronization likely reflects the combined influence of prior interventions, including the joint control program, Global Fund support (2010), intensified surveillance, and targeted vector control in high-risk areas. Nevertheless, as full elimination remained unachieved, a new national strategy—the 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan—was launched in 2019.

Given the resurgence observed during the plan, we further analyzed data from 2023, the year that recorded the highest malaria incidence after its conclusion. As in the 2018 analysis, the spatiotemporal resolution was set to 37 × 2937×29. The eigenspectrum and reconstruction results for this period confirm the accuracy of the CwDMD fitting (see Additional file 1: Figs. S3 and S7). A detailed visualization of the 2023 CwDMD analysis—including the dominant Oscillatory Mode, together with its magnitude, phase, and ridgeline plots—is presented in Additional file 1: Fig. S9.

The identification of an Oscillatory Mode suggests that inter-regional transmission dynamics had become cyclic, allowing infections to persist through recurrent temporal coupling. Magnitude again peaked in Paju, followed by Goyang, Southern Incheon, Yeoncheon, and Seoul. Importantly, phase differences between Paju and the other four regions shortened to approximately 2 weeks—much less than in 2018—indicating a reinforced temporal alignment among major hotspots. This renewed synchronization was consistent with the concurrent increase in malaria incidence, which rose from 576 cases in 2018 to 747 in 2023. Despite extensive measures under the 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan—including strengthened patient management in military units, intensified community surveillance, vector monitoring and control, physician training, and public awareness campaigns—regional synchronization was not disrupted. On the contrary, the plan coincided with reinforced temporal coupling of epidemics across high-risk regions, thereby sustaining transmission potential.

Collectively, these results suggest that the Korean 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan (2019–2023) was insufficient to achieve full elimination. Although the policy emphasized early case detection, the mean diagnosis delay continued to exceed the KDCA-recommended threshold of five days, averaging 6.7 days in high-risk areas and 5.8 days in other regions [48]. Overall, these findings highlight the limitations of current control strategies and underscore the need for more intensive, regionally adaptive interventions capable of disrupting persistent synchronization and achieving sustainable malaria elimination in South Korea.

Discussion

This study applied the Compatible-window Dynamic Mode Decomposition (CwDMD) framework to elucidate the complex dynamics of malaria transmission across South Korea from 2001 to 2024, with particular emphasis on two major intervention periods: the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011) and the more recent 5-Year Re-elimination Plan (2019–2023). The analysis was initially performed using monthly data across 17 administrative provinces and subsequently extended to weekly data across 37 subregions for higher spatiotemporal resolution. CwDMD effectively identified dominant transmission modes and revealed how structural shifts in malaria transmission networks were shaped by coordinated control policies and environmental feedbacks (see Additional file 1: Fig. S3, which illustrates the transition of dominant modes across intervention periods).

During the early years, strong synchronization of malaria dynamics was observed across the northern border provinces—Gyeonggi, Gangwon, Incheon, and Seoul—driven by proximity to North Korea and military-related exposures. However, the South–North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011) marked a critical turning point in Korea’s malaria transmission landscape. Prior to its implementation in 2007, high-risk regions exhibited strong temporal coupling, with phase lags of less than 2 weeks (Fig. 5). By 2011, however, interregional phase lags had expanded to as much as 6 weeks, as indicated by the 2011 CwDMD phase analysis (Fig. 6) and supported by the fitting results for 2007 and 2011 (Additional file 1: Figs. S4 and S5). This structural differentiation coincided with a substantial decline in malaria incidence, indicating that cross-border cooperation effectively disrupted entrenched transmission pathways and induced lasting network reorganization beyond the active intervention period.

In contrast, the 5-Year Re-elimination Plan (2019–2023) did not achieve a similar level of structural disruption. CwDMD analysis for 2018 (Additional file 1: Fig. S8) revealed considerable heterogeneity among major hotspots—Paju, Southern Incheon, Yeoncheon, Goyang, and Seoul—whereas by 2023 (Additional file 1: Fig. S9), these temporal disparities had diminished, with peak incidences occurring within approximately 2 weeks across most regions. This renewed synchronization corresponded with the rebound in national malaria cases reported by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), suggesting that the policy failed to produce durable suppression of transmission.

Beyond hotspot identification, CwDMD demonstrated strong capability in uncovering complex spatiotemporal relationships that conventional surveillance systems may not capture. By distinguishing synchronized from desynchronized regional patterns, the method provided valuable insight into the structural evolution of malaria epidemiology in South Korea. Over two decades, the eigenvalue spectra and dominant-mode decompositions (Additional file 1: Figs. S3, S8, and S9) delineate a clear trajectory: initially synchronized outbreaks in border provinces gradually transitioned to fragmented patterns with expanded temporal lags, followed by partial re-coupling in recent years. These long-term structural shifts reflect the cumulative influence of national control efforts, climatic variability, and inter-Korean cooperation on system stability.

By analyzing dominant modes, CwDMD enabled precise tracking of when and where regional decoupling occurred, offering actionable guidance for control strategies. In regions where synchrony persists, early-season vector control and cross-border surveillance remain critical to reducing transmission potential. Conversely, in areas such as Seoul, where transmission dynamics increasingly diverge from border-driven patterns, targeted approaches emphasizing imported infections, international travel surveillance, and urban vector control are warranted. Such differentiated strategies are essential for optimizing resource allocation and minimizing emerging vulnerabilities in the post-elimination phase.

While these findings provide important insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a descriptive analytical framework, CwDMD does not infer causality and therefore should be complemented by ecological, climatic, and behavioral analyses to clarify underlying drivers. Additionally, higher-frequency temporal data—such as daily or finer-resolution observations—would further enhance the model’s capacity to capture short-term transmission dynamics beyond monthly aggregates.

Rather than explicitly partitioning individual mechanisms, our CwDMD framework prioritizes the integration of key processes—including relapse and drug responses—into a unified, data-driven representation of transmission patterns. While CwDMD captures these complex factors implicitly, it has a limitation that it precludes the explicit isolation of individual biological and operational drivers. Consequently, the spatiotemporal patterns derived in this study should be interpreted as the final output of the system resulting from the combined interaction of these elements, rather than an attempt to isolate the individual contributions of each process.

Building on this holistic perspective, future research should focus on developing a hybrid modeling framework that explicitly integrates the mechanisms of hypnozoite-driven relapse and responsiveness to antimalarial drugs. Such an approach would enable the decoupling of individual biological drivers from the overall incidence trends, providing a more granular understanding of transmission dynamics. Furthermore, a critical subsequent task will be to precisely characterize the impact of environmental determinants on malaria persistence by incorporating high-resolution data on specific mosquito breeding sites, such as puddles and irrigation facilities, rather than relying solely on general rice paddy area ratios. By integrating these localized environmental factors, future models can more accurately identify the ecological drivers that sustain malaria in specific regions, thereby informing more targeted and effective vector control strategies.

Despite these limitations, the findings reaffirm the value of dynamic systems modeling in guiding public health decision-making. CwDMD offers a computationally efficient and interpretable approach for understanding how disease systems respond to policy interventions, structural changes, or shifting environmental conditions. Its flexibility highlights its potential applicability to other seasonal or vector-borne diseases—such as dengue, chikungunya, and influenza—where spatiotemporal coordination underpins outbreak persistence and control [29, 30].

Ultimately, this study underscores the need for adaptive, region-specific, and temporally flexible malaria control strategies. Continued application of advanced analytical frameworks such as CwDMD will be crucial for guiding targeted interventions, sustaining suppression of transmission, and developing resilient responses to Korea’s evolving epidemiological landscape.

Conclusion

This study applied CwDMD to malaria incidence data in South Korea (2001–2024), revealing structural shifts in transmission shaped by public health interventions. The South-North Joint Malaria Control Project (2008–2011) disrupted synchronized outbreaks and reduced incidence, whereas the Korean 5-Year Malaria Re-Elimination Plan (2019–2023) was insufficient to prevent resurgence, with synchrony across high-risk areas persisting. These findings underscore that sustainable elimination requires not only reducing case numbers but also weakening structural connectivity and temporal synchronization. Region-specific strategies, strengthened border cooperation, and timely diagnosis remain critical, and continued application of advanced analytical tools such as CwDMD will be essential for guiding adaptive malaria control policies.
