A metagenomic exploration of microbial distribution among the pond sediment, shrimp (Penaeus monodon) tissue and pond water: A special focus on nutrient cycling and shrimp viral pathogens
Authors: Avik Sarkar, Rakesh Kr Patra, Mathavan Muthaiyan, Ajoy Mallik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenaeus monodon, a major shrimp species cultivated in Eastern Asia, has faced setbacks in aquaculture due to disease outbreaks. Metagenomic studies offer insights into the pond microbiome, revealing its critical roles in nutrient cycling, water quality, and disease control. This study investigated microbial communities in sediment, water, and shrimp samples from an aquaculture pond in Chaital, West Bengal, India. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics analyses were used to assess taxonomic and functional profiles, focusing on nutrient cycling genes and viral populations. Sediments showed the highest microbial diversity, contributing to organic matter decomposition and ecological stability. Water microbes played roles in oxygenation and pathogen suppression, while shrimp tissues hosted microbes aiding digestion and immunity. Pathogenic viruses, especially Whispovirus (White Spot Syndrome Virus), dominated shrimp tissues. Functional gene analysis identified key pathways in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling. Integrating water quality data with microbiome profiles could help identify bioindicators and predict pathogen risks.Keywords: Shrimp pathogens, Microbial interaction, Penaeus monodon, Metagenomics

Introduction

Shrimp is one of the most economically important marine fishery products globally, contributing about 14 % of the gross value of fishery products (FAO 2011). The Penaeus monodon is a second-most productive captured and cultivated shrimp species, widely distributed in Eastern Asian countries.1 The P. monodon was the most popular cultivated shrimp species, accounting for over half of all shrimp production, after 2002 due to disease apprehension, the P. monodon shrimp farming sector has not progressed similarly with Litopenaeus. vannamei.2 It has been observed from Fish Stat J (2022) data that the aquaculture production of P. monodon is drastically reduced during the last decade with a CAGR value of 0.45 % as compared to the L. vannamei aquaculture production CAGR of 7.54 %. Common diseases affecting P. monodon include bacterial infections such as those caused by Vibrio species and penaeid viruses namely White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), Yellow Head Virus (YHV), Infectious Hypodermal and Hematopoietic Necrosis Virus (IHHNV), Taura Syndrome Virus (TSV), Shrimp Infectious Myonecrosis Virus (IMNV) have remained the cause of the huge economic devastation in the shrimp aquaculture industry.3 Rapid expansion of aquaculture practices, stocking overload, poor water quality, and inadequate biosecurity measures have resulted in increased disease outbreaks throughout the globe in different shrimp aquaculture systems.4 Long before their aetiology was understood and diagnostic procedures were developed, these pathogens had triggered pandemics.5

The diverse microbiome populations in aquaculture pond ecosystem, significantly contribute towards cycling of nutrients,6 maintaining water quality,7, 8 organic matter decomposition, defending against pathogens,9 potential shrimp pathogens and supporting host health.10 Metagenomics studies among sediments,11 water and shrimp tissue12 allow for the comprehensive investigation of microbial communities in shrimp aquaculture ponds and also help in the early detection of potential emerging pathogens. The knowledge on microbial diversity and their functional role analysis helps in implementing measures to promote beneficial microbes and control harmful ones.

The microbiome analysis through metagenomics study using next generation sequencing has been found to be useful in understanding the known pathogen diversity as well as in recognising new pathogens in shrimp aquaculture.13 Rapid detection and characterisation of suspected emerging viral pathogens is the most important strategy for better and sustainable disease management in aquaculture.14 Identification of the causative agents for viral diseases is very helpful in developing diagnostic tools which are suitable to screen the presence of viral pathogens in the breeding population of P. monodon to produce specific pathogen-free (SPF) seeds.15 Therefore, the metagenomics studies are helpful in controlling frequent disease outbreaks in shrimp aquaculture practice.

The study was aimed to identify and compare the microbial communities present in pond water, shrimp tissue and pond sediment using shotgun metagenomics, in order to investigate the potential role in maintaining overall pond ecosystem. This study will also explore the impact of microbiome composition on shrimp health, focusing on the different viral pathogens.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Sampling activities were conducted in a traditional and extensive black tiger shrimp aquaculture area (50 ha, water depth 1.2–1.6 m), which had repeatedly experienced production losses reported by farmers, situated in Chaital village, West Bengal, India (22°31′21.2″N, 88°47′32.1″E) on January 4, 2023. Surface-water samples (500 ml) were obtained in sterile plastic bottles from four separate points within the pond, at a depth of 40 cm, and subsequently pooled together. Additionally, bottom sediment samples (500 gm) were obtained in sterile plastic bags from the same four separate points within the pond. Shrimp samples (N = 12) weighing between 50 and 70 g were likewise obtained from the aforementioned pond. Samples were immediately transported to the laboratory on dry ice. Sediment and water samples were stored in −80 °C freezer, whereas shrimp tissues (Gill, hepatopancreas and pleopod) were dissected and pooled from shrimp samples. Subsequently, pooled tissue sample was frozen in −80 °C freezer. Samples were labelled accordingly: SED for sediment, WAT for water and TIS for shrimp tissue. The Institutional Biosafety Committee approved ethical clearance for this research (Reference No. RKMVCC/IBSC/2022/FC/001, dated July 30, 2022), and all procedures were conducted in compliance with these guidelines.

Isolation of genomic DNA and library preparation

DNA extraction was performed using the Xploregene DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions from pooled sediment, shrimp tissues, and water samples. The quality of the extracted DNA was assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry, with acceptable AA260/AA280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2, and gel electrophoresis.

High-quality DNA was used for library preparation following the manufacturer’s instructions using the KAPA Library Prep Kits. The process began with DNA fragmentation to an average size of ∼600 bp using the KAPPA fragmentation method. Subsequent end repair and A-tailing were conducted using the Hypa peep plus ERAT enzyme mix. Adapter ligation was immediately performed using DNA ligase to attach adapters to the end-repaired DNA fragments. The adapter-ligated DNA was then amplified using Illumina primers. The amplification process utilized 40 ng of input DNA with 10 pM of each primer and included an initial denaturation at 98 °C, followed by four cycles of 98 °C for 15 s (denaturation), 60 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 30 s (extension), with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a hold at 4 °C. The amplified libraries were purified with Ampure beads and quantitated using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity assay kit. Finally, sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, with size selection of fragments greater than 450 bp achieved using a 0.7x bead ratio.

Data QC, Pre-Processing and genome Assembly

The quality of the paired-end raw data from each sample was evaluated using SeqKit,16 FastQC (FastQCv0.12.1) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and MultiQC (MultiQC v1.17) software.17 The raw sequence data have been submitted to NCBI SRA database under the BioProject ID: PRJNA1176169 for data availability. Subsequently, the raw sequence reads underwent processing to eliminate adapter sequences and low-quality bases, achieved through Trim_galore (version 0.6.10) (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) utilizing default parameters. The resulting trimmed reads were then examined for potential human contamination by aligning them to the human reference genome (hg38.fa) using bowtie2 (version 2.5.2) with unmapped reads being selected for further analysis. However, the tissue sample underwent additional round of mapping with host genome of Penaeus monodon (NSTDA_Pmon_1) for potential host contamination removal using the same procedure. The pre-processed unaligned reads were utilized to assemble contigs using Megahit v1.2.9,18 employing k-mer sizes within the range of -k-min 31, -k-max 141. Contigs shorter than 200 base pairs were excluded from the assembly. The quality of the assembled genome was evaluated using QUAST v5.0.2.19.

Taxonomic classification and statistical analysis

The assembled genome to respective samples were analysed through Kraken2 tool20 which classifies the reads utilizing k-mer based homology against the PlusPF (contains Standard plus Refeq protozoa & fungi, dated-10/9/2023) database. Following taxonomic classification, the abundance of Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) was determined. The output from Kraken2 was transformed into an OTU table using a Kraken2 script and an in-house script. Additionally, a Krona plot was generated using the results obtained from Kraken2. The Phyloseq v1.38.021 was utilized for calculation of alpha diversity indices.

Viral community analysis

Prodigal22 was used to predict microbial open reading frames (ORFs). The resulting ORFs were translated to predicted protein sequences and annotated by aligning them to the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database using DIAMOND,23 a tool optimized for high-speed sequence alignment. Taxonomic annotations were then visualized in MEGAN6,24 which provides insights taxonomic composition of the microbial community.

Functional analysis: Role of microbiome in nutrient cycling

The analysis of critical genes associated with carbon, nitrogen, sulfur and other metabolism pathways were annotated by utilizing “kegg_annotation” function of DiTing tool which querying the translated protein sequences against the KOfam database (ftp://ftp.genome.jp/pub/db/kofam) using hmmsearch with KOfam suggested threshold values.25.

Results

Sequences quality and sequence assembly

Sediment sample produced maximum number of reads followed by water and shrimp tissue sample (S1: Table 1). The average read length is consistent at 151 base pairs across all samples, ensuring uniformity in the sequencing process (S1: Table 2). The GC % varies slightly among the samples, the sediment sample have higher GC content (∼56 %) compared to shrimp tissue with lower (∼43 %). The sequence reads of sediment have the highest Q30 % (∼94 %) followed by water (∼93 %), reflecting suitable sequencing quality (S1: Table 2). Although, shrimp tissue has slightly lower Q30 % (∼89 %). About 94.44 % to 99.79 % of sequences was retained from all samples after human contamination removal. However, in case of shrimp tissue sample, the host removal was performed and about 24.24 % sequence data was recovered and subsequently utilized in the downstream analysis (S1: Table 3).

Overview of assembly

Sediment samples show a rich and diverse microbial community with 62,987 contigs (≥200 bp), including significant counts across various length thresholds and a high GC content of 63.47 % (S1: Table 4). The largest contig in sediment is 35,556 bp and the total length is 48,288,133 bp. Though in tissue samples there were fewer in overall contigs (132,19; ≥200 bp), show long sequences, with fewer GC content of 39.55 % which aligns with a more eukaryotic or multicellular organism’s profile (many metazoans have lower GC content).The largest contig in tissue is 8090 bp, and the total length is 8,535,510 bp. Water samples have the highest number of contigs (217,045; ≥200 bp) but maintain a moderate GC content of 51.95 % and include maximum larger contigs among these samples. The largest contig in water is 61,982 bp, and the total length is 195,935,315 bp. Water has the highest N50 (877 bp) and N90 (544 bp), which suggests that the assembly is the best, with longer contiguous sequences. Sediment and tissue have lower N50 and N90 values, indicating shorter and potentially fragmented assemblies. Additionally, N50 and L90 values indicate that water samples have a more robust assembly with longer contigs, while tissue contigs are generally shorter and more fragmented, reflecting differences in organismal complexity and community structure.

Fig. 1 shows the diversity and abundance of bacterial phyla across three distinct samples, namely sediment, tissue and water. A total of 46 bacterial phyla were identified, each contributing to the microbial ecosystem (S2: Table 01, S3). Among these, the tissue sample exhibited only 13 phyla, while the sediment and water samples found to have 43 and 46 phyla respectively. Most prominent bacteria phylum i.e. belonging top five of different samples are mentioned in Fig. 1. Notably, Phylum Pseudomonadota emerged as the predominant phylum in all three samples, with the highest abundance observed in sediment (71.23 %) followed by tissue (63.68 %) and water (48.84 %). Phylum Actinomycetota constitutes 16.20 % and 13.28 % of the sediment, and the water samples respectively, while only 3.89 % of the tissue sample. On the other hand, phylum Bacillota and Bacteroidota shows different picture, they are predominant in tissue (respectively 11.91 %, 14.94 %) than sediment (respectively 1.44 %, 1.37 %) or water (respectively 2.85 %, 4.40 %). Phylum Myxococcota and Thermodesulfobacteriota are mainly present in sediment sample (respectively 3.53 % and 3.05 %).

A total of 1936 bacterial genera were identified at the genus level. Analysis of the top five most abundant genera in each sample unveiled intriguing patterns of distribution. Providencia emerged as the most dominant genus in tissue samples, constituting 35.76 % of the bacterial population. Burkholderia was predominantly present in sediment samples, comprising 11.29 % of the bacterial community, while Synechococcus dominated water samples with a relative abundance of 16.51 %. These findings underscore the niche-specific distribution of bacterial genera within the shrimp aquaculture system (sediment, tissue and water samples).

Alpha diversity among overall communities

Alpha diversity analysis (Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) among overall communities in sediment, water, and tissue samples (Fig. 5) revealed that the Chao1 index, which estimates species richness, is highest in water (10,548.93) and sediment (9,370.42), indicating a rich variety of species. In contrast, the tissue sample shows significantly lower richness (3,661.14). The Shannon index, measuring both richness and evenness, is similar for sediment (7.10) and water (7.07), pointing to moderate species diversity and even distribution. However, the tissue sample has a much lower Shannon index (0.96), indicating fewer dominant species and less even distribution. The Simpson index, assessing species dominance, is high in sediment (0.98) and water (0.99), reflecting high diversity and minimal dominance, whereas the tissue sample has a lower value (0.22), showing higher dominance by few species and lower overall diversity.

Nutrient cycling analysis

Carbon cycle

Mapping of the carbon metabolism pathways revealed that various carbon metabolic pathways are more abundant in the aquaculture pond sediments as compared to the pond water (Fig. 6). The data shows that anaplerotic genes, mainly found in sediment and water samples, are crucial for maintaining the citric acid cycle (CAC) in shrimp aquaculture ponds. These genes convert substrates like pyruvate into intermediates, supporting beneficial microbial growth, breaking down organic matter, recycling nutrients, and maintaining water quality.26 Fermentation to succinate genes, also predominant in sediment, play a significant role in converting organic compounds into succinate, aiding in organic waste breakdown and nutrient recycling. Photosystem II genes, found mainly in pond water with moderate amount, are associated with photosynthetic microorganisms like cyanobacteria and algae, providing essential oxygen and nutrients for the ecosystem.27 Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis genes are more prevalent in sediments as compared to the pond water for energy metabolism and nutrient cycling, supporting microbial growth and maintaining sediment health.

Nitrogen cycle

Fig. 7 represents the nitrogen metabolism pathways related genes in shrimp aquaculture pond sediment and water. NorBC genes are primarily found in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments and are nearly absent in pond water. They play a crucial role in denitrification, part of the nitrogen cycle, by converting toxic nitrate and nitrite into harmless nitrogen gas. The nirBD or nrfAH genes are the second most prevalent in pond sediment, followed by narGHI or napAB, all involved in nitrification within the nitrogen cycle. These genes encode nitrate reductase enzymes that convert nitrate (NO3–) to nitrite (NO2–). This conversion is vital for balancing nitrogen compounds, preventing toxic nitrate accumulation, and supporting beneficial microbial communities. The nxr-AB and NirA genes are moderately present in pond sediment. These genes in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments are vital for denitrification, converting nitrate (NO3−) to nitrite (NO2–), which is then further reduced to nitrogen gas (N2), reducing harmful nitrogen compounds, and maintaining water quality for healthier shrimp growth.28, 29.

Sulphur cycle

Fig. 8 characterizes the Sulphur metabolism pathways related genes in shrimp aquaculture pond sediment and water It has been observed that the fcc-AB genes are the primary sulfur cycle genes in sediment, converting toxic hydrogen sulfide (H2S) into sulfate (SO42−), thus it helps in reducing shrimp mortality and supporting growth.30 The dmd-BCD and acu-H genes, which are also abundant, degrade dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) from marine algae, aiding sulfur balance in the pond.31 Additionally, these genes facilitate acetate metabolism, enhancing the growth of beneficial microbes and promoting nutrient cycling for overall pond health.32.

The sat and apr-AB genes are the third most common sulfur-related genes in shrimp pond sediment. These genes facilitate sulfate transport, supporting essential metabolic functions for shrimp growth and health.33 However, apr-AB also encodes antibiotic resistance, which can help bacteria to survive in antibiotic-treated environments but poses a risk of spreading resistance to pathogens via horizontal gene transfer. This underscores the importance of prudent antibiotic management in aquaculture to mitigate the spread of resistance.28, 34, 13.

The dsr-AB genes are moderately abundant in pond sediment and play a role in reducing sulfate to hydrogen sulfide (H2S), contributing to sulfur cycling and facilitating toxic H2S buildup that could harm shrimp.31, 35 Efficient sulfur cycling helps in maintaining a balanced ecosystem, thereby preventing the accumulation of toxic H2S levels. Assimilatory sulfate reduction genes, also present at moderate levels, convert sulfate into organic sulfur compounds necessary for shrimp growth. This process supports the microbial community, enhancing nutrient cycling.33 In pond water, sulfur deoxygenase genes, more prevalent than in sediment, help oxidize sulfur compounds, promoting a healthier environment.36.

Other cycles

Fig. 9 highlighted other metabolism pathways related to genes in shrimp aquaculture pond sediment and water F-type ATPase genes are mostly predominant in pond sediment and water which help in synthesizing ATP. F-type ATPase genes support various cellular processes, such as growth, reproduction, and stress response. They also help microorganisms adapt to fluctuating environmental conditions and contribute to biogeochemical cycles like carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolism. Twin arginine targeting (TAT) genes are the second most predominant in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments. They encode proteins that transport other proteins across the cell membrane, which is crucial for nutrient acquisition, stress response, and maintaining cellular homeostasis.37, 38 Dissimilatory arsenic reduction genes are the third most predominant in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments which help in converting toxic arsenic compounds into less harmful forms. This process reduces the toxicity of the pond environment, promoting better shrimp health and growth. By managing arsenic levels, these genes contribute to a safer and more stable aquaculture ecosystem, ensuring the sustainability of shrimp farming practices. Other genes, namely, Sec-SRP (Signal Recognition Particle), encode components of the signal recognition particle, which helps direct newly synthesized proteins to their proper destinations within or outside the cell. Cytochrome c oxidase, cytochrome bd ubiquinol oxidase, and NADH quinone oxidoreductase are essential enzymes which support cellular respiration and energy production, vital for microbial health and organic matter breakdown.39, 40, 41 Together, these enzymes maintain water quality, reduce toxic substances, and promote a stable environment for aquatic life and ensure a sustainable and productive aquaculture system.

Discussion

Metagenomics is a powerful and widespread approach that allows researchers to investigate the entire microbial communities by sequencing all genetic material, eliminating the necessity to culture individual organisms.42, 43 Among the various approaches in metagenomics, shotgun metagenomics stands out as a comprehensive method that sequences the entire genomic content of a sample. Unlike amplicon-based techniques, which target specific genetic markers such as 16S rRNA, shotgun metagenomics captures the full spectrum of DNA from bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea. This approach provides both taxonomic and functional insights, revealing not only the diversity of microorganisms present but also their functional roles.44, 45 In this work, the shotgun metagenomic analysis of sediment, shrimp tissue, and water in an aquaculture pond provides insights into microbial diversity, uncovering unknown species, functional genes related to nutrient cycling, and the roles of microbes in disease spreading and ecological balance.

The tissue samples exhibited very less bacterial phylum diversity as compared to the pond sediment and water sample. Pseudomonadota was found to be mostly predominant phyla with maximum distribution in the sediment sample followed by shrimp tissue and water. Microbial genera belonging to this phylum are known to include pathogens that can cause significant health issues across a range of species.46 In shrimp aquaculture, these harmful microbes can impact not only the shrimp but also pose risks to humans who consume such contaminated shrimp.47 Actinomycetota is the second most predominant phylum in sediment and water samples. In aquatic environments, Actinomycetota can influence water quality by participating in the degradation of organic matter and contributing to the microbial ecology of the water and sediment.48, 49 Bacteroidota represents as the second most predominant phylum in shrimp sample, a phylum of Gram-negative bacteria. They play important roles in various ecosystems including shrimp gut microbiome and involved in the digestion of complex carbohydrates, breaking down polysaccharides into simpler sugars that can be absorbed by the shrimp.50 Members of the Bacteroidota phylum are known to interact with the host's immune system. In shrimp, they may help modulate immune responses, making the shrimp more resilient to infections. Some Bacteroidota species produce antimicrobial compounds that can inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria within the shrimp gut.51 By maintaining a dominant and healthy microbial community, Bacteroidota help prevent the colonization of harmful microbes that could cause diseases. A diverse and balanced microbial community in the shrimp's gut, including a significant presence of Bacteroidota, can provide resistance against diseases. Disruption of this balance, such as through the use of antibiotics, can lead to dysbiosis and increased susceptibility to infections like White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV) and Vibrio infections.52, 53.

A substantial amount of Bacillota has also been reported in the shrimp tissue. This phylum has been previously reported in the L. vannamei larvae microbiome54 and healthy juvenile shrimp.55, 56 On the other hand, Bacillota can also support energy storage in food through its involvement in polysaccharide degradation. It has been observed that the higher Bacteroidota/ Bacillota ratio induce higher survivability, and low ratio has negative impact in the growth of Acute Hepatopancreatic Necrosis Disease affected L. vannamei.10 Very little amount of Myxococcota phylum was observed in the sediment sample which play a beneficial role in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments by contributing to microbial balance57, nutrient cycling, antibiotics and other bioactive compounds production and potentially providing natural biocontrol against pathogens58.

The phylum Thermodesulfobacteriota accounted for approximately 3.05 % of the microbial community in the sediment sample of a shrimp aquaculture pond. Thermodesulfobacteriota contributes to essential ecological processes, the production of hydrogen sulfide as a byproduct can have negative effects on water quality if not properly managed.59 Cyanobacteriota (formerly known as Cyanobacteria) contribute about 2.74 % of the microbial community in the water sample of a shrimp aquaculture pond. Despite being a relatively small proportion, their presence is significant as they play a vital role in the shrimp aquaculture pond by contributing to primary production, oxygenation, and nutrient cycling. Under certain conditions, such as excess nutrients and high temperatures, Cyanobacteriota can proliferate rapidly, leading to harmful algal blooms. These blooms can deplete oxygen levels, release toxins, and negatively impact shrimp health.60.

The high abundance of Betaproteobacteria (40.48 %) in pond sediment highlights their critical role in nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, pathogen suppression, and maintaining the overall health of the shrimp aquaculture environment.61 Gammaproteobacteria is mostly predominant in shrimp tissue. They contribute to key processes such as decomposition, nutrient cycling, and bioremediation62 but also include potential pathogens namely, Vibrio harveyi.63 Alongside, it has been reported that the higher percentage of Gammaproteobacteria is corelated with the ammonia related diseased and dead shrimp samples.64.

The significant proportions of Cyanophyceae and Betaproteobacteria indicate a well-functioning microbial community in the pond water. Cyanophyceae contribute significantly to primary production, oxygenation, and nitrogen fixation, supporting the aquatic food web and water quality.65 Betaproteobacteria are essential for nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, and pathogen suppression, helping to maintain a balanced and healthy pond environment.

The presence of Burkholderia, Ralstonia, Pseudomonas, and Streptomyces in pond sediment indicates a diverse and functionally significant microbial community which includes nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, bioremediation, pathogen control, and overall sediment health.66, 67.

In shrimp tissue, Providencia is mostly predominant followed by Acinetobacter, Ralstonia, Cupriavidus, Flavobacterium. The presence of these bacteria indicates a complex interplay influencing shrimp health, growth, and adaptation in the aquaculture environment.68.

In shrimp aquaculture pond water, Synechococcus (16.51 %) represents substantial share which may contribute significantly to primary production and oxygenation through photosynthesis, supporting the aquatic food web.69 Burkholderia (6.19 %) is the second most predominant genus followed by Ralstonia (2.70 %) and Pseudomonas (2.69 %) which generally aids in nutrient cycling and bioremediation,70 contribute to organic matter decomposition and pollutant degradation, respectively.71 Streptomyces contribute 2.97 % which might play role in producing antibiotics that can help control pathogenic microorganisms. The identification and understanding the behavior of these pathogenic genera is crucial for the sake of disease risks management in aquaculture ponds and for ensuring the safety food supply.

The sediment sample is dominated by halophilic Archaeal genera that plays critical roles in nutrient cycling and organic matter decomposition, essential for maintaining sediment health and supporting overall pond function.72 In tissue sample methanogenic genera namely Methanosarcina is a versatile methanogenic archaeon capable of utilizing a wide range of substrates, including acetate, methanol, and methylamines, to produce methane. Its relatively high abundance in shrimp tissue (16.67 %) suggests that it plays an important role in the anaerobic digestion processes within the shrimp gut. Methanobrevibacter helps in reducing the partial pressure of hydrogen in the gut, which can enhance the efficiency of other microbial processes, such as fermentation by bacteria that degrade complex polysaccharides. Their presence at 8.33 % abundance in shrimp tissue indicates their role in managing hydrogen levels in the gut. Other genera namely, Halobaculum, Haloferax and Haloglomus support the saline environment and metabolic processes within the shrimp. In case of water sample Halobaculum, Halorubrum, Natrinema, Halomicroarcula, Methanobrevibacter are evenly distributed. The Halophilic namely Halobaculum, Halorubrum, and Halomicroarcula are involved in decomposing organic matters in the water and helps maintain water quality.73, 74 The Natrinema genus contributes to the salt tolerance and nutrient cycling.75, 76 The Methanobrevibacter genus is able to influence methane production and nutrient dynamics in the water.77.

Viral populations in sediment and water samples are shown to be relatively evenly distributed, while a notable presence of certain pathogenic viruses is observed in shrimp tissue. The study provides valuable insights into the health and environmental conditions of the aquaculture system, highlighting the importance of monitoring viral diversity for sustainable aquaculture practices.78 Viruses such as WSSV, Densovirinae, Penaeus monodon nudivirus, and nimaviruses primarily target shrimp tissues because of their host-specific tropism and the need for intracellular replication. These viruses depend on shrimp for survival, often integrating into the host genome (e.g., endogenous nimaviruses) or concentrating in organs like the hepatopancreas. They are less prevalent in water or sediment due to environmental degradation and their limited ability to live outside a host. Their presence in tissue is enhanced by vertical transmission and tissue-specific replication.

Whispovirus, specifically the White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), is one of the most devastating viral pathogens in shrimp aquaculture.79 Its presence in pond sediments at 6.57 % abundance is significant as it indicates the persistence of the virus in the environment. Gammanudivirus is a member of nudiviruses that infect arthropods, including shrimp.80 Nilusvirus, belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae, can potentially infect both vertebrates and invertebrates. Its detection in sediment samples at 5.05 % abundance suggests that it could play a role in shrimp disease dynamics. Sediments contaminated with Nodensvirus can be a direct source of infection for shrimp, particularly through contact during foraging and feeding behaviors. The presence of these viruses in shrimp culture pond sediments can contribute to cumulative disease pressure in ponds, affecting shrimp health and productivity.

The dominance of Whispovirus (92.06 %) in shrimp tissue suggests a severe infection, likely from White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), a major pathogen causing high mortality in shrimp aquaculture. The presence of Gammanudivirus (1.44 %) indicates potential chronic infections, while Pandoravirus (0.36 %) and Rahariannevirus (0.36 %) are rare, suggesting low-level infections or environmental exposure. The “Unknown” category (1.81 %) hints at unidentified or emerging viral threats.

In shrimp aquaculture pond water, the high presence of “Unknown” viruses (51.05 %) suggests the possibility of unidentified or novel viral pathogens, posing a risk to shrimp health. Nodensvirus (4.34 %) and Nilusvirus (3.05 %) could contribute to diseases affecting muscle tissues and behavior. Llyrvirus (3.80 %) and Bellamyvirus (1.97 %) are less prevalent but may still impact shrimp health.

Synechococcus phage S-SRM01 19.44 % and 31.57 % is the most abundant virus among pond sediment and water respectively that indicates a possible impact on cyanobacteria, which could affect pond ecology.81.

The Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices collectively highlight the diversity and balance of microbial communities across different environments. High values in water and sediment indicate rich, well-distributed communities that support ecosystem stability and functions like nutrient cycling and resilience to changes. In contrast, lower values in tissue suggest dominance by a few species, potentially impacting health and increasing susceptibility to diseases. These indices provide valuable insights into ecosystem dynamics and health.

Mapping of carbon metabolism pathways reveals a higher abundance of various metabolic pathways in pond sediments compared to water. Anaplerotic genes, crucial for the citric acid cycle, and fermentation to succinate genes in sediments support microbial growth, organic matter breakdown, and nutrient recycling. Photosystem II genes in water aid oxygenation and nutrient supply. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis genes in sediments enhance energy metabolism, supporting microbial growth and sediment health.26, 27.

The presence of NorBC, nirBD or nrfAH, and narGHI or napAB genes in shrimp aquaculture pond sediments are essential for denitrification and nitrification processes. They convert toxic nitrate and nitrite into nitrogen gas, maintaining water quality and promoting a healthier environment for shrimp growth. These genes support beneficial microbial communities and prevent harmful nitrogen compound accumulation.28, 29.

In shrimp aquaculture pond sediments, fcc-AB genes are most predominant that plays a key to the sulfur cycle, converting toxic hydrogen sulfide (H2S) into sulfate (SO42−), reducing shrimp mortality, and supporting growth.30 The abundant dmd-BCD and acu-H genes might be associated with the degradation of DMSP, aiding sulfur balance and acetate metabolism, thus enhancing microbial growth and nutrient cycling.31, 32 The sat and apr-AB genes are also present moderately that facilitate sulfate transport and metabolic functions but also pose antibiotic resistance risks.33 Moderately present dsr-AB genes may be helpful in the reduction of sulfate to H2S, contributing to sulfur cycling.35 In pond water, sulfur deoxygenase genes are most abundant which oxidize sulfur compounds to promote a healthier environment.36.

Overall the findings establish a valuable baseline dataset for the microbial and viral communities associated with shrimp aquaculture systems. By delineating the taxonomic and functional profiles of microbes across sediment, shrimp tissue, and water samples of aquaculture pond, this study provides critical reference points for monitoring ecosystem health and disease risk. The identification of key microbial phyla and genera with beneficial or pathogenic roles enables the future selection of bioindicator species, which can serve as early warning signs of environmental stress or disease outbreaks. Moreover, the presence of beneficial genera such as Streptomyces, Methanobrevibacter, and genus belongs to phylum Bacillota in shrimp tissues and environmental niches suggests their potential as next-generation probiotics to enhance shrimp immunity, nutrient assimilation, and overall pond health. Long-term monitoring using this baseline will aid in developing predictive models, designing targeted interventions, and implementing sustainable and resilient shrimp aquaculture practices.

Conclusion

The study revealed distinct microbial diversity across pond sediment, shrimp tissue, and pond water. Besides, this study also explored the interactions and relationships between the microbiomes of pond sediment, shrimp tissue, and pond water, emphasizing their impact on shrimp health and overall pond ecosystem stability. Pseudomonadota in sediment pose pathogenic risks to shrimp and humans. Actinomycetota and Betaproteobacteria aid nutrient cycling, while Bacteroidota enhances digestion and immunity. Bacillota promotes gut health and pathogen control. Halophilic archaea in sediment and water (e.g., Halobaculum, Halorubrum) support nutrient cycling and water quality, ensuring pond stability. Whispovirus (WSSV) persists in sediments as a reservoir and infects shrimp, as evidenced by its high abundance in shrimp tissue and moderate in sediment sample. Unknown viruses in pond water suggest potential novel pathogens, necessitating continuous monitoring. Synechococcus phage's abundance in sediment and water impacts cyanobacteria and pond ecology. The Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices indicate high species richness and diversity in sediment and water, essential for ecosystem stability through nutrient cycling and organic decomposition. In contrast, lower diversity in shrimp tissue suggests potential health impacts. Sediments exhibit abundant carbon metabolism pathways, supporting microbial growth and nutrient recycling. Key genes regulate nitrogen and sulfur cycles, energy production, and toxin management, ensuring sustainable aquaculture. This insight into microbial distribution can contribute to strengthening disease surveillance and optimizing shrimp aquaculture management. Future studies across diverse shrimp aquaculture ponds, integrating water physicochemical parameters, will further enhance disease monitoring and sustainable pond management strategies.
