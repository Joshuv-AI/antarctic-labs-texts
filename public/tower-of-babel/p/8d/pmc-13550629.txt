First-principles study of Y2CCl2 and Janus Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) MXenes for photovoltaic applications
Authors: A A Faysal, Swarup Ghosh, It Ee Lee, Qamar Wali, Muhammad Aamir, M A Ali, M M Hossain, N Jahan, M Z Hossain, Md Akhtaruzzaman, M M Uddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-dimensional (2D) Janus MXenes offer a promising platform for photovoltaic (PV) absorbers, as asymmetric surface terminations can simultaneously tailor band gaps, optical response, and built-in electric field, which promote charge carrier separation. Here, a systematic first-principles study of the symmetric halide-terminated MXene Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) using density functional theory has been performed. Hybrid-functional (HSE06) electronic structure calculations identify all compounds as indirect-gap semiconductors with gaps of 1.63 eV (Y2CCl2), 1.36 eV (Y2CClF), 1.57 eV (Y2CClBr), and 1.21 eV (Y2CClI). Orbital-resolved density of states, charge-partitioning, and electron-localization function analyses reveal charge transfer from Y toward C and the surface halogens, with Janus functionalization producing pronounced surface asymmetry. Janus functionalization further produces substantial surface asymmetry in the work function, yielding work function differences of 3.09, 1.63, and 2.10 eV for Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively, suggesting intrinsic fields that may assist carrier separation. Optical calculations show strong absorption in the visible window with coefficients on the order of 105 cm− 1. Carrier-transport descriptors reveal smaller electron than hole effective masses across the series, and the screened 2D Mott–Wannier model yields exciton binding energies of 1.09, 1.12, 1.02, and 0.91 eV for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively. Finally, PV metrics computed within the modified Shockley–Queisser formalism predict maximum efficiencies of 23.10%, 25.66%, 23.92%, and 23.01% for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively, with Y2CClF emerging as the most favorable absorber due to its near-optimal gap and highest maximum power density. Overall, this study demonstrates that halogen-functionalized Y2C-based Janus MXenes are fascinating materials for next-generation photovoltaic and optoelectronic devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62509-z.Keywords: Janus MXenes; First-principles DFT; Electric field, optical absorption; Photovoltaic efficiencySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The urgent global imperative to transition from fossil fuels to sustainable energy sources has intensified the search for advanced materials capable of efficient solar energy conversion1. In this pursuit, two-dimensional (2D) materials have emerged as a revolutionary platform, owing to their distinctive physical characteristics and strong prospects for integration into photovoltaic (PV) and optoelectronic technologies2. Their tunable band gaps3, high absorption coefficients4, and reduced carrier recombination5 make 2D semiconductors particularly attractive for solar applications, as these attributes collectively raise power-conversion efficiency. A wide range of 2D families, including MXenes6, transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)7, phosphorene8, silicene9, and 2D perovskites10, have been explored in this context, each offering distinct and tailorable properties for next-generation solar energy conversion and optoelectronic devices. Among these, MXenes, two-dimensional transition-metal carbides and nitrides, have emerged as a particularly versatile family due to their surface chemical activity and a relatively large set of functional properties11. MXenes are commonly produced by selectively etching the “A” atomic layers from layered MAX phases, Mn+1AXn, where M is an early transition metal, A is typically a group 13 or 14 element, and X is C or N, with n = 1–312–14. After etching, the outer metal layers are passivated by surface terminations that arise from synthesis conditions. Consequently, MXenes are often represented in the chemically extended form Mn+1XnTx, where Tx may include –O, –OH, –F, –H, and –Cl15. This termination chemistry provides an efficient route for band-structure engineering: while many pristine MXenes are metallic, appropriate functionalization can open semiconducting gaps and substantially enhance visible/UV optical activity16. Such tunability enables high MXene performance across diverse application domains, including energy storage devices17, gas sensing, supercapacitors, electromagnetic interference shielding, and photocatalysis18. In addition, incorporating MXenes into PV device architectures has been reported to improve operational stability and power conversion performance, highlighting their growing relevance to solar-energy technologies19.

Structural symmetry offers another strong lever for tailoring 2D materials. Breaking inversion symmetry can introduce built-in polarity and thereby generate novel properties that are absent in symmetric monolayers16. Janus monolayers, characterized by distinct atomic species or terminations on opposite surfaces, provide a route to such symmetry breaking. The experimental synthesis of Janus transition-metal dichalcogenides such as MoSSe via modified chemical vapor deposition has established the feasibility of realizing controlled surface asymmetry in 2D crystals, and room-temperature strategies for producing Janus TMDs and their heterostructures have also been reported20. In this scenario, formation of Janus structures and the associated asymmetries to MXenes can also be introduced via dual surface functionalization (e.g., M2CTT′)18 or through mixed-metal carbide frameworks of the type MM′CT221. Such Janus MXenes can exhibit significantly altered electronic and optical behavior compared with their symmetrically terminated counterparts21. Indeed, recent theoretical investigations have suggested that Janus MXenes may feature visible-range band gaps and favorable carrier-transport characteristics, indicating potential for optoelectronic and solar-related applications22. In addition, the termination-induced asymmetry in Janus MXenes can generate intrinsic polarity, often manifested as a surface-potential difference and a built-in electric field, thereby facilitating electron–hole separation and reducing recombination, making these systems particularly suitable for photovoltaic applications. To analyze these concepts, several studies have carried out PV-oriented investigations of Janus MXenes. For instance, Zhang et al. systematically investigated Janus-functionalized Sc2CTT′ MXenes (including Sc2COHCl, Sc2CHCl, and Sc2CFCl), reporting strong optical activity and indicators relevant to solar-energy conversion23. More recently, Ghosh et al. performed an explicit photovoltaic analysis of Sc2C-based Janus MXenes (Sc2CFBr, Sc2CFCl, Sc2CHCl, Sc2CHF), reporting indirect semiconducting behavior with HSE06 band gaps in the (1.61–1.88 eV) range and PV descriptors indicating notable solar-cell potential24. In addition, Amirian et al. investigated symmetric functionalized Y2CX2 (X = F, Cl, Br, I) monolayers and reported that Y2CCl2 is an indirect-gap semiconductor with HSE06 gaps of 1.6825. For Y2C-based systems, recent studies further suggest that Y2C-derived Janus MXenes naturally exhibit band gaps suitable for photovoltaic applications. Specifically, Aparicio-Huacarpuma et al. identified stable semiconducting Janus structures, including Y2CTI (T = Br, Cl, F, H) and M2CTT′ (M = Sc, Y; TT′ = FCl, FBr, ClBr) families, reporting indirect band gaps in the range of (1.2–1.90 eV)22,26. These findings reinforce that Janus halide-terminated compounds based on Y2CCl2 fall within the optimal band-gap window of (1–2.5 eV), widely regarded as ideal for photovoltaic materials, highlighting their emergence as a new class of potential PV absorbers. Despite the rapid growth of MXene research and the wide range of reported applications27, Janus MXenes, based on Y2CCl2 frameworks with asymmetric halide terminations remain limited, particularly a systematic photovoltaic-driven analysis that simultaneously addresses termination-induced polarity (e.g., work-function asymmetry), carrier transport, dielectric screening, excitonic effects, and corresponding PV efficiency.

Motivated by this research gap, the present study investigated the electronic, optical, built-in electric field, and photovoltaic properties of symmetric MXene Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) using first-principles DFT calculations. Structural relaxation and stability indicators are first established, followed by the determination of elastic properties to ensure mechanical viability. The electronic structures are evaluated at both the GGA-PBE and HSE06 levels to obtain reliable band-gap trends, and orbital-resolved density of states, together with charge analyses, are used to clarify the bonding and charge-transfer mechanisms induced by asymmetric terminations. Because Janus polarity is expected to play a functional role in solar devices, surface work functions are computed from planar and macroscopic electrostatic potentials, and the resulting potential difference and intrinsic electric fields are quantified. The optical response is then characterized by the complex dielectric function and absorption coefficient, and carrier effective masses and exciton binding energies are estimated to assess transport tendencies in the absorber material. Finally, photovoltaic performance is assessed within the modified Shockley–Queisser framework introduced by Henry, allowing us to analyze the four compounds in terms of J–V behavior, JSC, VOC, fill factor, and maximum conversion efficiency. The resulting structure, property, and performance establish halogen-functionalized Janus Y2C-based MXenes as promising materials for solar-cell applications. This study may also provide a promising pathway for the future synthesis and fabrication of Janus MXenes Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) derived from Y2CCl2 MXene.

Computational methodology

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory were carried out using the QUANTUM ESPRESSO (QE) suite28. The atomic structures of the parent MXene Y2CCl2 and the Janus MXenes Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI were optimized by relaxing both the in-plane lattice constant (a = b) and all internal atomic coordinates within the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) minimization scheme29. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was introduced along the crystallographic c-direction to suppress spurious interactions between periodically repeated monolayers. Electron–ion interactions were described using projector augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotentials30, while the exchange–correlation contribution was treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional31. The valence configurations employed for the constituent elements were Y: [Kr]5s2 4d1, C: [He]2s2 2p2, Cl: [Ne]3s2 3p5, F: [He]2s2 2p5, Br: [Ar]3d10 4s2 4p5, and I: [Kr]4d10 5s2 5p5. The electronic wave functions were expanded in a plane-wave basis with a kinetic-energy cutoff of 80 Ry. The self-consistent electronic minimization was converged to a total-energy tolerance of 10− 8 a.u., and structural relaxations were continued until the maximum residual Hellmann–Feynman force on each atom was below 10− 3 a.u. Brillouin-zone integrations for both structural optimization and self-consistent-field (SCF) calculations were performed using an 8 × 8 × 1 Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh.

Phonon dispersion relations of the Janus compounds were computed within the harmonic approximation using the linear-response formalism in the framework of density functional perturbation theory32. In this approach, the phonon dispersion curve is obtained from the diagonalization of the dynamical matrix D(q) sampled at representative wave vectors q. The mechanical response and corresponding elastic constants were determined using the energy–strain method, with the elastic tensors and derived quantities computed using the Thermo_pw.x post-processing package implemented in QE. Electronic band structures, total and orbital-projected densities of states, and Mulliken bond-population analyses were evaluated along the high-symmetry path K→Γ→M→K, where each high-symmetry segment was sampled using 80 densely distributed k-points. Band gaps were determined using both the PBE functional and the screened hybrid Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) functional to mitigate the well-known underestimation of semilocal DFT gaps. Owing to the broken inversion symmetry of the Janus Y2CClX structures, dipole corrections were included along the out-of-plane direction using the dipole-field formalism as implemented in Quantum ESPRESSO. This treatment suppresses artificial slab-slab interactions and enables reliable evaluation of the intrinsic electrostatic potential and built-in electric field. Charge-transfer characteristics were further quantified by Bader charge partitioning using the Bader Charge Analysis program developed by the Henkelman group33. The work functions of the studied systems have been calculated by considering dipole correction in SCF calculations and using the post-processing code pp.x. The dipole correction was applied along the direction perpendicular to the monolayer (z-axis) within the unit cell geometry to properly account for the potential shift caused by the asymmetric slab. Optical properties were obtained from the epsilon.x post-processing module of QE. The frequency-dependent complex dielectric function, including its real part ε1(ω) and imaginary part ε2(ω), as well as the absorption coefficient α(ω), was evaluated using an optical broadening of 0.15 eV. Although the present optical calculations were performed within the independent-particle approximation, a more rigorous description of excitonic effects on the absorption spectra can be achieved through GW-BSE calculations. Such an investigation is beyond the scope of the present work and will be considered in future studies of Janus MXene-based photovoltaic materials. Finally, key photovoltaic metrics for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, including the total current density J, short-circuit current density JSC, open-circuit voltage VOC, power density, fill factor, and power conversion efficiency η, were estimated using the modified Shockley–Queisser framework introduced by C. H. Henry34. This formalism is widely used to approximate the maximum attainable photovoltaic efficiency and is known to provide values that closely align with experimentally reported limits across a range of semiconductor absorbers35.

Results and discussion

Crystal structure and stability of MXenes Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I)

The pristine Y2C monolayer has been reported to crystallize into a three-layer hexagonal MXene slab with space-group symmetry P63/mmc (No. 194), in which a single carbon layer is embedded between two yttrium layers24,36. In this parent lattice, the two yttrium sublayers are crystallographically inequivalent, and their in-plane fractional coordinates correspond to distinct Wyckoff positions, with the top Y(t) and bottom Y(b) sheets located at (0.667, 0.333) and (0.333, 0.667), respectively. Starting from this framework, surface termination can be introduced on the two outer faces of the Y–C–Y geometry. In the present study, a symmetric reference structure, Y2CCl2, is obtained by substituting both surfaces with Cl, whereas the Janus variants Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI are constructed by replacing the bottom site of the two Cl-terminated surfaces with an alternative halogen species (X = F, Br, and I). This asymmetric functionalization removes inversion symmetry of the Y2CCl2 system with space-group symmetry P3m1 (No. 156) and produces polar compounds in which the two faces are chemically distinct, thereby enabling an intrinsic surface-dependent electrostatic response that is relevant to photogenerated charge separation.

Figure 1 displays the side views of the optimized Y2CCl2 monolayer and the corresponding Janus systems. In all cases, the relaxed geometries preserve the characteristic three-layer arrangement and maintain structural integrity upon halogen termination, with the functional groups occupying the outer adsorption sites on the two surfaces. The symmetric Y2CCl2 monolayer carries identical Cl terminations on both faces, whereas Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI exhibit dissimilar terminations on opposite surfaces, which establishes an atomically resolved polarity across the slab. This surface asymmetry provides an early indication of the built-in electric field, which, in turn, may promote charge-carrier generation and enhance PV efficiency. The optimized in-plane lattice constants (a = b) are found to be approximately 3.707 Å for Y2CCl2, which is consistent with prior theoretical reports. For the Janus structures, the lattice parameters are 3.638 Å for Y2CClF, 3.726 Å for Y2CClBr, and 3.782 Å for Y2CClI.

Elastic properties of MXenes Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I)

To examine the mechanical stability of Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, the in-plane elastic stiffness tensor components Cij (where i, j ∈ [1,…,6] within the Voigt notation were computed. Since the studied systems are two-dimensional monolayers with hexagonal symmetry, the in-plane independent elastic constants reduce to C11(= C22) and C12. Therefore, three in-plane stiffness components, namely C11(= C22), C12, and the shear component C66, were evaluated for each monolayer, where C66 = (C11 −C12)/2. The resulting stiffness constants are summarized in Table 1. For all four compounds, the elastic constants satisfy the Born–Huang stability criteria for a 2D hexagonal lattice22; specifically, (C11 −C12) > 0 and C66 > 0 are fulfilled in each case, corroborating that the relaxed ground-state structures of Y2CCl2 and the Janus derivatives Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) are mechanically stable. In addition to the stiffness constants, the derived in-plane elastic moduli, including the 2D bulk modulus B, Young’s modulus Y, shear modulus G, and Poisson’s ratio ν, are also listed in Table 1. Relative to graphene, the significantly smaller values of B, Y, and G indicate reduced in-plane rigidity and comparatively weaker interatomic bonding within the Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) layers. For photovoltaic operation, the reduced stiffness of the systems is not merely a mechanical characteristic; it may also benefit photogenerated-carrier utilization in Janus systems. In particular, moderate elastic moduli facilitate larger lattice polarizability and make the lattice more responsive to internal electrostatic asymmetry, which can enhance the built-in polarization associated with Janus functionalization. Such polarization-driven fields promote spatial separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, thereby suppressing recombination and improving carrier transport efficiency. Poisson’s ratio further provides insight into the deformation character of the monolayers. Y2CClI exhibits ν = 0.26, indicative of a more ductile response, whereas Y2CCl2 (ν = 0.24) and Y2CClF (ν = 0.20) remain higher than graphene (ν = 0.18), and Y2CClBr lies near the brittle–ductile threshold (ν = 0.25). While enhanced ductility is advantageous because mechanically compliant absorber layers better accommodate strain during processing and operation, comparatively brittle behavior does not necessarily preclude photovoltaic applicability, particularly when charge-transport efficiency, optical absorption, and carrier lifetime are the dominant determinants of device performance.

Electronic properties and charge transfer mechanism of MXenes Y2CCl2 and the Janus Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I)

A reliable evaluation of the photovoltaic potential of an emerging two-dimensional material requires an accurate description of its electronic band structure, because the nature and magnitude of the band gap directly determine the absorption onset, achievable photovoltage, and power-conversion efficiency. To obtain the band gap (Eg), the electronic band dispersions of Y2CCl2 and the Janus MXenes Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) were computed along the high-symmetry path K→Γ→M→K using both PBE hybrid HSE06 functionals. The band structure plots, as obtained from PBE functional, are depicted in Fig. S1 (Supplementary Information), while the main text focuses on the HSE06 orbital-resolved band structures and the associated projected density of states shown in Fig. 3. For all four monolayers, the calculated dispersions exhibit clear semiconducting behavior, and the resulting band structures reveal an indirect energy gap. In particular, the valence-band maximum is located at the Γ point, whereas the conduction-band minimum lies in the vicinity of the M point, as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 3. The PBE band gaps are approximately 0.91, 0.67, 0.87, and 0.49 eV for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively. The corresponding HSE06 band gaps are 1.63, 1.36, 1.57, and 1.21 eV, respectively. Thus, the HSE06 results retain the same indirect-gap character but yield larger gap values than PBE. The band gaps obtained from the HSE06 functional provide the most reliable basis for correlating the electronic, optical, and photovoltaic performance parameters. Interestingly, the Eg values obtained from HSE06 for the present monolayers fall within the window for a single-junction solar layer (approximately 1.0–2.5 eV)38, indicating that halogen-functionalized Y2C-based Janus MXenes are intrinsically well positioned as photovoltaic candidates. Notably, Y2CClF exhibits Eg of (1.36 eV), which is close to the optimum value (~1.34 eV) associated with the Shockley–Queisser detailed-balance limit35,39, suggesting a favorable balance between achievable photovoltage and photocurrent. In comparison, Y2CClI yields the smallest Eg of (1.21 eV), implying an earlier absorption onset that can benefit current generation, while Y2CCl2 (1.63 eV) and Y2CClBr (1.53 eV) are expected to support comparatively higher photovoltages due to their wider gaps.

Even when the identified gap is indirect, many semiconductors have a nearby direct transition (often only a few tenths of an eV higher). Sunlight photons can then be absorbed efficiently via direct transitions, while phonon-assisted processes enable absorption at the indirect edge. Therefore, this feature does not necessarily limit photovoltaic utility for Janus systems, particularly when strong optical absorption combined with intrinsic polarity (built-in electrostatic asymmetry), and favorable carrier transport cooperate to facilitate efficient photocarrier generation, spatial separation, and suppressed recombination. Furthermore, there have been several published reports of indirect-gap semiconductors, including Mg3NSb40, ZrIX (X = Br, Cl)41, Sn2SSe and SnGeS238, selected phosphides (PdP2, CdP4, MgP4, and BaP3)42, and chalcogenides such as TiS3 and Ga2Te542, have also been identified as promising photovoltaic candidates.

While the electronic band dispersions in Fig. 3 establish that Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) are indirect-gap semiconductors, further interpretation is needed to identify the atomic-orbital contributions that build the band edges and clarify how asymmetric termination redistributes electronic charge. Therefore, the orbital-resolved projected density of states (PDOS) has been calculated using the HSE06 functional, as shown in Fig. 3, and employing this analysis with orbital-resolved carrier charge densities in (Table 2) as well as real-space charge-transfer obtained from Bader charge and Mulliken analyses, as shown in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. Figure 3 reveals that the electronic states in the vicinity of the Fermi energy level are dominated by the orbital contribution of the Y and C atoms. In all four compounds, the valence-band region (below EF) is constituted primarily by the 2p orbital states of C, whereas the conduction-band region (above EF) is largely comprised of 3d orbital states arising from both the bottom [Y(b)] and top [Y(t)] layers of Y atom. This PDOS analysis further provides a consistent orbital-level interpretation of the electronic band gaps identified in Fig. 3: the VBM originates mainly from C-2p states, while the CBM is formed primarily by Y-3d states, so that the fundamental excitation corresponds predominantly to a C-2p→Y-3d orbital transition. In addition, the terminating halogens contribute a secondary p-orbital state in the valence region, which modulates the band-edge energies through hybridization with the C-2p states and, thereby, tunes the band gap across the Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) Janus MXenes. Therefore, this study indicates a direct correspondence between the electronic band structure and orbital-resolved projected density of states (PDOS) and dictates the underlying reason for the smaller Eg values of the respective Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) Janus MXenes.

The charge-transfer mechanism accompanying surface functionalization was investigated using Bader charge analysis, and the resulting charge transfer ∆Q is reported in Table 3. Here, ∆Q > 0 indicates net electron depletion (donation) and ∆Q < 0 indicates net electron accumulation (acceptance). Two features are particularly significant. First, the symmetric reference Y2CCl2 exhibits identical charge depletion on the two Y layers, with Y(b) = Y(t) = 1.83e, reflecting the equivalence of the two surfaces under identical Cl termination. In contrast, all Janus compounds exhibit a measurable asymmetric charge distribution between Y(b) and Y(t), confirming that the two metal layers are electronically inequivalent and, therefore, introduce a polarity across the compounds. Second, the dominant acceptor across all compounds is carbon, which consistently accumulates approximately (− 2.18e to − 2.20e). In addition to carbon, the surface terminations also accumulate charge, following the magnitude of electronegativity-driven trend: F exhibits the most negative charge (− 0.84e), followed by Cl (− 0.73e to − 0.75e), then Br (− 0.70e), and I (− 0.65e). This systematic ordering substantiates the role of surface electronegativity in shaping the internal charge redistribution and the built-in electrostatic gradient, which are expected to influence carrier separation and the surface-dependent work function, as discussed in a later section.

Furthermore, to understand the specific electron-transfer pathways, orbital-resolved populations and Mulliken charges were analyzed, as presented in Table 4. The orbital populations show that Y carries substantial d-electron weight, whereas carbon exhibits a pronounced p-electron character (C-2p populations near 3.59–3.60), consistent with the C-2p dominance in the valence region. The halogen terminations similarly display large p-electron populations, reflecting their role as electron acceptors and their hybridization with the C-2p near the valence manifold. The Mulliken charges reproduce the same qualitative donor–acceptor concept as the Bader analysis: Y atoms remain positively charged, carbon is the primary acceptor (about − 0.94e to − 0.95e), and the surface terminations carry negative charge whose magnitude depends on the terminating species (e.g., F is more negative than Cl, while I is less negative). Although the absolute magnitudes differ between the Mulliken and Bader schemes due to their distinct partitioning of electron density, the consistent trends across Tables 3 and 4 provide a quantitative picture in which electrons are transferred mainly from Y-derived states to C-2p states and, secondarily, to the halogen p orbitals. In Janus systems, the charge differences between Y(b) and Y(t) further imply an internal dipole moment across the slab, a favorable property for photovoltaic absorbers because it can assist carrier separation and reduce recombination losses. Therefore, the PDOS-derived orbital character, the enhanced carrier charge densities, and the consistent charge-transfer direction collectively support the photovoltaic candidacy of Y2CCl2 and Y2CClX.

Work function and electric field of MXenes Y2CCl2 and the Janus compounds Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I)

Janus functionalization breaks inversion symmetry in Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I), thereby rendering the surfaces electrostatically inequivalent. Consequently, these systems exhibit surface-dependent work functions and a finite out-of-plane potential difference across the monolayer. These quantities are directly relevant for photovoltaic operation46: A reduced work function on the surface termination site can lower the electron-extraction potential at the termination surface and thus favor larger short-circuit current density (JSC), whereas a nonzero potential difference provides an intrinsic electric field that can aid spatial separation of photogenerated carriers and suppress carrier recombination.

Additionally, the magnitude of Φ of the studied compounds is seen to be comparably smaller than other 2D materials that have been widely discussed for photovoltaic activity, such as ZrICl, ZrIBr, GaS, C2N, HfS2, and CH3NH3PbI341,47–49. Lower values of Φ facilitate easier electron extraction, which, in turn, is beneficial for photocurrent generation in photovoltaic devices.

Consequently, pronounced peaks in ε1(ω), together with the absorption features discussed in the following subsection, provide a consistent optical basis for the photovoltaic promise of the studied Janus MXenes.

The exciton binding energy (Eb) is a critical parameter regulating charge transport in optoelectronic materials, as it influences the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs. Generally, the exciton binding energy can be obtained rigorously through the GW–BSE approach54. However, GW–BSE calculations are computationally expensive due to stringent k-mesh convergence requirements. As an efficient and computationally affordable alternative, we employ the 2D screened hydrogen model for Mott–Wannier excitons (MWE) to estimate Eb55. It should be noted that this model treats the exciton within an effective-mass picture, where the electron and hole are assumed to occupy parabolic band edges and interact through a screened Coulomb potential parameterized by the reduced mass and the 2D dielectric response. Therefore, it does not explicitly include the full quasiparticle self-energy correction, the detailed momentum-dependent electron-hole kernel, local-field effects, or the band-to-band coupling that are treated more rigorously within the GW–BSE framework. Nonetheless, Mott–Wannier excitons (MWE) have been demonstrated to yield exciton binding energies in good agreement with GW–BSE results and even experimental measurements for comparable materials. For instance, MWE-calculated exciton binding energies for MAPbBr3 (27.33 meV), FAPbBr3 (22.06 meV), and CsPbBr3 (44.24 meV) closely match their respective experimental values (21 meV, 24 meV, and 40 meV), confirming the validity of this approach. Therefore, the MWE model provides an effective and practical method to estimate Eb for the present Janus MXenes, while acknowledging that the absolute values may differ slightly from those of a fully converged GW–BSE calculation. JSC and VOC are the two primary quantities that determine whether a high conversion efficiency can be achieved. The short-circuit current density JSC reflects how effectively the absorber converts the incident photon flux into extractable charge carriers and is therefore governed by the absorption onset and the strength of optical absorption across the solar spectrum. In contrast, VOC measures the maximum photovoltage that can be sustained when no net current is drawn and is controlled by the balance between carrier generation and recombination. Consequently, a larger JSC increases the available output current, whereas a larger VOC increases the available output voltage, and the maximum obtainable electrical power (and thus the efficiency) depends on their combined optimization. However, these two metrics are intrinsically coupled: decreasing Eg increases the photon flux above the gap and tends to raise JSC, but it also increases radiative recombination of charge carriers, thereby reducing the attainable VOC. This current-voltage trade-off is a central constraint in radiative-limit photovoltaics and explains why the highest-current absorber is not necessarily the highest-efficiency absorber.

The short-circuit current density JSC is evaluated from the J–V curve at V = 0. From Fig. 9(a), JSC is found to be ~ 200, 285, 218, and 304 Am− 2 for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively. The resulting trend is JSC(Y2CClI) > JSC(Y2CClF) > JSC(Y2CClBr) > JSC(Y2CCl2), while the open-circuit voltages follow the opposite ordering, VOC(Y2CClI) < VOC(Y2CClF) < VOC(Y2CClBr) < VOC(Y2CCl2). This inverse relationship is an intrinsic feature of photovoltaics: a larger JSC reflects a larger portion of the solar spectrum being absorbed, while the enhancement of radiative recombination losses reduces the attainable VOC and the fill factor. However, these predicted efficiencies of (23.01 -25.66%) should be interpreted cautiously. They represent upper-bound values under the assumptions of step-function absorption, purely radiative recombination, ideal carrier collection, and AM1.5G illumination. In experimentally fabricated 2D photovoltaic devices, several additional factors may reduce the realized efficiency, including finite optical absorption in atomically thin layers, exciton dissociation barriers, non-radiative recombination at defects and interfaces, contact resistance, band alignment with electrodes, environmental stability, and possible structural disorder during Janus functionalization. The synthesis of chemically asymmetric Janus MXenes may also introduce fabrication challenges, such as incomplete or mixed surface termination, which could weaken the built-in electrostatic asymmetry. Therefore, the values reported here should be viewed as comparative indicators of intrinsic photovoltaic promise rather than guaranteed device efficiencies. Within this framework, Y2CClF appears to be the most favorable member of the series, but experimental validation and device-level modeling will be needed to assess its practical performance.

Conclusions

We have performed a first-principles investigation of the symmetric halide-terminated MXene Y2CCl2 and its Janus derivatives Y2CClX (X = F, Br, I) as potential ultrathin photovoltaic absorbers. Structural relaxation shows that halogen functionalization preserves the Y–C–Y layered framework. The negative cohesive energies (-5.45 to -6.20 ~ eV/atom) and formation energies (-1.33 to -1.93 ~ eV/atom) of Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI indicate energetic favorability with respect to the chosen reference states. The absence of imaginary phonon modes and the satisfaction of the Born–Huang elastic stability criteria further confirm their dynamical and mechanical stability. Electronic-structure calculations reveal that all four monolayers are indirect-gap semiconductors, with the valence-band maximum at and the conduction-band minimum near M. The HSE06 band gaps of 1.63, 1.36, 1.57, and 1.21 ~ eV for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively, fall within a favorable range for single-junction solar absorption. Orbital-resolved analysis shows that the band edges are mainly composed of C-2p states at the Valence Band Maximum and Y-3d states at the Conduction Band Maximum. Bader, Mulliken, and ELF analyses consistently indicate charge transfer from Y toward C and the halogen terminations. In the Janus systems, this charge redistribution becomes asymmetric across the slab, producing polarity-driven work-function differences of 3.09, 1.63, and 2.10 ~ eV for Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively. Such built-in electrostatic asymmetry may assist photocarrier separation. The optical calculations show strong visible-light absorption on the order of 105 cm− 1, with ultraviolet absorption maxima approaching (7.5–9.6)×105 cm− 1. Carrier-transport analysis indicates that electron effective masses are smaller than hole effective masses, suggesting comparatively higher electron mobility. Exciton binding energies estimated using the screened 2D Mott–Wannier model are 1.09, 1.12, 1.02, and 0.91 ~ eV for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively, implying that exciton dissociation should be considered carefully in practical devices. Within the modified Shockley–Queisser radiative-limit framework, the predicted maximum efficiencies are 23.10\%, 25.66\%, 23.92\%, and 23.01\% for Y2CCl2, Y2CClF, Y2CClBr, and Y2CClI, respectively. These values should be interpreted as ideal upper-bound estimates, since the model assumes step-function absorption, AM1.5G illumination, and neglects non-radiative losses, contact effects, and fabrication-related disorder. Among the studied systems, Y2CClF emerges as the most promising candidate because its near-optimal HSE06 band gap of 1.36 ~ eV provides the best balance between photocurrent and photovoltage. Overall, the combination of stability, strong optical absorption, intrinsic polarity, and favorable radiative-limit efficiency suggests that halogen-functionalized Y2C-based Janus MXenes merit further investigation for next-generation 2D photovoltaic devices.
