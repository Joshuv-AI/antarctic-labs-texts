Data collection on rare bone and mineral conditions in Europe: The landscape of registries and databases
Authors: Ana Luisa Priego Zurita, Corinna Grasemann, Manila Boarini, Roland Chapurlat, Marina Mordenti, ERN BOND Working Group 5, Muhammad Kassim Javaid, Natasha M Appelman-Dijkstra
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Background

Rare diseases, as defined by the European Union (EU), are those affecting no more than one person in 2000. In the EU about 36 million people are affected by a rare disease (Comissiona).

Health care for rare diseases is highly variable due to a lack of expertise and evidence-based practice. Long-term outcomes are, in most cases, unclear. Research on the natural history of these conditions is necessary to fill in knowledge gaps and improve outcomes (Groft et al., 2021).

In 2016, 24 European Reference Networks (ERNs) were created, the European Reference Network for Rare Bone Diseases (ERN BOND) (https://ernbond.eu/) among them. (Héon-Klin, 2017). These networks were founded to improve the care of patients living with a rare disease in Europe. At the time the survey was conducted, ERN BOND included 38 highly specialized health care providers (HCPs) from 10 EU Member States and 18 affiliated centres from 11 EU Member States in combination with designated European Patient Advocacy Groups (ePAGs). Within the network structure, Working Group 5 (WG5) of ERN BOND aimed to develop the specification for an ERN supporting registry to advance the understanding of the natural history of rare bone and mineral conditions.

Registries and databases can facilitate pooling of data, expert collaboration and patient and health care provider interaction (EUCERD Core Recommendations on Rare, 2015, 2016). Therefore, disease registries may play a key role in covering unmet needs in the rare disease community (Javaid et al., 2021).

To avoid the development of new registries for conditions covered by existing ones and to map the use of (inter)national registries/databases, a survey was designed to identify bone and mineral diseases registries in Europe and their characteristics.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted via an electronic survey tool (SurveyMonkey®) targeting HCPs and patient groups (PGs) of ERN BOND as well as other European experts in the field. The content and the methodology for distribution were designed by the members of ERN BOND WG5. ERN BOND HCPs members were invited via electronic mail by the ERN BOND coordinating centre to complete the web-based survey and asked to support the dissemination among other health professionals. Patient groups were invited to contribute to the survey as well.

The survey link was published on the ERN BOND website and shared on social channels for public dissemination. The survey was open from August 8th to September 30th, 2019. Although this survey was designed and targeted primarily at members of ERN BOND, it intended to cover the geography of Europe.

In the survey, the terms registry and database were used interchangeably. In the context of this study, we defined registry as the act of registering data on a population characterized by a rare condition or disease. This definition covered different geographical and organizational scopes and means of collecting data. Hereafter, we use the term registry.

Survey and data collection

The survey was developed and disseminated in English. It had an anonymous design and consisted of 73 items: (i) terms and conditions (1 item), (ii) general information about the respondent including professional background (6 items), (iii) existing resources (8 items), (iv) the respondent research database (6 items), (v) assessments (21 items), (vi) data collection (18 items), (vii) data entry challenges (9 items using a Likert scale from 0 = not possible to 10 = easy achievable) and 1 open-ended question) and (viii) questionnaire feedback (3 items). The items were presented as closed-ended with defined response categories, structured multiple-choice questions with the option "other" containing an open-ended free-text field, and a Likert scale. The SurveyMonkey® platform was used for conducting this survey. A full copy of the survey is available as Additional file 1. When respondents participated in more than one registry, we asked them to complete one survey per registry. Furthermore, registries provided by the participants were cross-checked in recognized registries and databases such as the Rare Disease(RD)-connect Registry and Biobank Finder (http://catalogue.rd-connect.eu/), Orphanet (https://www.orpha.net) (Orphanet), the European Directory of Registries (https://eu-rd-platform.jrc.ec.europa.eu/erdridor/) (Comissionb) and ClinicalTrials.gov (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/) (ClinicalTrials.gov database). In addition, when these registries were not detected in any of these platforms a web search was conducted.

Responses were excluded from the analysis on the following criteria: (a) duplicated response and (b) substantially incomplete questionnaire.

Since this was a non-interventional survey, no ethical approval was required. However, respondents were informed that by responding to the questionnaire, they consented to participate in the study. The survey was conducted anonymously; therefore, no personal data were collected.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis of data was performed using absolute frequencies and percentage values. Survey responses for the Likert Scale were combined and grouped as: 0–1 extremely not possible, 2–3 not possible, 4–6 neutral, 7–8 achievable and 9–10 extremely achievable. Data analyses were performed in Microsoft™ Excel™ 2019 (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

After excluding ineligible responses, a total of 70 responses were collected (Fig. 1). Table 1, Table 2 detail the characteristics of the respondents. Sixty-three (90%) out of 70 responses came from HCPs, with Germany accounting for the highest proportion (29%), followed by the United Kingdom (UK) (20%) and Italy (17%). We identified 39 HCPs and 6 PGs from 13 European countries. Fifty-six per cent (25/45) of the reported affiliations were ERN BOND HCP members; 5 PGs reported collaborating with the network. Furthermore, 72% (45/63) of the HCP responses were delivered by 25 network members, with a response rate for ERN BOND members of 52% (25/38 centres).

Registries’ characteristics are listed in Table 4. Of these, 21 were reported as ongoing data collection registries, 4 were contact-only registries and 2 collected other participant details. Intellectual property was owned by the HCPs in 14 registries while governance of 14 registries was done by the institution holding the registry. Many registries lacked economic support; when funded, sources included institution, research, researcher, clinician, industry, societies, university and public funding. Different levels of patient involvement were reported, with 24% of registries lacking of patient involvement. However, 5 respondents reported the registry they used having a patient advisory group, 3 reported co-development of the registry with patients and 2 declared that the registry was being governed by a patient organization. Data collection and entry were often carried out by more than one person in a HCP. Clinicians collected the data the most (62%), followed by researchers (37%) administrators (29%) and patients/caregivers (8%). The data entry in the registry was done by clinicians in 54% of the cases, followed by researchers in 35%, administrators in 37% and patients/caregivers in 8%.

In the reported registries, the ORDO (Orphanet Rare Disease Ontology) (Orphanet) was the most frequently used coding system (27%), followed by the ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth revision) (Manchikanti et al., 2013) in 22%, HPO (Human Phenotype Ontology) (Köhler et al., 2021) in 19%, OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man) (Amberger et al., 2015) in 14% and the Nosology and Classification of Genetic Skeletal Disorders (Bonafe et al., 2015) in 5% of registries.

Variation

The HGVS (Human Genome Variation Society) nomenclature, ICPED (International Classification of Paediatric Diagnosis) and the ESPE classification (ESPE Classification of Paediatric Endocrine, 2007) were used each in 3% of the registries. All registries used by ERN BOND members reported using the ORDO classification. Multiple classifications were used per registry.

HCPs were asked about the frequency at which the patients were seen at their centres. Fifty percent (32/63) declared seeing them at least every 12 months, 33% (21/63) at least every six months and 17% (11/63) only when needed.

Disease coverage

Of the different diseases, osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) was the most commonly registered bone condition in 31/37 (84%) of the registries, followed by fibrous dysplasia/McCune-Albright syndrome (FD/MAS) in 26 out of 37 registries (68%), X-linked hypophosphataemia (XLH) in 23 out of 37 (62%) registries, fibrodysplasia ossificans progressive (FOP) and achondroplasia in 19 registries (51%), osteopetrosis in 16 registries (43%) and mineral disorders in 13%. Furthermore, 82% reported registering additional rare metabolic conditions or skeletal dysplasias (Fig. 2).

Challenges of using a registry

Having high-speed internet to access an online research database was not a challenge for 76% of the respondents, consenting participants for 62% and entry of all eligible participants for 44%. Getting national and local regulatory approvals were identified as challenges by 58%, completing a 10-min assessment per entry by 66% and completing a 20-min assessment per entry by 88%.

Discussion

This survey shows that using a disease registry is a common practice among HCPs in Europe, especially ERN BOND members, since most do it. Whilst national disease-specific registries are available for Ehlers-Danlos, multiple osteochondromas, Ollier disease, Maffucci syndrome and OI, there is a gap in coverage of conditions, especially at an international level where only registries for FOP, hypophosphatasia and osteopetrosis were reported and only one registry covering the wider group of rare diseases. Registries covering wider groups of conditions were mostly national registries. Since the survey took place, additional registries might have been installed. An updated query in the various database repositories only added the industry-sponsored international registry for XLH.

Two-thirds of the HCPs reported seeing the patients not more often than once a year. Registries aiming at collecting longitudinal data should take this into account during the planning phase. More frequent data entry should be patient-reported to avoid significant missing data.

Participation in disease registries is a widely spread practice. FAIR (Findable Accessible Interoperable and Reusable) principles gain importance since they might facilitate collaboration between registries while ensuring the high quality of the processes and collected data (Wilkinson et al., 2016). Participation in international registries is often considered to be a means for improving the quality of research, patient care and the external validity of registries for rare conditions (Kodra et al., 2018). The fact that this practice is already implemented in most centres and that most registries collect participant details in an ongoing manner might lead to the conclusion that the infrastructure and resources for such an activity are well established.

The European Platform on Rare Disease Registration (EU RD platform) recommends that rare disease registries in Europe also collect a standard set of 16 data elements referring to patients’ personal data, diagnosis, disease history, care pathway, information for research purposes and disability. Most registries collect at least half of these elements, however, this is insufficient to fulfil the purpose of increasing the interoperability of rare disease registries (European Platform on Rare Disease; Gainotti et al., 2018). ERNs promote the use of the ORDO classification to capture rare disease diagnoses. This survey shows that the ORDO classification is used by most of the reported registries, often in combination with another nomenclature. The FAIR data principles have been widely accepted as an IRDiRC-recognized resource (Wilkinson et al., 2016). These principles aim for registered data to be used as extensively and efficiently as possible.

As patient registries have emerged in the last years, patient organisations valuably contributed to this survey by pointing to international registries and local data collections. We only captured a small number of responses from PGs. A higher PG participation would have provided the authors with valuable information regarding the use of registries by this group. However, the information provided by HCPs suggested that in many registries, patients were not involved in developing or managing the registry.

According to the EU, patient representatives and organisations have a critical role as experts by experience and co-producers of knowledge in the functioning of rare disease ERNs (). This recommendation should also be applied to rare disease registries, which ideally should be governed by multi-stakeholder groups including patient organisations. Furthermore, a registry that allows direct data entry by patients should be considered in the planning of future registries as this will also tackle the long intervals between the regular clinical visits (Javaid et al., 2021). As for other challenges, asking for the completion of an extensive dataset will hold back data entries as respondents clearly reported that an increase from 10 to 20 min was a big challenge. Despite the challenges that obtaining national and institutional approvals might represent, having human and physical resources for using a registry and dedicating time for data entry seem to be the main obstacles. Datasets in registries must therefore, be relevant and kept to the minimum when possible, according to the objectives of the registry.

Efforts were undertaken to increase the geographical response; however, at the time of the survey distribution the number of countries with centres affiliated to ERN BOND was only 10. Despite promoting the survey via other channels like the ERN BOND website and social media, various countries are missing. Nevertheless, repetitive internet searches have not introduced overlooked registries.

This survey revealed a need for a European Registry covering a wider group of bone and mineral conditions with the capability of integrating HCPs, scientists, patients and patient groups. The European Registries for Rare Bone and Mineral Conditions (EuRR-Bone) (https://eurr-bone.com/) were therefore created in April 2020 and aim at maximising the opportunity for patients, health care professionals and researchers to use high-quality registries for conditions covered by ERN BOND. To maximise efficiency and optimise resources, EuRR-Bone shares a data collection platform with the European Registries for Rare Endocrine Conditions (EuRRECa) (Ali et al., 2020, 2021) and is open for all expert centres in and outside Europe. This allows participation of centres from countries such as the UK even after Brexit or from countries whose centres are not eligible for ERN membership.

Conclusion

This study mapped the use of existing registries and gaps in disease coverage highlighting the need for developing a common and high-quality platform for rare disease registration. The results of this survey led to the development a European Registry for Rare Bone and Mineral Conditions.
