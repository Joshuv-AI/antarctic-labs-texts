COVID-19 pandemic disruptions drive decreases in HPV prevalence and shift genotype distribution in Western China
Authors: Yulin Liu, Yu Xiang, Yaoping Liu, Huan Deng, Fengyu Zhang, Yongjie Liu, Xiaonan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis population-based study investigated longitudinal changes in the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV across the pre-pandemic (2017-2019), pandemic (2020-2022), and post-pandemic (2023-2024) periods in Western China.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of 129,585 women, stratified into three chronological cohorts: pre-pandemic (n=40,044), pandemic (n=43,717), and post-pandemic (n=45,824) cohorts. Cervicovaginal specimens were analyzed via real-time PCR-based genotyping to detect 16 high-risk and 7 low-risk genotypes. Age-specific prevalence patterns were evaluated across six demographic strata (<20 to ≥60 years). Age-standardized prevalence rates were calculated using 2020 Chinese census data as a reference population.ResultsThe total HPV prevalence decreased from 31.59% before the pandemic to 23.28% during the pandemic, stabilizing at 21.45% after the pandemic. Nonvaccine-targeted HR-HPV subtypes (HPV-52, HPV-58) maintained persistent dominance across all phases, with the prevalence of HPV-52 decreasing from 6.00% before the pandemic to 3.00% after the pandemic. Adolescents (<20 years) presented the lowest infection rates, whereas peak rates were reported for women aged 40-49 years.ConclusionsThe sustained reduction in total HPV prevalence is potentially associated with pandemic-related behavioral modifications and healthcare disruptions. The persistent circulation of nonvaccine-targeted subtypes highlights gaps in current prevention strategies. These findings emphasize the need for comprehensive surveillance integrating catch-up screening and genotype-specific vaccine development to address post-pandemic HPV transmission dynamics.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Genotype, Prevalence, COVID-19

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths in 2022 [1]. Notably, 94% of these fatalities occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). While global studies have extensively documented the dominance of HPV16/18 in cervical carcinogenesis, evidence from LMICs suggests that genotype heterogeneity is driven by socioeconomic and behavioral factors. However, longitudinal data on how pandemic containment policies reshape HPV transmission remain scarce, particularly in regions with fragmented healthcare systems. The disproportionate impact on younger demographics is reflected in the finding that cervical cancer accounts for 20% of maternal cancer-related orphanhood [2]. These epidemiological patterns underscore cervical cancer as a critical public health threat requiring urgent intervention.

In August 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer, outlining 2030 targets: 90% HPV vaccination coverage for girls by age 15; 70% screening coverage via high-performance tests for women at ages 35 and 45; and 90% treatment access for cervical neoplasia cases. The initiative aims to reduce the age-standardized prevalence to less than 4 cases per 100,000 women by the end of the century [3, 4]. Modeling studies conducted in China (2020–2021) corroborated that HPV vaccination coupled with DNA-based screening represents the optimal elimination pathway [5, 6].

Despite progress, China faces systemic barriers including: (1) suboptimal cervical screening coverage (< 30% nationally); (2) reliance on outdated cytology-based methods in population programs; (3) fragmented primary healthcare infrastructure; (4) geographical disparities in screening accessibility; and (5) the absence of national HPV vaccine subsidies. As of 2022, HPV vaccination rates remain critically low (< 1% for girls < 15 years), predominantly limited to self-paid adult recipients [7]. This coverage gap has been exacerbated by COVID-19-related service disruptions [8, 9]. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, HPV screening services were significantly affected by various restrictive measures implemented by the government to reduce virus transmission. At the end of 2019, China’s first domestically developed HPV vaccine (Cecolin™) was approved for market release by the National Medical Products Administration. This vaccine became the fourth HPV vaccine to enter the global market. Compared with the three previously available imported HPV vaccines, the domestic vaccine offers comparable protective efficacy, a simpler production process, and a lower cost. This development provides greater potential for including the HPV vaccine in China’s national immunization program [10].

Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) genotypes constitutes the principal etiological driver of cervical carcinogenesis. Among the 200 + identified HPV variants, 14 h-HPV types (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68) are classified as Group 1 carcinogens. HPV16/18 alone drive 70% of cervical cancers and 50% of high-grade precancers (CIN3+), whereas HPV6/11 cause 90% of genital warts [11]. Genotype distributions exhibit marked geographical heterogeneity [12–14]. Western populations demonstrate a predominance of HPV16/18/45/33/31, whereas Chinese women show distinct patterns influenced by regional socioeconomic factors [15–17]. In Western countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States, and Turkey, HPV16 is the most prevalent genotype causing cervical cancer, followed by HPV18, 45, 33, and 31. In China, the distribution of HPV infections varies among women from different regions and economic backgrounds [18]. A nationwide meta-analysis by Li et al. [19]. identified HPV16 (5.5%), 52 (4.2%), 58 (2.8%), 53 (2.1%), and 18 (1.8%) as the most prevalent HR-HPV types. The COVID-19 pandemic severely disrupted preventive services, with HPV screening participation declining by 40–60% during lockdown periods [8]. This crisis underscores the need for: (1) longitudinal surveillance of post-pandemic HPV prevalence trends; (2) age-stratified screening algorithms optimized for resource-limited settings; and (3) regionally tailored vaccines targeting locally prevalent HR-HPV variants.

This retrospective analysis evaluates the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic period, characterized by large-scale public health containment policies, on HPV prevalence trends and genotype distribution in Western China, providing critical insights.

Materials and methods

Study population and exclusion criteria

A total of 129,585 deidentified cervicovaginal specimens and associated demographic data were retrospectively obtained from the department of clinical laboratory medicine archives at Suining Central Hospital (Suining, Sichuan, China). These specimens were originally collected from female participants between January 2017 and December 2024: routine cervical cancer screening programs, pre-marital health examinations, or gynecological consultations for non-specific symptoms, and were categorized into three distinct phases based on chronological progression: the pre-pandemic phase (January 2017-December 2019), the pandemic phase (Jan 2020-Dec 2022), and the post-pandemic phase (January 2023-December 2024). The exclusion criterion was recent intravaginal medication use or douching (within 72 h prior to sampling). Sexual intercourse within 24 h prior to sampling. Active menstrual bleeding during examination. Prior to cervical treatment, the samples were incubated with acetic acid/iodine solutions.

Cervical sample collection and DNA extraction

Cervical samples were collected by inserting a cervical brush into the endocervical canal through a vaginal speculum, rotating it clockwise 3–5 times, and transferring the brush head into a preservation solution-containing tube after excess secretions were removed. Nucleic acid extraction was performed via a magnetic bead-based automated system (DR-4801-KZ, Chaozhou Kaipu Biochemical Co., Ltd.), where 300 µL of sample and 20 µL of proteinase K were loaded into the designated wells of a 96-well plate. The automated protocol included magnetic bead purification, with eluted DNA stored at − 20 ± 5 °C in nuclease-free tubes if not immediately analyzed.

HPV DNA genotype testing

HPV genotyping was conducted through real-time PCR amplification (SLAN 96 S system, Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology) using commercial reagents (Chaozhou Kaipu Biochemical) to detect 17 high-risk (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82) and 7 low-risk (6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 53, 81) genotypes under standardized cycling conditions: 95 °C for 10 min initial denaturation, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Quality control included parallel processing of commercial positive/negative controls (Guangzhou Bondsen Biological Technology). The infection prevalence was calculated as the percentage positivity across six age strata (< 20, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years).

Statistical analysis

The prevalence rates of HPV infection, both overall and type specific, were determined as the percentage of positive HPV cases among all tested samples. The results were imported into an Excel spreadsheet, filtered, and stratified by age groups (< 20 years, 20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and ≥ 60 years). Data analysis was performed via SPSS version 23, and the chi-square (χ²) test was employed to analyze differences in HPV infection rates among the different age groups. For all the statistical analyses, the p values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

During the pandemic, the overall HPV prevalence decreased to 23.28% (10,180/43,717) (Table 1), with single infections accounting for 73.54% (7,487/10,180). Multiple infection rates declined to 18.37% (1,870/10,180), 5.04% (513/10,180), 1.92% (195/10,180), 0.75% (76/10,180), 0.26% (26/10,180), and 0.13% (13/10,180) for the respective categories (Table 2.).

After the pandemic, the prevalence of HPV further decreased to 21.45% (9,829/43,717) (Table 1), with the prevalence of single infections increasing to 76.07% (7,477/9,829). Age-specific HPV prevalence

Age-stratified analysis revealed dynamic shifts (Table 3). Pre-pandemic, the highest HPV prevalence occurred in the 40-49-year age group (30–39, 20–29, 50–59, ≥ 60, ≤20 years, descending order). During the pandemic, the 40-49-year group retained peak prevalence, followed by the 30–39, 50–59, 20–29, ≥ 60, and ≤ 20 years groups. Post-pandemic, the 30-39-year group surpassed the other groups (40–49, 50–59, 20–29, ≥ 60, ≤20 years). Intergroup differences within each period were statistically significant (Table 3) (Chi-square (χ²) test, p < 0.05). A declining trend in HPV prevalence was observed in the < 20, 20–29, and 40-49-year groups across all periods, whereas the 30–39, 50–59, and > 60-year groups showed increasing trends.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly impacted global healthcare systems, including HPV screening and prevention programs. This study provides critical insights into the pandemic’s influence on HPV prevalence and genotype distribution in Suining, Western China, complementing existing research from Zhejiang [21] and other regions [22]. Our findings revealed a significant decline in overall HPV infection rates during and after the pandemic (31.59% pre-pandemic vs. 23.28% pandemic vs. 21.45% post-pandemic), aligning with trends observed in Beijing [16], where the prevalence of HPV decreased from 33.54% to 29.43% during the pandemic. However, regional variations in infection dynamics and genotype distributions highlight the complexity of HPV epidemiology under pandemic conditions.

The decrease in HPV prevalence during the pandemic may reflect reduced sexual activity, delayed healthcare-seeking behavior, and disruptions in screening services. Similar patterns have been reported globally, with cervical cancer screening rates decreasing by 40–60% in high- and low-income countries, respectively [23]. While lockdowns reduced HPV transmission, evidenced by declining incidence, SARS-CoV-2 infection may dysregulate host immunity [24]. Potential mechanisms include T-cell exhaustion and cytokine imbalances, which could theoretically impair HPV clearance. However, our data show no significant HPV rebound post-pandemic, suggesting behavioral factors, reduced sexual partnerships, improved hygiene, outweighed transient immunological perturbations. In Beijing [16], reduced outpatient visits due to stringent lockdowns likely contributed to lower detection rates. Conversely, our study revealed a gradual recovery in screening after the pandemic, yet the prevalence of HPV prevalence continued to decline, suggesting additional factors such as increased public health awareness or behavioral changes. Notably, the persistently low HPV18 prevalence in Suining (ranking sixth to seventh among HR-HPV types) contrasts with its global prominence in cervical carcinogenesis, underscoring regional genotype variability. This finding aligns with studies in mainland China, where HPV52 and HPV58 dominate [25, 26], unlike other countries where HPV16 and HPV18 prevail [27, 28]. Our data from Western China (HPV52/58 dominance) align with Kazakhstani studies reporting non-16/18 predominance in Central Asia. Bekmukhambetov et al. [29]identified HPV53/66 as prevalent genotypes in Western Kazakhstan, contrasting with our HPV52/58 profile. This heterogeneity underscores how socioeconomic factors drive regional genotype variations across LMICs, necessitating geographically tailored prevention strategies.

While the Beijing study reported increasing HPV prevalence among males during the pandemic (23.93% to 25.86%), our data focused on female populations but did not include a limited number of male samples. The higher male infection rates in Beijing may reflect increased clinic referrals for HPV testing among the sexual partners of HPV-positive females, emphasizing the role of males in HPV transmission. Age-specific trends in Suining revealed shifting peaks: the highest infection rate shifted from 40 to 49 years before the pandemic to 30–39 years after the pandemic. This contrasts with other regions, where the < 30, 31–40 age group consistently exhibited the highest prevalence [30, 31]. Such differences may arise from regional demographic variations, healthcare access, or sexual behavior patterns.

Persistent immune dysregulation in long COVID-19 could theoretically exacerbate HPV persistence [32]. However, an Italian cohort study [33] found no significant HPV progression risk elevation in long COVID-19 patients. In our cohort, declining HPV rates across all age groups-including high-risk 30-49y-suggest minimal clinical impact of such interactions in Western China.

HPV52, HPV58, and HPV16 remained the dominant HR-HPV genotypes in Suining across all phases, which is consistent with findings from Beijing and other Chinese regions [16–35]. The stability of these genotypes despite their declining overall prevalence highlights their persistent oncogenic threat. However, the low prevalence of HPV18, which is responsible for 10–15% of cervical cancers globally suggests that current vaccines (e.g., Cecolin™) targeting HPV16/18 may offer suboptimal protection in regions dominated by HPV52/58. This finding level of reinforces the need for regionally tailored vaccines, such as the 9-valent HPV vaccine, which includes HPV52/58. The declining LR-HPV prevalence (e.g., HPV53 and HPV81) further underscores the pandemic’s differential impact on HPV transmission dynamics.

The persistent dominance of non-vaccine-targeted genotypes (HPV52/58) highlights the therapeutic gap for existing cervical lesions. Emerging options like topical imiquimod show promise for high-grade CIN, offering non-surgical alternatives in resource-limited settings [36]. Integrating such therapies with genotype-specific screening could optimize post-pandemic management. The pandemic-induced decline in HPV prevalence should not overshadow the urgent need to strengthen screening and vaccination programs. China’s low level of HPV vaccination coverage (< 1% among girls under 15) and regional disparities in healthcare access exacerbate the risk of cervical cancer. Integrating HPV vaccination into national immunization programs, expanding screening via self-sampling kits, and leveraging digital health platforms could mitigate pandemic-related disruptions. Furthermore, public education campaigns addressing HPV transmission and prevention are critical to sustaining the behavioral changes observed during the pandemic.

Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the pandemic’s lasting effects on the prevalence of HPV-related cancer. Research should prioritize genotype-specific vaccine efficacy, male HPV epidemiology, and the impact of delayed screenings on precancerous lesion progression. Additionally, cost-effectiveness analyses of tailored vaccination strategies and decentralized screening models will inform policy-making in resource-limited settings.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and reliance on hospital-based data may introduce selection bias, as symptomatic individuals are overrepresented. Longitudinal follow-up is needed to assess the impact of delayed screening on the prevalence of cervical cancer. Second, the exclusion of general population data limits the extrapolation of findings to broader demographics. Third, the post-pandemic phase (2023–2024) overlaps with ongoing pandemic recovery, potentially confounding long-term trend analysis. Finally, the study did not include HPV samples from males, warranting future studies to explore sex disparities.

Finally, we propose leveraging the existing nucleic acid sampling network to conduct integrated screening, incorporating HPV testing into health management programs for COVID-19 survivors. A risk prediction algorithm should be developed on the basis of age, clinical symptoms, and HPV genotypes, with an AI-based optimization strategy for refining screening models to increase precision and efficiency.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic has reshaped HPV epidemiology in Western China, with a declining prevalence but persistent dominance of nonvaccine-targeted genotypes. These findings underscore the necessity of resilient, equitable prevention strategies to achieve global cervical cancer elimination goals. By addressing regional genotype profiles and pandemic-induced healthcare gaps, China can enhance HPV control in the post-COVID-19 era.
