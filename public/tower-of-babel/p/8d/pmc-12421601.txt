Safety and efficacy of personalised delayed anticoagulation for reducing perioperative blood loss in total knee arthroplasty patients: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Xuefeng Luo, Runxing Kang, Yinsong Sun, Mingfei Dong, Wei Huang, Junyi Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe management of bleeding and coagulation after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has long been recognised as a significant challenge for orthopaedic surgeons. Despite the notable success of empirical anticoagulation in preventing venous thromboembolism (VTE) following TKA, the increased risk of postoperative bleeding has also raised extensive concern. Ecchymosis, as one of the most common manifestations indicating postoperative bleeding, holds the potential to indicate the balance of bleeding and hypercoagulation. However, there is still a lack of evidence-based medical research to determine the importance of postoperative ecchymosis and related personalised anticoagulation therapy. Therefore, we have designed a randomised controlled trial aimed at assessing the safety and efficacy of personalised delayed anticoagulation strategies in the management of postoperative bleeding in TKA patients.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, randomised, controlled trial. Patients diagnosed with end-stage knee osteoarthritis will be grouped based on the presence of ecchymosis after TKA. Those without ecchymosis will receive standard anticoagulation therapy, while those with ecchymosis will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio into either the standard anticoagulation group or the delayed anticoagulation group. The primary outcomes will compare the blood routine examination, coagulogram, thromboelastography and the incidence of VTE. The secondary outcomes will include surgical-related complications. Additionally, patient baseline data and surgery-related data will also be recorded and analysed.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (2024-194-01). The results will be disseminated at international conferences and in peer-reviewed publications.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400084440.Keywords: Anticoagulation, Adult orthopaedics, Knee, Bleeding disorders & coagulopathies

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is widely recognised as one of the most effective treatments for end-stage osteoarthritis.1,4 With the high prevalence of osteoarthritis and an ageing global population, the demand for TKA is projected to rise significantly.5 Alongside this trend, there is a growing focus on managing postoperative complications such as venous thromboembolism (VTE), infections and chronic pain.6 VTE is particularly concerning due to its high incidence and severity, affecting 40%–60% of patients undergoing major orthopaedic surgeries.7 Alarmingly, up to 70% of VTE cases are asymptomatic, and approximately 6% of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and 12% of pulmonary embolism (PE) patients die within a month of diagnosis.8 Thus, preventing VTE is a critical priority in postoperative care. The implementation of Antithrombotic Therapy and Prevention of Thrombosis- 9th Edition (ACCP-9) anticoagulation guidelines and enhanced recovery after surgery protocols in orthopaedics has significantly reduced the incidence of VTE to 0.5%–1% in TKA patients.9,11

However, alongside the widespread and standardised use of anticoagulation therapy, the increasing postoperative bleeding complications, such as ecchymosis, wound bleeding and gastrointestinal haemorrhage etc,12,15 have also increased perioperative safety risks and economic burden for TKA patients. Furthermore, systemic coagulation dysfunction and major systemic bleeding would also significantly raise the risk of death in patients.16,18 Therefore, maintaining the balance between coagulation and bleeding remains a significant subject for clinicians.19 Several previous studies have proposed the concept of personalised anticoagulation for patients receiving TKA and found that some risk factors, such as body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, postsurgery serum albumin level and postsurgery serum haemoglobin etc, were associated with postoperative bleeding complications such as ecchymosis.20,22 Meanwhile, perioperative coagulation status evaluated by thromboelastography (TEG), anti-factor Xa activity (aFXa), etc was regarded as an effective method to predict postoperative bleeding events.19,2123 24 In addition, alternative anticoagulation strategies such as oral aspirin, combined with the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) etc, were identified as effective for VTE prevention and decreasing hidden blood loss (HBL) after TKA.25,28 However, there is still a lack of an easy and effective way to decrease perioperative blood loss and bleeding-associated complications without increasing the risk of VTE.

Among bleeding-related complications, perioperative ecchymosis around the surgical site has a high incidence rate of around 40%.20 29 30 Ecchymosis is defined as a subcutaneous haemorrhagic lesion larger than 3 mm, appearing as flat, round or irregular blue or purple patches.31 It represents bleeding into the third space beneath the skin and is a type of haematoma,19 often accompanied by severe swelling and pain, prolonging the recovery period after TKA and may even increase periprosthetic infection rates.32 Moreover, postoperative surgical site ecchymosis was a strong evidence of hypocoagulation status, which indicates the potential for prediction of postoperative blood loss and bleeding-associated complications.24 33 34 Growing evidence has shown that regular anticoagulation in patients with ecchymosis not only increases the involved area but also increases the risk of secondary postoperative bleeding.19 Therefore, the continued use of anticoagulants in patients with postoperative ecchymosis remains controversial.20

Based on our retrospective review data, we characterised that postoperative ecchymosis was closely associated with a hypocoagulation status, which continues within 48 hours.35 Therefore, personalised post-TKA anticoagulation therapy could be based on the appearance of ecchymosis. Therefore, we have designed a randomised controlled trial (RCT) to test the following hypotheses: (1) Patients with ecchymosis after TKA have relatively low coagulation function and a higher tendency for postoperative bleeding. (2) Implementing an ecchymosis-based personalised and delayed anticoagulation regimen in ecchymosis patients can effectively reduce perioperative blood loss without increasing the risk of VTE.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This prospective, randomised, double-blind RCT design adheres strictly to Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines.36 The study has received ethical approval from the local ethics committee and has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 16 May 2024 (trial registration number: ChiCTR2400084440) prior to enrolment. To ensure precision, all surgeries will be performed by the same surgical team, following standardised perioperative management protocols, including anaesthesia, pain management, medication, rehabilitation and nursing care.

Participants

From July 2024 to August 2026, patients diagnosed with end-stage osteoarthritis and scheduled for TKA at our medical centre will be screened based on the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria detailed in table 1. Eligible patients will be fully briefed on all potential benefits and risks before enrolment and will only be included in the research after providing informed consent (online supplemental file 1). If they experience any discomfort or adverse effects, patients can exit the trial and will be compensated appropriately. The perioperative examinations and tests for all patients on each day are available in online supplemental file 2.

Ecchymosis

Ecchymosis is defined as a haemorrhagic lesion on the lower limb skin, greater than 3 mm in diameter, forming a flat, round or irregular blue or purple bruise.31 Our team comprises experienced professors who have dedicated numerous years to surgical procedures and the management of postoperative complications. Commencing 12 hours postoperatively, and then every 12 hours thereafter, the researchers will meticulously evaluate and document the presence of ecchymosis in patients. The size of the ecchymosed area will be estimated by comparing it to the patient’s palm, which is roughly equivalent to 1% of the total body surface area. Furthermore, the skin condition of each patient will be photographed and recorded both preoperatively and postoperatively. In cases where ecchymosis is present, its size will be measured and compared at various postoperative time points to track its progression or resolution.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Following two observations of ecchymosis in patients postoperatively, specifically at the 24-hour mark, patients will be categorised based on the presence of ecchymosis for further intervention. Patients without ecchymosis will receive routine anticoagulation therapy. Participants with ecchymosis will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the routine anticoagulation group or the personalised delayed anticoagulation group (figure 1).

Randomisation sequence generation

An independent statistician, who is not involved in patient recruitment, intervention delivery or outcome assessment, will generate the randomisation sequence using SPSS V.24.0 software (IBM Corporation). The sequence will employ block randomisation with variable block sizes (2, 4 and 8) to ensure balanced group allocation.

Allocation concealment

The generated sequence will be securely stored in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Each envelope will contain the assigned group (routine or delayed anticoagulation) and will be managed by an independent research coordinator with no role in patient enrolment or clinical care. When a patient meets the inclusion criteria and is confirmed to have ecchymosis at 24 hours postoperatively, the research coordinator will open the next sequential envelope. The assigned intervention will be communicated to the treating clinicians (nurses and doctors administering LMWH) exclusively via a secure, password-protected electronic platform.

Blinding procedure

Treating clinicians (nurses and doctors administering LMWH) will receive instructions only through the electronic platform and will have no access to the randomisation sequence or envelope contents. After receiving the instruction, the doctor gives a prescription, and the nurse carries out the doctor’s orders by administering an injection to the patient. Patients, outcome assessors, data analysts and all other study personnel (except the independent statistician and research coordinator) will remain blinded to group assignments until the conclusion of the trial. To ensure blinding integrity, the LMWH administration protocol (routine vs delayed) will be labelled as ‘Group A’ or ‘Group B’ in the electronic system, with no explicit reference to the intervention type.

Data management

All data (including group assignments and clinical outcomes) will be collected by a blinded statistician, encrypted and stored in a restricted-access database. Any unblinding requests must be approved by the ethics committee and documented.

Intervention

Subcutaneous low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) therapy is initiated 24 hours following surgery at a dosage of 4000 IU, with subsequent administrations of 4000 IU every 24 hours thereafter. In patients with ecchymosis, those in the routine anticoagulation group will continue to receive LMWH at the same dose of 4000 IU. For those in the personalised delayed anticoagulation group, LMWH will be discontinued immediately on the appearance of ecchymosis and will be resumed on postoperative day 3 (POD3).

Surgical procedure

All TKA surgeries will be performed by the same surgical team. Patients will be positioned supine, with a pneumatic tourniquet applied to the proximal thigh of the affected leg. After anaesthesia is administered, an incision will be made along the anterior midline, accessing the knee through the quadriceps tendon and medial patella. The procedure involves removing hypertrophic osteophytes, synovium, menisci and the anterior cruciate ligament while preserving the posterior cruciate ligament. Following standard TKA protocols, the osteotomy will be completed and the appropriate knee prosthesis will be installed accordingly. Intraoperative X-ray fluoroscopy will be used to ensure proper prosthesis placement before gradually closing the wound. No drainage tubes will be placed postoperatively. All patients will receive continuous electrocardiographic monitoring and supportive and symptomatic treatment during surgery.

General perioperative management

(1) Anaesthesia protocol: all patients will receive the same anaesthetic regimen during TKA surgery, including midazolam and propofol for sedation, remifentanil for analgesia and vecuronium for muscle relaxation. (2) Pain management: postoperative pain management for all patients will involve a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia pump containing sufentanil (0.1 mg) and flurbiprofen axetil (100 mg). (3) Infection prevention: to prevent infection, all patients will receive 1.5 g of cefuroxime intravenously every 12 hours from POD1 to POD3. (4) Mechanical prophylaxis for VTE prevention: intermittent pneumatic compression will be administered to all postoperative patients, two times per day for 20 min per session. (5) TXA protocol: TXA, an antifibrinolytic agent, will be administered to all enrolled patients to reduce perioperative blood loss. Each patient will receive 1.5 g of intravenous TXA preoperatively, 1 g of local TXA at wound closure and 1.5 g of intravenous TXA every 12 hours postoperatively. (6) Nursing care: all enrolled patients will be managed by the same perioperative nursing team. This team will ensure the administration of the same type and dose of intravenous fluids postoperatively while closely monitoring and recording fluid intake and output within the first 24 hours.

Nutrition management

All patients will adhere to a nutrition plan devised by the same nutritionist to ensure adequate intake and support. On the morning of surgery, patients will consume a short peptide and whole protein enteral nutrition supplement, with their fluid intake and output meticulously recorded. Nutritional status, including albumin and prealbumin levels, will be closely monitored both preoperatively and postoperatively. If any signs of malnutrition are detected, the nutrition plan will be promptly adjusted to restore normal nutritional status.

Rehabilitation management

All enrolled patients will follow a rehabilitation plan devised by the same rehabilitation physician starting on the first postoperative day to ensure rapid adaptation to the knee prosthesis and early recovery. Initially, the rehabilitation physician will supervise and guide patients in performing exercises such as straight leg raises, knee flexion and knee extension while in bed to strengthen the muscles around the knee joint. If knee motion is limited, the physician will assist patients with bedside exercises to improve flexion and extension, ensuring an optimal range of motion early on. Additionally, the physician will help patients with ambulation, protecting them from falls while facilitating quicker adaptation to the knee prosthesis, promoting a swift return to normal activities.

Thrombosis monitoring

Before being enrolled in the study, all patients must undergo a preoperative colour Doppler ultrasound of the lower limb arteries and veins to rule out VTE. Postoperatively, patients are encouraged to engage in physical exercises to enhance VTE prevention. On POD3, each patient will undergo a follow-up colour Doppler ultrasound of the lower limbs to check for any short-term VTE occurrence. Additionally, if any patient exhibits symptoms or signs indicative of PE during the trial (such as cyanosis, chest pain or shortness of breath), a CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) will be promptly performed for definitive diagnosis and appropriate management.

Blood transfusion criteria

Our medical centre has established a stringent allogeneic blood transfusion (ABT) protocol. Under this policy, ABT is administered strictly when haemoglobin levels drop below 70 g/L. Furthermore, in cases of symptomatic anaemia—marked by palpitations, lowered arterial blood pressure and diminished urine output—ABT may be considered if haemoglobin levels range from 70 to 100 g/L.37

Serious adverse events (SAEs) are defined as any incidents during the trial that may lead to extended hospitalisation, cause significant disability, result in major congenital anomalies in the patient’s offspring, threaten the patient’s life or require immediate medical intervention to prevent more severe complications. Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was performed using PASS software (V.11; NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA). According to a previous study, the HBL for patients who developed ecchymosis following TKA was recorded as 349.39±118.49 mL, which is approximately 100 mL greater than the average blood loss observed in patients without ecchymosis.20 The aim of this study is to introduce a delayed anticoagulation protocol for patients susceptible to ecchymosis, with the goal of reducing postoperative blood loss and thereby decreasing the risk of ecchymosis. Therefore, we calculated that a minimum sample size of 42 patients per group was required to detect a 100 mL difference in HBL, assuming a power (1−β) of 0.95, a two-sided α of 0.05 and a 10% dropout rate.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study are blood routine examination, coagulogram, TEG and the incidence of VTE.

Blood routine examination will be used to measure HBL and haemoglobin drop (ΔHb) on POD1 and POD3. Additionally, any instances of blood transfusion will be meticulously recorded and monitored.

The coagulogram indicators are prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), international normalised ratio (INR), fibrinogen, fibrinogen degradation products (FDP) and D-dimer. PT and APTT are classic parameters representing the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways of coagulation, respectively. TT measures the conversion rate of fibrinogen to fibrin. INR, a standardised result of PT, eliminates interlaboratory differences and is used to monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulant therapy. Fibrinogen, an essential plasma coagulation protein, is closely monitored. FDP and D-dimer levels reflect fibrinolytic activity within the body.

TEG is a dynamic technique that evaluates whole blood coagulation, offering detailed insights into the entire process from clot initiation to fibrinolysis.40 TEG measures several key parameters: reaction time (R), which indicates the time until thrombin formation and reflects coagulation factor activity; clot formation time (K), measuring the time from the end of R until the clot reaches a certain strength, indicating fibrinogen and platelet function; alpha angle (α), representing the speed of clot formation; maximum amplitude (MA), reflecting the maximum strength of the clot and indicating platelet function and fibrinogen content; lysis at 30 min, showing the degree of fibrinolysis by measuring the percentage decrease in clot strength 30 min after reaching MA; and the coagulation index, a composite measure of overall coagulation status calculated from R, K, α angle and MA values. Together, these parameters provide a comprehensive assessment of the coagulation process.

VTE will be uniformly detected in the preoperative period and on POD3 using colour Doppler ultrasound of the lower limb arteries and veins, encompassing cases of PE and DVT.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes involve the surgically related complications in patients, including poor wound healing, wound infection, swelling around the incision, vascular injury, nerve damage, cardiopulmonary complications, joint stiffness and allergy to the prosthesis. These will also be closely monitored and accurately documented.

Other outcomes

Patient baseline data will include gender, age, height, weight, BMI, surgical side and medical history of hypertension, diabetes and coronary artery disease. Surgery-related data will include operative time, IBL, incision size, perioperative inflammatory markers (C reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and the use of ABT. Additionally, Visual Analogue Scale—the most used pain assessment tool in orthopaedics, Hospital for Special Surgery Score—the most used functional assessment in orthopaedics, and length of hospital stay will be meticulously recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V.23.0 software (IBM). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess the normality of the data distribution. Data with a normal distribution are presented as means and SD. For quantitative data that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate differences between groups. For non-normally distributed data or data with heterogeneous variances, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Qualitative data are expressed as frequencies and percentages, with group differences assessed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

Discussion

Researchers have continuously refined strategies for managing postoperative bleeding and coagulation. According to the ACCP-9 guidelines, anticoagulation should be cautiously adjusted or discontinued in high bleeding risk patients.41 Continuing empirical anticoagulation in this context not only worsens ecchymosis but also increases the risk of severe bleeding events. Our retrospective review data also support that postoperative hypocoagulation status recovers within 48 hours.35 Given these findings, personalised delayed anticoagulation emerges as the optimal approach for postoperative hypocoagulation patients, yet the lack of conclusive clinical evidence highlights the urgent need for further investigation. This RCT is strategically designed to bridge this evidence gap.

It is well known that coagulation primarily consists of three processes: the extrinsic pathway, the intrinsic pathway and the common pathway. These processes facilitate haemostasis by promoting the binding of thrombin and factor Xa to form blood clots after the body sustains an injury and bleeding occurs.42 However, when the body exhibits a hypercoagulable state due to various diseases, the risk of thrombosis significantly increases, necessitating anticoagulant therapy.43,45 LMWH is one of the most commonly used anticoagulants in clinical practice. It primarily works by binding to antithrombin, enhancing its inhibitory effect on coagulation factor Xa. This mechanism inhibits key steps in the coagulation cascade, thereby reducing thrombus formation.46 Consequently, aFXa has gradually become a new reference for determining whether to continue the use of anticoagulants. Wang et al observed that patients with ecchymosis exhibited higher aFXa.19 The use of LMWH did not mitigate the elevated aFXa in these patients; rather, it intensified the Xa inhibitory effect, thereby increasing the risk of bleeding. Therefore, delaying the initiation of anticoagulation therapy may be a rational and effective strategy for these patients.

This protocol details a forthcoming prospective RCT aimed at assessing the efficacy and safety of a tailored, delayed anticoagulation regimen in patients presenting with ecchymosis post TKA. This design encompasses notable strengths deserving of emphasis. First, the protocol’s emphasis on personalised, delayed anticoagulation strategies addresses a critical clinical gap, aligning therapeutic approaches with each patient’s unique bleeding and thrombotic risk profile. Second, the RCT format stands as the pinnacle for intervention efficacy assessment, furnishing a solid structure for bias reduction and ensuring that observed outcomes are attributable to the intervention rather than extraneous variables. Third, the comprehensive assessment of a spectrum of outcomes—encompassing VTE rates, blood loss, haemorrhagic events and coagulation indices—promises an exhaustive evaluation of the proposed delayed anticoagulation approach. At last, the incorporation of physical prophylactic measures, such as pneumatic compression therapy, introduces an extra tier of safety for patients at elevated bleeding risk, potentially diminishing the necessity for pharmacological thromboprophylaxis. However, despite the RCT’s inherent bias reduction capabilities, the potential for selection bias persists, particularly in the event of substantial participant withdrawal. Additionally, the outcomes are dependent on accurate observations and reporting of ecchymosis, which may vary from other hospitals. Nevertheless, this RCT is meticulously crafted and could significantly impact clinical practice by providing a safer and more effective strategy for managing postoperative bleeding risks in patients with ecchymosis following TKA.
