Shaking and withering intensity from oolong tea processing alters the chemical and sensory quality of tobacco
Authors: Keqin Li, Yukun Liu, Dong Wang, Wen He, Junfeng Chen, Xianhai Yang, Zhijun Xu, Linjian Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tobacco curing is decisive for final leaf quality, but conventional methods rely mainly on temperature and humidity regulation, resulting in flavor profiles that are overly dependent on raw leaf material and offer limited plasticity. To actively design aroma characteristics, this study introduces the shaking-withering principle from oolong tea processing, applying controlled mechanical and oxidative stress to steer the aroma-chemical transformation of tobacco leaves. We systematically compared this novel approach with conventional curing using Yunyan 87 leaves, focusing on how shaking and withering intensity drive chemical and sensory outcomes. Shaking-withering fundamentally remodeled the volatile profile by enriching key alcohols and terpenes, which promoted desirable caramelized and nutty aromas, improved chemical harmony, and reduced pigment content—collectively enhancing sensory quality. Processing intensity served as a critical control point, with shaking and withering differentially regulating distinct chemical pathways. The strong correlation between volatile composition and sensory indicators confirmed the high tunability of the process.In summary, this study establishes shaking-withering as a viable and innovative curing strategy that bridges a technological gap. It provides a new tool for actively designing and diversifying tobacco flavor through precise stress-based processing, offering a novel pathway for developing products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40349-1.

Introduction

Curing orchestrates the metabolic transformation of leaf components, ultimately defining tobacco quality1–3. Although intensive curing is central to industrial production, it often fails to meet growing market demands for diversified and refined flavor profiles4,5. This limitation has driven research into optimizing curing parameters6,7; however, fundamentally alternative processing paradigms remain largely unexplored.

We propose that moving beyond this flavor bottleneck may require insights from related agricultural processing fields. In tea production, deliberate biotic and abiotic stresses—such as black tea fermentation8,9, green tea fixation10, and oolong tea shaking-withering11,12—are employed to remodel aroma chemistry by modulating metabolic pathways. For example, the mechanical injury and oxidative stress induced during oolong tea shaking-withering efficiently convert glycosidically-bound precursors into potent floral and fruity volatiles13–15. Although morphological differences between tobacco and tea leaves prevent direct technique transfer, the core principle of applying controlled physical stress to direct aroma formation is broadly relevant16–18. Supporting this cross-domain feasibility, recent studies adapting black tea processing to tobacco through leaf fragmentation have successfully altered flavor characteristics19,20.

The sensory quality of tobacco stems from synergistic interactions among its chemical constituents21,22. Critical metrics include sugar ratios (e.g., reduced sugar/total sugar ~ 0.9) for sweetness and nitrogen/alkaloid balance (~ 1.0) for smoothness23. Importantly, the degradation of plastid pigments (chlorophylls and carotenoids) yields essential isoprenoid-derived aroma compounds24,25. Key volatiles include ketones (e.g., solanone) that define tobacco’s essence, sesquiterpenes (e.g., neophytadiene) that mitigate harshness, and heterocyclic compounds (e.g., pyrazines) that impart roasted notes26–29. Consequently, any novel processing method must precisely influence these underlying metabolic pathways and chemical synergies.

Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that applying the shaking-withering principle—a technique designed to induce controlled stress and redirect metabolism—to tobacco processing would remodel its volatile profile, reduce reliance on pigment-derived aromas, and ultimately generate a distinct and desirable sensory quality unattainable through conventional curing alone. To test this hypothesis, the present study aimed to: (1) systematically compare the chemical and sensory outcomes of shaking-withering against standard flue-curing using Yunyan 87 tobacco; (2) identify the key volatile metabolites, particularly alcohols and terpenes, responsible for the observed differences; and (3) elucidate how processing intensity governs the final quality attributes. This work validates a novel multidisciplinary approach and provides a framework for the targeted development of diversified tobacco flavors.

Materials and methods

Tobacco samples

Fresh tobacco leaves (cultivar Yunyan 87) were harvested in June 2023 from standardized flue-cured tobacco fields in Yong’an, Liuyang City, Hunan Province. Leaves were collected from the 8th–9th node positions of the middle stalk at optimal maturity. The experiment was conducted with three independent biological replicates. A total of 180 kg of fresh leaves were randomly divided into three equal batches (each 60 kg), with each batch constituting one biological replicate. For each replicate, leaves were further allocated into two processing groups: a control group (CK, 40 kg) that underwent conventional bulk curing in a local intensive curing barn, and a treatment group (FS, 20 kg) that was subjected to the shaking-withering process.

Tobacco curing methods

The tobacco leaves underwent a sequential processing protocol consisting of withering, shaking-withering, enzymatic oxidation stage, and drying stages. This protocol was adapted from an established method19,20, with specific process durations and environmental conditions optimized for the present study(Detailed process flow and time distribution are presented in Fig. 1; Table 1).

Withering was conducted under controlled conditions (dry-bulb temperature: 38 °C; wet-bulb temperature: 36 °C) until the leaf moisture content reached approximately 60%. This target moisture level was set to achieve adequate leaf softening while maintaining structural integrity for subsequent processing steps. The withered leaves were then manually deboned and cut into sections measuring 10 cm × 10 cm.

Shaking-withering was performed in a climate-controlled chamber maintained at 26 °C and 60% relative humidity. These conditions were chosen to support enzymatic activity and aroma formation. The shaking intensity was mechanically defined by tumbling in a rotary shaker at 60 revolutions per minute (rpm), and the withering phase involved spreading leaves to a uniform thickness of 3 cm. The process involved a cyclic protocol of four alternating shaking and static withering phases. For experimental groups FS1–FS3, the shaking intensity was varied while the withering duration was held constant at 8 h. Conversely, for groups FS4–FS6, the withering duration was varied while a standardized shaking intensity (20 min per cycle) was applied.

Enzymatic oxidation stage followed immediately, during which the leaves were kept under controlled conditions (41 °C, 80% relative humidity) for 13 h. The leaves were covered with a moisture-retentive cloth throughout this stage to promote the desired biochemical transformations.

Finally, drying was carried out according to the referenced thermal protocol19,20, comprising an initial dehydration step at 85 °C for 10 min, a 30-min equilibration period, and a final curing phase at 105 °C until the leaves were fully desiccated.

Sensory evaluation

Cigarette samples were conditioned and prepared in strict accordance with the GB/T 16,447 − 2004 standard. Sensory evaluation was performed by a panel of five trained expert assessors from Hunan Tobacco Industrial Co., Ltd. Prior to formal assessment, all panelists completed a standardized training program encompassing odor recognition, intensity scaling, and descriptive terminology specific to tobacco, using a set of reference samples. This ensured consistent perception and scoring across the panel.The evaluation followed a single-blind design, where sample codes were randomized and assessors were unaware of the treatment identities. Each sample was evaluated in triplicate across independent sessions to account for intra-panelist variation.

Assessment was conducted with reference to the company‘s internal standard for mono-varietal tobacco evaluation. Specific indicators and scoring systems were as follows: Aroma quality, Volume of aroma, Transdermal, Offensive taste, Concentration, Soft and delicate, Strength, Irritation, After-taste, Sweetness, Dryness, and Cleanliness were rated on a 9-point scale; Combustibility on a 5-point scale; Ash color on a 3-point scale. The intensity of specific aroma characteristics (e.g., caramelized, nutty) was descriptively scored based on perception using a six-level scale: none (0), slight (1), somewhat (2), moderate (3), relatively full (4), and full (5). Intensity differences among samples within the same group were assessed using a 0–9 point scale. Final scores for each sample represent the mean values from all assessors and replicates. Detailed scores and descriptors are provided in Table A1.

Main chemical components

The seven conventional chemical composition indexes of tobacco leaves, including total plant alkaloids, reducing sugars, total sugars, total nitrogen, potassium, chlorine and starch were determined using the methods specified in YC/T 160–2002, YC/T 159–2002, YC/T 161–2002, YC/T 217–2007, YC/T 162–2002, and YC/T 216–2013, respectively.

Aroma precursor substances

Quantitative analysis of key polyphenols and flavonoids in tobacco leaves was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography(AGILENT 1260 Infinity III with Cogent Bidentate C18, 4 μm, 100 A, Microsolv Technologies) with external calibration curves derived from authentic standards. Target compounds, including chlorogenic acid, scopoletin, kaempferol, and rutin (YC/T202-2006), were quantified by measuring the absorbance of extracted samples (Table A2).

Determination of plastid pigment chemistry indexes (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and β-carotene) was conducted using ethanol-spectrophotometry21.

Volatile aroma substances

Volatile aroma compounds were extracted from tobacco leaf samples via simultaneous distillation-extraction (SDE). Briefly, 5 g of dried tobacco powder, 5 µL of nitrobenzene (internal standard, chromatographic grade), 10 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate, and boiling chips were placed in a 250 mL round-bottom flask with 105 mL of distilled water. The flask was connected to the aqueous phase of the SDE apparatus and the contents were boiled using a heating mantle. For the organic phase, 150 mL of dichloromethane (analytical grade) was added to a separate 250 mL flask, connected to the organic phase port, and heated at 60 °C in a water bath. Distillation commenced upon condensation, with extraction lasting 2 h. The organic solvent was combined, dehydrated with anhydrous magnesium carbonate, vacuum-filtered, and concentrated to 1 mL at 45 °C using rotary evaporation. The concentrate was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane and stored in vials for analysis.

A gas chromatograph coupled with a mass spectrometer(GC-MS QP2010 SHIMADAZU) and SH-I-5Sil capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, SHIMADAZU) were used for measurement. Helium (> 99.999% purity) at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min was used as the carrier gas. The injection volume was 1 µL, the split ratio was 10:1, and the injector temperature 250 °C. Solvents were rinsed two times pre- and post-injection, and samples were rinsed three times. The GC was programmed as follows: Initial: 50 °C (held for 2 min); Ramp 1: 4 °C/min to 200 °C (held for 10 min); Ramp 2: 2 °C/min to 215 °C (held for 5 min); Ramp 3: 0.5 °C/min to 220 °C (held for 2 min); Ramp 4: 2 °C/min to 300 °C (held for 10 min); Total run time: 115 min. The MS parameters were as follows: Ionization mode: electron impact at 70 eV; ion source temperature: 230 °C; interface temperature: 250 °C; solvent delay: 6 min; scan range: 40–550 m/z; detector voltage: automatic optimization.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using a combination of statistical and multivariate tools. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were computed in Microsoft Excel 2019. One-way ANOVA with Duncan’s post hoc test was conducted in SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM Corp.). PLS-DA was carried out in SIMCA 14.1. Furthermore, cluster heatmaps, stacked bar charts, and correlation plots were generated using Python 3.8 with the seaborn and matplotlib visualization libraries.

Results

Sensory analysis of tobacco leaves

All tobacco leaf samples maintained consistent overall sensory grading levels (Table A1) with equivalent combustion indices. However, statistical analysis revealed significant variations in specific sensory intensity parameters among treatments (p < 0.05).

As illustrated in Fig. 2, within shaking intensity treatments, FS2 demonstrated a significantly superior overall evaluation. It outperformed other treatments in volume of aroma, soft and delicate, after-taste, and dryness (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). In contrast, the conventional flue-cured tobacco (CK) showed no statistically significant advantage over the shaking-withering groups in offensive taste and aroma quality.

Among withering-intensity treatments, FS4 achieved significantly higher comprehensive ratings (p < 0.05), exceeding other treatments across all metrics except Aroma quality and Strength, where differences were not significant. Notably, FS6 (prolonged shade-curing) showed significantly diminished scores across all sensory parameters (p < 0.05).

The descriptive aroma profiles (Table 2) aligned with quantitative scores: FS2 and FS5, which displayed prominent nutty aromas, corresponded to the groups receiving higher preference scores in the statistical analysis.

Analysis of main chemical components

Shaking-withering significantly modulated the primary chemical matrix of tobacco leaves, enhancing its chemical coordination (Table 3). Compared to CK, treated leaves exhibited elevated reducing sugars, total alkaloids, and total nitrogen, alongside a reduced total sugar content. This shift resulted in optimized sugar ratios and nitrogen-to-alkaloid balance, key indicators of smoking smoothness.

In the shaking treatment group, reducing sugars, total alkaloids, and total sugars were the significantly altered indicators. Specifically, the reducing sugar content exhibited a V-shaped trend, with FS2 decreasing by 4.72% and 6.78% compared to FS1 and FS3, respectively. The total alkaloid content increased with intensity, with FS2 and FS3 showing a 10.81% increase compared to FS1. The nitrogen-to-alkaloid ratio and the ratio of two sugars in FS1, FS2, and FS3 all fell within the range of high-quality tobacco leaves (nitrogen-to-alkaloid ratio: 0.9–1.1; ratio of two sugars: 0.85–0.9). However, the nitrogen-to-alkaloid ratio showed significant differences, with FS2 decreasing by 6.67% compared to FS1.

In the withering treatment group, reducing sugars were the only component with significant changes, and their content increased with the withering intensity. FS4 showed increases of 5.49% and 7.67% compared to FS5 and FS6, respectively. Overall, shaking-withering modulation enhanced the carbon and nitrogen metabolism levels in tobacco leaves, increased the contents of sugars and alkaloids, and thereby improved the chemical composition coordination of flue-cured tobacco.

Analysis of aroma precursors

Shaking-withering drove a targeted transformation of aroma precursor pools (Table 4). Overall, it reduced total polyphenol content versus CK, primarily by decreasing chlorogenic acid and rutin, while elevating the stress-responsive compounds scopoletin and kaempferol.

Under different shaking intensities, significant differences were observed in the contents of chlorogenic acid, rutin, kaempferol, and total polyphenols. Specifically, FS2 exhibited the highest levels of chlorogenic acid and kaempferol, exceeding FS1 and FS3 by 4.71% and 45.26%, and 30.61% and 39.06%, respectively. FS1 showed the highest rutin content, surpassing FS2 and FS3 by 11.76% and 33.09%, respectively. Different withering intensities significantly affected rutin content in tobacco leaves. Specifically, rutin content decreased with prolonged withering time, with FS6 showing 8.58% and 18.14% lower levels compared to FS4 and FS5, respectively.In summary, shaking and withering processes had distinct effects on polyphenols in tobacco leaves. Chlorogenic acid and kaempferol did not exhibit a clear linear relationship with shaking intensity, while prolonged withering led to rutin degradation.

The degradation of plastid pigments was even more pronounced and treatment-specific (Table 5). Shaking-withered leaves showed significantly lower chlorophyll and carotenoid levels than CK. Carotenoid degradation was most severe in FS2, whereas prolonged withering (FS5, FS6) primarily accelerated chlorophyll breakdown. Starch content remained unaffected across all treatments.

Under different shaking intensities, FS2 exhibited a 33.33% reduction in chlorophyll a content compared to FS1, while FS3 showed no significant difference from FS1. Both FS2 and FS3 demonstrated a 50.00% decrease in chlorophyll b content relative to FS1. Carotenoid content was most affected in FS2, showing a 58.33% reduction compared to FS1 and FS3, with no significant difference between FS3 and FS1. Total chlorophyll content decreased by 46.15% in FS2 and 38.46% in FS3 compared to FS1. Similarly, under different withering intensities, FS5 and FS6 showed a 40.00% reduction in both chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b contents compared to FS4, resulting in lower total chlorophyll levels. These results indicate that shaking intensity (particularly FS2) had the greatest impact on pigment degradation, with carotenoids being most susceptible, while prolonged withering time primarily affected chlorophyll content during the shaking-withering process.

Analysis of volatile aroma compounds

Analysis of the types and contents of volatile aroma compounds in different treatments

This study employed SDE coupled with GC-MS for the detection of volatile compounds. This method offers advantages such as high reproducibility and enrichment efficiency, enabling the effective detection of trace volatile components in processed tobacco leaves (detection limit: 0.01–0.05 µg/g). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate to ensure data reliability, and only compounds with accurate identification (match factor ≥ 85%) and good reproducibility were selected for further analysis (Table A3, Table A4, Fig A3 and Fig A4).

As shown in Figs. 3A, 50 and 73 volatile compounds were detected in CK and shaking-withering tobacco, respectively (Table A5 and Table A6). In each sample, 27 and 50 unique compounds were identified, and only 23 shared compounds were detected, indicating distinct compositional differences between the two tobacco types. In the FS treatment, the contents of terpenes and alcohols were higher than in CK, primarily due to isosativenediol and trans-squalene, while the levels of heterocyclic and aromatic compounds were significantly reduced.The volatile composition under different shaking-withering intensities is illustrated in Fig. 3B. The total volatile content varied among treatments, with FS2 and FS5 exhibiting higher levels, which is consistent with their prominent nutty aroma in sensory evaluations. However, FS5 showed an abnormal increase in alkanes, which are high-boiling-point compounds that may impair the volatile characteristics of the tobacco leaves. This increase lead to a significantly lower sensory evaluation score compared to other treatments.

All data were subjected to Z-score normalization prior to principal component analysis (PCA) to eliminate dimensional discrepancies among variables. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) cumulatively accounted for 55.02% of the total variance, with PC1 alone explaining 34.00% (PC2: 21.02%). The score plot revealed significant separation patterns among treatment groups along the PC1 axis, which indicates pronounced heterogeneity in volatile compound profiles across samples. Specifically, FS2 and FS5 clustered in the fourth quadrant due to their analogous processing protocols, while distinct spatial distributions were observed for other treatments: FS1 occupied the first quadrant, FS3 localized to the second quadrant, and FS4 and FS6 segregated into the second and third quadrants, respectively. These spatial divergences underscore the specificity of volatile metabolite signatures across the PC1 and PC2 dimensions.

Variable loading analysis identified nonanal (loading = 0.96), methyl linoleate (loading = 0.91), and methyl salicylate (loading = 0.91) as the dominant drivers of PC1 variation, collectively demonstrating the strongest positive contributions to this component. These findings indicate that the PCA model effectively disentangled inter-sample metabolic disparities.

Characterization and evaluation of crucial volatiles

This study investigated the effects of shaking and withering processes on volatile compound profiles in tobacco leaves using PLS-DA. The established models for 73 shared volatile compounds demonstrated high interpretability (shaking: R²X = 0.83, R²Y = 0.98, Q²=0.97; withering: R²X = 0.81, R²Y = 0.99, Q²=0.95) and predictive capability (Fig A1 and Fig A2), confirming the robustness and reliability of the models without overfitting. Key aroma-active compounds were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values.

In the shaking treatment group (Fig. 5A), 24 key volatile substances (VIP > 1) were screened, showing distinct expression patterns across treatments. In FS2, 19 substances were upregulated and 4 downregulated. Farnesyl acetone, Viridiflorol, Decanal, Citronellal, 4-Oxo-isophorone, Damascone, Dihydroactinidiolide, Indole, Dehydrodihydro-β-ionone, 5-Methyl-2-furanmethanol, Heptanal, and Angelica lactone exhibited significantly increased contents under this intensity. The levels of Furfural, Undecane, Toluene, Ethyl acetate, Ylangene, and Dodecanol were slightly lower than those in FS1, while 3-Hydroxy-2-butanone, Caryophyllin, 7-Tetradecene, and Hexanol showed significant reductions.

In the withering treatment group (Fig. 5B), 40 volatile substances with VIP > 1 were identified, with 20 compounds (VIP > 1.08) selected for discussion. Compared to the shaking treatment, the VIP values of volatile substances in withering were all below 1.25. FS5 treatment demonstrated upregulated levels of 15 substances, including Decanal, Pristane, Nerylacetone, Trans-2,cis-6-nonadienal, Mesitylene, Citronellal, Solanone, 5-Methyl-2-furanmethanol, and Phenylacetaldehyde, which showed significantly elevated relative contents. Conversely, Indole, Dodecanol, Citronellol, Undecanol, and Ethyl stearate gradually decreased with prolonged withering time.

In summary, varying intensities of shaking and withering significantly influenced the composition of volatile substances in tobacco leaves. The shaking treatment (FS2) exhibited more upregulated substances, while the withering treatment (FS5) displayed higher overall contents.

Discussions

Mechanical stress and metabolic regulation

This study introduced the shaking-withering technique, adapted from oolong tea processing, into tobacco curing. This technique proactively applies controlled mechanical damage and oxidative stress during the critical yellowing stage—a period decisive for macromolecule degradation, plastid pigment conversion, and aroma accumulation in tobacco. Unlike conventional curing, which relies primarily on temperature and humidity modulation, shaking-withering employs mechanical stress as a key manipulated variable to direct metabolic reprogramming.

Our findings demonstrate that this treatment successfully harmonized key chemical components in tobacco leaves. The significant increase in reducing sugars is likely associated with the upregulation of defense-related secondary metabolic pathways, stemming from the “wound respiration” triggered by mechanical damage30,31. This process redirects carbon flux, potentially by activating glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway, leading to the degradation of polysaccharides into monosaccharides32 and altering the flow of precursors into pathways for terpenoid (e.g., the MEP pathway) and alkaloid synthesis33. The concurrent accumulation of the stress-response marker squalene34 further confirms that shaking-withering effectively induces a controlled stress response, steering leaf metabolism away from primary growth pathways and toward the generation of flavor-related compounds. Collectively, these changes indicate that shaking is not merely a physical treatment but acts as a “metabolic switch.” By activating signaling pathways analogous to plant stress responses, it systematically reprograms carbon and nitrogen allocation in tobacco leaves, laying the metabolic foundation for subsequent aroma formation.

Oxidative degradation of pigments and polyphenols

The shaking-withering process profoundly accelerated the oxidative degradation of key structural and protective compounds, namely plastid pigments and polyphenols. Compared to conventionally flue-cured tobacco, shaking-withered leaves exhibited significantly reduced contents of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids, with the extent of degradation highly dependent on processing intensity. Notably, carotenoids were most susceptible to the optimal shaking intensity (FS2), showing a reduction of 58.33%.

Mechanistically, this degradation is linked to the synergy between mechanical damage and the induced oxidative environment. Mechanical injury disrupts cellular compartments, while the process likely activates NADPH oxidase, promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and increasing polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity35,36. Chlorophyll degradation results from the direct physical disruption of membrane structures37, whereas carotenoids, acting as antioxidants, are consumed in scavenging ROS38. This controlled oxidative burst also drives the oxidation and decomposition of polyphenolic aroma precursors such as chlorogenic acid and rutin39—explaining the significant decrease in chlorogenic acid content observed in FS2 (Table 4)—while potentially inducing the accumulation of stress-responsive phenolics like kaempferol and scopoletin. Therefore, shaking-withering acts as a targeted regulatory tool, degrading these pigment and polyphenols and converting them into a reservoir of reactive intermediates for subsequent aroma formation. Critically, this degradation is not an endpoint but an essential initiation step for aroma genesis. The breakdown products of carotenoids and other pigments serve as direct precursors for the volatile compounds that define the final sensory profile.

Formation of aroma-active volatiles and sensory quality

The reactive intermediates generated from the degradation of pigments and polyphenols are directly channeled into the biosynthesis of key aroma-active volatiles. The strategic degradation of these precursors directly fuels the formation of a distinct and enriched volatile profile. Carotenoid degradation serves as a primary source for norisoprenoid aroma compounds. The significant loss of carotenoids in FS2 correlated with the sensory prominence of nutty and caramelized aromas, suggesting the generation of compounds such as β-damascenone and dihydroactinidiolide, which contribute sweet and fruity notes. This pattern mirrors the aroma development observed in oolong tea processing.

Shaking and withering intensities differentially altered the volatile spectrum. Optimal shaking (FS2) favored the accumulation of key volatiles derived from carotenoid degradation (e.g., damascone), phenylpropane degradation, and Maillard reactions, contributing to tea-like, rosy, and sweet aromas40,41. In contrast, extended withering promoted the synthesis of olefins, alcohols, and aldehydes via terpenoid (MEP/MVA) and fatty acid oxidation pathways42, yielding citrus and herbal notes from compounds like citronellol and citronellal. The overall elevation of terpenes and alcohols in FS2, coupled with a relative decrease in ketones and heterocyclic compounds, collectively shifted the aroma profile from the smoky character of traditional flue-cured tobacco43toward a more nuanced style enriched with nutty notes. This shift aligns with the higher scores for “soft and delicate” and “sweetness” in the sensory evaluation of FS2.

Ultimately, this remodeled chemistry translated into superior sensory quality. Treatments subjected to balanced stress (e.g., FS2 and FS4) received higher comprehensive sensory ratings. Their aroma profiles, characterized by prominent caramelized, roasted, and nutty notes, were chemically underpinned by the effective conversion of precursors via the induced stress-response pathways. This confirms that regulating the intensity of mechanical and oxidative stress is a critical lever for tailoring the final sensory quality of tobacco. Of particular note, the significant accumulation of 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol (with a caramel-like aroma) and damascone (with a fruity-floral aroma) in FS2 corresponded directly to the high scores for “caramelized” and “nutty” aromas in sensory evaluations, establishing a clear link between specific volatile compounds and their perceived sensory attributes (Fig. 7).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that shaking-withering technology can fundamentally redirect tobacco aroma formation, shifting it toward a more diverse volatile profile rich in alcohols, terpenes, and norisoprenoids, which directly translates into superior sensory qualities with distinct caramelized, nutty, and roasted notes.

For the tobacco industry, this method introduces a novel paradigm for flavor design. It provides independent control over mechanical stress and oxidative duration as precise levers to steer metabolism, enabling the targeted degradation of pigments and polyphenols for the creation of unique, reproducible flavor profiles. This addresses a key market need for diversified, premium products.Crucially, the process shows strong commercial feasibility. The core operations are mechanically simple and compatible with existing barns through modular retrofitting. Our finding that optimal quality is achieved at defined, moderate intensity (e.g., FS2) indicates that commercial viability does not depend on prohibitively long cycles or excessive energy input, although uniform scaling requires further engineering optimization.

In summary, shaking-withering presents a viable and innovative strategy for the next generation of tailored tobacco processing.
