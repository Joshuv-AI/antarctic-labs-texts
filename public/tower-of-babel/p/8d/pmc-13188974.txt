Surfactant use and outcomes in middle-income versus high-income countries
Authors: Johan van Dyk, Michael C Harrison, Erika M Edwards, Danielle E Y Ehret, Lloyd Tooke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreterm birth remains a leading cause of neonatal mortality, disproportionately affecting low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). Surfactant replacement therapy (SRT) is a key intervention for neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS). However, due to cost and differing guidelines there are likely differences in SRT in different parts of the world. This study compared surfactant use, timing of administration, and associated neonatal outcomes in very low birth weight (VLBW) infants born in high-income countries (HICs) versus middle-income countries (MICs), with further analysis of public and private centers within MICs. This secondary analysis of prospectively collected data using the Vermont Oxford Network database included 271 826 inborn VLBW infants from 2018 to 23. Data were stratified by country income classification and ownership (public center vs. private center) in MICs. Surfactant use was highest in private MIC centers (64.1%), followed by HICs (55.3%), and public MIC centers (43.5%). Median time to first surfactant dose was shortest in private MIC centers (60 min) and longest in public MIC centers (120 min). Infants in HICs were more likely to receive delivery room continuous positive airway pressure and earlier surfactant. Survival post-SRT was highest in HICs (88.1%) and lowest in public MIC centers (69.5%) with sepsis being a particular problem in MICs. Disparities in the proportion of infants receiving SRT, the timing of doses, and neonatal outcomes such as mortality and sepsis rates persist between HICs and MICs, and between public and private sectors in MICs.

Introduction

Preterm birth remains a global problem, accounting for up to 35% of all neonatal deaths [1]. Most preterm deliveries occur in low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) with over 81% of preterm births occurring in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. Over 99% of the estimated neonatal deaths caused by preterm birth are in LMICs [2, 3].

Along with antenatal steroids (ANS), caffeine, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), and mechanical ventilation, exogenous surfactant administration is one of the interventions that decrease neonatal respiratory mortality and complications. Surfactant replacement therapy (SRT) decreases the need for ventilatory support, the risk of pulmonary air leaks, mortality, and the combined outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), without increasing adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [4]. Surfactant has been on the World Health Organization (WHO) Essential Medication list since 2009.

Despite WHO recommendations, the availability of these interventions is lagging in many LMICs. Only 63% of African countries had any access to CPAP in 2020 and only 33% had SRT available, with additional differences found within countries between public and private hospitals [5]. Even when available, the cost burden of surfactant may be borne by the infant’s family and may exceed the per-capita gross domestic product (GDP) (300–500 USD per dose) [6]. Therefore, for both families and healthcare systems, the price of surfactant remains an obstacle to accessibility even though SRT has the potential to lower the cost of overall care in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [7].

Although the field of neonatology no longer recommends prophylactic SRT [8], the optimal timing of early rescue SRT following optimized CPAP is still unknown. Many guidelines use the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) as a proxy for underlying pulmonary compromise. A meta-analysis published in 2022 looked at SRT at different FiO2 thresholds. There were no significant differences in outcomes seen with ranges between 0.3 and 0.5; however, an increase in retinopathy of prematurity was seen at a FiO2 threshold of 0.6 [9]. Given this uncertainty in the literature, there are variable thresholds set, such as FiO2 > 0.3 in the 2022 European Consensus Guidelines on the Management of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) and FiO2 > 0.5 from the Canadian Pediatric Society [4]. Additionally, many countries lack national guidelines, and surfactant is given according to unit protocol or physician discretion. Thresholds based solely on FiO2 are recognized as imperfect as oxygenation is influenced by several factors, including mean airway pressure. Other methods such as respiratory scoring charts and lung ultrasound are also used to guide decisions [10, 11].

The aim of SRT is to reduce the need for mechanical ventilation and its associated complications by giving surfactant early in the course of RDS, preferably using methods such as less invasive surfactant administration (LISA), InSURE (Intubation-SURfactant-Extubation), and SALSA (Surfactant Administered via Laryngeal or Supraglottic Airway) [10]. To optimize effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, SRT is optimally used in combination with good clinical guidelines, including ANS, thermoregulation, safe oxygen use, and non-invasive ventilation support [12]. Other methods of administration such as aerosolization are still undergoing clinical trials [13].

To date, no large-scale studies compare the use of SRT in HICs and MICs. It is important to identify differences that could guide practice or stimulate further research. Differences between private and public facilities may be more marked in MICs due to the probable difference in resource availability and clinical protocols which guide surfactant administration. Because only three low-income countries (LICs) participated in Vermont Oxford Network (VON) at the time of the study, there were insufficient data to include a LIC group for analysis, particularly as surfactant is often not widely used in these countries.

Study type and objectives

This study was a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data. The primary objective was to compare surfactant use between HICs and MICs in terms of mortality, complications diagnosed during NICU admission [retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), late-onset infection], and respiratory care characteristics. Hospitals in MICs were further divided into public and private categories. The secondary objective was to compare antenatal and postnatal characteristics, management, and outcomes between these groups.

Materials and methods

VON is a nonprofit voluntary collaboration of health care professionals working together to change the landscape of neonatal care. VON manages the largest voluntary very low birth weight (VLBW) database in the world, which includes 1200 centers in 39 countries [14]. Countries were stratified by receipt of care at a hospital in HIC compared to MIC as defined by the World Bank in 2022 [15]. HICs included: Austria, Canada, Chile, Denmark, Finland, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Ireland, Kuwait, Latvia, Poland, Portugal, Puerto Rico, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Slovenia, Spain, Switzerland, Taiwan, United Arab Emirates, United Kingdom, and the United States. MICs included: Argentina, Botswana, Brazil, China, Colombia, India, Malaysia, Mozambique, Namibia, Pakistan, South Africa and Turkey. MICs were further divided into patients managed in public (government) hospitals and private hospitals.

Definitions used were as per the VON manual of operations [16]. Data on hospital characteristics, including ownership, were derived from the VON membership survey.

Infants included in this study were inborn with birth weights 501 to 1500 grams from January 2018 to December 2023. Infants were excluded if they had any life-threatening congenital abnormalities or if they were missing information on surfactant exposure. Not all variables were available or applicable for each infant, which affects the denominator of each item.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics [percentages; median and quartile 1 (Q1) and quartile 3 (Q3)] were used to describe differences in hospital characteristics, maternal characteristics, infant characteristics, delivery room interventions, surfactant delivery, complications, and discharge characteristics. All analyses were done in SAS 9.4.

This analysis is descriptive and did not consider hypotheses. Therefore, we did not calculate P-values, which are the products of hypothesis tests, or build regression models to test hypotheses [17].

Ethics

The Human Resource Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town (HREC number 341/2024) granted approval. The VON research repository is a deidentified, HIPAA-compliant dataset with University of Vermont Institutional Review Board approval.

Results

There were 1065 centers included in the analysis of which 877 were in HIC, 37 were MIC public centers, and 151 were MIC private centers. The median (Q1, Q3) number of NICU beds was 28 (18, 45) at HIC centers, 32 (23, 48) at MIC public centers, and 12 (8, 18) at MIC private centers.

A total of 271 826 infants were included, representing both HICs and MICs. Among the included infants, 229 524 (84.4%) infants were from HICs while 42 302 (15.6%) from MICs, with 22 078 (52.2%) from public centers and 20 224 (47.8%) from private centers (Fig. 1). Maternal and infant characteristics, along with delivery room interventions, are detailed in Tables 1 and 2. In HICs, a higher percentage of mothers received ANS (90.9%) compared to those in MICs (80.0%). Chorioamnionitis was recorded as more prevalent among mothers in HICs across all categories, with the lowest incidence observed in private MIC centers. Additionally, the rate of vaginal deliveries was lowest in the private MIC centers compared to both HICs and public MIC centers.

The mean birth weight as well as median gestational ages at delivery were lower in the groups that received surfactant compared to the groups that did not receive surfactant. There was also a lower percentage of small for gestational age (SGA) babies in HICs compared to MICs. Infants that received surfactant received more delivery room interventions than those that did not receive surfactant, and Apgar scores were lower in the groups that received surfactant. Middle-income private centers were least likely to use delivery room CPAP for all infants compared to high-income and middle-income public centers.

The highest usage of surfactant was observed in the private MIC centers at 64.1%, followed by the HIC group at 55.3%, and the public MIC centers at 43.5% (Table 3).

HICs demonstrated a greater likelihood of administering surfactant in the delivery room, with rates of 35.6% compared to 13.1% in MIC public and 24.1% in MIC private centers (Table 3). Overall, the timing of surfactant administration varied between hospital types. When administered, the initial dose of SRT was given at a median of 62 min in HICs and 60 min in private MIC centers while at 120 min in public MIC centers. Infants in HICs who received surfactant were more frequently placed on ventilators and treated with steroids for BPD compared to their counterparts in other settings.

Complications of prematurity were more common in all groups that received surfactant (Tables 4 and 5). BPD was most common in the HIC group, although the rate of severe BPD was lowest in this group. Severe ROP was most common in the HIC group and late-onset infection was highest in the public MIC centers.

In the group that received surfactant, MIC infants in public centers had a lower survival rate of 69.5% compared to 78.2% in private MIC centers and 88.1% among HIC centers (Tables 4 and 5). Survival rates were higher across all groups that did not receive surfactant with a smaller disparity between HIC, MIC public, and MIC private centers. Among infants who died, those that did not receive surfactant had shorter lengths of stay prior to death. A greater proportion of HIC patients were discharged home on oxygen therapy and monitoring, with 20.6% of infants in the HIC surfactant group receiving oxygen therapy at discharge in contrast to 7.2% in MIC public and 5.3% in MIC private settings. Survivors discharged from MICs had higher rates of discharge weight less than the 10th percentile, regardless of surfactant exposure, compared to HICs.

Discussion

This study, which is one of the first to compare SRT between HICs and MICs and utilizes a large well-established database, found disparities in surfactant use, timing, and neonatal outcomes between HICs and MICs, and between public and private sectors in MICs. Private MIC centers were most likely to use surfactant followed by the HIC group and the public MIC centers. Use of other interventions that can reduce respiratory mortality and associated complications, such as ANS and delivery room CPAP, also differed between HIC and MIC centers.

The WHO advocates for the administration of ANS to pregnant women at risk of imminent preterm birth at 24 to 34 weeks of gestation. A 2020 Cochrane review found that ANS exposure significantly lowers the likelihood of perinatal and neonatal mortality. Additionally, it decreases the occurrence of RDS and probably reduces the risk of IVH, NEC, and confirmed infection [18].

This study revealed that approximately 10% more mothers in HICs received ANS compared to those in MICs. This trend was observed both among mothers who had infants that later required surfactant and those who had infants that did not require surfactant. A study conducted in Cape Town found that 74.3% of mothers received “any steroids”; however, only 17.3% of these cases were deemed “optimal”, meaning two doses of betamethasone had been administered more than 24 h but less than 7 days before delivery [2]. The timing and type of antenatal corticosteroid administration to the mother are not captured in the VON database; only the presence of “any steroids” is recorded.

More male infants received surfactant across all three categories. Male infants born ≤32 weeks GA have a decreased rate of survival and an increased rate of respiratory morbidity compared to female infants [19]. The median gestational age in the surfactant groups was between 27 and 28 weeks whereas it was (as expected), higher (30 and 31 weeks) in the groups that did not receive surfactant. Fewer SGA infants were given surfactant compared to appropriate for gestational age (AGA) infants. There is still ongoing debate whether SGA infants have a higher, lower, or similar risk for RDS compared to AGA babies [20, 21].

There were more delivery room (DR) interventions among the infants that received surfactant at any time, suggesting these infants were generally more unwell from birth. Among infants that received SRT, over 70% received face mask ventilation and between 40% and 60% were intubated in the DR with a higher frequency in the HIC centers. In contrast, the infants that did not receive any surfactant received DR face mask ventilation approximately 45% of the time and had delivery room intubation rates falling below 9%. CPAP use in the DR in MICs was lower than in HICs, whether the groups received surfactant or not, and was lowest in the MIC private centers. Early CPAP in the delivery room leads to a reduction in the need for intubation and SRT [22]. This aligns with our findings, where the lowest rate of DR CPAP and highest rate of SRT occurred in MIC private centers. There are notable discrepancies in the percentage of infants receiving surfactant any time: 54.9% in HICs compared to 65.6% in MIC private centers and 44.1% in public MIC facilities.

For infants born between 22 and 27 weeks of gestational age, the use of SRT was lower in public MIC centers. However, starting from 28 weeks GA, the difference in surfactant usage between HICs and public MICs diminished. In contrast, private MIC centers consistently demonstrated the highest rates of surfactant administration, particularly pronounced in the 28–32 weeks GA range. Reasons for this higher surfactant use in the private centers may include less reliance on protocols with decisions to use surfactant based on the attending physician and/or parent expectations. There are also fewer financial limitations compared to public facilities. Public MIC centers may have different protocols/thresholds for the use of SRT compared to HICs or private MIC centers. Data from public centers in India and South Africa suggests that SRT is limited to rescue therapy in babies with the potential for better survival, usually 27–28 weeks gestation [2, 6].

The average time to first dose of surfactant was 62 min in HICs versus 120 min in public MICs, and 60 min in private MIC facilities. This disparity in dosing time may be attributed to fewer staff members being available to monitor and treat infants in public MIC facilities, as well as a higher threshold for administering surfactant. SRT may also be delayed if families must finance the surfactant themselves.

Invasive ventilation was performed on 71.3%–82.2% of infants who received surfactant. With the advent of INSURE and more recently LISA techniques, there is potential for improvement in this area, as ventilation is known to contribute to BPD and increase the risk of infection. Additionally, there was a significantly higher utilization of nitric oxide (NO) among infants in HICs. This treatment modality is costly with questionable benefit, which may explain its limited use in MICs for preterm infants [23].

Common complications of prematurity were more frequently documented in the groups that required surfactant, except for growth faltering with discharge weight below the 10th percentile. Complications are likely attributable to several factors, including a greater degree of prematurity, severity of illness, and complications arising from therapy, such as ventilation. Discharge weight was less in the group not exposed to SRT, probably due to a higher proportion of SGA infants at birth.

In the groups that received SRT, BPD rates were highest in HIC centers, despite these centers reporting the lowest rates of severe BPD. In HICs, 20.5% of infants received postnatal steroids for BPD, compared to 15.6% and 16.3% in public and private facilities in MICs, respectively.

Infection remains a significant concern in MIC public centers, with sepsis rates at 29.1% in the SRT group and 12.6% in the group not exposed to SRT. Neonatal sepsis continues to be a major cause of mortality and complications worldwide, largely due to inadequate preventive and therapeutic strategies in LMICs, as well as the increased survival of preterm and low-weight newborns who experience prolonged hospital stays [24]. In LMICS, especially in the public sector, the goal should be to optimize the survival with minimal complications in VLBW infants, given the long-term economic and psychosocial impact complications have on families caring for infants with complex health needs. However, until infection rates are brought under control this will not be realized.

Survival rates were notably lower in the groups that received SRT, due to reasons previously discussed. The most significant disparity between the surfactant and non-surfactant groups was observed in the MIC public centers, with survival rates of 69.5% compared to 87.4%. Smaller and more vulnerable infants often necessitate extended intensive care, which may not always be readily available or of high quality in MICs. The length of stay was also significantly longer for the HIC group that received surfactant, although the postmenstrual age at discharge was similar amongst all groups, likely reflecting the earlier gestational ages cared for in HICs. The median day of death was earlier in the groups that did not receive surfactant. In HICs, this finding may be due to extremely unwell infants who died in the delivery room before surfactant could be given. An additional consideration in MICs could be smaller infants not meeting the criteria for surfactant treatment [7]. More than one in five infants (20.6%) in HICs who received surfactant were discharged home on oxygen. This situation requires considerable resources and follow-up care is not always available in LMICs.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study are the large sample-size, and that it uses a well-established database which can also differentiate between public and private institutions. There are several limitations to this study. World Bank classifications do not consider variations of income within the same country. Pregnancies in MICs may not have as many early obstetric ultrasounds resulting in less accurate gestational age estimations. As the information for this study was taken from an existing database, more nuanced data such as indication for surfactant delivery, response to administration and the need for subsequent doses were not available.

Conclusion

This study highlights substantial differences in the use and timing of SRT between HICs and MICs, as well as between public and private centers within MICs. Differences in outcomes cannot solely be attributed to surfactant use as SRT is part of a bundle of care and sepsis remains a major problem especially in MICs. The optimal indications for SRT are still under debate and should incorporate both cost and efficacy for all regions.
