Return of genetic results to persons with Parkinson's disease: Blessing or foe?
Authors: Tamara Shiner, Roy N Alcalay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson's disease (PD) is increasingly understood as a biologically heterogeneous disorder, with genetic contributions ranging from high-penetrance monogenic variants to common polygenic risk. As testing becomes more accessible and clinically relevant, the key question has shifted from whether to test to whether -and how- to return results. Most PD associated variants confer probabilistic risk rather than deterministic outcomes, limiting individual-level prediction and complicating disclosure. Yet genetic findings may inform prognosis and increasingly determine eligibility for targeted therapies and clinical trials. Large-scale genetic studies show both feasibility, demand and utility with pathogenic variants identified in approximately 10–20% of patients. The ethical balance differs by context. In patients with established PD, results may provide insight and research access but require careful framing to avoid overinterpretation. In at-risk individuals, predictive testing offers limited clinical utility and can introduce psychological, familial, and insurance implications but can also allow informed family planning and participation in follow up and prevention trials. Current practice remains inconsistent, with major gaps in access to testing and counselling. Expanding disclosure without the appropriate infrastructure risks misinterpretation and widening disparities. We argue that return of genetic results in PD and at-risk individuals should be structured, and longitudinal. As clinical utility evolves, preparedness, will determine whether genetic testing becomes a part of standard care.

Introduction

Counseling is central to movement disorders care, encompassing diagnosis, prognosis, and management in Parkinson's disease (PD). With advances in PD genetics, this role now increasingly includes genetic counseling. We argue that this is not fundamentally different from existing clinical counseling, but rather an extension of it and should be integrated into routine practice with appropriate training.

Similar dilemmas arise elsewhere. When rapid progression raises concern for atypical parkinsonism such as multiple system atrophy, clinicians must decide whether to disclose uncertain information that may not alter immediate management. Genetic counseling presents parallel challenges: probabilistic results, limited immediate actionability, and potential emotional impact. Yet many patients prefer disclosure, as it supports planning and decision-making, including consideration of clinical trials. 1 Here, we focus on genetic counselling for people with PD or at-risk for PD.

PD is increasingly understood as a biologically heterogeneous disorder, encompassing a spectrum of genetic and molecular subtypes rather than a single clinicopathological entity. 2 Over the past two decades, discoveries of monogenic causes, risk-modifying variants, and polygenic contributions have reshaped understanding of PD pathogenesis and variability in clinical presentation and treatment response.3–5 In parallel, advances in next-generation sequencing and declining costs have made genetic testing more accessible in both research and clinical contexts. It is now thought that major pathogenic variants in a PD-associated gene occur in approximately 10–20% of patients with PD.6,7

Unlike fully penetrant monogenic neurodegenerative disorders, most genetic findings in PD confer probabilistic risk rather than deterministic outcomes.4,7 Variable penetrance and limited disease-modifying options complicate interpretation and disclosure.

On the other hand, the emergence of genetically stratified clinical trials, particularly those focusing on lysosomal and mitochondrial pathways, 8 has made genetic information that was previously viewed as largely explanatory to be increasingly perceived as actionable. This shift has brought renewed attention to two central questions. First, should emerging genetic results be returned to people with PD, and if so, under what conditions? Second, how to approach genetic testing and counseling in at-risk family members?

The ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice remain central. 9 Respect for autonomy supports an individual's right to access personal genetic information. However, meaningful autonomy depends not only on comprehension but also longitudinal support. Data from PD cohorts suggest that understanding and recall of genetic information may diminish over time, raising concerns that one-time counseling may be insufficient.10,11

Beneficence (acting to promote well-being) and non-maleficence (avoiding harm) require careful consideration of potential psychological impact. Disclosure of variants such as GBA1 status, given its association with a more aggressive clinical course, 12 may cause distress to patients and their families. However, withholding this information is also problematic, as it may be obtained through other routes without appropriate counselling. The ethical basis for returning results rests not only on the right to know, but on whether disclosure offers clear clinical or personal value when weighed against possible harm.

Justice introduces additional complexity. Access to genetic testing and counseling remains uneven across regions. 13 Expanding disclosure without a parallel investment in infrastructure risks widening existing disparities. Genetic information also has implications beyond the individual, which may create tension between personal confidentiality and its relevance for family members. While positivity rate differs across different ethnic groups (e.g., higher in Ashkenazi Jews and North Africans),14,15 we would advocate against limiting genotyping to selected ethnic groups given the high positivity rate among many populations.

Uncertainty itself presents an ethical challenge, and disclosure in PD genetics is best considered an ongoing process rather than a single event. Returning genetic results should therefore be selective and supported by sustained follow-up.

In this review, we critically examine whether returning genetic results to patients with PD and at-risk individuals represents a benefit or a burden, and we aim to propose a balanced framework to guide practice in the current transitional era towards action from genetic testing.

Genetic architecture of Parkinson's disease

An understanding of the spectrum of genetic contributions to PD is important when considering the return of genetic results. Identified variants differ substantially in penetrance, clinical relevance, and potential implications for prognosis and management.

High penetrance monogenic Parkinson's disease (SNCA, VPS35, selected LRRK2 mutations)

Rare pathogenic variants in a small number of genes are sufficient to cause PD in an autosomal dominant or recessive manner. Autosomal dominant mutations in SNCA, selected LRRK2 mutations, and VPS35 typically result in PD that clinically may be similar to sporadic disease, though particularly within LRRK2 penetrance may be incomplete for some variants. Autosomal recessive mutations in PRKN, PINK1, and PARK7 generally cause early-onset PD, often presenting before the age of 50 years, and are associated with slower progression and fewer non-motor complications in many cases. 16 Although collectively uncommon at the population level, these monogenic forms carry high personal and familial relevance, particularly for reproductive decision-making and testing of at-risk relatives. Their identification may provide an etiological explanation and, more recently, inform eligibility for gene-specific therapeutic strategies.17,18

Reduced penetrance genetic Parkinson's disease (selected LRRK2 and GBA1 variants)

Between rare monogenic causes and common polygenic risk factors lie genetic variants that substantially increase susceptibility to PD while demonstrating incomplete and age-dependent penetrance, most prominently within the LRRK2 and GBA1 genes (Figure 1).3,5,14,19 These variants occupy a particularly complex position in the return-of-results debate, as they confer meaningful biological and clinical information without providing deterministic prognostic certainty.

Genetic architecture of PD.

The LRRK2 p.G2019S mutation represents the most frequent known autosomal dominant genetic contributor to PD in specific populations, including individuals of Ashkenazi Jewish and North African Berber ancestry. 20 Despite its clear pathogenic role, penetrance is incomplete and highly variable. 21 Large international studies estimate lifetime penetrance ranging from approximately 25% to over 70% by advanced age, reflecting modifying influences from genetic background, sex, and environmental exposures (19). Clinically, LRRK2-associated PD typically resembles idiopathic PD in motor presentation, response to dopaminergic therapy, and disease course, which limits the ability to derive individualized prognostic predictions based solely on genetic status. 22

Heterozygous variants in GBA1 which encode the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase, are the most common genetic risk factor for PD worldwide. These variants are present in approximately 5–20% of individuals with PD, depending on ancestry and the spectrum of variants.14,18,23 Importantly, GBA1 mutations do not confer deterministic risk: many carriers never develop PD, and penetrance varies by mutation severity and age. Among individuals who do develop PD, GBA1 variants are associated with earlier age at onset, faster motor and cognitive progression, and increased risk of dementia and psychosis.3,24,25

Another difficulty is the wide allelic heterogeneity of the GBA1 mutation, 26 which ranges from mild risk alleles to severe pathogenic variants associated with Gaucher disease which further complicates genetic counseling and risk communication. Even within research settings, conveying the distinction between susceptibility, prognosis, and diagnostic certainty remains challenging, particularly when variant classification evolves over time. It is important to acknowledge that the impact of genetic findings on response to therapies such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) remains uncertain. There is an urgent need for controlled studies to test the utility of integrating genetic data (e.g., GBA1 status) into clinical decision-makingDespite these uncertainties, LRRK2 and GBA1 occupy a pivotal position within PD genetics. Both genes are currently the focus of multiple ongoing gene-targeted and genetically stratified clinical trials, some which are aimed at modulating LRRK2 kinase activity and others examining the effect of enhancement of glucocerebrosidase function.27,28 As a result, genetic findings in these genes increasingly influence trial eligibility even in the absence of approved disease-modifying treatments.

Low penetrance (risk factors) for pd – the polygenic risk score

The majority of genetic contribution to PD arises from the cumulative effect of numerous common variants, each conferring a small increase in risk. Genome-wide association studies have identified more than 90 independent risk loci for PD, implicating pathways related to synaptic function, lysosomal biology, mitochondrial processes, and immune regulation.3,4 Individually, these variants have limited predictive value. When considered collectively, they can be incorporated into polygenic risk scores (PRS), which stratify relative disease susceptibility at the population level.

Polygenic risk scores reflect probabilistic susceptibility, and individuals in the highest quartile of risk have several-fold higher odds of developing PD, similar odds ratio to that seen in dominant genes, compared to those in the lowest quartile. 4 Like carriers of reduced penetrance mutations (e.g., GBA1), PRS scores do not yet reliably distinguish at the individual level who will or will not develop disease. 3

Return of results to patients with Parkinson's disease

A consistent finding across studies is that many people with PD express an interest in knowing their genetic status. At least four recurrent reasons emerge.

For many individuals, genetic testing offers an explanation. Identifying a pathogenic or susceptibility variant can provide a biological context for disease, particularly in cases previously considered sporadic.29,30 Many studies have shown positive attitudes towards genetic testing among patients with PD with the vast majority expressing interest in testing.30–32 Even without direct therapeutic implications, some patients value this information, and relatives of people with PD may encourage patients to pursue testing in order for them to estimate their own risk. In this setting, return of results may be appropriate with adequate counselling and explicit communication of uncertainty. Although most PD-associated variants do not determine a fixed disease course, certain genotypes have been associated with specific phenotypic patterns. This has implications for how some individuals interpret genetic information. GBA1 variants have been associated in multiple cohorts with earlier cognitive involvement, faster motor progression, and greater non-motor burden compared with non-carriers.14,24 LRRK2-associated disease, in contrast, is often clinically similar to idiopathic PD, although phenotypic variability remains substantial.6,21 Recent large-scale studies in the context of gene-targeted trials indicate that genotype may help inform subtype classification, while individual-level prediction remains limited. 18 As mentioned above, the increasing development of gene-targeted therapies has altered the relevance of genetic testing in PD. Variants in genes such as LRRK2 and GBA1 are now used to define eligibility for clinical trials, and genetic status increasingly determines access to precision-medicine studies.8,33,34 Large international programs illustrate the scale of this shift. In PD GENEration, more than 30,000 individuals with PD underwent structured genetic testing and counseling, with approximately 13% found to carry a pathogenic variant in a PD-associated gene. 23 Similarly, the ROPAD study reported pathogenic variants in approximately 15% of tested individuals across multiple countries. 18 This indicates that a substantial group of patients may qualify for genotype-stratified research.

In addition to the individual patient, knowledge of genetic status has clear implications for family members. This is particularly relevant in PD, where many variants confer increased risk rather than deterministic inheritance.19,21,35,36 In patients of reproductive age, it can inform family planning (e.g., partner testing in recessive conditions such as PRKN), while in older patients, results may be shared with offspring to clarify potential risk. This may be particularly informative in populations with higher variant prevalence, such as GBA1 mutations in Ashkenazi Jewish populations. 37

Discussions should include the availability and limitations of predictive testing and, in some regions, prenatal or preimplantation genetic testing for high-penetrance variant.38,39 At the same time, it must always be emphasized that declining testing does not alter underlying genetic risk. The decision is therefore not whether risk exists, but whether knowing the risk provides more clarity than anxiety. Careful counseling is required to support families in weighing these considerations.

Despite increasing discussion of precision medicine in PD, access to genetic testing and structured counseling remains inconsistent. A 2023 Movement Disorder Society Taskforce survey across four regions, demonstrated access to testing and counseling remains uneven. 13 Even in high-income settings, testing is not routinely offered and counseling capacity is limited.13,40

Disclosure without adequate infrastructure is problematic. Because most PD genetic findings are probabilistic and require clear explanation, without structured counseling, misunderstanding and inequity may increase. Here, caution by providers may reflect limitation of resources rather than resistance to testing and disclosure.41,42

Concerns regarding insurance discrimination are frequently raised in discussions of genetic testing. However, this issue may be less central for affected individuals. Patients who already carry a clinical diagnosis of PD are typically assessed by insurers on the basis of manifest disease rather than genetic status, however there is the concern that insurers could potentially categorize genetic conditions as pre-existing diseases, which could result in denial of coverage or increased premiums . In such cases, genetic information may add limited additional impact to existing medical documentation. 43

Return of results to at risk individuals

Respect for autonomy supports an individual's right to access personal genetic information, even in the absence of disease. Nevertheless, family members often overestimate their own personal risk. Here, appropriate counseling and clear explanation of penetrance and variability, may offer reassurance. 10 The knowledge of carrier status may provide clarity, particularly in families with known pathogenic variants where uncertainty is already present.

As the field shifts toward earlier intervention, genetic status is becoming increasingly relevant for enrollment in prevention or prodromal-stage trials. In this context, identification of pathogenic variants may enable participation in trials designed to delay phenoconversion or modify disease biology before clinical onset. In order to improve the design of prevention of PD trials, it is essential to better understand the natural progression of disease and therefore studies like the Parkinson Progression Marker Initiative (PPMI) 44 which enroll and follow up asymptomatic mutation carriers are key. For some at-risk individuals, access to research participation represents a meaningful benefit. 45

The argument for disclosure is strongest in the context of high-penetrance or autosomal dominant variants. For example, SNCA variants demonstrate age-dependent penetrance, and certain forms of monogenic PD confer substantially increased lifetime risk.6,21 In families with known pathogenic variants, predictive testing may directly inform reproductive decision-making and further testing within the family. As in other neurogenetic disorders, structured pre- and post-test counseling is essential.38,39,46 In this context, non-disclosure may limit informed planning for individuals who wish to make reproductive decisions based on genetic risk.

For asymptomatic individuals, disclosure may introduce psychological burden without altering medical management. At present, there is no established preventive therapy for genetic PD. Knowledge of carrier status may well change perception of health without changing care. Unlike patients who already have PD, at-risk individuals may live for many years with probabilistic information and no defined intervention. In this setting, the clinical utility of immediate disclosure is not always clear. While structured counseling is essential, longitudinal data on the psychological impact of predictive testing in PD remains limited. 47 Another important consideration is that the consequences of genetic testing may vary across cultural and social contexts. In some settings, genetic findings may affect family relationships, marriage prospects, or reproductive decision-making. Stigma associated with inherited disease may extend beyond the individual tested and, in certain cultures, responsibility for a genetic condition may be disproportionately attributed to women, particularly mothers. These potential consequences should be considered during pre-test counselling and disclosure discussions.

Insurance considerations are vastly more consequential for asymptomatic at-risk individuals than for patients who already have PD. In several jurisdictions, protections against genetic discrimination are incomplete. For example, U.S. federal law (GINA) prohibits use of genetic information in health insurance and employment, but does not extend to life, disability, or long-term care insurance, and does not apply once disease is manifest. 43 For at-risk individuals who are otherwise healthy, genetic labeling may therefore carry tangible implications. These considerations require explicit discussion during pre-test counseling.

As discussed above, uncertainty regarding penetrance remains a central limitation in predictive disclosure. Penetrance estimates for variants such as LRRK2 and GBA1 vary across cohorts, and translation of genotype to individual-level prediction remains imprecise.18,21,35,36 This variability complicates disclosure, as probabilistic risk may be interpreted as certainty despite careful counseling.

Current international practices in genetic testing and result disclosure

The most comprehensive assessment of global practice to date derives from the 2023 Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Taskforce survey of international members. 13 Across 568 respondents spanning all MDS geographic regions, substantial heterogeneity in access to genetic testing and counseling was identified. Major barriers included cost (79%), access to testing (64%), and limited clinician knowledge (60%). Notably, 59% of respondents reported low or no access to genetic counseling; however, nearly half of counseling was performed by neurologists, reflecting the growing integration of this role into routine clinical practice.

Even in high-income settings, testing frequency remained low, and marked regional disparities were observed. Pre- and post-test counseling were inconsistently implemented, and structured processes for handling variants of uncertain significance were lacking in many centers. Importantly, no uniform international standard for PD genetic testing or result disclosure currently exists.

In addition to documenting disparities, the Taskforce noted that genetic testing in PD should not take place in the absence of appropriate counselling infrastructure, and that policies regarding disclosure in research settings should be clearly specified within study protocols. Genetic counselling resources should be planned and budgeted prospectively and the task force advised against mandatory disclosure where possible. 13

International practice regarding genetic testing and result disclosure for individuals at risk of PD remains variable and largely non-standardized. Predictive testing in asymptomatic relatives is typically offered in specialist or research settings rather than routine clinical care and is most commonly considered in the context of known familial variants. 10 Overall, current international practice reflects cautious, case-by-case implementation rather than uniform guidance.

Discussion: Returning genetic results in Parkinson's disease. Toward a Pragmatic Framework

Genetic testing in PD is transitioning from a research-focused activity to an increasingly relevant component of clinical care. Large-scale implementation programs demonstrate that testing can be delivered responsibly within routine settings when supported by appropriate counseling infrastructure.29,31

A central consideration of returning genetic results is that the clinical meaning of genetic information in PD is evolving. At present, most findings inform risk stratification, prognosis, or research eligibility rather than direct treatment decisions. However, this distinction is unlikely to persist. As gene-targeted therapies for variants such as LRRK2 and GBA1 move closer to clinical application, genetic status may become integral to therapeutic decision-making. A similar transition has already occurred in Alzheimer's disease, where APOE genotyping has entered clinical practice in the context of anti-amyloid therapies.48,49 In PD, such developments would shift the role of genetic testing from optional to potentially standard of care, reframing the debate from whether patients wish to know their genetic status to whether clinicians are equipped to deliver testing and interpret results appropriately.

This anticipated shift underscores the need for proactive infrastructure development. Without adequate preparation, particularly in counseling capacity, clinician education, and equitable access, implementation risks becoming reactive and may exacerbate disparities. Furthermore, when available treatment options are financially or geographically inaccessible, patients and their families may experience additional psychological distress. Formal studies are required to assess the advantages and downside of returning results in such circumstances. Genetic testing delivered without structured support increases the likelihood of misinterpretation and inappropriate clinical use potentially causing harm to participants. 50

Despite these challenges, the clinical relevance of genetic information in at-risk individuals may increase. As prevention trials expand, genetic status may define eligibility for early interventions. Evidence from Alzheimer's disease suggests that genetic risk may interact with modifiable factors. For example, the detrimental effects of APOE ε4 on cognition have been shown to depend on specific modifiable risk factors such as hypercholesterolemia, cardiovascular disease 51 and hypertension, 52 implying that managing these conditions could attenuate the cognitive vulnerability conferred by the ε4 allele . Although such relationships are not yet established in PD, future work may clarify gene-environment interactions relevant to phenoconversion and progression, enhancing the role of genetics in individualized risk management.

In this context, preparedness is critical. Genetic testing in PD is no longer theoretical, it is increasingly embedded in clinical and research practice. The central challenge is to ensure that the return of results is aligned with evolving clinical utility, ethical principles, and healthcare capacity. A measured, infrastructure-supported approach offers the best path forward, balancing patient autonomy with responsible implementation and equitable access (Figures 2 and 3).
