Aerogel library with varying porous structures and mechanics regulates motor neuron progenitor differentiation for spinal cord injury repair
Authors: Jinfei Hou, Junjin Jie, Di Zhang, Jun Chen, Huimin Fang, Di Sun, Muran Zhou, Jiaming Sun, Lin-Ping Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe properties of external environment have been acknowledged as important factors influencing cell differentiation. Although efforts have been spared to study the influence of properties on cell differentiation, it is still unknown about how porous structure and mechanics of materials regulate the differentiation of motor neuron progenitors (MNPs) for spinal cord repair. Aerogel library with varying pore sizes and moduli fabricated by methacrylated gelatin and various sacrificial biomaterials according to binary cooperative complementary have been built to study the regulation on differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells-derived motor neuron progenitors. After cell implantation via gas-liquid technique, MNPs are more likely to differentiate into motor neuron in materials of smaller pore sizes and lower moduli within the proper range (0–100 μm and 0–3 kPa). The experiments for spinal cord injury model revealed that cell-loaded aerogels with pore size of 20 μm and modulus of 0.4 kPa possessed best repair effects. The research provides a promising strategy for fabrication of structurally multiscale composites and clarified the rules of influences of pore sizes and mechanics on the differentiation of motor progenitors, offering theoretical support for manufacture of neural scaffold materials in clinic.

Introduction

The implantation of biomaterials loaded with stem cells after three-dimensional (3D) culture has been widely applied in bone, skin, vessel, and neural tissue engineering in the field of regenerative medicine [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The 3D culture environment of biomaterials is beneficial for cell differentiation, cell proliferation, and construction of the extracellular matrix [5,6]. With the technological advancements in the past decades, the transplantation of stem cells combined with biomaterials can now achieve the expansion of the target cells in the body, and even the formation of tissues and organs. However, the harsh microenvironment of the damaged tissue usually leads to dedifferentiation of target cells or differentiation of stem cells in the non-target direction, resulting in poor therapeutic effects. Further, it still remains challenges to control the differentiation direction of pluripotent stem cells in vivo and ensure the precise differentiation direction of the target terminal cells [7,8]. Consequently, studying the influences of properties of biomaterials on cell differentiation of stem cells is vital.

Biomaterials have been utilized to influence the directed differentiation of progenitor/stem cells, mainly in biochemical induction and physical stimulation [9]. Inducible biochemical factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) can promote vascular and neural differentiation of stem cells, however the issue of in vivo biosafety has not been resolved [10,11]. In recent years, genetic engineering methods that precisely induce stem cells to perform targeted differentiation by regulating the expression of specific genes have received increased research interest, whereas the balance between inefficiency of gene transfection and cytotoxicity is difficult to achieve [12]. In order to overcome these shortcomings, researchers have proposed methods to realize the differentiation of stem cells through the micro-nano structure of materials, and biological materials can serve as synthetic niches to provide a suitable environment for stem cell differentiation [13]. To examine these effects and apply them to achieve directional differentiation of stem cells by purposely changing the micro/nanostructure and characteristics of biomaterials, previous studies proposed the use of various methods such as 3D printing and nanopillar arrays to fabricate various materials and evaluate their effect on cell differentiation [[14], [15], [16]]. Although limited success has been achieved, their application has been hampered by various obstacles. First, a fabrication technique compatible with biocompatible materials is required to construct scaffolds with a wide range of pore sizes and mechanical properties [17]. Second, a method to examine the mechanism by which the pore sizes and mechanical properties of materials affect cell differentiation—including the triggering mode using the cytoskeleton—needs to be developed [18,19]. Finally, building a stable system to evaluate the dynamic changes in cell differentiation after cell-loaded scaffolds are implanted in vivo is important [20].

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a global health disease that often involves infarction, necrosis, and scar formation of spinal cord tissue, whereas the neuronal injury is usually irreversible [21,22]. As the survival rate of postmitotic motor neurons remains poor, stem cells therapy exhibited great therapeutic effects because the directional differentiation of motor neurons (MNs), particularly as stem cells-derived MNs and motor neuron progenitors (MNPs) are potential regenerative transplantation therapies, and even small changes in endogenous neuron implantation can have a huge impact on motor recovery and quality of life [[23], [24], [25]]. Compared with embryonic stem cells and mesenchymal stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-derived neurons have more application potential, due to the ease of obtaining and minimized ethical concerns. Thus, there is growing interest in spinal cord motor neuron replacement strategies [26,27]. Du et al. utilized small molecules that regulated multiple signaling pathways to guide iPSCs to an enriched population (>90 %) of functionally mature MNs in vitro [28]. However, pure cell transplantation of MNPs without scaffolds or with inapposite scaffolds can result in dedifferentiation or differentiation of progenitors in other directions owing to the undesirable mechanics or microstructure.

Consequently, it is important to study the effects of the micro/nanostructures and mechanics on the differentiation of MNPs and to fabricate scaffolds with sepcific structure and moduli that can guide specific differentiation to MNs. Banerjee et al. reported that neural stem cells do not survive well in very soft (<0.1 kPa) or very hard (>100 kPa) materials and can be prone to neuronal differentiation in reasonably soft materials (≈0.1−1 kPa) and glial differentiation in slightly stiffer materials (≈7−10 kPa) [[29], [30], [31]]. However, the effects of materials with moduli of less than 1 kPa on the cell differentiation efficiency of MNs remains unclear. Additionally, it has been reported that pore sizes less than 100 μm contributes to neural differentiation and formation of neural stem cells [32], whereas the effects of pore size less than 100 μm on the differentiation of MNPs cells have rarely been studied [33].

To overcome these problems, a biomaterial system was developed to detect the differentiation rules of MNPs regulated by microstructures and mechanical properties. Here, we constructed a scalable aerogel library (AL) of methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) as a testing platform based on the binary cooperative complementary concept, which is analogous to the Yin-Yang principle in Chinese philosophy. In other words, these aerogels before freeze-drying and incubation were mainly consist of water-soluble materials and water-insoluble materials. It is theoretically feasible to control the mechanical properties and pore sizes of GelMA aerogels by adding very few sacrificial biomaterials to change the growth and sublimation of ice crystals during freeze–drying [[34], [35], [36]]. After incubation in an aqueous solution, these water-soluble biomaterials are eluted, and GelMA aerogels with varying pore sizes and moduli can be fabricated. The preparation of scaffolds with special topologies has also been explored. Besides, MNPs are implanted into scaffolds using a novel method named the Er-Qi technique, based on the gas–liquid exchange, to avoid the negative effects of the top-down strategy. The effects of the structural and mechanical characteristics of scaffolds on the transformation of MNPs can then be evaluated in vitro. Additionally, the regulatory mechanism of its influences can be further examined by observing the changes in the cytoskeleton and expression of Yes-associated proteins (YAPs). Finally, the cells-loaded scaffolds were transplanted into a mouse model of SCI and the behavior and histological evaluation were performed to further determine their influence on cell differentiation.

Materials and methods

Preparation of aerogel library

The synthesis of GelMA was described as before. Briefly the 10 % gelation solution was prepared and added with methacrylamide solution at the speed of 20 μL/min in stirring (1000 revolutions per minute). The mixture solution was stirred for at least 2 h and then separated by 12 kDa dialysis bag. After freeze-drying, GelMA is stored at 4 °C. For the construction of scalable aerogel library, 5 % GelMA solution was prepared and the corresponding materials was added into the solution. The sacrificial materials include collagen (0.2 %, 0.5 % and 0.8 %), chitosan (0.1 %, 0.2 % and 0.3 %), PEG (0.1 %, 0.5 % and 0.75 %), PAM (0.1 %, 0.2 % and 0.3 %), PVA (0.1 %, 0.2 % and 0.3 %) and F-127 (0.1 %, 0.5 % and 0.75 %). 0.05 % LAP was dissolved in the solution as photo-initiator and then the solution was put into mold for photo-crosslinking under condition of blue light. The parameters for freeze-drying of samples were set as −70 °C and 4 Pa for 48 h. Then aerogels were sterilization in ethylene oxide and then incubated in deionized water or basal medium at 37 °C for 3 days to elute the sacrifice materials. The deionized water or basal medium were changed once per 8 h. To ensure the complete elution of sacrificial materials, FTIR spectrum of aerogels before and after incubation were conducted in the frequency range of 4000–400 cm−1 in FTIR spectroscopy (VERTEX 70, Bruker, Germany).

Characterization, morphology and mechanical properties of scalable aerogel library

To observe the surface structure of aerogels, a scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU-70, HITACHI, Japan) was applied. The accelerating voltage was set at 10 kV and the surfaces of aerogels were sprayed with platinum using a sputter coater prior to measurement. The elastic modulus tests were measured via an all-electric dynamic test instrument (Instron, British). The aerogels were fabricated as cylinders with diameter of 8 mm and height of 3 mm and the surface have to be smooth. The incubated aerogels were sterilized as mentioned above and then immersed in F12 medium with 1 % Matrigel solution after 72 h. The samples placed in the central of platform and were compressed at a constant rate of 1 mm/min. The experiments were ended at a 50 % strain and then strain-stress curves were plotted and moduli were calculated. All of the above data and plots were obtained using OriginPro software (OriginLab, USA). The coefficient of variation (CV) for six batches in each group was calculated via the ratio of standard deviation to mean value. For swelling ratio experiments, aerogels were immersed in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) at 37 °C for 24 h and the weight of aerogels before and after immersion was measured by analytical balance (GL124i-1SCN, Sartorius, German). Generation and expansion of MNPs from induced pluripotent stem cells

The skin fibroblasts derived iPSCs were obtained from South Stem Cell Bank in Guangzhou (Ethical batch number: GIBH-IRB2019-032), were generated by transduction using pMX-based retroviruses by supplement of Vitamin C (Vc) and valproic acid (VPA) as described and The iPSCs were dissociated with Disapse (1 mg/mL) and split into the Matrigel coated cell-culture plates. The basal medium and induction medium were compounded as illustrated in Table S1. After cultured for 6 days in induction medium (A) and following 6 days in induction medium (B) at 37 °C under 5 % CO2, MNPs can be harvest.

The Er-Qi technique for cell implantation of AL

To avoid the disadvantages of the top-down strategy, aerogels were preprocessed as following. Aerogels were sterilized and incubated in F12 medium with 1 % matrigel for 72 h. Then the incubated aerogels were vacuum applied with homemade air pump in four directions for 2 h. The cell suspension at the concentration of 106/mL was dropwise added into the aerogels. After cell implantation, the aerogels were put on the thermostatic oscillator in the cell incubator for 4 h. Finally, the medium was added into the culture dish until aerogels were completely submerged.

In vitro cell biocompatibility of AL

MNPs were implanted in aerogels via Er-Qi technique. After culture for 3 days at 37 °C under 5 % CO2, FDA/PI staining of samples were performed as previously reported. The CCK-8 assay was performed as follows. After sterilization, aerogels were cut into slides with a diameter of 2 mm, and immerse in 24-well plates filled with induction medium (B). MNPs were collected and prepared at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/mL, and the 100 μL of the cell suspension was added to each well of a 96-well plate. The cell culture medium was replaced with 200 μL supernatant (complete medium for blank group) of ever group at time point of 1, 3 and 7 days. Besides, for CCK-8 experiments a mixture of 100 μL complete medium and 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The absorbance of culture medium after incubation at 450 nm were tested at microplate reader (ELx800, BioTek, USA). Red blood cells (RBCs) in hemolysis experiments were isolated from mice in accordance with the relevant ethics committee (Approval No. N2019066). After washing and concentration in 0.9 % normal saline, plasma was removed and RBCs were collected and finally suspended in pH 7.4 PBS. The RBCs suspension were contacted with PBS (negative group), 1 % Triton X-100 solution (9036-19-5, Sigma-Aldrich, America) (positive group) and different aerogel groups for 3h. The supernate of different group was transferred to a new 96-well plate and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm with microplate reader.

Fabrication and characterization of aerogels with complex topological structures

The 3D printer was used to fabricate aerogels with complex structures. The 3D models of concentric circle structures, multiple-layered structures and word with two or more kind of aerogels were printed. The pre-crosslinking solution were frozen under 25 °C, then added to the sample tube and extruded to construct scaffolds as STL files plotted via software NX10.0. After polymerization under UV light, the scaffolds were frozen-dried for 24h.

The preparation of cells-loaded aerogels was mentioned before. Briefly, the samples were fixed and processed by the method of gradient dehydration. In vivo implantation evaluation on mouse spinal cord injury model

Aerogel scaffolds were fabricated as cylinder of diameters of 5 mm and height of 2 mm via the method of molding and were implanted into the mice spinal cord injury model to assess the cell differentiation in aerogels in vivo further. All procedures involving animals surgery and care were performed at the Guangzhou Institute of Biomedicine and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences. These procedures were reviewed and approved by the relevant ethics committee (Approval No. N2019066). The non-obese diabetic scid gamma mice (NSG) mice were chosen for this research. The mice were accepted with general anesthesia with isoflurane in oxygen-enriched air using an operating microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). After the mice were completely unconscious, the hair on the neck were shaved and applied with hair removal cream. Then the mice were fixed on rodent stereotaxic apparatus (David Kopf, Tujunga, CA) and operation region (T10 segment of the thoracic vertebrae) was disinfected. The dorsa skin and subcutaneous fascia was incised until the spine could be seen. The spinous process and surrounding muscle tissue in the T9-11 segment area were sheared off to fully expose the T9-11 segment lamina. The T10 segment lamina was removed with hemostatic forceps and the T10 segment spinal cord was exposed. The T10 segment of spinal cord were carefully removed with microsurgical scissors and then aerogels loaded with MNPs were implanted in injury region. Finally, subcutaneous fascia tissue and skin were sutured layer by layer. Besides, the sham group was treated with the procedures except the injury of spinal cord. The mice were evaluated in 1, 2 and 3 months.

The BMS locomotor rating scale was used to access locomotor function and the standard has been illustrated before. Moreover, a series of behavioral assessments were conducted. Open-field evaluation was applied on mice at 1, 2 and 3 months after implantation and they were put into a plastic square room with black and non-slippery base (45 cm length of side, 21 cm height). The movement has been recorded via digital camera for 10 min and data were analyzed by software. The red lines indicated the movement trails and the distance moved and duration of not moving were measured. Gait analysis experiments were carried out via DigiGait instrument (Mouse Specifics Inc, America) on mice at 1, 2 and 3 months after implantation. The belt remained flat and its speed of belt was set to 10 mm/s. After mice ran on the belt for at least 10 s, corresponding parameters were recorded. The grip strength tests were also performed. The force of forelimbs and hindlimbs were measured and recorded via Bio-GS3 (RWD, Chnia).

Electrophysiological assessment was performed on mice implanted with cell-loaded aerogels at 1, 2 and 3 months by using an electromyography system (Neuro-MEP-Micro, Russia). After anesthesia, the dorsal hair was shaved, and a stimulation electrode was inserted into the rostral extremity of the exposed spinal cord region (C10 segment of thoracic vertebrae) to induce motor evoked potentials (MEPs). Besides, one electrode was placed on the gastrocnemius muscle and another electrode was placed on the lower jaw. The stimulus current was applied at intensity of 1 mA and pulse duration was 0.1 ms with bandpass filtering from 10 Hz to 10 KHz. Three different samples (n = 3) were analyzed for each group.

The histological analysis was mainly performed in H&E staining and immunofluorescent staining. The detailed information about antibodies used was listed in Table S2. The HE staining images were captured by scanning microscope (VMV1 VMDPCS, Motic, China) and laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM800, Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation. Each assay had at least three replicates and was repeated three times independently. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey, Sidak, or Bonferroni correction or the two-tailed Student's t-test was performed to analyze the statistical significance. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of AL

Fig. 1A shows the AL fabrication procedure. Aerogels were fabricated using GelMA in combination with various water-soluble sacrificial biomaterials, such as collagen, chitosan, polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyacrylamide (PAM), and Pluronic F127. The addition of these materials changes the structural formation and sublimation process of the ice crystals during freezing and drying of the gels, forming aerogels with different internal structures, thereby further affecting their pore sizes and moduli. This result also demonstrates that by changing the concentration and variety of the sacrificial material, it is possible to achieve controllable and scalable fabrication of aerogels with different pore sizes and mechanical properties within a certain range.

Fabrication and characteristic of aerogel library. A, Illustration of fabrication process for aerogel library. B, The pore sizes and elastic modulus of aerogels.

The corresponding pore sizes of aerogels and histograms illustrating the pore size distribution of aerogels are shown in Fig. 1B and Figs. S1 and S2 (see supporting information), which indicated that the pore sizes of aerogels are in the range of 0–100 μm. After incubation in deionized water for 3 days, the sacrificial biomaterials were eluted, and the moduli of the GelMA aerogels decreased. To confirm the complete elution of sacrificial materials, FTIR spectrum were utilized to clarified the composites of scalable aerogls with the other sacrifice materials at maximum concentration. From Fig. S3, characteristic peak has been marked for different aerogels before and after incubation. After incubation, these curves exhibited almost identical characteristic peaks, proving the complete elution and no-residual of the other biomaterials.

The swelling ratio of aergels in 24 h were shown in Fig. S4. The results indicated that typical groups of aerogels presented a swelling ratio of ≈2000 % owing to its inherently highly porous and low-density properties. Comparatively, the elution of sacrifice materials have minor impact on the weight change of aerogels after incubation. Moreover, the modulus of the scalable aerogels decreases from the megapascal (MPa) to the kilopascal (kPa) range, making them more suitable for culturing MNPs. It is evident from Fig. 1B and Fig. S5 that the modulus is in the range of 0–3 kPa. It worth noting that we have analyzed CV of moduli of aerogels after incubation for data reliability. As can be seen from Table S3, CV of moduli of most aerogels (>70 %) were lower than 10 % and CV of all aerogels were lower than 15 %. It has been reported that materials with CV < 20 % were supposed to posse high reproducibility and minimal variation between batches [37], which confirmed the robustness of our fabrication process.

Cell implantation of MNPs and biocompatibility of AL

The induction of iPSCs to MNPs follows serial and overlapping developmental processes, as reported in a previous study [28]. A simple identification of iPSCs was shown in Fig. S6. Twelve days after introduction, neural spheres were evident and distinct biomarkers of Olig2 appear around the MNPs (Fig. S7). Choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) positive MNs could be sequentially induced in a specific medium from MNPs (efficiency >90 %) in vitro.

The biocompatibility of implant materials is vital and determines their application potential [6]. Consequently, fluorescein diacetate (FDA)/propidium iodide (PI) staining of MNPs on scaffolds in vitro was used to observe cell adhesion and proliferation in the aerogels (Fig. 2A and Fig. S8). MNPs could adhere and proliferate on scaffolds at different time points, and the differences of viability between each aerogel group in the library are not distinct. CCK-8 assay results supported the above findings, demonstrating the aerogels' biocompatibility and lack of cytotoxicity (Fig. 2B). For biosafety of blood contact, the hemolysis experiments for most common groups in aerogel library were performed. As can be seen from Fig. S9, all aerogel groups did not occur obvious hemolysis and absorbance at 540 nm of aerogel groups were significantly lower than these of negative group, which further demonstrated the biosafety of aerogel library.

Because of the technical limits of fabrication, MNPs have to be implanted into aerogels via the conventional top-down strategy. The disadvantage of this strategy was that cells are prone to aggregate, whereas the essential focus of this study is the effect of the internal structure of biomaterials on cell differentiation of MNPs [6]. The gas-liquid exchange technique—inspired by the concept of “Qi” in China and referred to as the Er-Qi technique—was implemented to distribute cells evenly in the scaffolds (Fig. 2C). Along with the pressure of exchange, the cell suspension circulated through the scaffolds, with MNPs being much more evident in the central region using the Er-Qi technique than via conventional techniques. Moreover, they are evident in every vertical hierarchy of the scaffolds (Fig. 2D and Fig. S10A and S10B). Additionally, the results shown in Fig. S10C demonstrated that potential pressure damage to cells was absent.

Fabrication of topological structures is essential for biomaterials, particularly for tissues and organs with complex compositions [38,39]. Because of 3D-printability of GelMA hydrogels, aerogels can be constructed as neural scaffolds with different pore sizes and moduli after freeze-drying of 3D printed scaffolds [40]. The porous structures of the 3D-printed models with two or three materials were shown in Fig. 2E, where a clear dividing line between the different pore sizes is evident. Additionally, concentric circles and layered scaffolds of various pore sizes were printed (Fig. S11). The moduli of the scaffolds of various materials exhibited a similar pattern in the stress–strain curve (Fig. 2F).

In vitro evaluation of effects of aerogels’ structures on MNP differentiation

To evaluate the effects of aerogels on the differentiation of MNPs, groups of P25M0.4, P50M0.4, P70M0.4, P25M0.6, and P25M1 (“P” representing the pore size and “M” representing the modulus) with gradually increasing pore sizes and moduli were selected for the study. To ensure the reliability of cell differentiation experiments, selected aerogel scaffolds were cultured in medium for 7 days and the relative modulus before and after culture was tested. The results shown in Fig. S12 illustrated that aerogels could maintain their mechanical properties significantly unchanged. After transplanting MNPs on the scaffolds via the Er-Qi technique, the induction medium was replaced with a basal medium. As shown in Fig. 3A and Fig. S13A and S13B, the MNPs were prone to differentiate into ChAT positive MNs with decreasing pore size and increasing modulus. Interestingly, the number of Olig2 positive MNPs decreased as expected, however the difference between the groups was not apparent (Fig. S13C and S13D). Additionally, biomarkers of astrocytes (GFAP) and oligodendrocytes (CNPase) were detected in the MNP-loaded aerogels (Fig. S14). Moreover, to minimize the potential impact of residual sacrificial materials on cell differentiation, aerogels fabricated from different materials but with comparable pore sizes and mechanical properties were selected for subsequent cytological analysis (Fig. 3B). As shown in Fig. 3C and D and Figure S15, there was no significant difference in neuronal differentiation between the P25M0.4 and 0.1 % PVA or P25M0.6 and 0.2 % PAM, which indicated that generally no-residual sacrificial materials that might affect cell behavior stayed inside aerogels.

The efficiency of induction is vital, and the basal medium was replaced with an induction medium to determine the status of MN differentiation (Fig. 3E). The experiments demonstrated that the percentage of ChAT positive MNs in the P25M0.4 group was more than that in the tissue culture plate (TCP) on Day 6 and lower in the P70M0.4 and P25M0.6 group than that in the TCP group on Day16 (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the induction effects of the 3D culture of MNPs were compared with those of the 2D culture in the aerogels (Fig. 3G). The number of ChAT positive MNs in the 3D culture was much higher than that in the 2D culture, particularly for the P25M0.4 group, a phenomenon that was not evident in the P25M0.6 group (Fig. 3H and I).

The cytoskeleton is closely associated with stem cell differentiation [41]. The internal structure and mechanical properties of materials can affect the cytoskeleton by reconstructing the F-actin network and initiating a cascade signaling pathway, thereby affecting the final direction of cell differentiation [42]. Consequently, cytoskeletal staining and distribution analyses were performed on the cells implanted in the aerogels (Fig. 4A. The formation of actin stress fibers was confirmed to be related to the internal structure of the material [43]. The cytoskeleton of the P25M0.4 group was considerably slender and more dispersed among cells than those of the P50M0.4 and P70M0.4 groups, confirming that it was prone to differentiating into neurons. Additionally, the correlation between the actin fiber arrangements in the cells was examined. It is evident from images analysis that in the P25M0.6 and P25M0.4 groups, the cytoskeleton presents a looser arrangement, and the actin arrangement becomes more uniform with a decrease in the modulus, which is also evidence of cell differentiation into neurons. In fact, it has been reported that pluripotent stem cells present a dispersed cytoskeletal arrangement, whereas differentiated cells often present an aligned and consistent cytoskeletal morphology [44]. This confirmed that the internal mechanical properties and structure of materials regulate the process of cell differentiation and are closely related to cytoskeletal changes. Additionally, the aerogels loaded with cells were observed using SEM (Fig. 4B and Fig. S16). The results of cell morphology indicated that the cells in the P25M0.4 group were longer and more slender than those in the P50M0.4 and P70M0.4 groups and the cells in the P70M0.4 group merged and remained present like MNPs. The cells in the P25M0.4 group appeared more like neurons than those in the P25M0.6 and P25M0.6 groups. The results for the cell area further verified this phenomenon.

The mechanism underlying the effects of aerogels on cell differentiation of MNPs was also investigated. YAP, a subcellular transcriptional coactivator associated with cytoskeletal tension, can gauge the mechanical stimulation of the signaling pathway and affect stem cell differentiation (Fig. 5A and B) [18]. The cytoskeleton and YAP were stained using immunofluorescence and the effects of cytoskeletal tension produced by the actin fibers on the level of nuclear translocation and differentiation of YAP were investigated. As shown in Fig. 5C and D and Figure S17, the cytoplasmic localization of the YAP was controlled by the moduli and pore sizes of the aerogels. The YAP remained in the cytoplasm with a gradual change in pore size. Meanwhile, the YAP migrated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, with a decline in the mechanical properties of the aerogels, indicating that MNPs changed from a deactivated state to an activated state [45]. The characteristics of the stained cytoskeleton were further examined, with the results of Feter's diameter of cells and the percentage of ChAT positive cells indicating neuronal differentiation (Fig. 5E). To further investigate this mechanism, the MNP-loaded aerogels were treated with Y27632, an inhibitor of RhoA-associated protein kinase (ROCK). Following YAP inhibition, the negative effects of mechanical cues on neuronal differentiation were abolished, and the differences among the P25M0.4, P25M0.6, and P25M1 groups were no longer observed (Fig. 5F and G). For further investigation of the role of YAP in regulation on cell differentiation, we performed YAP knockdown experiments based on siRNA. As could be seen from Fig. S18A, the expression of YAP was significantly down-regulated, which indicated the effectiveness of siRNA transfection. In this condition, we further analysis the differentiation of MNPs in Fig. S18B. The results illustrated that the expression of ChAT in P25M0.4-siRNA group was significantly lower than that of P25M0.4 group, which indicated that the influence of mechanical properties on cell differentiation significantly weakened after knockdown of YAP. This finding has further confirmed by the results that there were no significant difference of relative expression of ChAT between P25M0.4-siRNA group and P25M1-siRNA group.

In vivo investigation of aerogels implanted in a mouse model of SCI

To assess the effects of the mechanical properties and surface characteristics of the aerogels on in vivo neural regeneration, a mouse SCI model was established, in which the repair outcomes were evaluated based on the extent of neural differentiation [46]. In the animal experiments, MNPs-loaded aerogels from the same groups as in the cytological experiments selected from a scalable aerogel library were implanted in the injured region to repair the defects. After 1, 2, and 3 months, the mice underwent behavioral evaluation and electrophysiological assessment and were harvested for histological analysis (Fig. 6A). The Basso mouse scale (BMS) score was used to evaluate the repair effects (Fig. 6B) [47]. Minimal locomotor recovery was evident in the first week. Most mice in the SCI group exhibited complete hind limb paralysis after 2 weeks. At the same time point, the mice in the P25M0.4 group exhibited better repair effects than those in the other groups, with this difference becoming more distinct after 6 weeks. The mice in the P70M0.4 group exhibited worse repair effects than those in the other aerogel-implanted groups after 3 months. In contrast, most mice in the P25M0.4 group showed continuous recovery, including restored walking ability, weight support, and the initiation of coordinated movement between the forelimbs and hind limbs. The macroscopic appearance of the hind limbs was also consistent with the results shown in Fig. 6C.

To further investigate the repair effects, gait analysis was performed to assess motor impairments associated with SCI [48,49]. The gait position, time-paw area curves, and variations in paw area at different time points are shown in Fig. 6D, Fig. S19 and S20. The mice in the SCI group could barely be recorded, with minimal intermittent signal noise, and the variation in the paw area was low. In contrast, regular gait rhythms were evident in the mice of the P25M0.4 group, with the variation in the paw area being most similar to the sham (placebo) group after 3 months. After 1 month, the mice in the P50M0.4 group exhibited paw area curves comparable to those of the P25M0.4 group. It remained superior to that of the P70M0.4 group, despite a decline in performance after three months. Additionally, differences in the paw area curves of the P25M0.6 and P25M0.6 groups were not evident, and the gait pattern of mice in the P25M0.6 group was more stable than that of mice in the P25M0.6 group. The variant rate for the paw area (MAX dA/dT) became more obvious among the groups after 3 months (Fig. 6E).

Postural parameters are also important for gait analysis [50]. A wider base of support for the hind limbs and a smaller rotation angle indicate better recovery of the motor function. Fig. 6F showed that the base of support for the hind limb in the P25M0.4 and P50M0.4 groups was wider than that in the P70M0.4 group, and the stance of the P25M0.6 group was wider than that of the P25M0.4 group. The P25M0.4 group exhibited the smallest rotation angle among all the groups. The rotation angle of the P50M0.4 group is smaller than that of the P70M0.4 group after three months, and the rotation angle of the P25M0.6 group is smaller than that of the P25M0.6 group (Fig. 6G).

Open field tests were conducted to evaluate the activity of the mice [51]. The results of the movement trial were shown in Fig. 7A. In terms of density and complexity, the mice in the cell-loaded aerogel groups behaved better than those in the SCI group and recovered better after 3 months than during the first 2 months. The mice of the P25M0.4 group moved more actively than those of the P50M0.4 and P25M0.6 groups. Additionally, the mice in the P70M0.4 group moved far less, and the mice in the P25M0.6 group barely moved at all, particularly after 1 month (Fig. S21). Electrophysiological assessments were also used to determine the repair effects [52,53]. Electrical stimulation was triggered at the high position of the cervical cord (C7) and electrical signals were detected in the gastrocnemius muscle of the hind limbs (Fig. 7B). The mice in the P25M0.4 group achieved a motor-evoked potential (MEP) amplitude that was higher than those in the P50M0.4 and P25M0.6 groups, indicating enhanced recovery of evoked responses by scaffolds with a smaller pore size and lower modulus (Fig. 7C). The latency results were similar to the amplitude results, with the latency of the P25M0.4 group being the shortest and similar to that of the sham group after 3 months (Fig. 7D). Finally, grip tests were conducted to assess hind limb recovery (Fig. 7E). As shown in Fig. 7F, mice in the P25M0.4 and P25M0.6 groups had a higher grip strength in the hind limbs than those in the P50M0.4 group, with the grip strength of the P25M0.6 and P70M0.4 groups being lower after 3 months. The results of the fore limb grip test were the opposite, partly because of compensatory mechanisms.

To delineate the specific contribution of the transplanted cells, P25M0.4-cell free (P25M0.4-CF), P70M0.4-cell free (P70M0.4-CF) and P25M1-cell free (P25M1-CF) aerogels were implanted in vivo to investigate the influences of autologous characteristics of biomaterials on neural differentiation. As can be seen from Fig. S22A and B, the BMS score of P25M0.4-CF group was significantly higher than that of P70M0.4-CF group and P25M1-CF group, and lower than that of P25M0.4 group in Fig. 6B. The results of open field tests in Fig. S22C and D showed that mice in P25M0.4-CF group moved much longer distances than the other two groups. This indicated that cell-free scaffolds might provide a supportive bridge at the lesion site and build a better micronenvironment in vivo to some extent.

Histological analyses were performed to evaluate cell differentiation and tissue repair. The results of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining are shown in Fig. S23 and the general repair effects were observed. After 1 month, most groups showed lesion volumes and disordered structures. However, after 3 months, no obvious cavities were found, and a linear and ordered structure was evident in the regenerative regions in the P25M0.4 group. Consequently, aerogels loaded with MNPs promoted SCI recovery and reduced the cavity volume. Besides, we performed migration rate of interfacial cells into aerogels in 1 month. It could be seen from Fig. S24, every aerogel group showed cell migration in depth of 1 mm, which might be correlated with high porosity of aerogels, and there was no significant difference among aerogel group. Furthermore, as vascular regeneration is essential for repair of spinal cord injury, CD31 immumohistochemical staining was conduct to evaluate the angiogensis in aerogels after implantation in 3 months [54]. The results shown in Fig. S25 indicated that the number of CD31 positive blood vessels in the aerogels groups was significantly higher than these in SCI group, suggesting enhanced angiogenesis in the aerogel-implanted sites. Interestingly, the number of vessels in groups with P25 was higher than that of groups with P50 and P75, and there was no different among groups with different moduli. These findings were consistent with the improved functional recovery observed in P25T0.4 group.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as the main method for evaluation in vivo [55,56]. Firstly, as the first 7-day acute phase is crucial for repair effects after SCI, inflammation-related immunofluorescence staining has been performed to analyze the overall infiltration of microglia (Iba1, ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1) and their polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (iNOS, inducible nitric oxide synthase) in the lesion region [57]. As can be observed in Fig. S26A, Iba1 positive cells in SCI group were aggregated and their shapes became rounder, more likely activated than these in other groups. For quantitative analysis in Fig. S26B, we specifically focused on the ratio of number of iNOS positive cells to Iba1 positive cells, a reliable indicator of acute pro-inflammatory response. Compared to the SCI group, the lesion area at 7 days post-SCI in aerogels group showed a significant lower ratio of iNOS+/Iba1+ cells. The difference among aerogels group were not obvious. These new findings strongly indicated that aerogel could modulate immune microenvironment to a certain extent during the acute phase of injury. More importanly, for the differentiation of transplanted cells, as shown in the images of the injured and normal regions in Fig. 8A and Fig. S27, tissue regeneration was evident 1 month after the transplantation of the cells-loaded aerogels. Regular cord-like tissue could be observed in the P25M0.4 group, whereas many cell masses remained deposited in the other aerogel groups. It was evident that the cells in each group were neatly arranged to form fibrous tissue after 2 months. At the end of tissue repair, the regenerating axon fibers in each group filled the entire area of injury, forming a connection with the normal spinal cord tissue. Subsequently, cell differentiation of MNPs after aerogel implantation in vivo was investigated. Here, 1 and 2 months after transplantation, the ChAT positive cells in the P25M0.4 group were the highest among all the aerogel groups (Fig. S28). The distinction became clear until 3 months, and it was evident from Fig. 8a that the number of ChAT positive cells was as follows: P25M0.4 > P50M0.4 > P70M0.4 and P25M0.4 > P25M0.6 > P25M0.6 group. After 1 and 2 months, there was no major difference in the number of Olig2 positive cells between the cell-loaded aerogel groups (Fig. S29). After 3 months, the number Olig2 positive cells in the P25M0.4 and P50M0.4 groups was much lower than those in the P70M0.4 group. The number of Olig2 positive cells in the P25M0.4 group was similar to those in the P25M0.6 and P25M0.6 groups. Consequently, we can infer that this phenomenon can be associated with the recruitment of endogenous cells.

To determine whether the mechanism of aerogel implantation and tissue regeneration was related to cell recruitment, we immunostained samples with hNA (a biomarker of human cells) after 1, 2, and 3 months postoperatively to trace the endogenous cells (Fig. 8B and C, and Figure S30) [58]. It was inferred from Fig. 8b that a considerable enrichment of ChAT positive cells is evident in most groups, particularly after 3 months. In the P70M0.4 group, plenty of cells remained undifferentiated or differentiated to another direction. Moreover, ChAT positive MNs could be differentiated from native Olig2 positive or Olig2 negative MNPs, and it was possible that the surrounding ChAT positive MNs migrated to the injured region.

Interestingly, not all Olig2 positive MNPs differentiated into ChAT positive MNs, even in the P25M0.4 group, making it important to examine the differentiation fate of these MNPs. MNs expressing MNX1 were in an intermediate state at the stage of differentiation from Olig2 positive MNPs to ChAT positive MNs [28]. Consequently, immunofluorescence staining for MNX1 and Tuj1 was performed (Fig. 8A and D, and Figure S31). The number of MNX1 positive cells of the P25M0.4 group was much lower than those in the other aerogel groups, and the differences between these groups were not apparent. The results of Tuj1 staining showed that the length of Tuj1 in the P70M0.4 group was much shorter than those in the other aerogel groups, indicating that MNPs in the P70M0.4 group might differentiate into immature MNs and remain in this stage, even after 3 months.

Lastly, the maturity of MNs is another factor that affects cell differentiation. Consequently, MAP2 was stained and its length was measured (Fig. 8A and E, and Figure S32). After 1 month, the MN lengths were: P25M0.4 > P50M0.4 > P70M0.4 and P25M0.4 > P25M0.6 group. Nevertheless, the difference between the P25M0.6 and P25M0.6 groups was not significant. After 3 months, it was evident that the length of MAP2-stained fibers in the P70M0.4 group was much shorter than those in the other aerogel groups.

Discussion

In this study, a scalable aerogel library with different porosities and mechanics was constructed based on GelMA. GelMA is a common biomaterial widely used in regenerative medicine, and plenty of research have verified its biosafey and biodegradability [59]. It mainly relies on enzymatic degradation and can be degraded by matrix metalloproteinases into non-toxic amino acids and peptides, demonstrating its feasibility of long-term application in vivo [60]. Our research focused on confirming biocompatibility of GelMA via several experiments such as FDA/PI staining, CCK-8 assay and hemolysis experiments. The results also indicated the good biosafey and biocompatibility, which was consistent with reported literature [55]. Associated research on the influence of material characteristics—such as the surface structure and mechanical properties—on cell differentiation has been conducted in recent years [9,10]. Vining et al. reported that the mechanical properties of myelofibrosis—namely, the liquid-to-solid properties (viscoelasticity) of bone marrow—contributed to the aberrant differentiation of monocytes [61]. Chaudhuri et al. revealed that the cell spreading, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells were enhanced in cells cultured in gels with faster relaxation [62]. Zhang et al. reported that the osteogenic differentiation of human adipose-derived stem cells can be driven by nanopillar arrays, particularly those with a diameter of 200 nm [14]. Although the above research is of great significance, the rules of neural differentiation impact by properties of materials within a certain range needs to be studied. According to the reported literatures, neural stem cells were more likely to differentiate into neurons in materials with a modulus within the range of 0.1–1 kPa [[29], [30], [31]]. Meanwhile, pores of the order of 30 μm in diameter can promote cell infiltration and microvascularization [63]. Therefore, this research is an in-depth study based on the aforementioned results and selected aerogels with pore sizes and moduli within the range of 0–100 μm and 0.1–1 kPa, respectively. The results showed that MNPs in aerogels with a pore size of 25 μm and modulus of 0.4 kPa were more likely to differentiate into ChAT positive MNs and the specific directional differentiation efficiency decreased with the increase in pore size and mechanical properties within the above range. The rules mentioned above holds vital guiding significance for the subsequent preparation of materials used for neural differentiation and repair.

To fabricate scaffolds with different micro/nano surface characterizations and various mechanical properties, a scalable aerogel library was built based on the theory that the freeze-drying process affects the formation of the internal structure. In this field, considerable effort has been made to fabricate scaffolds. For instance, Zeng et al. reported a high-throughput combinatorial printing method capable of fabricating materials with compositional gradients at microscopic spatial resolution [17]. Stevens et al. explored the application of complementary network bioinks in tissue engineering and ultimately satisfied the biological needs of cell culture and tissue engineering by screening the appropriate concentration of hydrogel stiffness to achieve 3D printing and culture of astrocytes [64]. In this study, we used sacrificial materials with water solubility to change the mode of ice crystal formation, thereby directing scaffolds with the same composition but different characteristics. For the research of mechanics influence on biology, this principle provides an effective and versatile solution for constructing complex platform.

Besides, it is important to ensure a uniform and random distribution of cells in the scaffolds in this type of research, avoiding local aggregation that leads to inaccurate results [3]. Although a “bottom-up” strategy eliminates such drawbacks, the dilemma remains in studies in which cells cannot be added during scaffold fabrication, for instance, in our research. Consequently, we proposed a novel strategy (known as the Er-Qi technique) that depended on gas–liquid changes to achieve uniform cell distribution. After cell implantation via Er-Qi technique, the cell number in central showed no significant different with cell number in the edge, similar results shown in the every vertical hierarchy. This operation was easy and simple to perform in the laboratory and exhibited virtually no negative effects on the cells, demonstrating its potential for future research.

The cellular mechanisms underlying the factors that affect differentiation were also examined in this study. Surface characterization affects cell differentiation by altering the patterns of cell proliferation or morphological characteristics of cell adhesion [65]. It has been reported that human adipose-derived stem cells implanted into films with pillars of different diameters exhibited different cells-spreading areas and morphologies, resulting in different osteogenic differentiation efficiency [14]. This study demonstrated that the pore size affects MNPs differentiation mainly by changing the contact inhibition and aggregation effects of cells, for instance, larger pore sizes caused stem cells to aggregate and they were less likely to differentiate into neurons. Additionally, mechanical properties can affect the differentiation and transformation of cytoskeletal dynamics and YAP-associated signal transduction [37]. As demonstrated in previous studies, different mechanical properties of the interface can affect the elongation direction and thickness of the cytoskeleton, and unfavorable mechanical cues can misdirect the cell fate by activating the nuclear localization of the YAP [66]. In this study, the lower the elastic modulus of the material, the more elongated the cytoskeleton, with the YAP appearing outside of the nucleus, making it easier to differentiate into specific cells.

Finally, considering the complexity of the in vivo environment, more comprehensive indicators are required to evaluate cell differentiation after cell scaffold transplantation in vivo. Cell differentiation can be assessed by behavioral evaluation [67]. In this study, evaluations were divided into direct and indirect evaluations. The indirect evaluation primarily relied on functional recovery to evaluate the spinal cord repair effects, reflecting cell differentiation to a certain extent, whereas the direct evaluation used cell immunostaining to analyze the percentage of specific cell populations and evaluate the effects of exogenous cell recruitment. The combination of these two methods played an important role in this study and demonstrated considerable potential for future cell implantation SCI therapies [68].

However, this study also has some limitations. First, a wider range of pore sizes and modulus should have been included in the study and it is essential to examine more types of neural stem cells to verify the effectiveness of the influence rules. Second, as we mainly focus on cell differentiation of human-derived MNPs, the animal model used in this study was immunodeficient mice to avoid the immune system's attack on human-derived cells [69]. Although the weakened inflammatory response proved its safety of implantation of aerogels, it cannot be denied that the further clinical translation in the future indeed required more reliable evaluation such as large animal model. Last but not least, for further clinical application, a small rodent animal, such as mice, is not enough to conform its availability, and hence, it should be tested in other larger animal models such as cynomolgus monkey.

Conclusion

In summary, a new library with tunable porosity and modulus based on scalable aerogels was constructed to evaluate the effect of structure on MNPs. In this study, a technique using gas and liquid exchange was developed to eliminate uneven distribution of cells after cell implantation. Moreover, the results demonstrated that MNPs in materials with smaller pore sizes and lower moduli within a certain range were prone to differentiate into ChAT positive MNs, and this process was associated with the YAP-related cell signaling pathway. Most importantly, after implantation in mice with SCIs, the aerogels groups with a pore size of 25 μm and modulus of 0.4 kPa exhibited the best recovery. The effect of biomaterial characteristics on neural differentiation can affect the fabrication of neural tissue engineering scaffolds and improve the treatment of spinal cord diseases. AL also provided an effective platform for further studies on the effects of materials on stem cell differentiation.
