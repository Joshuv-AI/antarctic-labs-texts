Intrapleural Steroid Therapy for Recurrent Malignant Pleural Effusion: A Case Report
Authors: Shima Rani Sarker, Imtiaz Faruq, Jheelam Biswas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalignant pleural effusion (MPE) is a common complication in advanced cancer, often causing significant dyspnea. We present a case of a 57‐year‐old woman with recurrent MPE who was managed with intrapleural triamcinolone acetate. The intervention delayed fluid reaccumulation by 15 days and improved her symptoms and functional status, with no adverse effects observed. The proposed mechanism involves VEGF inhibition and reduced vascular permeability. This case highlights intrapleural corticosteroids as a potential palliative option in managing recurrent MPE when conventional treatments are not suitable.

Introduction

Pleural effusion is a common complication associated with both thoracic and extrathoracic malignancies. It may result from the direct involvement of the pleura by a tumor or from metastatic spread of malignancy originating elsewhere in the body [1]. Cancer cells from various primary sites can infiltrate the pleural space, leading to fluid accumulation. Malignant pleural effusion (MPE) is typically diagnosed by the detection of malignant cells in pleural fluid or tissue samples [2]. The majority of MPE cases arise secondary to metastatic disease, with lung cancer being the most common cause in males and breast cancer in females. The incidence of MPE exceeds 150,000 cases annually, with approximately 125,000 related hospitalizations each year. The median duration of hospitalization is about 6 days [3]. The median survival for patients diagnosed with MPE ranges from 3 to 12 months [4].

The management of recurrent symptomatic MPE in advanced cancer is primarily palliative, with the main goal being the alleviation of dyspnea and enhancement of the patient's quality of life [3]. The most commonly employed treatment strategies include therapeutic large volume thoracentesis or pleurodesis [5]. Approximately 60% of patients with MPE experience recurrence within 48 days following initial thoracentesis, and about 34%–48% require multiple repeated thoracenteses. Additionally, up to 16% of patients may require thoracentesis even months after a seemingly successful pleurodesis [6]. In cases where the patient has a limited life expectancy or when pleurodesis is unsuccessful, ambulatory pleural fluid drainage via indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) serves as an alternative approach [5]. However, all of these interventions are invasive, and patients receiving palliative care—particularly those in terminal stages—may often be unfit to undergo such procedures [7].

In palliative care settings, the clinical objective shifts toward minimizing symptom burden with the least procedural intervention. Corticosteroids, particularly triamcinolone acetate, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing the frequency of recurrent fluid accumulation in different clinical settings, including recurrent malignant ascites and inflammatory pleural effusions, such as tuberculous pleuritis, eosinophilic effusions, and post‐transplant pleural effusions [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Although systemic corticosteroids are often avoided in the management of MPE due to concerns about impairing the efficacy of pleurodesis, intrapleural administration offers the advantage of localized therapeutic effects with minimal systemic effects. Corticosteroids reduce pleural fluid accumulation by downregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines and increasing anti‐inflammatory mediators, while also potentially delaying recurrence through inhibition of VEGF secretion from metastatic tumor cells and suppression of related transcriptional pathways [13, 14, 15, 16]. Although there is increasing interest in the use of intrapleural triamcinolone in palliative care, the supporting evidence remains very limited. A multicenter study in Japan reported the successful use of intrapleural triamcinolone in patients with recurrent MPE who were unfit for pleurodesis or indwelling drainage, resulting in extended recurrence‐free intervals and improved survival outcomes [7]. Another randomized controlled trial reported a minimal reduction in thoracentesis frequency among patients with MPE following intrapleural steroid administration compared to placebo [17]. Given this limited but promising evidence, intrapleural corticosteroids may be considered a novel palliative strategy in patients with recurrent MPE, particularly when conventional options are contraindicated or poorly tolerated.

This case report presents a 57‐year‐old woman with recurrent symptomatic MPE, highlighting the therapeutic effect of intrapleural corticosteroids in managing her condition.

Case History

A 57‐year‐old woman with breathlessness (NRS score 7) was admitted to the Palliative Medicine Department of Bangladesh Medical University (BMU) on January 28, 2025. In November 2024, approximately 2 months prior to the current presentation, the patient was admitted to the Oncology Department of the National Institute of Cancer Research and Hospital (NICRH) with a 1‐month history of progressive dyspnea and intermittent hemoptysis.

During her previous admission to NICRH, several investigations were done to evaluate her symptoms. A chest ultrasonogram performed on December 2024 demonstrated bilateral pleural effusion. She underwent diagnostic pleural fluid aspiration from the right side on December 25, 2024. During that episode, 800 mL of pleural fluid was drained and sent for laboratory evaluation. The fluid appeared reddish in color. Biochemical analysis revealed a pH of 7.4, a glucose level of 45 mg/dL, a protein concentration of 6.3 g/dL, and a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level of 1500 U/L. Cytological examination revealed 60% lymphocytes, numerous red blood cells, and the presence of malignant cells. A subsequent pleural biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of metastatic adenocarcinoma. Despite further investigations, the primary site of the malignancy could not be identified. She was advised to undergo palliative chemotherapy; however, due to her poor performance status (ECOG 4), she was unable to continue treatment. Consequently, she was referred to the Palliative Medicine Department of BMU for best supportive care.

Upon admission to the palliative medicine department on January 28, 2025, her breathlessness (NRS score 10) worsened to the extent that she was confined to bed and unable to lie down. On examination, she was found in severe respiratory distress. Chest auscultation revealed absent breath sounds bilaterally from the fifth intercostal space downward. Her oxygen saturation (SpO2) was 89% on room air.

On January 29, 2025, a chest X‐ray (posteroanterior view) demonstrated bilateral pleural effusion (Figure 1). High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest further revealed bilateral pleural effusion with collapse and consolidation of the adjacent left lung, as well as hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 2). Laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin level of 13.1 g/dL, serum albumin of 3.78 g/dL, serum creatinine of 0.49 mg/dL, and serum SGPT of 35 U/L. The serum electrolytes and coagulation profile were within normal limits.

Method/Management

To relieve her breathlessness immediately, ultrasound (USG)‐guided pleural fluid aspiration was performed at the bedside from the left side, and 1 L of hemorrhagic pleural fluid was aspirated. Two days later, on January 31st, 2025, a second pleural fluid aspiration was conducted on the right side, resulting in the removal of 500 mL of fluid. Following the initial aspiration, the patient's dyspnea improved markedly, with the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score decreasing from 10 to 6. After the subsequent aspiration from the right side, her breathlessness further improved to an NRS score of 4, accompanied by an increase in oxygen saturation (SpO2) to 93% on room air.

On February 2, 2025, a bedside chest ultrasonography demonstrated recurrence of moderate bilateral pleural effusion. During this period, her breathlessness gradually worsened from NRS score of 4 to 6. The patient was offered chest tube drainage followed by pleurodesis; however, due to her poor performance status and lack of consent for the invasive procedure, the plan was abandoned.

One week later, on February 10, 2025, the patient again developed severe breathlessness (NRS score 10), prompting another bedside ultrasound‐guided pleural fluid aspiration under local anesthesia from the left side. This procedure yielded 1.5 L of pleural fluid. Immediately following the aspiration, 440 mg (8 mg/kg) of intrapleural triamcinolone was administered via the drainage line into the pleural space. Due to the absence of specific guidelines for steroid use in malignant pleural effusion, the dosage was determined based on amounts reported in previous case studies and phase II trial reports [7, 8, 10]. Each vial contained 1 mL (40 mg) of steroid; a total of 10 vials (10 mL) of undiluted triamcinolone was instilled and subsequently flushed with 10 mL of normal saline. The drainage line was then closed, and the patient was instructed to lie in the left lateral decubitus position for 30 min. No immediate adverse effects such as pain, fever, or shivering were observed.

Two days later, another USG‐guided pleural fluid aspiration was performed from the right side, and 400 mL of fluid was aspirated. Similarly, 440 mg of intrapleural triamcinolone was instilled into the pleural space via the drainage line using the same method described above. This time, she was instructed to lie in the right lateral decubitus position for 30 min. No immediate adverse effects were observed.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient's breathlessness noticeably improved following the initial episode of pleural fluid aspiration and intrapleural steroid administration, with her NRS score decreasing to 5. A marked reduction in dyspnea was observed after the second episode, with the NRS score further improving to 2, and the patient was able to lie down comfortably. Two days after the intrapleural steroid administration, on February 14, 2025, a bedside chest ultrasonogram was performed, which revealed only minimal pleural effusion. No adverse effects related to steroid administration—such as fever, pain, or signs of infection—were observed. The patient was subsequently discharged home in stable condition after 2 days.

Fifteen days after discharge, on March 2, 2025, the patient presented to the outpatient clinic of the Palliative Medicine Department at BMU for routine follow‐up. A chest X‐ray (posteroanterior view) was performed, revealing only minimal pleural effusion on the left side (Figure 3). At follow‐up, the patient reported experiencing only mild breathlessness (NRS score 2) on exertion and was able to perform self‐care activities with minimal assistance (ECOG 3). She also scored less than 5 out of 10 in multiple symptom domains—including dyspnea, pain, constipation, depression, anxiety, and overall well‐being—on the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS), indicating an overall improvement in symptom burden.

Discussion

The present case study suggests that intrapleural administration of triamcinolone may delay the recurrence of pleural effusion in terminally ill cancer patients with MPE. Currently, there is limited literature investigating the efficacy of intrapleural corticosteroids in the management of recurrent MPE. To date, only one study from Japan has reported the successful use of intrapleural triamcinolone acetate in patients with recurrent MPE. In that study, the average interval between thoracentesis increased by approximately 10 days following steroid administration [7]. In other clinical contexts, such as inflammatory pleuritis and post‐lung transplantation effusions, studies have reported a shorter delay in recurrence, ranging from 4 to 21 days [9, 10, 11, 12]. In contrast, our patient experienced a 15‐day delay in pleural fluid reaccumulation, indicating a potentially sustained therapeutic response. Although survival outcomes were not assessed in the present case, the Japanese study documented prolonged survival ranging from 39 to 537 days. Additionally, they reported improvements in patients' performance status—an observation that was also consistent with our patient's clinical course [7].

The exact mechanism by which intrapleural corticosteroids delay the recurrence of MPE remains unclear. However, it is plausible that their therapeutic effect is closely related to the pathophysiology of MPE. MPE typically arises from direct pleural invasion by tumor cells or lymphatic obstruction due to malignant infiltration. Cancer cells in the pleura provoke an inflammatory response, leading to an exudative process and fluid accumulation in the pleural space [18]. Additionally, cancer cells produce vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which plays a central role in both pleural effusion formation and tumor progression. Within the pleural space, VEGF promotes pleural effusion through several mechanisms: it induces morphological and functional changes in vascular endothelial cells, alters gene expression, stimulates vascular endothelial cell proliferation and migration, inhibits apoptosis, and induces vasodilation. These effects facilitate the translocation of plasma and inflammatory cells into the pleural space. It also disrupts the integrity of the mesothelial monolayer, further contributing to fluid leakage. VEGF expression levels have been shown to directly correlate with the volume of malignant pleural effusion [19, 20].

Corticosteroids counteract the pathophysiological mechanisms of MPE through multiple pathways. Their anti‐inflammatory effects are primarily mediated by transcriptional modulation, involving the upregulation of anti‐inflammatory mediators, such as annexin A1 and the suppression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IFN‐γ. This dual regulatory action reduces the local inflammatory milieu within the pleural space, thereby decreasing capillary permeability, cellular infiltration, and protein leakage [14]. Additionally, corticosteroids inhibit VEGF production by vascular smooth muscle cells and lung fibroblasts, particularly when VEGF expression is induced by platelet‐activating factor (PAF) or platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF). This inhibition appears to occur at the transcriptional level, either through interference with the AP‐1 transcription factor complexed with corticosteroid receptors or via negative glucocorticoid‐responsive elements (nGREs) within the VEGF gene promoter. Collectively, these actions reduce vascular permeability and protein leakage, thereby contributing to delayed fluid reaccumulation in pleural cavities [15, 16]. Furthermore, corticosteroids may exert direct antitumor effects on malignant cells obstructing lymphatic channels, thereby improving lymphatic drainage and reducing effusion formation [21].

Although no studies have explicitly investigated the mechanism of intrapleural corticosteroids in delaying MPE formation, several reports have described the successful use of intrapleural triamcinolone acetate in managing recurrent malignant ascites—a condition with similar underlying mechanisms [7, 8]. Additionally, the dosage of intrapleural steroid in our case was determined based on evidence from phase II trials and case series involving recurrent malignant ascites and post‐lung transplantation effusions [7, 8, 10]. However, the effective therapeutic doses and safety profile of intrapleural corticosteroids in the context of recurrent MPE remain underexplored.

No adverse effects related to intrapleural steroid administration were observed in our patient. Similarly, the Japanese multicenter study reported no complications among its participants [6]. However, other studies involving intrapleural corticosteroids have documented adverse effects such as chest pain, fever, local infection, and failure of subsequent pleurodesis procedures [14]. In our case, a notable reduction in symptom burden—including dyspnea—was observed during the follow‐up visit. This contrasts with findings from a previous study that reported no significant improvement in dyspnea following intrapleural steroid administration [17].

Current practices in managing malignant pleural effusion (MPE) involve repeated thoracentesis, chemical pleurodesis, and the placement of indwelling pleural catheters (IPCs). Although repeated thoracentesis provides quick symptom relief, the need for multiple hospital visits and the associated risks of pneumothorax and infection often become burdensome for terminally ill patients. Chemical pleurodesis may offer a more effective and long‐term solution for recurrent MPE, but it is not suitable for patients with trapped lung or poor performance status. Furthermore, the procedure can increase symptom burden due to the strong inflammatory response it induces. Although IPCs provide patients with greater mobility and reduce hospital visits, they require continuous medical attention and are associated with increased risks of infection and persistent discomfort from the catheter [3]. In contrast, intrapleural corticosteroid administration remains a less invasive, symptom‐based alternative that locally modulates inflammation and vascular permeability to slow fluid reaccumulation. This method may be advantageous for patients who are not candidates for pleurodesis or long‐term catheterization. However, further investigation is needed to determine the efficacy, safety, and optimal clinical role of intrapleural steroids in the management of MPE.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the potential role of intrapleural triamcinolone as a palliative treatment for recurrent malignant pleural effusion in patients unfit for invasive procedures like ambulatory pleural fluid drainage or pleurodesis. The intervention effectively delayed effusion recurrence, improved the patient's dyspnea, and enhanced her functional status without any adverse effects. Although these findings are promising, they are dependent on a single case; hence, more randomized controlled trials are needed to determine the safety, efficacy, and clinical usefulness of this intervention in larger patient groups.
