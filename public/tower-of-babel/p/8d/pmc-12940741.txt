Peptide OM-LV20 demonstrates neuroprotective effects by attenuating mitochondria-mediated neuronal apoptosis and dysfunction in mice with traumatic brain injury
Authors: Chen-Jun Ye, Li-Na Liu, Min-Nan Lu, Tao Hu, Ying Zhou, Dan Wang, Hao-Ran Zhao, Guo-Ji Yan, Xin-Ying Shi, Jing-Feng Song, Shan-Shan Li, Sai-Ge Yin, Shi-Tong Zong, Xin-Wang Yang, Yan-Bin Xiyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a major public health challenge worldwide, particularly among individuals under 45 years of age, resulting in profound physical disability, neuropsychiatric impairment, and socioeconomic burden. Existing therapeutic strategies offer limited efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for novel treatment modalities. This study evaluated the neuroprotective potential of OM-LV20, an amphibian-derived bioactive peptide, in a murine model of TBI. Intraperitoneal administration of OM-LV20 following controlled cortical impact significantly reduced brain water content and attenuated neurological and cognitive deficits. Histological analysis, TUNEL staining, and ultrastructural assessments revealed suppression of neuronal degeneration and mitigation of mitochondrial dysfunction. Transcriptomic profiling, together with RT-qPCR and western blot analyses, revealed modulation of the expression of apoptosis-related genes and proteins, indicating a robust anti-apoptotic effect mediated through mitochondrial pathways. Additionally, OM-LV20 exhibited efficient permeability across the blood-brain barrier. These findings indicate that OM-LV20 exerted strong neuroprotective activity through targeted suppression of mitochondria-centered neuronal apoptosis, supporting its potential utility in ameliorating neurological dysfunction post-TBI. The diverse mechanistic actions identified for OM-LV20 provide a novel therapeutic avenue with considerable promise for enhancing neurological recovery in individuals with TBI.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) represents a major global cause of mortality and disability, with disproportionate impact on individuals under 45 years of age (Canseco et al., 2022; Kaur & Sharma, 2018). Each year, approximately 70 million people are affected worldwide, with over 100 000 individuals experiencing permanent functional impairment (Ahmed et al., 2017; Dewan et al., 2018). TBI initiates complex and multifaceted neurological dysfunctions, including motor impairments, cognitive deficits, epilepsy, and language disorders, all of which significantly impact quality of life (Ahmed et al., 2017; Graham & Sharp, 2023; Klein et al., 2018; Weymann & Rourke, 2021). Furthermore, accumulating evidence has linked TBI to increased risk of developing neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s Disease (PD) (Rajan & Kaas, 2022) and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) (Shin et al., 2021). The pathological cascade triggered by TBI encompasses acute mechanical damage followed by secondary processes including brain edema, intracranial hypertension, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis, ultimately leading to progressive neurological deterioration (Bernard, 2023; Hakiminia et al., 2022; Pavlovic et al., 2019). Despite these challenges, effective drugs or treatments targeting TBI-induced neurological dysfunction remain limited, primarily due to the heterogeneity and complexity of post-traumatic pathophysiology.

Peptides have recently emerged as promising therapeutic agents for human diseases due to their small molecular weight, high selectivity, structural diversity, and considerable adaptability (Chen et al., 2023; De La Torre & Albericio, 2020; Wang et al., 2025). Among these, amphibian skin-derived peptides constitute a rich source of bioactive molecules with demonstrated antimicrobial, antioxidant, wound-healing, and neuroprotective properties (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2021, 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). Recent investigations have further highlighted the therapeutic relevance of such peptides in neurological contexts (Lu et al., 2024; Yadav et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021), where they modulate neuronal apoptosis, enhance neurotrophic support, and promote both structural and functional recovery following central nervous system (CNS) injury (Chiu et al., 2017; Kour et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023).

OM-LV20, a novel low-molecular weight peptide originally isolated from the skin secretions of a unique amphibian species native to Southwest China (Li et al., 2018), has demonstrated potent neurotropic activity in multiple experimental systems. Prior studies have shown that OM-LV20 protects astrocytes from oxidative injury (Yin et al., 2022), facilitates axonal repair and nerve regeneration following spinal cord damage (Zhao et al., 2022), improves behavioral performance, and prevents hippocampal neuronal loss in models of cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury at low doses (Yin et al., 2021). These findings suggest broad neuroprotective potential for OM-LV20 across various neurological conditions, including trauma and ischemic stroke. This study investigated the potential neuroprotective effects of peptide OM-LV20 in a murine model of TBI and elucidated its underlying molecular mechanisms. Notably, OM-LV20 conferred neuroprotection by reducing cerebral edema, limiting mitochondrial dysfunction, and inhibiting neuronal apoptosis, thereby offering a mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategy for improving neurological outcomes in a condition where effective treatments remain elusive.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Peptide synthesis

The OM-LV20 peptide (amino acid sequence: LVGKLLKGAVGDVCGLLPIC) was commercially synthesized by Wuhan Tanda Biotechnology Co, Ltd (China), with a purity exceeding 95%.

Animals and ethics statement

Adult male and female C57BL/6 mice (20–22 g, 6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Department of Kunming Medical University. The mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in a controlled facility with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle with lights on at 0800h and a constant temperature of 22±2°C. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal experiments were conducted following the approval of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (License No. KMMU 2020025; Kunming, China).

Mouse groups and TBI model

Mice were randomly divided into three groups: sham (subjected to craniotomy without cortical impact), TBI+OM-LV20 (received controlled cortical impact followed by intraperitoneal injection of OM-LV20 at 8 ng/kg body weight, three times daily for 3 or 7 consecutive days), and TBI+Vehicle (received equal volumes of sterile water via intraperitoneal injection on the same schedule at 3 or 7 days post-injury (dpi)). Group sizes and further experimental assays are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

The controlled cortical impact (CCI) model was established based on previously described methods (Fox et al., 1998; Hu et al., 2015). Following anesthesia, mice were secured on a stereotaxic device, and a moderate CCI was delivered to the right parietotemporal cortex. Impact parameters included a velocity of 6.0 m/s, deformation depth of 1 mm, and contact duration of 200 ms. Sham-operated mice underwent identical surgical procedures without cortical impact.

Behavioral assessments

Modified neurological severity score (mNSS)

Neurological function was assessed at 1, 3, and 7 dpi using the mNSS, a composite scale ranging from 0 to 14 that evaluates motor performance (muscle status and abnormal movement), sensory responses (visual, tactile, and proprioceptive), reflexes, and balance (Chen et al., 2005; Li et al., 2001). Three blinded researchers independently scored each animal, and the mean value was used for analysis. One point was assigned for failure to perform a specific task, and one point was deducted for absent reflexes, with higher scores reflecting more severe neurological impairment.

Open field test (OFT)

Anxiety-like behavior and spontaneous locomotor activity were assessed using the OFT following standard procedures (Hazra et al., 2014). Each mouse was placed in a well-illuminated circular arena (40 cm long×40 cm wide×40 cm high) for 10 min to assess overall activity levels, gross locomotor activity, and exploratory behavior using the TOPSCAN tracking system (Clever Sys Inc., USA). Parameters included: (i) horizontal activity (total distance traveled), (ii) vertical activity (number of rearing events), (iii) grid crossings (frequency of line crossings), (iv) time spent in peripheral vs. central zones, and (v) number of fecal boli. These metrics provided quantitative measures of exploratory behavior, general activity, and anxiety-related responses.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze apparatus consisted of three identical rectangular arms (30 cm long×8 cm wide×15 cm high) positioned at 120° angles from each other. Mice were allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min to assess spontaneous alternation behavior. The percentage of correct alternations was calculated using the formula:

1\begin{document}$ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{The\;percentage\;of\;correct\;alternations=(number\;of\;correct\;}\\ &{\mathrm{alternations}}/({\mathrm{total\;number\;of\;armentries}}-2)) \times 100\% \end{aligned}$ \end{document}

After a minimum 24-h rest period to reduce spatial memory carryover, the test was repeated using the SuperMaze software system (Xinruan, China). One arm was designated as the initial arm, another as the novel arm, and the third as the other arm. The novel arm was initially blocked, and mice were allowed to freely explore the two remaining arms for 15 min. After 1 h, the barrier was removed, and a formal test was conducted for 5 min. During this session, exploration trajectories, number of entries into the novel arm, and percentage of time spent in the novel arm were recorded for each mouse. These measures provided insights into spatial learning and memory abilities as well as exploratory behavior in response to novel environments.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

Spatial learning and memory were evaluated using the MWM test, consisting of a circular pool (diameter: 100 cm; height: 50 cm; water depth: 20 cm) filled with opaque white water maintained at 24±1°C (XR-XM101-Z, Shanghai XinRuan Information Technology Co., Ltd., China), following previously established protocols (Xiyang et al., 2016).

Prior to the acquisition phase, mice underwent habituation to the water environment. Each mouse was allowed to swim freely for 10 s, followed by brief placement on the hidden white platform submerged just below the surface for 1–2 s. The hidden platform position was changed for the subsequent regular testing sessions.

During the acquisition phase, mice were trained to locate the submerged platform over five consecutive days, and escape latency (time to reach the platform) was recorded. On day 6, a probe trial was conducted in which the platform was removed, and mice were allowed to swim for 60 s to assess memory retention. Parameters included percentage of time spent in the target quadrant, path length in the target quadrant, number of crossings over the target platform location, and swim speed (mm/s). Additionally, time spent and distance traveled in all four quadrants were noted.

Animal movement was continuously tracked by an overhead video camera, and data were analyzed using the Animal Behavior Video Analysis System (Shanghai XinRuan Information Technology Co., Ltd., China). These measures provided insights into spatial learning, memory, and navigational abilities in response to changing environmental cues.

Novel object recognition (NOR) task

The NOR task was conducted in the same open-field arena (40 cm long×40 cm wide×40 cm high) used for locomotor assessment, following established protocols (Figueiredo et al., 2013; Lourenco et al., 2013). Objects used for testing were 4–5 cm in height and composed of glass or plastic materials, differing in shape, texture, color, and size.

During the initial training phase, each mouse was placed at the center of the arena with two identical objects and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The time spent interacting with each object was recorded independently by trained researchers. After a 1 h post-training interval, mice were reintroduced to the arena for a 10 min test session, during which one familiar object was replaced with a novel object of identical height but distinct color and shape. Object exploration was defined as direct contact by the nose or front paws.

In the formal testing phase, exploration time towards the novel and familiar objects was measured, and the Discrimination Index (DI) was calculated using the formula:

2\begin{document}$ \mathrm{DI=(NT-FT)/(NT+FT)} $ \end{document}

where NT represents time spent exploring the new object and FT represents time spent exploring the familiar object. This index served as a metric of recognition memory and cognitive discrimination capacity.

Brain water content detection

Cerebral edema was evaluated by measuring brain water content at 3 dpi, a time point corresponding to peak edema severity. Following TBI, mice were sacrificed by decapitation at the designated time point, and whole brains were immediately extracted. Wet weight was measured before samples were dehydrated in an oven set at 65°C for 72 h. Dry weight was then measured.

Brain water content was calculated using the formula:

3\begin{document}$ {\mathrm{Brain\;water\;content=((wet\;weight-dry\;weight)/wet\;weight}})\times 100\% $ \end{document}

This method provided a quantitative estimate of tissue water accumulation, allowing assessment of edema severity following TBI.

Histological preparation and analysis of brain tissue

Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde via the right atrium. Brains were rapidly dissected and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded and sectioned coronally at 5 μm. Sections were then deparaffinized and rehydrated using standard protocols prior to staining procedures.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (C0105S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) to visualize general histoarchitecture and cellular morphology.

Nissl staining was carried out using a Nissl Staining Kit (G1036, Servicebio, China) to assess neuronal integrity. Stained sections were sealed with neutral gum film and observed under a microscope (DM4000B, Leica, Germany). Nissl bodies in five randomly selected cortical and hippocampal fields were quantified using ImageJ software.

For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin-embedded brain slices were subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (C1032, Servicebio, China). After blocking with 5% sheep serum, slices were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-NeuN primary antibody (1:400; NBP1-92693, NOVUS, USA), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor-488 secondary antibody (1:200; 8890S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Apoptotic cells were detected using a TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1089, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and nuclei were counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, C0065, Servicebio, China). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DM4000B, Leica, Germany) to analyze the distribution and number of positive cells in five randomly selected fields.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ultrastructural analysis of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus was performed using TEM, following the method described by Li et al. (2022). Briefly, tissue blocks (1 mm3) were dissected from each region and fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde, followed by post-fixation in osmium tetroxide to preserve membrane integrity. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (50–100 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome and stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were then imaged via TEM to assess ultrastructural features, including synaptic vesicles, synaptic clefts, postsynaptic densities, and mitochondria in both cortical and hippocampal regions.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Sample collection and preparation

Mice from each experimental group were sacrificed, and brain tissues were submitted to Biotree Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (China) for transcriptomic profiling. Total RNA integrity and purity were evaluated prior to library construction. RNA degradation and contamination were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA purity was determined using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, USA), and RNA integrity was confirmed using an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Library preparation and sequencing

For each sample, 1 µg of RNA was used for library construction using the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The procedure included poly(A) mRNA purification, fragmentation, first- and second-strand cDNA synthesis, end repair, adaptor ligation, size selection, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Unique index codes were assigned to individual libraries for multiplexing. Cluster generation was performed using a TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, USA) on the cBot Cluster Generation System. Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

RNA-seq data analysis

Quality control

Raw sequencing reads in fastq format were processed using in-house Perl scripts. Low-quality reads, reads containing adapter sequences, and reads with poly-N regions were filtered to generate clean reads. Quality metrics, including Q20, Q30, and GC content, were calculated to assess data quality.

Read alignment

Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v.2.0.5. Genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from an established genome database. A genome index was constructed and paired-end reads were aligned to the reference using default parameters.

Gene expression quantification

Read counts per gene were determined using featureCounts v.1.5.0-p3. Expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), calculated based on read counts and gene length.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential expression analysis between groups (two biological replicates per condition) was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (v.1.16.1). Adjusted P-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Genes with an adjusted P<0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. GO terms and KEGG pathways with corrected P<0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was conducted using the standalone GSEA analysis tool (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp). Enrichment analyses were conducted across multiple curated datasets, including GO, KEGG, Reactome, Disease Ontology (DO), and DisGeNET, to identify gene sets with consistent expression differences between experimental conditions.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis using gene-specific primers and the TaqMan MicroRNA Assay. Each PCR reaction consisted of 2×Taq Pro SYBR qPCR Master MIX (R333, Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), 0.25 µL of primer (10 pmol/L), 1 µL of cDNA template, and 8.5 µL of PCR nuclease-free water. The primer sequences were as follows: Bcl-2 (GeneID, #12043), forward, 5′-GAG TAC CTG AAC CGG CAT CTG and reverse, 5′-CAG AGA CAG CCA GGA GAA ATC A-3′; Bax (GeneID, #12028), forward, 5′-GAT CCA AGA CCA GGG TGG C-3′and reverse, 5′-CTT CCA GAT GGT GAG CGA GG-3′; Caspase-1 (GeneID, #12362), forward, 5′-GGA CCC TCA AGT TTT GCC CT-3′ and reverse, 5′-AGA CGT GTA CGA GTG GTT GT-3′; Caspase-3 (GeneID, #12367), forward, 5′-CTG ACT GGA AAG CCG AAA CTC-3′and reverse, 5′-CGA CCC GTC CTT TGA ATT TCT-3′; Caspase-4, (GeneID, #12363) forward, 5′-CAA AGT CAT CAT TGT GCA GGC-3′ and reverse, 5′-CCT CTG CAC AAC TGG GGT T-3′. β-actin (GeneID, #11461; forward, 5′-CAT TGC TGA CAG GA TGC AGA AGG-3′ and reverse, 5′-TGC TGG AAG GTG GAC AGT GAG G-3′) was used as internal control. All primers were synthesized by Takara Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Each reaction was performed in triplicate to ensure consistency and reliability of the results. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCq method, with β-actin as the internal reference.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation followed by immediate decapitation to minimize suffering. Brains were rapidly removed, and the right frontal cortex and hippocampus were dissected and divided into two portions—one allocated for mitochondrial isolation and the other for additional downstream assays.

Mitochondria, mitochondrial proteins, and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated using a Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3606, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Fresh tissue samples were weighed and homogenized in ice-cold homogenization buffer. Homogenates were then centrifuged at 600 ×g for 5 min at 4°C to isolate the nuclear fraction. The resulting supernatants were further centrifuged at 11 000 ×g for 10 min at 4°C to isolate the mitochondria. The isolated mitochondria were used for biochemical analyses and protein extraction.

Western blot analysis

Proteins (30 μg per sample) were extracted from mitochondrial, cytoplasmic, and whole-brain tissue lysates and separated using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA), which were placed in blocking solution to reduce nonspecific binding and washed with Tris-Buffered Saline Tween-20 (TBST). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-actin (1:5 000, AA32121, Bioworld Technology, USA), anti-COXIV (1:1 000, 11967s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-cytochrome c (1:1 000, ab53056; Abcam, UK), anti-B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) (1:1 000, ab59348, Abcam, UK), and anti-Bax (1:500, sc-4239, Santa Cruz, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with secondary antibodies (1:1 000, A23220/A23410, Abbkine, USA), then visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blot detection reagents (Millipore, USA). Bands were imaged with the Bio-Rad Gel Imaging system (ChemiDoc™XRS+, Bio-Rad, USA), and relative band densities were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina v.1.2.3. The three-dimensional crystal structures of Bax (UniProt ID: Q07813) and Bcl-2 (UniProt ID: P10417) were obtained from the UniProt Protein Data Bank. All water molecules, cofactors, and native ligands were removed using PyMOL v.2.5.4. Hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger charges were added using AutoDock Tools v.1.5.7, with protonation states optimized at pH 7.4. The OM-LV20 peptide (LVGKLLKGAVGDVCGLLPIC) structure was predicted using I-TASSER, followed by energy minimization using the AMBER ff14SB forcefield (steepest descent: 2 000 steps; conjugate gradient: 3 000 steps). Partial charges were assigned, and files were converted to PDBQT format. During the docking process, the Bax and Bcl-2 proteins were maintained in a rigid state while the OM-LV20 peptide was maintained in a flexible state (Cha et al., 2024). For each protein target, nine generated conformations were ranked by binding free energy (ΔG). The lowest-energy conformation was selected as the optimal binding pose. Docking results were visualized by PyMOL v.2.5.4.

Mitochondrial function evaluation

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

Mitochondria isolated from the frontal cortex and hippocampus of mice subjected to various treatments were evaluated for MMP using the tetramethyl rhodamine methyl ester (TMRE)-based Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified mitochondrial suspensions (0.1 mL, 80 μg protein) were incubated with 0.9 mL of TMRE staining solution. Fluorescence intensity of TMRE was measured using a FACSCanto SORP flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 550 nm and emission filter 585/42 BP. Relative fluorescence values (RFU) were used to compare MMP levels across groups.

Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activities

The activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I, II, III, IV, and V were measured using corresponding kits (BC0510, BC3230, BC3240, BC0945, BC1445, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Determination of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

At 3 and 7 d dpi, mice were sacrificed, and brain tissues were collected, homogenized in lysis buffer (S0026, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and incubated on ice for 30 min. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12 000 r/min (centrifugal radius of 10 cm) for 10 min at 4°C.

ATP levels were measured using an ATP Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total SOD activity, including Cu/Zn-SOD and Mn-SOD, was assessed using a Total SOD Assay Kit with WST-8 (S0103, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies

Eighteen female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized with isoflurane and intravenously administered 10 mg/kg OM-LV20 via the caudal vein. Blood samples were collected at 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, and 24 h post-administration. Samples were immediately chilled on wet ice and centrifuged within 15 min to obtain plasma. Quantification of plasma OM-LV20 levels was performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), following previously reported protocols (Li et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019). Plasma samples were applied to a C18 reverse-phase HPLC column (Hypersil BDS C18, 4.0 × 300 mm, Elite, China) pre-equilibrated with 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in water. Elution was achieved using a linear gradient of 0%–70% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% (v/v) TFA over 70 min at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Absorbance was monitored at 215 nm, and OM-LV20 was quantified by integration of the peak corresponding to its retention time.

Plasma concentration-time curves indicated that OM-LV20 declined biexponentially, following a two-compartment model with two phases: distribution (rapid initial decline in blood concentrations) and elimination (slower subsequent decline in blood concentrations) (Supplementary Figure S1). In the biexponential model used for OM-LV20, C0 was extrapolated after a logarithmic transformation of the distribution data. After an intravenous injection, C0 is equivalent to Cmax. Additionally, other pharmacokinetic parameters—including apparent distribution and elimination half-lives (T1/2α, T1/2β), plasma clearance (Cl), central volume of distribution (VC=D0/C0), and area under the concentration–time curve (AUC)—were calculated using the PKSolver Excel add-in (Zhang et al., 2010).

The plasma concentration of OM-LV20 at time t, C(t), was modeled according to the two-compartment biexponential equation:

4\begin{document}$ {C} _{ \mathrm{(t)}} \mathrm{=A.e}^{ {-\alpha t}} \mathrm{+B.e}^{ {-\beta t}} $ \end{document}

where A and B are the intercepts representing the distribution and elimination phases, and α and β are the respective rate constants.

Biodistribution analysis

OM-LV20 was radiolabeled with N-succinimidyl propionate-2,3-[3H] (3H-NSP, Moravek, USA). Briefly, 1 mCi aliquots of 3H-NSP were evaporated under nitrogen, resuspended in 0.1 mol/L sodium bicarbonate buffer (pH 8.3) containing 50 nmol OM-LV20, and incubated with shaking for 5 h at room temperature. The radiolabeled molecules were purified by dialysis using a Micro Float-A-Lyzer and stored at –20°C in 50% glycerol. The specific activity of 3H-OM-LV20 was 3.02 µCi/µg. Radiolabeled 14C-sucrose (1.5 µCi/µg) was purchased from Moravek Biochemicals (USA).

Ten-week-old male mice (approximately 35 g in weight) were intravenously injected via the caudal vein with 13.7 µg/kg 3H-OM-LV20. At 1 or 5 h post-injection, mice were anesthetized with xylazine (Bayer, France; 8 mg/kg) and ketamine (Parke Davis, France; 140 mg/kg) and sacrificed by cold phosphate-buffered (PBS) intracardiac perfusion. Doses of radiolabeled OM-LV20 were selected to yield detectable tissue radioactivity well above background disintegrations per min (dpm).

Tissues from brain, lung, heart, liver, spleen, pancreas, muscle, and kidney (approximately 80 mg per organ) were collected, weighed, and solubilized overnight at 50°C in SOLVABLE. To prevent chemiluminescent quenching, lung, heart, kidney, spleen, and liver were incubated with 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (200 µL) following solubilization (Reveret et al., 2023). Blood samples were centrifuged to separate. For scintillation counting, 9 mL of Ultima Gold cocktail (PerkinElmer, USA) was added to all samples, including plasma and tissue digests. Apparent distribution volume (VD, µL/g) for each tissue was calculated by dividing the concentration in organs relative to weight (dpm/g) by the concentration in blood relative to volume (dpm/µL), thereby normalizing tissue accumulation to systemic exposure. Protein-bound radioactivity in blood was confirmed by trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation, with recovery rates ranging from 90% to 100% at 1 h post-injection. All radioactivity measurements were performed using a Hidex 300 SL Liquid Scintillation Counter.

In vitro and in vivo permeability experiments

Passive membrane permeability in MDR1-MDCKII cell

Permeability of OM-LV20 was evaluated using MDR1-MDCKII cell monolayers as an in vitro model for passive membrane diffusion. All reagents—including cell culture media, bovine serum albumin, medium supplements (L-glutamine, 20 mmol/L sodium bicarbonate, nonessential amino acids, sodium pyruvate, and colchicine), and Hanks’ balanced salt solution were purchased from Invitrogen (USA). Transwell plates (12 well; 1.13 cm2 area; 0.4 μm pores) were purchased from Corning Life Sciences (USA). Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate buffer was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (USA). MDR1-MDCK type II cells were obtained from the National Institutes of Health (USA). Cells were maintained in Eagle’s minimum essential medium for permeability experiments according to previously established methods (Summerfield et al., 2007).

Cells were seeded at 60 000 cells/cm2 onto rat type I collagen-coated polycarbonate membranes in 12-well Transwell plates and cultured for 8 days prior to experimentation. Permeability assays were conducted in Hanks’ balanced salt solution containing 10 mmol/L HEPES and 15 mmol/L glucose (pH 7.4). Cells were dosed with test compounds on both the apical and basolateral sides and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 90% relative humidity. Orbital shaking (150 r/min) was applied to reduce the influence of the unstirred water layer. OM-LV20 was tested at a final concentration of 5 μmol/L.

Bidirectional transport studies were performed in both apical-to-basolateral (A→B) and basolateral-to-apical (B→A) directions, with triplicate wells per condition. Donepezil served as a control with a high permeability coefficient in the in vitro blood-brain barrier permeability assay (Summerfield et al., 2007), while atenolol was used as a low-permeability control (Wu et al., 2019). Samples were collected 90 min post-dosing. The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp, 10-6 cm/s) was calculated as:

5\begin{document}$ {P} {app=({\mathrm{dCr/dt}})\times ({\mathrm{Vr/(A}}\times {\mathrm{C}}0))} $ \end{document}

where dCr/dt is the slope of the cumulative concentration in the receiver compartment versus time (μM/s), A is the area of the cell monolayer, C0 is the drug concentration in the dosing solution, and Vr is the volume of the receiver compartment.

In situ brain perfusion model

Blood-brain-barrier (BBB) transport of OM-LV20 was measured in mice using the in situ brain perfusion technique, as described previously (Cisternino et al., 2001; Dagenais et al., 2000). Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of xylazine (Bayer, France; 8 mg/kg) and ketamine (Parke Davis, France; 140 mg/kg). The right common carotid artery was catheterized with polyethylene tubing (0.30 mm i.d.×0.70 mm o.d., Biotrol Diagnostic, France) mounted on a 26-gauge needle and pre-filled with heparin (25 U/mL). Before insertion of the catheter, the common carotid artery was ligated caudally, and the external carotid artery was ligated rostrally to the occipital artery at the bifurcation.

Perfusion fluid was delivered using a syringe connected to an infusion pump (Harvard PHD 2000, Harvard Apparatus, USA) at a flow rate of 15 mL/min. Before perfusion, the thorax was opened, the heart was transected, and perfusion was initiated. The perfusate consisted of Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate buffer containing 10 μmol/L OM-LV20 or 20 μmol/L donepezil (positive control), equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 to maintain physiological pH (7.4) and prewarmed to 37°C (Summerfield et al., 2007). Each compound was tested in five animals. Perfusion was terminated by decapitation at 30, 60, 90, or 120 s.

Following perfusion, brains were immediately removed, and the left cerebral hemisphere was excised and homogenized in 5 mL of a methanol-water mixture. Homogenates were stored at −80°C until analysis.

BBB transport was quantified using three parameters derived from single-time point perfusion data: apparent volume of distribution (Vbrain, μL/g), transport coefficient (Kin, μL/s/g), and brain permeability (P, cm/s), calculated as follows:

6\begin{document}$ {V} \mathrm{brain=Cbr(T)/Cpf(T)} $ \end{document}

7\begin{document}$ {Kin} \mathrm= {V} \mathrm{brain/T} $ \end{document}

8\begin{document}$ {P} \mathrm= {Kin/V} \mathrm{vasc} $ \end{document}

where Cbr/Cpf is the apparent brain distribution volume, Cbr is the peptide amount in brain tissue (ng/g tissue), Cpf is the peptide concentration in the perfusion fluid (ng/mL), T is the net perfusion time (s), and Vvasc is the apparent vascular space of the brain (15 µL/g tissue; Cisternino et al., 2001).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (v.22.0, USA). Data are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD). One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for analysis among multiple groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied when variance was normally distributed. For non-normally distributed data, Dunnett’s T3 test was used. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Secondary injury after TBI typically involves cerebral swelling and neuroinflammatory cascades that exacerbate neuronal damage. OM-LV20 administration markedly attenuated this edema, restoring brain water content to levels indistinguishable from those of the sham group (TBI+Vehicle vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P<0.05; sham vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P>0.05; Figure 1A).

Here, behavioral deficits following TBI were assessed via a comprehensive battery of tests. In the OFT, TBI mice exhibited decreased locomotor activity and increased anxiety-like behaviors, characterized by significant reductions in grid crossings, total distance traveled, center-field time, and rearing frequency (sham vs. TBI+Vehicle, P<0.05; Figure 2A, B, E, F), alongside increased peripheral-field occupancy and fecal boli (sham vs. TBI+Vehicle, P<0.05; Figure 2C, D). OM-LV20 mitigates neuronal damage and loss associated with TBI

Histopathological assessment via H&E staining revealed pronounced neuronal damage in the frontal cortex and CA1 region of the hippocampus following TBI. Brains from sham-operated mice displayed abundant morphologically intact neurons with clearly defined cytoplasm, nucleoli, and well-organized cytoarchitecture, whereas brains from TBI mice exhibited widespread neuronal loss, characterized by fusiform or irregular cells with blurred boundaries, cell edema, nuclear fragmentation, and increased vacuolar structures indicative of heightened apoptosis (Figure 3A). In contrast, the TBI+OM-LV20 group showed some cell death and loss but most cells in these regions were intact, with fewer vacuolar structures, reduced nuclear fragmentation and edema, and a more orderly arrangement (Figure 3A).

OM-LV20 modulates transcriptomic changes in apoptosis-related genes following TBI

To elucidate the molecular basis of OM-LV20-mediated neuroprotection after TBI, transcriptomic profiling was performed on the injured frontal cortex and hippocampus of mice treated with vehicle or OM-LV20 at 3 or 7 dpi, alongside sham-operated controls. Differential expression analysis revealed extensive gene regulation following TBI, with 464 and 2 755 DEGs in the frontal cortex and hippocampus, respectively, at 3 and 7 dpi relative to sham mice (Supplementary Data S1). Temporal comparison of the TBI+Vehicle group between 3 and 7 dpi yielded 2 911 DEGs (2 121 up-regulated, 790 down-regulated), while OM-LV20 treatment altered the expression of 458 genes (124 up-regulated, 334 down-regulated) across the same interval. Direct comparison of OM-LV20– and vehicle-treated TBI mice revealed 433 DEGs (118 up-regulated, 315 down-regulated) at 3 dpi and 1 724 DEGs (1 354 up-regulated, 370 down-regulated) at 7 dpi, with the majority (1 354) up-regulated by OM-LV20 at the later time point, indicating a broad reprogramming of post-injury transcriptional activity (Supplementary Figure S2A; Supplemental Data S1).

To identify transcriptomic targets associated with the neuroprotective effects of OM-LV20, overlapping DEGs across key comparisons were visualized using Venn analysis (Supplementary Figure S2A). GO classification of these DEGs into biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) revealed the top 10 enriched DEGs in each domain (Supplementary Figure S3). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further indicated that OM-LV20 treatment post-TBI affected various cellular pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, apoptosis, B cell receptor signaling, c-type lectin receptor signaling, NF-kappa B signaling, chemokine signaling, osteoclast differentiation, and phagosome pathways (Supplementary Figure S2C–G; Supplementary Data S2). Among these, apoptosis consistently ranked in the top 20 enriched pathways in four out of five comparisons (Supplementary Figure S2C–F, red arrows).

We then examined the mRNA levels of genes enriched in the apoptosis pathway and observed alterations in the expression of mitochondria-dependent apoptosis-regulating proteins following OM-LV20 treatment in TBI mice. Subsequent RT-qPCR validation confirmed these findings, showing a significant reduction in the mRNA levels of the endogenous apoptosis inhibitor Bcl-2 post-TBI (sham vs TBI+Vehicle, P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S2H, I), which were reversed after OM-LV20 administration (3 dpi, TBI+Vehicle vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P<0.05; 7 dpi, TBI+Vehicle vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S2H, I). Conversely, the mRNA expressions of apoptosis enablers and executors, including Bax, Caspase-1, Caspase-3, and Caspase-4, were significantly elevated post-TBI (sham vs TBI+Vehicle, P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S2H, I), but these elevations were mitigated by OM-LV20 treatment (3 dpi, TBI+Vehicle vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P<0.05; 7 dpi, TBI+Vehicle vs. TBI+OM-LV20, P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S2H, I).

Overall, these findings suggest that the neuroprotective effects of OM-LV20 may be mediated by inhibiting apoptosis via the Bcl-2 pathway.

Pharmacokinetic characteristics of OM-LV20 in mice

PK profiling was conducted following a single intravenous (i.v.) injection in the caudal vein of mice (Supplementary Table S2). Plasma concentration-time curves and associated parameters are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. The concentration at time zero (C0), equivalent to Cmax for intravenous bolus dosing, was extrapolated from the y-intercept. As expected, the PK concentration-time pattern of OM-LV20 fit a two-compartment model, separating distribution and elimination phases (Supplementary Figure S1). The metabolic half-life (T1/2) of OM-LV20 was 2.79, in agreement with previous data (Yin et al., 2021). Plasma detection of OM-LV20 by LC-MS/MS showed a fast distribution phase (T1/2α, ~0.14 h) followed by a slower elimination phase (T1/2β, ~6.59 h). The central volume of distribution (VC) exceeded 60 mL/kg, considerably larger than the total plasma volume in mice (~58.5 mL/kg), suggesting extensive tissue distribution.

Biodistribution studies

Biodistribution analysis was conducted to evaluate tissue-specific distribution of 3H-OM-LV20 at 1 and 5 h following i.v. injection via the tail vein. Apparent volume of distribution (VD, expressed in µL/g) for each organ was calculated by normalizing tissue radioactivity (dpm/g) to corresponding plasma radioactivity (dpm/µL). Radiolabeled OM-LV20 was detected in all major organs at both time points (Supplementary Figure S5A), with the highest accumulation observed in the liver, lung, brain, and kidney (Supplementary Figure S5B), consistent with preferential uptake by highly vascularized organs. Plasma concentrations of 3H-OM-LV20 decreased from 1 to 5 h post-injection (Supplementary Figure S5C). Despite this reduction, VD values were higher at 5 h than at 1 h in several organs, including the brain, heart, spleen, muscle, and kidney (Supplementary Figure S5A, B), indicating prolonged retention and slower clearance relative to plasma pharmacokinetics.

Permeability profile of OM-LV20

OM-LV20 permeability was first evaluated using MDR1-MDCKII monolayers to assess transcellular diffusion across a model epithelial barrier. Bidirectional permeability coefficients (Papp, A→B and B→A) for OM-LV20 were significantly greater than those of atenolol, a low-permeability reference compound, but remained lower than those of donepezil, a high-permeability control (P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S6A, B). These data indicate that OM-LV20 exhibited moderate-to-high passive membrane permeability in vitro.

In situ brain perfusion was also employed to study transport efficiency in mice. Results revealed that from 30 s to 120 s, OM-LV20, donepezil, and atenolol each showed time-dependent increases in apparent brain distribution volume (Vbrain, µL/g), transport coefficient (Kin, µL/s/g), and permeability index (P, cm/s) (Supplementary Figure S6C). OM-LV20 demonstrated significantly higher Vbrain, Kin, and P values than atenolol, although each parameter was lower than those of donepezil at 120 s (Supplementary Figure S6D). These findings support OM-LV20 as a BBB-permeable compound with favorable brain delivery kinetics.

DISCUSSION

TBI remains a major global health burden, disproportionately affecting young children (0–4 years), adolescents and young adults (15–24 years), and adults over 65 years (Galgano et al., 2017). Despite widespread use of clinical strategies—such as head elevation, hyperventilation, seizure prophylaxis, hyperosmolar therapy, medically induced coma, therapeutic cooling, intracranial pressure monitoring, and neurosurgical decompression—treatment outcomes remain suboptimal. Although novel therapeutic approaches are under active investigation, none have yet demonstrated definitive clinical benefit (Galgano et al., 2017; Pischiutta et al., 2025).

Cognitive impairment constitutes one of the most persistent and disabling sequelae of TBI, affecting an estimated 43% of individuals with moderate to severe injury (Lai et al., 2022; Selassie et al., 2008). Patients frequently experience slowed processing speed, memory deficits, mental fatigue, attentional disturbances, and heightened anxiety (Vallat‐Azouvi et al., 2018). While some symptoms resolve within 1–2 weeks post-injury, cognitive impairment can persist for 1–3 months (Zhang et al., 2023) and may remain unresolved for over a year in approximately 15% of cases (Eme, 2017). To date, no pharmacologic intervention has proven effective in reversing or ameliorating these deficits, underscoring the urgent need for advancing both translational research and clinical management in TBI.

The present study demonstrated that administration of the bioactive peptide OM-LV20 markedly improved both locomotor and cognitive outcomes in a CCI mouse model of TBI. Notably, daily intraperitoneal delivery of OM-LV20 for 7 dpi significantly attenuated cerebral edema and facilitated functional recovery across neurological and behavioral domains. Histological and ultrastructural analyses, including H&E staining, Nissl staining, and TEM, confirmed restoration of neuronal architecture, reduced cellular degeneration, and prevention of neuronal loss. Additionally, transcriptomic profiling via RNA-seq, followed by targeted verification experiments, indicated that OM-LV20 induced shifts in gene expression signatures within the frontal cortex and hippocampus. These alterations were consistent with inhibition of mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathways, suggesting that the therapeutic efficacy of OM-LV20 involves preservation of neuronal viability through inhibition of intrinsic cell death signaling.

OM-LV20 is a bioactive polypeptide previously isolated from the skin secretions of Odorrana margaretae, an odorous frog species native to Southwest China. Peptide-based agents have emerged as a rapidly expanding class of drug candidates noted for their high target specificity, potent bioactivity, and low systemic toxicity, similar to the impact of insulin (Apostolopoulos et al., 2021; Banting, 1922; Ru et al., 2025). Amphibian skin serves as a rich reservoir of defense-related peptides, shaped by evolutionary pressure to withstand pathogens and environmental challenges, and has yielded numerous compounds with therapeutic potential (Barros et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025). Natural sources continue to inform pharmaceutical innovation, contributing to over half of currently available drugs (Newman & Cragg, 2007). Representative examples include Aggrastat, derived from saw-scaled viper venom (Valgimigli et al., 2010), Exenatide, developed from the lizard-derived peptide exendin-4 (Parkes et al., 2013), and Ziconotide, based on ω-conotoxin MVIIA from marine mollusks (Saulino et al., 2014). Despite promising preclinical success, most neuroprotective agents have failed to show clear benefit in large-scale randomized controlled trials. Even repurposed compounds with established safety profiles, such as progesterone, have yielded negative outcomes in pivotal phase III trials for acute TBI (Cook et al., 2025; Stein, 2015). Bioactive peptides offer unique advantages in this context, combining structural modifiability, strong pharmacological activity, and favorable safety profiles. The present findings establish OM-LV20 as a promising therapeutic candidate for TBI intervention.

Although peptide-based drugs can be limited by short half-lives, restricted BBB permeability, and poor oral bioavailability, recent advances in chemical modification and nanomaterial delivery platforms have markedly expanded their applicability for CNS diseases (Jia et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2023; Muttenthaler et al., 2021; Mwangi et al., 2023). OM-LV20, as a low-molecular-weight polypeptide (<2 kDa), demonstrated promising pharmacokinetic and distributional properties supportive of CNS targeting. Following intravenous administration, OM-LV20 exhibited a rapid distribution phase, consistent with its compact molecular size, enabling efficient tissue penetration. Biodistribution analyses revealed prolonged residence time in target tissues, particularly the brain, despite declining plasma levels between 1 and 5 h post-injection, indicating sustained CNS exposure. The apparent volume of distribution increased over time across several peripheral organs, suggesting progressive accumulation. In parallel, OM-LV20 demonstrated efficient permeability coefficients in both in vitro and in vivo BBB assays. Collectively, these findings support OM-LV20 as a viable candidate for CNS drug development. Its promising neuroprotective effects in TBI models highlight the therapeutic promise of bioactive peptides derived from natural sources. Further investigation is warranted to delineate the mechanistic pathways underlying OM-LV20 transport and activity within the brain microenvironment.

TBI initiates a complex cascade of secondary pathophysiological changes in brain tissue, characterized by neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, accumulation of excitatory amino acids, activation of lysosomal enzymes, and mitochondria-mediated neuronal apoptosis (Lin et al., 2016; Mantash et al., 2025; Prabhakar et al., 2022; The Lancet Neurology, 2022). Among the regulators of programmed cell death in response to cellular injury, the Bcl-2 family, alongside apoptosis promoters like Bax and Bad, plays pivotal roles by influencing mitochondrial membrane permeability (Quarato et al., 2022). Neurons lacking Bcl-2 often display heightened vulnerability to injury-induced apoptosis (Bähr, 2000; Flores-Romero et al., 2023). Notably, Bax oligomerization forms transmembrane pores facilitating cytochrome c release from the mitochondrial intermembrane space (Du et al., 2016), triggering a cascade of events involving caspase activation—particularly the effector Caspase-3—ultimately leading to caspase-mediated apoptosis (Elmore, 2007; Gu et al., 2021). Our study corroborated these mechanisms, demonstrating that TBI markedly increased Bax, Caspase-1, and Caspase-3 levels, while reducing Bcl-2 mRNA levels. Mitochondrial fractions exhibited increased Bax accumulation with concomitant cytoplasmic depletion, alongside reduced mitochondrial cytochrome c and increased cytoplasmic cytochrome c content—hallmarks of mitochondrial membrane destabilization. Elevated Bax/Bcl-2 ratios and caspase up-regulation in whole-brain lysates further supported activation of mitochondria-dependent apoptosis pathways following injury. Administration of OM-LV20 post-TBI attenuated these alterations by suppressing Bax translocation, restoring Bcl-2 expression, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and preventing cytochrome c efflux, thereby disrupting downstream caspase activation. These findings establish OM-LV20 as a potent modulator of mitochondria-centered neuronal apoptosis, offering a mechanistic foundation for its therapeutic efficacy in mitigating TBI-induced neurological impairment.

A key limitation of the present study is the unresolved molecular mechanisms and targets through which OM-LV20 inhibits mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. Given the complex cascade of events initiated upon CNS entry, the next investigative phase will focus on screening potential targets using molecular docking techniques, followed by rigorous verification. This approach aims to elucidate how OM-LV20 interacts with specific targets associated with mitochondria-mediated apoptosis.

In conclusion, OM-LV20 administered intraperitoneally conferred robust neuroprotection in a murine TBI model by suppressing mitochondria-mediated neuronal apoptosis. These findings highlight OM-LV20 as a promising therapeutic agent for mitigating neurological impairments resulting from TBI and establish a strategic framework for future translational development targeting mitochondria-centered neurodegeneration.
