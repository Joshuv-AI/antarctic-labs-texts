Sociotechnical safety risks in oncology: a framework-guided qualitative study
Authors: Chantelle Recsky, Michelle B Tam, Arlyn Heywood, Ruth A Dillon, Charlene E Ronquillo, Sandra B Lauck, Leah K Lambert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze oncology nurses’ accounts of technology-related safety events and the sociotechnical factors contributing to patient-, workforce-, and system-level harms.Materials and MethodsA framework-guided qualitative descriptive study was conducted with 28 oncology nurses from outpatient, chemotherapy, inpatient, and radiation settings at a large Canadian cancer center. Semistructured interviews and small focus groups elicited 98 technology-related safety events. Data were analyzed using a framework-guided qualitative descriptive approach informed by the WHO International Classification for Patient Safety, a sociotechnical model of health information technology, and a health data–related harm framework.ResultsReported adverse events most often involved clinical administration, documentation, and organizational processes. Contributing sociotechnical factors included workflow complexity, communication breakdowns, people-related challenges, and interface usability issues. Harms extended beyond patients and included system inefficiencies, delays in care, and adverse effects on workforce well-being such as frustration and moral distress.DiscussionNurses described technology-related safety issues as recurring sociotechnical patterns arising from workflow, documentation, and organizational misalignments, rather than isolated technical failures. These patterns had interconnected impacts across patients, care delivery, and the healthcare workforce. Structured framework analysis of clinicians’ narratives helped illuminate these relationships.ConclusionTechnology-related safety events in oncology reflect recurring sociotechnical misalignments, rather than isolated technical failures, and have consequences that affect patients, care processes, and the healthcare workforce.

Introduction

Health information technology (HIT) is deeply embedded in oncology care, where complex treatments and multidisciplinary coordination demand accurate, timely documentation and communication. While HIT promises efficiency and safety, evidence shows that it also introduces new risks.1–3 Nurses are central to oncology care and frequently witness technology-related safety risks, particularly those involving administrative failures, emotional distress, and systemic gaps; however, their insights are often underrepresented in research and formal reporting systems.4 Guided by a systems-level sociotechnical perspective, this study aimed to describe oncology nurses’ accounts of technology-related safety events and identify the administrative, documentation, and workflow misalignments that contributed to interconnected patient-, workforce-, and system-level harms.

Previous research has documented discrete HIT usability issues and safety events,5–7 but less attention has been paid to how different dimensions of harm intersect and affect patients, staff, and systems simultaneously. Moreover, standard reporting frameworks do not adequately capture the complexity of oncology workflows, which involve high cognitive load, frequent interruptions, and nonlinear processes.8–11 As a result, many technology-related safety risks are either rendered invisible, categorized as low-severity administrative issues, or dispersed across reporting systems in ways that limit organizational learning and system-level response. Consequently, administrative breakdowns, incomplete documentation, and resource constraints remain undercharacterized as safety threats.

Methods

Design and setting

We conducted a framework-guided qualitative descriptive study of oncology nurses’ accounts of technology-related safety events. In this study, we use “technology-related” to describe events involving digital systems in clinical care, “HIT-related” when referring to broader informatics or system-level issues, and “EHR-related” for problems specific to electronic health record functions. Our design combined deductive coding using three frameworks, with inductive interpretation that examined how patterns related across the three frameworks, consistent with qualitative descriptive and applied nursing research methods. This qualitative design allowed for inductive interpretation while using theoretical scaffolding to support clinical relevance.12–14 Our investigation focused on point-of-care nursing experiences across diverse oncology contexts. Some members of the research team were situated within an urban Canadian cancer center, providing contextual understanding and enhancing the clinical applicability of interpretations.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were registered nurses and nurse practitioners with at least 6 months of oncology experience and routine use of the electronic health record. Purposive and snowball sampling strategies were used to ensure representation across the various clinical areas in the center. Recruitment occurred through posters in clinical areas and announcements at clinical unit meetings, with interested nurses contacting the research team directly. A total of 28 nurses participated: 16 in one-on-one interviews and 12 in three small-group sessions (2–5 participants each). Participants ranged in age from 28 to 65 years (mean, 42). Most identified as women (n = 25), and 13 self-identified as belonging to one or more equity-deserving groups. The sample included 25 registered nurses and 2 nurse practitioners (1 person did not specify their license), with nursing experience ranging from 3 to 41 years (mean, 18) and 1 to 38 years of experience in oncology (mean, 9.5). Clinical areas included radiation therapy (n = 9), surgical oncology (n = 8), systemic therapy (n = 7), supportive services (n = 3), and inpatient oncology (n = 1). All participants worked within the same cancer center using a single institutional EHR; nurses working in radiation settings also accessed a separate radiation-specific EHR.

Data collection

The interview guide was developed inductively, informed by recurring concerns voiced by oncology nurses in clinical settings. Open-ended questions were designed to surface safety-related experiences with technology, consistent with a sociotechnical systems perspective. Semistructured interviews and focus group discussions were conducted between April and June 2024. Participants were asked to share examples of challenges, frustrations, and harms related to HIT, including incidents perceived as potentially harmful. Prompts regarding “frustrations” encouraged reflection on risks not initially framed as safety events. Sessions were in person, audio-recorded, auto-transcribed in Zoom, and manually verified for accuracy and de-identified. Interviews and focus group discussions ranged from 16 to 51 minutes (average ∼30 min for individuals, longer for groups). The same interview guide was used across both individual interviews and focus group sessions. Focus groups were facilitated to encourage participation from all members. Because individual narrative events rather than participants were treated as the unit of analysis, accounts were analyzed based on their content regardless of whether they emerged from individual or group discussion. Field notes documented contextual observations.

Analytic approach

We used a framework-guided qualitative descriptive analysis, combining deductive coding with inductive interpretation.12,15 Narrative events were extracted from transcripts by the lead author and included if they described an unwanted outcome or risk of harm involving technology. Where one participant described multiple discrete events, each was retained as a separate unit. Although similar issues were sometimes raised across participants, accounts were treated as distinct cases reflecting individual experiences rather than duplicate reports. Each narrative event was treated as the unit of analysis, and we used three frameworks to structure coding: the World Health Organization (WHO) International Classification for Patient Safety (ICPS) (what happened),16 a sociotechnical model of HIT (contributors),17 and a health data–related harm framework (impacts).18 Together, these frameworks supported analysis linking event types, contributing conditions, and impacts. Each event was independently coded by four team members using all three frameworks. Framework categories were applied as predefined codes, with definitions refined through team calibration and iterative discussion. We assigned one primary WHO ICPS incident type to align with the organization’s voluntary safety reporting system, which requires a primary incident classification, and to maintain a clear distinction between incident type (what happened) and sociotechnical contributors (why it happened). While the WHO ICPS does not mandate multi-label assignment, this introduces some simplification at the level of event grouping. We addressed this by allowing non-mutually exclusive coding for sociotechnical contributors and harm types, which more fully captured the complexity of these events. Beyond assigning framework codes, the team examined patterns of co-occurrence and contradictions across categories and developed narrative summaries of how incident types, sociotechnical contributors, and harms interacted in practice. These interpretive memos informed the final synthesis of patterns across frameworks.

A 4-person team with clinical, informatics, and quality expertise (CR, MT, RD, AH) conducted the analysis and iteratively refined the coding approach through team discussion. To support calibration, coders trained on 5 synthetic case narratives designed to reflect common safety event patterns. Coding definitions were refined collaboratively, and team members then independently coded narratives in batches of 25. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved in consensus meetings, with the shared codebook updated throughout. Analytic memos captured evolving interpretations, and particular attention was paid to contradictions or overlaps across frameworks.

Trustworthiness

Rigor was supported through team-based coding, consensus meetings, and the use of 3 complementary frameworks to compare interpretations. The team included clinicians and informatics researchers embedded in the study setting, and discussions explicitly considered how these roles shaped interpretation. Coding discrepancies were treated not as a limitation but as an opportunity to deepen analysis through disagreement. The approach emphasized collective meaning-making and clinical relevance, consistent with qualitative descriptive studies in applied clinical settings.12–14 To enhance methodological rigor and transparency, we aligned our reporting with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) framework.19

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia Behavioral Research Ethics Board and the BC Cancer Research Ethics Board (H23-3446). All participants provided written and verbal informed consent.

Results

Overview of events

Events were included if they (1) involved technology and (2) described either harm or clear risk of harm. Initially, 102 narrative accounts involving technology and potential or actual harm were identified; 4 were excluded due to insufficient detail, leaving 98 for analysis. Events varied in type and severity, but all were technology-related, and resulted in downstream effects on care processes, patient outcomes, or workforce well-being. Patterns across frameworks

Some patterns emerged through examining how findings converged across all 3 frameworks. Events categorized as administrative, documentation, or organizational issues were often associated with workflow, communication, and interface-related challenges and linked to broader impacts on patients, care delivery, and the healthcare workforce. This cross-framework analysis highlights how incident types, contributing conditions, and harms interacted in practice.

Administrative breakdowns were often traced to communication problems, personnel issues, and workflow misalignments in nurses’ accounts, with some examples highlighting how interface design contributed to fragmented tasks and coordination challenges. When issues such as missed bookings, lost orders, or patients falling through the cracks occurred, nurses traced them back to software structures that failed to align with clinical workflow. For instance, a physician could enter orders for hydration without activating a separate scheduling order, resulting in a patient not being booked because the interface treated these as independent steps. Similarly, when medication orders were entered in pharmacy under a different encounter than the one linked to the patient’s current appointment, nurses were left searching across more than a dozen encounters to locate the order. In both examples, what appeared to be administrative or communication failures were described as consequences of interface designs that fragmented information. These breakdowns point to the encounter structure as a persistent source of administrative failure.

Cascading harm was also a recurring pattern across accounts. Seemingly minor technical limitations escalated into consequences far beyond the initial failure. A delay in updating a patient’s status as deceased in the EHR illustrates this, as staff unknowingly proceeded to contact the bereaved family to confirm upcoming appointments, and both staff and families may experience harm because of this system delay. This pattern appeared across multiple event types. A missed scheduling step delayed treatment, an order copied forward carried outdated lab requisitions, and an interface that failed to flag the completion of a chemotherapy protocol nearly resulting in a medication error. Small system misalignments were associated with downstream effects for patients, families, and staff, highlighting how compounding issues within care processes can quickly amplify harm.

Finally, nurses consistently described performing additional, largely invisible, safety work to keep care on track when technologies fell short. When information was fragmented, they searched across encounters. When orders were missed or incorrect, they tracked down providers to ensure corrections were made. When alerts proved unreliable, they developed their own double-checks. This safety work was neither planned nor formally recognized; rather, it was developed out of necessity. Across accounts, this kind of informal buffering was common. Nurses described it as part of their everyday work, forming a layer of safety activity that remained largely invisible to the organization and was not consistently captured in safety surveillance systems.

Discussion

Main findings

In our sample, nurses rarely described technology-related safety issues as discrete technical malfunctions. Instead, these issues reflected patterns embedded in clinical workflows and systems arising from sociotechnical misalignments, particularly administrative breakdowns, incomplete documentation, and organizational resource constraints. Harms extended beyond patients to include workforce strain and system inefficiencies, and these impacts often reinforced one another. Nurses frequently compensated for these issues by monitoring for gaps and taking on additional coordination work. When examined across incident types, contributing conditions, and resultant harms, these accounts highlighted how technology-related safety issues impact patients, care processes, and the healthcare workforce. Methodologically, using multiple complementary frameworks with clinician narratives helped illuminate safety patterns that may not be fully captured in routine reporting.

Interpretation in context

Our findings are consistent with prior HIT-safety research on workflow complexity and usability-related risks,2,3,17,20 while also adding oncology-specific insights. The encounter-based EHR structure fragments information critical for continuity, and recurring medication-ordering challenges reflected usability issues unique to high-stakes oncology treatments. Importantly, participants described moral distress, frustration, and burnout as direct consequences of these inefficiencies, underscoring that workforce harm is connected to patient harm.18

These findings carry particular relevance within oncology, where several contextual features amplify the consequences of sociotechnical failures. Cancer care requires precise adherence to complex, high-stakes medication protocols, in which deviations carry serious risk of harm, and sustained coordination across multiple disciplines and treatment phases, including transitions between chemotherapy and radiation. Nurses must also track information from sources outside the organization, including community physicians and hospitals, while caring for patients who may have traveled significant distances for specialized care and have limited capacity to absorb disruptions. Within this context, the emotional burden is considerable, and technology failures add to the strain experienced by patients and families already managing the demands of serious illness.

Implications for system design and safety improvement

The findings suggest that more can be done to identify, analyze, and mitigate technology-related risks. Participants described formal incident reporting as time-consuming and offering little feedback to support learning.4 Using structured frameworks to analyze clinical narratives may help identify risks not routinely captured in existing safety monitoring approaches. Beyond reporting, interventions must address neglected human factors in system design: workflows need to reflect real-world interruptions and nonlinear care processes, and technology should better support seamless information continuity. These findings highlight how safety risks emerge through misalignments across interdependent components of care, reinforcing the need to approach HIT as a ‘system of systems’ during implementation.21

Oncology EHR design needs to align with the actual structure of oncology work rather than treating interdependent processes as separate administrative tasks. In our data, encounter fragmentation, disconnected ordering steps, and information split across treatment contexts created recurring safety risks. Design efforts should therefore focus on making clinically related steps and information relationships visible across encounters, roles, and treatment phases, including coordination across chemotherapy and radiation settings.17

Organizations should identify and address known cascade points where small technical or administrative failures can propagate into more serious downstream harm. In these accounts, delayed status updates, order carry-forward, incomplete protocol signaling, and cross-encounter or cross-system disconnects all created conditions in which relatively minor misalignments could escalate. Rather than relying on clinicians to catch these failures downstream, safety efforts should prioritize prospective safeguards at these predictable points of vulnerability.22,23

The informal safety surveillance performed by nurses should be made visible, as both a source of organizational learning and a signal of system weakness. Nurses in this study routinely compensated for fragmented records, missed orders, and unreliable alerts in ways that helped prevent harm but remained largely invisible to formal systems. Organizations should monitor and learn from this compensatory work, including its burden on the workforce, and treat reduced reliance on such informal buffering as a key indicator of safer system design.24–25

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a diverse oncology nursing sample, triangulation across 3 complementary frameworks, and rigorous team-based analysis. Limitations include a nursing-specific focus, self-selection of participants, reliance on recalled events, and lack of linkage to audit logs or objective timeliness metrics, as well as the single-institution setting, which may limit transferability. Focus group dynamics may have influenced which events were shared, and the use of a single primary WHO incident type may not fully capture the complexity of individual events. Additionally, equity impacts were not directly assessed. Because the researchers were situated within the study organization, interpretations may have been influenced by local assumptions; however, this embedded perspective also strengthened the analysis by grounding it in nuanced clinical and organizational contexts. Nonetheless, the detailed accounts and multiframework approach offer insights that may be informative for other high-complexity care settings.

To strengthen generalizability, future studies should incorporate multiple professional perspectives, ensuring that recommendations reflect the diverse realities of clinical practice. Methodologically, combining retrospectively described events with those prospectively captured in institutional safety reporting systems will allow testing of targeted interventions such as encounter-linking enforcement, scheduling guardrails, and shared clinical views, while also clarifying what each approach reveals or may miss. Longitudinal studies could be used to assess impacts on key metrics, including order-to-execution lag, missed appointments, and nurse-reported stress. Additionally, ethnographic observation should complement traditional incident reporting to better capture the hidden labor of workarounds and provide a fuller picture of system adaptation and strain. Future research should also prioritize equity-focused evaluations to determine whether risks and improvements vary across geography, language, race, age, and other access-related factors.

Conclusion

This study highlights how administrative, documentation, and workflow issues manifest as sociotechnical safety risks in oncology care. These findings suggest that technology-related safety issues are best understood as recurring sociotechnical patterns embedded in clinical systems, with impacts across patients, care delivery, and the healthcare workforce and often mitigated through largely unrecognized nursing work.
