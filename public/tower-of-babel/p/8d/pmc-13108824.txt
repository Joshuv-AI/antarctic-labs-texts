Medication Adherence and Clinical Outcomes among Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease: A 6-month Prospective Evaluation using Individual Adherence Assessment
Authors: Seow Yee Ng, Farida Islahudin, Adyani Md. Redzuan, Francis R. Capule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medication adherence is a critical issue in managing chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients for slowing CKD progression.Objective:The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between individual medication adherence and secondary clinical outcomes (pill burden, medication knowledge, and CKD stages) over a 6-month period. This prospective observational study with repeated measures was performed among patients with CKD in a tertiary hospital in Malaysia.Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted among CKD patients, with assessments at baseline, month 3, and month 6. Individual adherence, pill burden, medication knowledge, and CKD stages were assessed at each visit. Groups were compared using Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-squared, T-test, McNemar test, or ANOVA as necessary.Findings:A total of 200 patients were included, with 140 completing the 6-month follow-up. Medication adherence remained stable over time, with 58.0% (n = 116/200) adherent at baseline, 59.7% (n = 86/144) at month 3, and 59.3% (n = 83/140) at month 6 (all P > 0.05). For the secondary outcomes, adherent patients had a significantly lower pill burden at baseline (mean 9.9 vs. 11.5 pills; P = 0.033), although this was not observed at month 3 or month 6. Medication knowledge scores were consistently higher among adherent patients at baseline (P < 0.001), month 3 (P = 0.002), and month 6 (P < 0.001). CKD stages differed significantly between adherent and nonadherent patients at all time points (baseline, P = 0.005; month 3, P = 0.006; month 6, P = 0.001).Conclusion:These findings highlight the clinical value of pharmacist-led, medication-level adherence assessment, enabling targeted interventions to optimize therapy, reduce complications, and improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In Malaysia, the prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is approximately 9.07% of the population, with 0.36% of cases progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD).[1] Alarmingly, the burden of CKD has risen over the past two decades,[2] underscoring the critical importance of optimizing medication adherence. Medication adherence remains a major challenge,[3] among CKD patients, particularly due to their complex regimens that further compromise consistent treatment use.[345] As a result, the lack of medication adherence in CKD may lead to accelerated disease progression and increased risk of ESRD. This ultimately contributes to greater healthcare utilization and costs, making it a challenge for both patients and the healthcare system.[345]

Medication adherence is vital in CKD patients to ensure optimal outcome. In CKD, predialysis patients report moderate adherence, while only a third of patients achieved good adherence.[6] By contrast, among dialysis patients, more than 60% demonstrate low adherence, with very few reporting good adherence.[5] The most common medications showing low adherence rates are phosphate binders, consistently reported as the least adhered to medications due to dosing frequency with meals and gastrointestinal side effects.[789] Antihypertensives also show notably low adherence, especially during earlier stages of CKD, where patients may be asymptomatic, reducing their motivation to maintain strict blood pressure control.[3]

CKD patients are often on multiple medications with a high pill burden. On average, CKD patients are prescribed between 8 and 12 medications daily depending on the CKD stages and associated comorbidities to manage renal dysfunction and concurrent conditions.[3] Islahudin et al. reported an average of 7.0 medications per patient,[8] reflecting the complexity of managing multiple comorbidities alongside renal disease. Similarly, ESRD patients were reported to use an average of 8.2 medications.[1]

With multiple medications, it is imperative that adequate medication knowledge is also addressed in CKD patients. A key barrier to adherence among hemodialysis patients was poor medication knowledge, highlighting the importance of patient education even in end-stage renal impairment. Enhancing patient knowledge through pharmacist-led counseling has been shown to significantly improve adherence and clinical outcomes in CKD populations.[10] A structured educational intervention using booklets and counseling sessions to improve both medication and disease knowledge has shown to be beneficial tools used among CKD patients.[11] Therefore, targeted education and pharmacist-led interventions are essential strategies to improve treatment outcomes in CKD management.

Improving adherence is crucial to ensuring that patients receive the full therapeutic benefits of their treatment, as nonadherence is associated with accelerated CKD progression.[34] Low medication adherence was significantly associated with faster CKD progression, with poorly adherent patients experiencing a greater decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and increased hospitalization.[4] Current evidence directly comparing individual adherence with pill burden, medication knowledge, and progression in CKD stage remains limited.[12] Therefore, the aim of this work is to assess individual medication adherence and its effects on clinical outcome. An individualized approach allows pharmacists to tailor interventions to specific adherence barriers related to each medication.[1314]

METHODS

This prospective observational study with repeated measures was performed among patients with CKD, dialysis, and renal transplant in a tertiary hospital in Malaysia with a specialist nephrology clinic in Johor Bahru, Malaysia. Patients were recruited using convenient sampling on clinic days. Questionnaires were then administered face-to-face. Patient recruitment and follow-up were carried out from July 2024 until July 2025.

The target population was patients with documented chronic renal impairment, with or without dialysis as well as renal transplant. Eligible patients were identified from the nephrology clinic and ward. Patients were enrolled if they met the following criteria: adult patients aged 18 years and above, CKD stage 1 or more, and prescribed more than one type of medication. Patients who did not complete the assessments were excluded.

A total sample size of 158 participants was calculated to provide 80% statistical power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05 based on an anticipated medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) between adherent groups. This number included a 20% adjustment for potential loss to follow-up, ensuring sufficient power to detect meaningful differences in study outcomes.[15] The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparison of two independent means: n = (2[Z1-α/2 + Z1-β]2)/d2, where Z1-α/2 = 1.96 for a two-sided significance level of 0.05, Z1-β = 0.84 for 80% power, and d = 0.5 represents a medium effect size (Cohen’s d).

Demographic details, clinical data, medication adherence, burden, and knowledge were collected through a questionnaire based on previous studies.[916] Demographic data included age, gender, ethnicity, and education level. Clinical data included years diagnosed with CKD, comorbidities, eGFR, albuminuria level, CKD stage, blood pressure, prescribed medication, and complementary alternative medicine. Both demographic and clinical data were collected from the patient as well as from medical records.

Individual medication adherence was measured by calculating the number of missed doses of each individual medication reported by the patient for the past 1 month.[916] Patients were considered adherent if the percentage of consumption of prescribed medications was higher than or equal to 80%. For individuals who used more than one medication, the overall individual medication adherence was determined by considering patients who were adherent to all medications (achieved >80% adherence for each individual medication taken), as adherent.[916] Reasons for nonadherence to each medication were noted and categorized based on several studies, such as forgetfulness, complex dosing schedule, fear of adverse effects, unpleasant taste, do not believe it works, and feeling better.[916]

Medication knowledge was assessed for each medication taken using an assessment of medication dose (D), frequency (F), indication (I), and administration (T).[916] Medication knowledge was measured based on the number of correct responses as a correct answer scored as “1,” while an incorrect answer scored “0.”[916]

Study outcomes were measured at baseline and 3 and 6 months. The primary outcome was individual medication adherence. The secondary outcomes were defined as pill burden, medication knowledge, and CKD stages. CKD stages were based on eGFR and albumin levels (KDIGO 2021).

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the National Medical Research Register (NMRR) (Approval No: NMRR ID-24-01908-CVZ) and the Research Ethics Committee, The National University of Malaysia (Approval No: JEP-2024-836). Permission to conduct the study was granted by the hospital administration prior to data collection. All participants were informed about the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were ensured by assigning unique identification codes, removing all personal identifiers from the dataset, and storing data in password-protected files accessible only to the research team, in accordance with institutional policies and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All data obtained in this study were analyzed using Jamovi version 2.6.44, an open-source graphical statistical platform. Descriptive analysis for respondents’ demographic and characteristics was presented as number, percentage, and mean with (±) standard deviation where necessary. Groups were compared using Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-squared, T-test, McNemar test, or ANOVA as necessary. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Further analysis using Mann–Whitney U and Chi-square tests revealed several significant associations between patient characteristics and medication adherence. Nonadherent patients were significantly older than adherent patients (57 ± 15.6 vs. 47 ± 16.0 years, P = 0.032), and gender was also associated with adherence, with females demonstrating higher adherence rates than males (68.1% vs. 49.1%, P = 0.010).

At month 3 follow-up, anticoagulants exhibited good adherence (n = 7, 100%), whereas phosphate binders (n = 44, 73.3%) had the lowest adherence. By month 6 follow-up, anticoagulants maintained the highest adherence (n = 4, 100%), while phosphate binders (n = 40, 71.4%) were the lowest.

Medication knowledge scores were consistently and significantly higher among adherent patients at all time points (baseline P < 0.001, month 3 P = 0.002, month 6 P < 0.001), indicating that better medication knowledge was strongly associated with adherence throughout the study. When analyzed using the Friedman test, no statistically significant differences were found in medication knowledge scores across baseline, month 3, and month 6 follow-up for both adherent (χ2(2) = 0.146, P = 0.929) and nonadherent groups (χ2(2) = 1.50, P = 0.472).

At baseline, significant differences were observed in CKD stages (P = 0.005), with higher adherence seen in patients with milder CKD and kidney transplants, while nonadherence was more common among those on dialysis or with advanced CKD. This trend persisted at month 3 (P = 0.006) and month 6 (P = 0.001). When analyzed over time using the Friedman test, CKD stages across baseline, 3-month follow-up, and 6-month follow-up showed no significant change among adherent patients (χ2(2) = 1.30, P = 0.525) and nonadherent patients (χ2(2) = 2.00, P = 0.905), suggesting that CKD stage progression was generally stable over time in both the groups.

DISCUSSION

Medication adherence is a vital component of CKD patient management. Indeed, the lack of adherence in CKD patients poses a significant and increasing health burden in Malaysia.[17] This study highlights the critical importance of assessing individual medication adherence in CKD management, given the complexity and diversity of drug regimens required for these patients. CKD management typically involves multiple therapeutic classes, each with distinct dosing schedules, side effects, and monitoring requirements.[18] As such, evaluating adherence at the individual drug level provides a more nuanced understanding of patients' medication taking behaviours, which demonstrated that adherence is typically better for essential life-sustaining therapies such as immunosuppressants and insulin, but substantially poorer for supportive or preventive agents, including phosphate binders and vitamin supplements. This disparity is often attributed to factors such as high pill burden, unpleasant taste, and a perceived lack of immediate clinical benefit, consistent with findings from previous studies.[78]

These findings also align with prior research, which emphasized that individualized adherence assessment allows for the detection of specific adherence barriers, such as forgetfulness, side effects, pill burden, and misconceptions about the necessity of certain medications, and supports the design of tailored interventions.[16] In CKD, where polypharmacy and treatment fatigue are common, understanding these medication-specific patterns is crucial for optimizing pharmacotherapy, preventing complications like hyperphosphatemia or anemia, and improving long-term renal outcomes.[19] Consequently, focusing on individual adherence rather than general adherence measures enables more effective patient counseling, pharmacist-led monitoring, and personalized intervention strategies to enhance medication management and treatment success.

In this study, pill burden did not show statistically significant changes across the follow-up period, possibly indicating minimal treatment modification. Despite the overall stability, important patterns emerged when comparing adherent and nonadherent patients. At baseline, adherent patients had a significantly lower pill burden than nonadherent individuals, reflecting the well-documented relationship between regimen complexity and early adherence behavior. High pill burden is a recognized barrier to adherence among CKD patients, who commonly take 7–12 medications daily.[38] Studies consistently show that a greater number of medications increases the risk of nonadherent to medications.[16] Prior studies have similarly identified polypharmacy as a significant barrier to adherence in CKD, where patients are often prescribed multiple medications for blood pressure, phosphate control, anemia, and comorbid conditions.[20] However, this association diminished over the study period, where no significant differences in pill burden were observed between adherent and nonadherent groups, suggesting that pill burden influence weakens over time as patients adapt to their regimens.

Medication knowledge scores remained high and stable throughout the study period, with no significant variation across time points. However, adherent patients consistently demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores than nonadherent ones at all intervals. This underscores the strong link between understanding one’s medications and adherence behavior. Patients who are more knowledgeable about their medication indications, dosing schedules, and potential adverse effects are better equipped to manage their regimens effectively, reducing the likelihood of intentional or unintentional nonadherence.[2122] Studies have shown that medication knowledge strongly predicts adherence, with one large cohort demonstrating that patients scoring ≥80% in knowledge were significantly more likely to be adherent.[9] The consistently high scores observed may also indicate effective pharmacist-led knowledge assessments within the study population.

The study findings demonstrated a statistically significant progression in CKD stages across the 6-month follow-up, reflecting the chronic and progressive nature of renal disease. This trend aligns with the natural course of CKD, where renal function decline accelerates as patients transition through later stages due to the cumulative effects of comorbidities.[1823] Adherent patients consistently exhibited more favorable outcomes compared to their nonadherent counterparts, particularly in relation to CKD stages, where those with milder disease and transplant recipients showed higher adherence rates, while nonadherence was predominant among dialysis and advanced CKD patients within the current work. These findings support existing literature suggesting that disease burden, treatment fatigue, and complex therapeutic regimens in advanced CKD contribute to poorer adherence.[1922] Hence, improving adherence in late-stage CKD is crucial, as poor medication-taking behavior can exacerbate disease progression and increase the risk of dialysis dependence.

This study has several inherent limitations. First, the questionnaire-based study was dependent of patients’ self-reported adherence, which depends on the honesty of patients. Repeated surveys over the 6-months may lead to response variability, survey fatigue, and loss to follow-up, all of which can reduce data quality and statistical power. As patients were aware they were being monitored, this may cause patients to modify their behavior toward medication assessments being made. Finally, because most such studies are conducted at a single center or within a specific patient population, the generalizability of the findings to wider CKD or chronic disease populations may be limited.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the significant impact of individualized medication adherence assessment on CKD management, showing that targeted interventions can address medication-specific barriers. Adherence was influenced by medication knowledge, pill burden, and disease severity, with nonadherence more common in advanced CKD or complex regimens. These findings highlight the clinical value of pharmacist-led, medication-level adherence assessment, enabling targeted interventions to optimize therapy, reduce complications, and improve patient outcomes. Future studies should evaluate long-term outcomes of adherence-focused interventions across diverse CKD populations and explore strategies to integrate them into routine clinical practice.
