Fibrin Glue in Gastric Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection: A Seal for the Acute, Not the Chronic Bleeding?
Authors: Chen-Shuan Chung
Published: 2025

Gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become the cornerstone treatment for early gastric neoplasms, offering curative resection with organ preservation. However, the risk of post-ESD bleeding—both immediate (within 48 hours) and delayed (after 48 hours)—remains a significant concern, particularly in high-risk patients such as those with large mucosal defects or those on antithrombotic therapy. The overall post-ESD bleeding rate varies between studies and immediate and delayed bleeding may occur in 3%–6% and 5%–6%, respectively.1,2 Conventionally, endoscopic thermocoagulation or clipping of vessels with active bleeding are the mainstay management modalities. To address this, endoscopists have increasingly turned to topical tissue adhesives (e.g., fibrin glue, cyanoacrylate) and mucosal covering techniques (e.g., polyglycolic acid [PGA] sheets with fibrin glue) as potential prophylactic strategies.

Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have attempted to clarify the efficacy of these interventions. A multicenter Korean RCT by Lee et al.3 evaluated fibrin glue application after gastric ESD in high-risk patients (125 patients in the fibrin glue group, 122 patients in the control group). While overall post-ESD bleeding at 4 weeks did not differ significantly (12.0% in fibrin group, 13.1% in control, p=0.79), the study found a statistically significant reduction in acute bleeding (0.8% vs 5.7%, p=0.03) within the first 48 hours in the fibrin glue group. This suggests a role for glue as a temporary protective barrier during the high-risk postoperative period.

Conversely, Kataoka et al.4 conducted a prospective multicenter Japanese RCT investigating PGA sheets with fibrin glue (67 patients in the treatment group, 70 control patients). Despite the biological plausibility, the reduction in delayed bleeding was not statistically significant (4.5% in treatment group, 5.7% in control, p>0.99), though bleeding occurred earlier in the intervention group, raising questions about the duration of protection provided by these materials.

Interestingly, an earlier RCT by Zhang et al.5 in China explored the use of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate sprayed on post-ESD ulcers. Their results were compelling: 0% delayed bleeding in the glue group versus 4.9% in controls (p=0.04), supporting the potential efficacy of stronger synthetic adhesives, though safety and scope compatibility must be further evaluated.

To synthesize findings across variable studies, Peng et al.6 conducted a meta-analysis including 15 studies (seven focused on PGA+fibrin glue). The pooled data demonstrated a significant reduction in delayed bleeding with mucosal covering, with a relative risk of approximately 0.35 (p<0.001), validating the approach in select patients. This aggregated evidence supports a role for covering techniques in clinical practice, even if individual trials show mixed results.

Beyond trials, multiple high-volume observational studies offer real-world perspectives. Tsuji et al.7 compared patients receiving PGA+fibrin glue to historical controls and noted a significant reduction in delayed bleeding (6.7% vs 22.0%). Additional Japanese studies further reinforce this pattern, particularly in high-risk groups, such as patients on antithrombotic therapies. For instance, Kawata et al.8 showed that PGA+fibrin glue significantly reduced bleeding in patients on continued antithrombotic agents (5.8% vs 20.8%, odds ratio 0.23, p=0.04). Likewise, Kikuchi et al.9 observed lower bleeding and transfusion rates using autologous fibrin glue with PGA—a practical and safe strategy for patients with bleeding risk. During endoscopic follow-up, attachment of autologous fibrin glue was observed in 81.8% and 68.2% of lesions on first day and 7 days after ESD, respectively.

This well-designed randomized controlled trial by Kim et al.10 explores an important clinical question: does the routine application of fibrin glue after gastric ESD reduce post-procedural bleeding? The study, involving 262 patients, demonstrates methodological rigor, including clear definitions of endpoints, appropriate subgroup analyses, and adherence to ethical standards. However, the findings reveal no statistically significant reduction in bleeding rates with fibrin glue use in the general population, with bleeding rates remaining around 10% in both groups. Notably, exploratory subgroup analyses indicate a potential benefit in high-risk patients, particularly those taking antithrombotic agents and with large resected specimens (≥4 cm), where early bleeding appeared reduced numerically in the fibrin glue group (overall bleeding rate 12.5% vs 22.7%, p=0.675). Though these findings were not statistically significant, they offer clinically relevant signals that merit further targeted investigation. A key limitation of the study is the likely short duration of fibrin glue adhesion due to the gastric acidic environment, as discussed by the authors. This underscores the need to explore whether stronger acid suppression could prolong its hemostatic effect. Additionally, the open-label design introduces possible bias, despite patient blinding. This trial provides valuable evidence that challenges the routine use of fibrin glue post-ESD in average-risk patients. However, it opens avenues for future research focused on optimizing outcomes in specific high-risk cohorts and refining supportive pharmacologic strategies.

In conclusion, evidence from RCTs, meta-analyses, and large observational studies suggests that fibrin-based or cyanoacrylate glues and PGA-based mucosal coverings may reduce post-ESD bleeding, particularly in selected high-risk cases. Although some well-conducted RCTs report no significant effect on overall bleeding rates, these interventions appear to provide early post-procedural protection, which may diminish over time due to detachment. Future research should focus on refining indications, standardizing techniques, and comparing newer coverings to prolong adherent time of the applied materials. For now, these strategies represent a promising adjunct to traditional hemostasis and may soon become routine in personalized bleeding prevention protocols following gastric ESD, particularly in high-risk patients for bleeding.
