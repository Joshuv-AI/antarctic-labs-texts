Cognitive Impairment Predicts Adverse Outcomes in CKD
Authors: Hélène Levassort, Yousra Hassan, Julie Boucquemont, Sophie Liabeuf, Solène M Laville, Céline Lange, Natalia Alencar de Pinho, Ziad A Massy, Marion Pépin, CKD-REIN Study Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCognitive impairment (CI) affects self-management in chronic diseases, leading to poor decision-making, delayed care, and increased mortality. The specific impact of CI on adverse outcomes in chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains poorly explored.MethodsThe French CKD – Renal Epidemiology and Information Network (CKD-REIN) cohort included 3033 patients with CKD stage 2 to 5 and 5 years of follow-up. CI was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation. Cox models evaluated the risks of all-cause mortality, kidney replacement therapy (KRT) initiation, and major adverse cardiovascular (CV) events (MACEs).ResultsA total of 3004 patients were included in the analysis (mean age: 67 years, mean eGFR: 34 ml/min per 1.73 m2), and 64%, 23%, and 13%, respectively had MMSE scores > 26, from 24 to 26, and < 24 at baseline. During the follow-up period (mean: 3.87 years), 21.5% of patients initiated KRT, 13.4% died, and 15.3% experienced a MACE before KRT or non-CV death. In adjusted Cox models, patients with a MMSE < 24 had a higher risk of clinical adverse outcome, relative to those with a MMSE > 26: hazard ratio (HR) [95% confidence interval] was 1.42 [1.12–1.81], 1.57 [1.19–2.07], and 1.32 [1.02–1.70] for KRT initiation, all-cause mortality, and MACE, respectively. In addition, CI was associated with all-cause mortality in the MMSE of 24 to 26 group (HR: 1.45, 95% confidence interval: 1.15–1.83).ConclusionIn CKD, a baseline MMSE score < 24 predicts higher overall-death, KRT initiation, and MACEs, relative to a baseline score > 26. These results highlight CI’s prognostic value, and suggest that earlier detection could better personalize management, particularly for kidney and CV complications.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, cognitive impairment, kidney replacement therapy, major adverse cardiovascular event, mortality

CKD represents a major, growing global health concern; the worldwide prevalence of 10% is continuing to increase.1 In addition to progression to end-stage kidney disease, CKD is linked to increase CV morbidity and mortality, functional decline, and poor quality of life.2, 3, 4 Recently, CI (previously known as dementia) has emerged as another frequent, clinically significant complication in patients with CKD.5, 6, 7

The prevalence of CI increases with CKD severity and reaches 40% in the later stages of the disease.6 CKD is a known risk factor for CI, and many studies have evidenced consistent associations between impaired kidney function and cognitive decline.7,8 The suggested mechanisms include vascular and atheromatous damage driven by conventional CV risk factors and CKD-specific injuries, such as endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Uremic toxins further exacerbate vascular damage and contribute to microscopic brain lesions (neuroinflammation, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, impaired neurogenesis, and dysfunction of the glymphatic system).9 Collectively, these factors contribute to cognitive decline.7,10, 11, 12

CI adversely affects patient self-management in chronic illnesses such as heart failure and diabetes, with impaired decision-making, worse self-monitoring, and delayed health care engagement contributing to complications, disability, and suboptimal medication adherence.13, 14, 15 Although most research studies have examined CKD as a factor in cognitive decline, less is known about whether CI independently contributes to worse outcomes in individuals with CKD.

In 2024, Merlino et al.16 reported that patients with CKD with a confirmed diagnosis of CI had significantly higher risks of mortality, infections, psychiatric complications, and neurological events than patients with CKD without CI. However, these diagnoses were based on medical records (in which cognitive disorders are often underreported) and not on standardized assessments. Furthermore, the study populations had less advanced CKD (mean eGFR: ∼60 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and kidney outcomes (such as dialysis initiation, kidney transplantation, or MACEs were not evaluated.16

In this context, a specific assessment of the impact of CI on the overall prognosis of CKD complications in patients with advanced CKD is required. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to determine whether CI is associated with occurrence of events related to CKD progression (KRT) initiation, all-cause death mortality, and MACEs.

Methods

Data Source and Population

This study was based on data from the French prospective CKD-REIN cohort. Between July 2013 and April 2016, the cohort enrolled 3033 patients with CKD stage 3 to 4 from 40 nephrology units located across 22 counties in mainland France.17 The eligibility criteria included age ≥ 18 years, the ability to provide informed consent, the absence of KRT at baseline, and 2 eGFR measurements of between 15 and 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at least 1 month apart. Patients who had progressed to stage 2 or 5 of CKD between the census and inclusion without starting KRT were included among the 3033 patients; we assumed that this would reduce selection bias due to variability in kidney function.

According to the CKD-REIN study protocol, data were collected at baseline and annually during the 5-year follow-up period or for up to 6 months after the initiation of KRT. These data were collected by clinical research associates through structured interviews, self-administered questionnaires, the collection of biological samples, and hospital record abstraction. The CKD-REIN protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the Institut National de la Santé et de la Recherche Médicale (Paris, France; reference: IRB 00003888).

Cognitive Assessment

Cognitive performance at baseline was assessed by trained clinical research associates using the MMSE, a 30-item standardized instrument evaluating the following 5 domains: orientation, memory, attention and calculation, language, and praxis. Each domain contributes a specific number of points, with a total score ranging from 0 to 30. The MMSE is widely used as a screening tool to identify individuals who may require further cognitive evaluation.18 In the present study, we chose to compare the MMSE score with the eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio measured from 3 months before the MMSE assessment to 3 months afterwards, so that the measurements were as concomitant as possible. We excluded patients without MMSE data at baseline (Figure 1).

An MMSE score < 24 is the threshold typically used in epidemiological studies to detect major CI, and a threshold ≤ 26 is frequently used to detect mild CI.19,20 As often seen in the literature, we decided to categorize the MMSE scores into 3 predefined groups: < 24 (impaired cognitive performance), 24 to 26 (moderately impaired cognitive performance), and > 26 (relatively preserved cognitive performance – the reference group).

Outcomes

The following 3 clinical events were considered separately as coprimary outcomes: (i) KRT initiation, defined as kidney transplantation or the initiation of dialysis; (ii) all-cause mortality; and (iii) MACEs, including myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, and CV death.

In a sensitivity analysis, we analyzed a “kidney failure” outcome, defined as either having an eGFR that fell below the threshold of 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or having initiated KRT during the follow-up. Patients who had an eGFR < 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at baseline were excluded from the sensitivity analysis.

All these outcomes were evaluated over a 5-year follow-up period.

Data

Patients were screened for a history of diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, current smoking, cerebrovascular disease, and CV disease. The sociodemographic characteristics recorded were age, sex, educational level, functional autonomy (assessed as an instrumental activities of daily living score), and living alone or not.21 Symptoms of depression were assessed using the short-form Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale, on which a higher score indicates a higher depressive symptom burden. Current depression was defined as either the use of antidepressant medication or a Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression short form score ≥ 10 out of 30. Several studies have shown that this cut-off is effective for detecting depression.22 Systolic blood pressure, height, and weight were measured at baseline, and body mass index was calculated. A specific electronic case report form (linked to the international Anatomical Therapeutic and Chemical thesaurus) was used by the clinical research associates to record the drugs prescribed to patients in the 3 months before the enrolment visit. Compliance was evaluated using the Girerd self-questionnaire.23 All patients were prescribed a set of standard blood and urine tests (recommended by the French health authorities as part of routine CKD care) to be performed at their usual medical laboratory. eGFR was estimated using the 2009 creatinine-based CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation with the ethnic variable.24 Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio was either measured directly or estimated from proteinuria measurements.2,25 Further details of data collection are given in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

First, baseline clinical, laboratory, and sociodemographic characteristics were described for the overall study population (n =3004) and then for the 3 MMSE score categories (< 24; 24–26; > 26). An analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to continuous variables (depending on the normality of distribution), and a chi-square test was applied to categorical variables.

We estimated the cumulative incidence of each event when considering competing risks and we checked the proportional hazards assumption by using the “cmprsk” and “survival” packages in R software, respectively.26 To account for competing risks, the dates were censored as follows: for the all-cause death outcome, data were censored at the date of KRT initiation; for the KRT outcome, data were censored at the date of all-cause death before the KRT initiation; and for the MACE outcome, data were censored at the date of KRT initiation or non-CV death before the MACE.

The association between cognitive performance (the MMSE score) at baseline and the occurrence of clinical outcomes (KRT initiation, all-cause death, and MACEs) during follow-up was assessed using separate, cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models for each outcome. For the adjusted Cox models, we examined and accounted for several confounding factors known to be associated with cognitive function and CKD, based on a directed acyclic graph. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and educational level. Model 2 additionally included CV risk factors (systolic blood pressure [per 10 mm Hg increment], diabetes, dyslipidemia, current smoking, obesity), a history of CV disease, and current depression. Model 3 further adjusted for eGFR (per 5 ml/min per 1.73 m2 decrement), and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. The MMSE score was included in all models as a categorical variable, using the > 26 group as the reference. Associations were expressed as HRs with their 95% confidence interval.

As mentioned above, kidney failure was considered as an outcome in a sensitivity analysis. For this analysis, we exclude patients with eGFR < 25 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at baseline. We applied the same analytical approach (including cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models) and censored data at the date of all-cause death before kidney failure.

An alternative approach to accounting for competing risks was adopted in a second sensitivity analysis. Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models were used for each outcome, via the “adjustedCurves” package in R.27 Cox cause-specific models were used to estimate the instantaneous HR of the event of interest among subjects who were still event-free at a given time point, treating competing events as censoring. These models are particularly informative for identifying etiological associations and risk factors. In contrast, Fine and Gray models were used to directly estimate the cumulative incidence of the event of interest, representing the absolute probability of experiencing the event over time, while explicitly accounting for the occurrence of competing events that may preclude the event of interest. The use of both approaches provides complementary information, allowing distinction between factors associated with the instantaneous risk of an event and those influencing its overall cumulative incidence in the presence of competing risks.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (with the “mice” package from R) and the generation of 30 imputed datasets after 30 iterations. The imputation model included all variables used in the multivariable models. All analyses were conducted using R software (2025.05.0).28

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

After the application of our exclusion criteria, 3004 patients were included in the analysis (Table 1 and Figure 1). The mean (SD) age was 67 (13) years, and the mean (SD) eGFR was 34 (13) ml/min per 1.73 m2. Of the 3004 patients included in the study, 393 (13.1%) had an MMSE score < 24, 691 (23.0%) had an MMSE score of between 24 and 26, and 1920 patients (63.9%) had an MMSE score > 26. Patients with a low MMSE score (i.e., a score < 24) had a significantly lower eGFR than patients with an MMSE score between 24 and 26 or > 26. Furthermore, patients with an MMSE score < 24 were older and had a lower educational level, a higher prevalence of CV disease, and had a higher systolic blood pressure value than those with a higher MMSE score (all P < 0.001). The prevalence of an MMSE score < 24 or between 24 and 26 was higher among patients with advanced CKD than among the other patients (Figure 2).

The Association Between the Baseline MMSE Score and Incident Events During Follow-Up

Of the 3004 patients, 647 (21.5%) initiated KRT and 402 (13.4%) died before initiating KRT. During the study’s 5-year active follow-up period, 459 patients (15.3%) presented ≥ 1 MACE before KRT initiation or death (Figure 3). The mean (SD) length of follow-up before KRT initiation or death was 3.9 (1.6) years. Patients with an MMSE score < 24 had a significantly shorter mean length of follow-up (3.5 [1.7] years) than those with scores between 24 and 26 or > 26 (3.8 [1.6] years, and 4.0 [1.5 years, respectively; P < 0.001).

In the MMSE < 24 group, the cumulative incidences (95% confidence interval) at 5 years of KRT, all-cause death, and MACEs in the context of competing risks were 25.5 (21.0–30.1), 22.0 (17.7–26.4), and 26.1 (21.5–30.6), respectively. In the MMSE > 26 group, these cumulative incidences were 22.1 (20.1–24,1), 11.0 (9.5–12.5), and 13.5 (11.9–15.1), respectively (Figure 3).

In the Cox proportional hazard models, patients with MMSE score < 24 had a higher risk of all-cause mortality, KRT initiation, or MACEs than patients with an MMSE score > 26. In the fully adjusted model, the HRs were 1.57 (1.19–2.07), 1.42 (1.12–1.81), and 1.32 (1.02–1.70) for the MMSE < 24, MMSE of 24 to 26, and MMSE > 26 groups, respectively (Table 2). Patients with an MMSE score between 24 and 26 were at a significantly greater risk of death than with those with an MMSE score > 26 (HR: 1.45 [1.15–1.83]) in the fully adjusted model but were not at a significantly greater risk of KRT or MACEs.

Sensitivity Analysis

In a sensitivity analysis, we considered the occurrence of kidney failure (defined as a decline in eGFR < 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or the initiation of KRT). Of the 2918 patients who had an eGFR ≥ 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at baseline, 824 (27.4%) progressed to kidney failure before the occurrence of death (Supplementary Figure S1). The cumulative incidence at 5 years of kidney failure was 33.7 (28.6–38.7) in the MMSE < 24 group and 28.5 (26.4–30.7) in the MMSE > 26 group.

Patients with an MMSE score < 24 were at a greater risk of progressing to kidney failure than those with an MMSE score > 26 (Supplementary Table S2); the HR in a fully adjusted Cox model of 1.32 (1.07–1.64). However, patients with an MMSE score of 24 to 26 were not at a greater risk of progressing to kidney failure.

In adjusted Fine and Gray models, the MMSE < 24 group was significantly associated with an elevated cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality (subdistribution HR: 1.47 [1.17–1.85]; as was the MMSE 24–26 group: subdistribution HR: 1.43; [1.13–1.79]), relative to MMSE > 26 (Supplementary Table S3). However, the association between the MMSE score at baseline and the cumulative incidence of KRT initiation, MACEs, or kidney failure was not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study of the French CKD-REIN cohort, we found a significant association between a lower baseline MMSE score and an increased instantaneous risk of KRT, all-cause mortality, and MACEs among patients with CKD. These associations were still significant after adjustments for sociodemographic risk factors, CV risk factors, CV diseases, current depression, the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and the eGFR. Our sensitivity analysis for kidney failure gave similar results.

Patients with an MMSE score < 24 or between 24 and 26 had a significantly higher instantaneous risk and cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality, relative to those with an MMSE score > 26. A recent meta-analysis of data on over 55,000 nondialysis patients with CKD reported a pooled HR (95% confidence interval) of 1.73 (1.39–2.16) for the association between CI and all-cause mortality.29 Similarly, Merlino et al.,16 identified excess mortality among patients with CI. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that this relationship persists in 2 analyses that account for competing risks in different ways.

Regarding KRT initiation, kidney failure, and MACEs, an MMSE score < 24 was associated with a significantly elevated instantaneous risk in cause-specific Cox models. However, in the Fine and Gray models (which account for competing events differently), only a nonsignificant trend was observed for the cumulative incidence, with CIs at the threshold of statistical significance. As explained above, Fine and Gray models estimate the actual cumulative incidence (i.e., the probability of event occurrence over time) while accounting for competing risks. Considering the high risk of death in patients with impaired cognitive performance, the cumulative incidence of death appeared to dominate over that of KRT initiation, kidney failure, and MACEs; even though an elevated instantaneous risk of these events was associated with impaired cognitive performance. These results align with clinical observations and previous studies showing that patients with CI often exhibit a more pronounced frailty profile and a higher risk of developing interrelated geriatric syndromes (such as falls, malnutrition, delirium, or sarcopenia), which can collectively lead to earlier mortality, often preceding complications specifically related to chronic diseases.30,31

In Lee et al.’s29 meta-analysis, the relationship between CI and kidney disease progression or KRT initiation was not consistent. Notably, this meta-analysis did not report studies examining the effect of CI on self-management. Regarding CV events, Merlino et al.16 reported that patients with CI had higher instantaneous risks of stroke and arrhythmia; however, the MACE criteria were not applied, and competing risks were not considered. These findings highlight the complexity of managing patients with advanced CKD and CI; they face many factors that may increase mortality risk and modify the CKD trajectory, including malnutrition, functional decline, polypharmacy, and concomitant metabolic disorders. All these factors highlight the need for careful consideration of KRT initiation in these patients. Although the Girerd compliance score did not vary according to CI in our study, the worse blood pressure control observed in patients with more impaired cognitive function probably reflects more complex patient management and poorer CKD control in this group. A low MMSE score might reflect an overall frail profile that encompasses vascular aging, brain aging, social isolation, and impaired self-management. Cognitive performance is rarely assessed in isolation and inherently reflects a global evaluation of the patient, integrating multiple clinical, functional, and social dimensions. CI may therefore reflect and contribute to frailty and loss of autonomy, approaching frailty from a cognitive perspective; and should be interpreted as a marker of vulnerability, multiple components of which are associated with an increased risk of adverse events. These findings further reinforce the need for the coordinated comanagement of patients with CKD and CI.32

Several studies have shown that CI in CKD often affects executive functions (e.g., mental flexibility, planning, and attention) and language.33, 34, 35, 36 In the general population, executive dysfunction is more strongly associated with all-cause mortality and CV mortality than overall cognitive decline—probably because it better reflects systemic vascular damage from conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, and might indicate underlying cerebrovascular lesions.37, 38, 39 The evidence from magnetic resonance imaging studies supports this hypothesis, by linking structural brain damage to mortality.40, 41, 42 In addition, executive function predicts functional decline, which is a known driver of poor outcomes.43 Our findings suggest that cognitive screening should be part of routine patient management in advanced CKD. The literature data and the results of one of our previous studies suggest the need for a focus on executive dysfunction in the early stages of CI screening in patients with CKD, to better identify high-risk individuals and thus provide for tailored interventions.33

Our study had several limitations. First, our study population was cognitively homogeneous with generally preserved function. This cohort consists of patients followed-up with in nephrology care and is therefore not representative of the broader population with CKD. Patients with advanced CI are often less closely monitored for their chronic conditions, including CKD. This might have reduced the strength of the association in the Cox models and the Fine and Gray models. Second, the MMSE was the only cognitive test available in the CKD-REIN cohort. Although the MMSE can detect early cognitive decline in CKD, it is less sensitive than the Montreal Cognitive Assessment for the detection of executive dysfunction.36 Relying solely on the MMSE probably reduced our ability to detect CI. Moreover, because the MMSE is a 30-point scale with 1 point per item, its metric properties did not allow us to treat it as a continuous variable. Instead, we used it as a discrete variable by applying cut-off points, which may have reduced the statistical power of our models. Finally, although follow-up in the CKD-REIN cohort extended until 2020, allowing the capture of several evolutions in CKD management, the cohort was initially enrolled between 2013 and 2016. As a result, some recent major advances, such as the widespread use of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (gliflozins) and structured physical activity interventions, were not fully implemented or systematically captured, which may partially limit the extrapolation of our findings to current clinical practice.

However, our study had several strengths. First, the CKD-REIN cohort provided access to a rich dataset and allowed us to examine a wide range of variables and adjust our models for many potential confounders. Even after the application of our exclusion criteria, we were still able to include 3004 patients. Second, the MMSE was administered by trained clinical research associates, to ensure standardized data collection. Finally, our findings were robust because we used several analytical approaches to explore the clinical outcomes. Future research should investigate cognitive trajectories and their influence on treatment decisions.

Conclusion

In our study of patients with CKD, a baseline MMSE score < 24 was significantly associated with an elevated instantaneous risk of KRT initiation, kidney failure, all-cause mortality, and MACEs, in Cox models. However, the high competing risk of mortality resulted in a lack of significance for KRT initiation, kidney failure, and MACEs, in Fine and Gray models; therefore, the elevated risk of death among cognitively impaired patients might have masked (at least partially) these outcomes. Our present findings highlight the prognostic value of cognitive status in CKD and suggest that a systematic cognitive assessment would be of relevance in routine nephrology care. An integrated, personalized patient management strategy that takes account of cognitive performance might help to anticipate clinical care trajectories and optimize prevention and support strategies, particularly in the CV domain.
