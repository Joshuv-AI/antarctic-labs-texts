Targeted Geriatric Assessment to Predict Outcomes in Older Adults With Heart Failure
Authors: Caio AM Tavares, João Otávio Ferreira Meyer, Scott L Hummel, Silvia de Fátima Takahashi, Felipe Vecchi Moreira, Lucca Moreira Lopes, Vagner Madrini-Junior, Marcelo Eidi Ochiai, Silvia Moreira Ayub-Ferreira, Fabio Fernandes, Fernando Bacal, Wilson Jacob-Filho, Márlon Juliano Romero Aliberti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth care for older adults with heart failure (HF) has primarily focused on cardiovascular assessment, with limited attention to geriatric conditions that might affect meaningful clinical outcomes.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to evaluate whether the 10-minute Targeted Geriatric Assessment (10-TaGA) enhances the prediction of clinical outcomes in older adults with HF.MethodsProspective cohort study of HF patients consecutively admitted to a geriatric day hospital in Brazil. Cox proportional hazard models assessed 1-year all-cause mortality, hospitalization, HF hospitalization, and days of health lost among 10-TaGA score tertiles (low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups), adjusted for clinical variables and the Cardiac and Comorbid Conditions Heart Failure risk score.ResultsAmong the 326 patients included (mean age 80.9 ± 7.7 years; 37.7% female; 59.8% identified as White), 76 died within 1 year. We observed a graded association between 10-TaGA risk categories and the primary outcome of all-cause mortality or hospitalization (low-risk, reference; intermediate-risk, adjusted HR: 1.41 [95% CI: 0.96-2.05]; high-risk, adjusted HR: 1.97 [95% CI: 1.30-2.99]). Similar trend was found for days of health lost (mean difference of +22 days [95% CI: −2 to +45] and +49 days [95% CI: +9 to +88] for the intermediate- and high-risk groups). Adding the 10-TaGA to clinical variables and the Cardiac and Comorbid Conditions Heart Failure improved discrimination for all-cause mortality/hospitalization (time-specific difference in area under the curve at 1 year: 0.04 [95% CI: 0.01-0.08]; P = 0.025).ConclusionsIn older adults with HF referred to the geriatric day hospital, the 10-TaGA provided clinically relevant information beyond standard cardiac assessments and may support effective patient-centered care.Key words: domain management, geriatric assessment, heart failure, older adults

The population of older adults living with heart failure (HF) is rapidly increasing worldwide, with this trend expected to continue for the next several decades due to longer life expectancy. As HF prevalence rises with advanced age, it often intertwines with geriatric syndromes,1 such as physical and cognitive impairments, which can modulate disease outcomes, quality of life, and HF management.2 For example, an 82-year-old man with HF requiring acute care for worsening fatigue and pulmonary congestion may also face social isolation, depression, and challenges with medication management—factors that potentially contribute to his symptoms, whether or not they stem directly from his cardiac condition.3 This profile can also indicate frailty—a key geriatric syndrome marked by a decreased functional reserve that makes older HF patients particularly susceptible to reduced therapeutic efficacy and poorer prognosis.2

Older patients with cardiovascular disease experience an average of 2 geriatric syndromes, including polypharmacy, sensory deficits, falls, urinary incontinence, cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms, frailty, undernutrition, and functional disability.1 Coexisting chronic conditions with HF double the risk of developing additional geriatric syndromes, accelerating HF progression and impacting outcomes.4 Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) is the gold standard for identifying these syndromes, as it integrates physical, behavioral, psychological, and social health domains.5 Although CGA captures the overall burden of geriatric syndromes and provides crucial insights for prognosis and care in older adults, its impact remains understudied in older HF patients.6, 7, 8 Few studies exist, and they typically use screening tools that capture only some geriatric syndromes or rely on CGA-based tools that are time-intensive and challenging to implement in acute, resource-limited settings.5,9,10

The 10-minute Targeted Geriatric Assessment (10-TaGA) is a free, quick, and easy-to-administer geriatric assessment tool.5 It screens 10 health parameters through a structured combination of self-reported measures and performance-based tests. This multidimensional tool enables prompt identification of medical, psychosocial, and physical limitations among older adults. These meaningful prognostic factors in older adults are commonly overlooked under the heart-centric model of care. The 10-TaGA has demonstrated excellent effectiveness in helping clinicians identify acutely ill patients at high risk of functional decline, unplanned hospitalization, and mortality.5,10 The tool does not require specialized training and has excellent interrater reliability across health care professionals from diverse backgrounds.5,11 In this study, we aimed to 1) evaluate the relationship between the 10-TaGA and 1-year patient-centered outcomes in older adults (≥60 years) with HF requiring short-term acute care and 2) determine if this tool adds valuable prognostic information to existing HF prognostic tools.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

We used data from a prospective cohort study that included patients admitted to a geriatric day hospital (GDH) within a tertiary health care system in São Paulo, Brazil. This service is part of an academic medical center at the University of São Paulo Medical School, the largest public hospital complex in Latin America. It provides care through 8 specialized institutes to 35,000 hospitalized and 720,000 ambulatory patients annually, nearly 30% of whom are older adults.12,13 The GDH operates within a 25-bed general day hospital. It offers short-term acute care for patients aged 60 years or older presenting with nonsurgical medical illnesses that require hospital-level care for ≤12 hours (eg, intravenous therapy, laboratory test, radiology, health education).10 The primary goal of the GDH is to avoid full-time hospitalizations or serve as an alternative to an emergency department (ED) visit, especially among those at risk of functional decline or adverse hospital-related outcomes (eg, delirium, malnutrition). Referrals are made by clinicians from any specialty in outpatient units, the ED, inpatient units, and home care services. A multidisciplinary team led by geriatricians and nurse practitioners delivers integrated, patient-centered care from admission onward, with follow-up visits as needed until the patient can resume their usual care. Detailed information on the GDH can be found elsewhere.13

In this study, we included patients consecutively admitted to the GDH with a clinical diagnosis of HF across the entire spectrum of left ventricular ejection fraction and any etiology between May 2014 and April 2019. We excluded patients experiencing severe acute events requiring immediate full-time hospitalization, those receiving exclusive palliative care, and those with an elective hospital admission scheduled for the next 12 months after their GDH admission.

The local ethics committee approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. All study data were recorded in dedicated electronic case report forms using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDcap).14

Chronic conditions and heart failure characteristics

Trained investigators conducted structured interviews with patients or their relatives, complemented by a detailed review of medical charts during the first GDH visit to obtain baseline information on medical history, laboratory results, and imaging parameters. The Charlson comorbidity index was computed as a measure of chronic condition burden15 and the Cardiac and Comorbid Conditions Heart Failure (3C-HF) risk score16 was calculated to stratify HF-related risks. Detailed information on information collected in the GDH visit is shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Geriatric and frailty assessment

Study investigators performed a multidimensional evaluation using the 10-TaGA at the first GDH visit.5 The 10-TaGA evaluates 10 parameters: social support, health care utilization (ED visits and hospital admissions) in the past 6 months, number of falls in the last year, number of daily medications, basic activities of daily living,17 cognitive performance,18 self-related health, depressive symptoms,19 nutritional status, and gait speed. Each parameter is scored as either normal (0), mildly impaired (0.5), or severely impaired (1). A total score ranging from 0 (best possible score) to 1 (worst possible score) is calculated by dividing the sum of points by the total parameters evaluated (Central Illustration). Supplemental Tables 2A to 2C provide detailed information on the health conditions assessed and thresholds used. Frailty was also assessed using the physical frailty phenotype proposed by Fried et al,20 which evaluates 5 criteria: weakness (handgrip strength), slow walking speed, low physical activity, unintentional weight loss (4.5 kg within the past year), and self-reported exhaustion. Based on the presence of these criteria, patients were classified as robust (0), prefrail (1-2), or frail (3-5).20,21

Outcomes and follow-up assessments

The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality and unplanned hospitalization within 1 year of GDH admission. Secondary outcomes included all-cause unplanned hospitalization, HF hospitalization, all-cause mortality, and a composite of HF hospitalization or all-cause mortality, all assessed during the 1-year post-GDH admission period. We also measured days lost from health care utilization and death, a complementary measure to days alive and out of hospital, designed to capture the cumulative impact of health care use or lost to mortality throughout the HF trajectory.22, 23, 24 Days of health lost was defined as the total number of calendar days spent in the hospital due to admissions, ED visits, or stays in a skilled nursing facility, plus the number of days from death through day 365, when applicable. For patients without complete 1-year follow-up, the last available contact date was used as the final time point for this calculation.

Investigators, blinded to baseline data, conducted monthly structured telephone interviews with patients or families to collect outcome information. Unplanned hospitalization was defined as any hospital admission requiring more than 24 hours for unanticipated medical treatment, excluding ED visits without admission. Admissions occurring after GDH care were considered as unplanned. The length of each hospital stay was recorded in days. Hospital admission information was obtained during follow-up and confirmed using hospital records when necessary. HF hospitalizations were classified by blinded clinicians as admissions with HF signs and symptoms requiring optimization of HF care. Patients were followed up for 1 year or until death, with multiple contact attempts (up to 10 calls monthly) to ensure cohort retention.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± SD or as median with 25th and 75th percentiles (Q1-Q3), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The 10-TaGA score index was categorized into 3 levels of estimated risk based on tertiles of data distribution: low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. This categorization was done to facilitate interpretation to clinicians and is aligned with how geriatric syndromes are frequently quantified in commonly used instruments (ie, the definition of frailty as robust, prefrail, and frail).20 Baseline characteristics across 10-TaGA tertiles were compared using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables.

We used Cox proportional hazards models to investigate the association of the 10-TaGA tertiles with primary and secondary outcomes, adjusting for HF risk (3C-HF score), sex, annual household income, and comorbidity burden (Charlson comorbidity index). Adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs are reported for the intermediate- and high-risk categories, using the low-risk group as the reference group. A cause-specific hazard model was used to assess the outcomes of all-cause and HF hospitalizations while accounting for the competing risk of death.25 Proportional hazard assumptions were checked by plotting Schoenfeld residuals. Calibration for all Cox regression models (agreement between predicted and observed risk) was assessed visually by plotting bootstrap estimates of observed vs predicted probabilities at 1 year, using smooth calibration curves estimated by restricted cubic splines with bootstrap resamples. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for the primary outcome, all-cause mortality, and the combined outcome of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization, with groups compared using the log-rank test. A negative binomial regression model was used to analyze the outcome of days of health lost, adjusted for the same set of covariates included in the Cox models. Results are reported as average marginal effects in days, comparing the intermediate- and high-risk groups to the low-risk group.

To further investigate the incremental prognostic value of the 10-TaGA beyond comorbidity burden and HF-related risk, we assessed the discrimination of Cox regression models with and without the 10-TaGA score, using areas under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC). Time-specific AUC values at 30 days and 1 year are reported, and absolute differences between models (with 95% CIs) are presented and compared.26 We used Spearman’s correlation coefficient and linear regression between 10-TaGA and 3C-HF scores to examine whether these measures capture distinct phenomena.

To test the robustness of our findings, we performed 5 sensitivity analyses: 1) the HRs for all-cause and HF hospitalization were also calculated using Fine and Gray hazard models to account for the competing risk of death; 2) Cox regression models were adjusted for frailty based on the physical frailty phenotype to examine the associations between the 10-TaGA score and outcomes independently of frailty status; 3) the association between the continuous 10-TaGA score and all binary outcomes was explored and graphically represented using restricted cubic splines (with 3 knots) to accommodate possible nonlinear effects; 4) the impact of each component of the 10-TaGA score on primary outcome was assessed using Cox regression models, with each health condition evaluated individually and adjusted for the same variables as in the primary analysis; and 5) Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed for outcomes at 30 days. This landmark analysis was added due to its relevance for hospital HF quality metrics and reimbursement purposes. Complete data were available for all study participants for the variables included in the regression models, and no data imputation was performed for any analysis. A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant and was not adjusted for multiple comparisons. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.3 or higher (R Core Team [2024], R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Overall

Of the 413 patients admitted to the GDH with HF, 87 were excluded for the following reasons: immediate need for full-time hospitalization (n = 19), exclusive palliative care (n = 27), planned elective hospital admission within 12 months (n = 26), and refusal to participate (n = 15). This resulted in a final sample of 326 participants, with a mean age of 80.9 ± 7.7 years; 37.7% were female, and 59.8% identified as White. The most common chronic conditions were hypertension (87.7%), diabetes (42.3%), anemia (35.3%), and chronic kidney disease (33.4%).

The 10-TaGA score ranged from 0.1 to 0.85, with scores of 0.4 (Q1-Q3: 0.3-0.4), 0.5 (Q1-Q3: 0.5-0.6), and 0.7 (Q1-Q3:0.6-0.7) in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. After stratifying the sample based on the 10-TaGA tertiles, 130 patients (39.9%) were classified as low risk, 117 (35.9%) as intermediate risk, and 79 (24.2%) as high risk (Table 1). Higher 10-TaGA scores were associated with male sex, certain chronic conditions (chronic kidney disease and dementia), and polypharmacy. No association was observed between 10-TaGA score levels and HF-related medication use, left ventricular ejection fraction, NYHA functional class, or HF-specific risk score (3C-HF). Frailty status was more common among patients with higher 10-TaGA score levels: 45.4% at low risk, 74.4% at intermediate risk, and 94.8% at high risk. Primary and secondary outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 365 days (Q1-Q3: 335-365), 154 patients experienced at least one unplanned hospitalization, including 53 with admissions due to decompensated HF. In total, 76 patients died, and 164 patients had a primary outcome event (either died or were hospitalized during the follow-up period). Among the 13 participants (4.0%) for whom vital status at 1 year was not obtained, the minimum follow-up was 153 days (median, 211 days [Q1-Q3:181-271]). We observed a graded decrease in event-free survival for all-cause hospitalization or mortality across tertiles of the 10-TaGA risk score, with a significant difference across the 3 groups (log-rank P < 0.001), as shown in Figure 2A. Event-free survival for all-cause mortality and the composite of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization are shown in Figures 2B and 2C, respectively. The cumulative incidence of HF hospitalization alongside the competing event of death during follow-up across the 10-TaGA risk groups is presented in Supplemental Figure 2.

In the Cox proportional hazards models, after adjusting for comorbidity burden and HF-related risk, the high-risk group in 10-TaGA score was associated with increased all-cause mortality or hospitalization compared to the low-risk group, with an aHR 1.97 (95% CI: 1.30-2.99) (Table 2). Similar findings were observed for all-cause hospitalization (aHR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.33-3.15). Although the high-risk group in 10-TaGA showed a tendency toward higher rates of other secondary outcomes compared to the low-risk group, the results were not statistically significant (Table 2). For the secondary outcome of days of health lost, the mean difference in additional days lost within 1 year of follow-up was 22 days (95% CI: −2 to +45 days) for the intermediate-risk group and 49 days (95% CI: +9 to +88 days) for the high-risk group, compared to the low-risk group (Table 2).

AUC of models with and without the 10-TaGA

The time-specific AUCs at 1 year were higher for all models that included the 10-TaGA score, suggesting that adding the 10-TaGA score improved the identification of patients at risk for worse clinical outcomes. The largest absolute differences (AUC for models with the 10-TaGA score minus AUC for models without) were observed for all-cause unplanned hospitalization (absolute difference, 0.05 [95% CI: 0.01-0.09]; P = 0.025) and the primary outcome of all-cause mortality or unplanned hospitalization (0.04 [95% CI: 0.01-0.08]; P = 0.025), as shown in Table 3. At 30 days, adding the 10-TaGA risk categories to the Cox regression models did not significantly improve model performance (Supplemental Table 5). Time-dependent AUC plots from 30 days to 1 year for all binary study outcomes are shown in Supplemental Figures 3 to 7. Calibration plots for all Cox regression models are shown in Supplemental Figure 8.

Sensitivity analyses

The results from the Fine and Gray models, considering death as a competing risk for all-cause and unplanned HF hospitalization, were consistent with those from the cause-specific hazard models. For all-cause unplanned hospitalization, the aHRs were 1.37 (95% CI: 0.92-2.04) and 1.98 (95% CI: 1.29-3.06) for the intermediate- and high-risk groups, respectively. For HF hospitalization, the corresponding aHRs were 1.55 (95% CI: 0.82-2.93) for the intermediate-risk group and 1.24 (95% CI: 0.58-2.65) for the high-risk group (Supplemental Table 6).

Notably, patients classified as high risk based on the 10-TaGA score had higher rates of all-cause mortality or unplanned hospitalization compared to those at low risk, even after adjusting for frailty status (Supplemental Table 7). For each health condition evaluated in the 10-TaGA, a dose-response relationship was observed between mild and severe impairment (using no impairment as the reference) and 1-year all-cause mortality or hospitalization, with the largest effect size for gait speed (aHR: 2.65 [95% CI: 0.95-7.34] for mild impairment and 3.19 [95% CI: 1.16-8.76] for severe impairment). aHRs for all health conditions are presented in Supplemental Table 8. A nonlinear relationship was noted when evaluating the 10-TaGA score as a continuous variable for HF hospitalization, indicating lower hazards for patients with a 10-TaGA score below 0.4. The association between the range of 10-TaGA scores and clinical outcomes is shown in Supplemental Figure 9.

Discussion

In this single-center prospective cohort study of older adults (≥60 years) with HF referred for short-term acute care, we found that a quick and easy-to-administer multidimensional geriatric assessment was associated with increased unplanned hospitalizations and all-cause mortality over a 1-year follow-up. The 10-TaGA score provided valuable prognostic information by capturing functional measures rarely included in standard prediction models for HF. Adding the 10-TaGA to clinical and HF-related measures, led to an increase of the AUC for the combined outcome of all-cause hospitalization and mortality of 0.04 (95% CI: 0.01-0.08). Our findings demonstrate that incorporating geriatric assessment into clinical practice should be a core component of cardiovascular care, routinely used to evaluate overall health and vulnerability to adverse outcomes in older patients with HF.

We included patients at very high risk for adverse outcomes, demonstrated by 1-year follow-up data where nearly one-quarter of participants died, and almost half had at least one hospitalization. Notably, only 34.4% of these hospitalizations were attributed to HF itself. Our findings are consistent with the recent EMPEROR-Preserved (Empagliflozin Outcome Trial in Patients with Chronic Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction) trial, which showed that among older adults with HF and an ejection fraction of 40% or more, only 12% of all hospitalizations were primarily classified as HF hospitalizations.27

Our findings showed the prognostic value of the 10-TaGA score in risk-stratifying older adults (≥60 years) with HF using days of health lost, a patient-centered measure reflecting both survival and time spent at health care settings. This measure captures the combined burden of early mortality and recurrent health care use, providing a more holistic perspective on health trajectories following short-term acute care.24 In our cohort, patients in the high-risk 10-TaGA groups lost 49 more days of health compared to those in the low-risk group over the 365-day follow-up period. We also found that older patients with 10-TaGA scores below 0.4 had a lower rate of HF hospitalizations, either alone or combined with all-cause mortality.

The American College of Cardiology, through its Geriatric Cardiology Section Leadership Council, advocates for a multidimensional approach to managing older HF patients.3 It incorporates physical, behavioral, psychological, and social domains along with medical assessments. Although tailoring guideline-directed therapy to fuller patient profiles may offer a more effective approach than traditional forced titration, few studies have examined the link between structured geriatric assessments and clinical outcomes in patients with HF.28 CGA-based tools, such as the Multidimensional Prognostic Index—a measure requiring geriatric expertise and 25 minutes to administer—have predicted 30-day mortality in older adults with HF.29 Similar findings have been reported for CGA predicting 2-year mortality.30 Other easy-to-apply geriatric tools, such as the 6-item Identification of Seniors at Risk, have also been linked to 30-day mortality but evaluate fewer health domains, limiting their ability to capture patients’ vulnerability to adverse outcomes.5,9,10 As populations age everywhere, there remains an urgent need for pragmatic CGA-based measures in cardiology practice.

The identification of roughly two-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality and hospitalization in the 10-TaGA high-risk group indicates the critical importance of screening for and addressing geriatric conditions that coexist with HF morbidity. In this population, maintaining independence and quality of life are often the top priorities. Focusing exclusively on the HF-attributable burden misses the broader goal of aligning care with what truly matters to these patients. Older adults with HF seek to avoid not only HF-related hospitalizations —they want to stay out of the hospital altogether.

Our study has practical implications for managing HF in older adults. The 10-TaGA is a practical, multidimensional tool that identifies key geriatric syndromes commonly coexisting and interacting with HF, including cognitive deficits, physical impairments, undernutrition, and psychosocial vulnerability.3 These geriatric syndromes often complicate HF management and impact treatment adherence and outcomes. By using 10-TaGA, clinicians can implement tailored strategies such as physical rehabilitation for falls and slow walking speed, nutritional guidance for undernutrition, caregiver support, and cognitive-friendly interventions for cognitive impairment.10,31 Combining HF-specific tools like the 3C-HF with 10-TaGA may contribute to more informed discussions on prognosis and goals of care.32

Study limitations

Some study limitations need to be acknowledged. First, our study was conducted in a single, large referral medical center, including older HF patients referred to a day hospital providing short-term acute care, and findings should be validated in other clinical settings. Second, the limited number of HF-specific hospitalizations restricted our ability to explore this outcome, particularly over shorter follow-up periods (eg, 30 days postacute event). Additionally, determining the specific cause of hospitalization in older adults with multiple chronic conditions is complex. Although misclassification risk cannot be excluded, our outcome definition followed criteria aligned with those widely used in cardiology clinical trials and practice.33 Third, we could not assess whether the 10-TaGA score provides additional prognostic information beyond unavailable HF biomarkers such as natriuretic peptides. However, some strengths of the study should also be highlighted. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between a multidomain CGA-based screening tool and HF outcomes in a low- and middle-income country. In this context, combining easy-to-use bedside tools like the 3C-HF and 10-TaGA offers a thorough yet pragmatic approach to operationalizing integrated care in cardiology settings where clinicians work with limited time and resources.31 As we deepen our understanding of the value of multidimensional screening for older adults with HF, future studies are needed to determine whether this holistic model improves patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusions

Higher scores on the 10-TaGA are associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and hospitalization within 1 year following short-term acute care in older adults (≥60 years) with HF. This quick, easy-to-administer multidimensional tool provides valuable prognostic information by capturing common geriatric syndromes in HF patients—factors often absent from standard HF-specific prediction tools. With the growing number of older adults with HF across aging societies, these findings suggest that the 10-TaGA can support clinicians in making informed clinical decisions and implementing a more patient-centered model of care for older HF patients.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In older adults (≥60 years) with HF, geriatric syndromes frequently coexist, and the use of a brief multidimensional geriatric assessment tool improves risk stratification for all-cause mortality and unplanned hospitalization within 1 year after a GDH admission when added to known clinical prognostic variables and an HF-specific risk score.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Routine use of quick, easy-to-apply geriatric assessment tool could enhance risk assessment and help clinicians address the unique needs of older adults with HF. The clinical impact of this patient-centered approach warrants investigation in future studies.
