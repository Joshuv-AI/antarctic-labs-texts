Biochemical characterisation and substrate-specific proteolytic diversity of venom metalloproteinases in African puff adders
Authors: Mark C Wilkinson, Cassandra M Modahl, David TK Waterhouse, Anthony Saviola, Laura-Oana Albulescu, Frank-Leonel Tianyi, Taline D Kazandjian, Wolfgang Wüster, Francois Theart, George O Oluoch, Robert A Harrison, Nicholas R Casewell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe puff adder (Bitis arietans) is a highly venomous viper responsible for many snakebite fatalities in Africa, yet there have been few geographically comprehensive analyses of its venom proteins, particularly of the proteases that play a key role in pathology of envenoming. To address this, we have isolated, identified and characterised the bioactivity of the venom metalloproteases of puff adders obtained from a range of localities. Prominent in all venoms was a PI snake venom metalloproteinase (SVMP), derived from a larger PII precursor. This protein existed as either non-glycosylated (21 kDa) or glycosylated, the latter containing either one (26 kDa) or two N-glycans (30 kDa). All the venoms we tested contained either one or the other form: none had both. The 21 kDa form proved to be highly bioactive, with alpha-, beta- and in some cases gamma-fibrinogenase activities and was very destructive towards laminin. Prothrombin and factor X were also extensively degraded by the 21 kDa SVMP, but in neither case did this result in generation of the respective active forms of these clotting factors. This protein was also shown to specifically and efficiently convert angiotensin I to the vasodilatory product, angiotensin 1-7. In contrast, the two-glycan forms were markedly less active against all of these substrates. The one-glycan form isolated from a Kenyan venom possessed activities that were intermediate between the non- and two-glycan forms. Using insulin B chain as a substrate, it was shown that these SVMPs cleave at the same peptide bonds as those observed for similar proteases found in other snake venoms, but the higher potency of the 21 kDa form was due to its ability to cleave peptide bonds C-terminal to glycine residues. The PIII SVMP content of the puff adder venoms was quite low, atypically for African vipers. In some Kenyan venoms, however, there was an abundant PIII SVMP with strong gelatinase activity. This protein possesses an unusual oligomeric structure, being a 140 kDa homodimer (c.f. PIII-c SVMP) but without the disulphide bonds that normally hold the monomers together in this class of SVMP. This diversity in venom metalloprotease activities is discussed with reference to the potential implications on the pathology of envenoming and the development of therapeutic interventions.Keywords: Puff adder, Toxins, Pathology of envenoming, Laminin, Angiotensin

Introduction

Puff adders (Bitis arietans) are found throughout much of the African continent and western Arabia and considered to be one of the most medically important snakes, accounting for a high proportion of the 200-300,000 annual envenomings in sub-Saharan Africa (Chippaux, 2011). Despite this, there have been few studies on the clinical effects of puff adder envenoming. Most notable of these is that of Warrell et al. (1975), who recorded details of 10 Nigerian patients envenomed by puff adders, and more recently Tianyi et al. (2025) carried out a thorough case-series review of puff adder bites treated at two primary healthcare facilities in Kenya, complemented with a scoping review of published cases of individual bites. Both studies found local and systemic clinical features of puff adder bites to be diverse and inconsistent; they included pain, swelling, necrosis, haemorrhage, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, fever, and hypotension/shock.

These diverse pathologies of puff adder envenoming are due to the proteins in the injected venom, whose composition is broadly similar to that of other vipers (Casewell et al., 2014; Currier et al., 2010; Fasoli et al., 2010, Dawson et al., 2024). The three most abundant protein classes in this venom, C-type lectin-like proteins (CLPs), snake venom metalloproteases (SVMPs) and snake venom serine proteases (SVSPs), are highly likely to be those responsible for the bulk of the venom-induced pathologies. Owing to their well-documented effects on coagulation and platelet physiology (Arlinghaus and Eble, 2012; Morita, 2005), the CLPs will be partially responsible for two of the known systemic effects of puff adder envenomating: thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy. It is likely, however, that the SVMPs and SVSPs also play a major role in coagulopathy and are undoubtedly responsible for other commonly observed haemotoxic effects such as spontaneous haemorrhage and hypotension. A few early studies identified EDTA-inhibited haemorrhagic components in puff adder venom, but only in two cases has an SVMP with this activity been isolated. Thus, Van Der Walt and Joubert (1972) purified and characterised a 21 kDa SVMP, and Omori-Satoh et al. (1995) found two distinct haemorrhagic SVMPs with molecular weights of 68 and 75 kDa. Hypotension in patients with puff adder envenoming can be an indirect consequence of haemorrhage but also of the direct action of adenosine (Graham et al., 2005), bradykinin potentiating peptides (Kodama et al., 2015) and kallikrein-like (kinin-releasing) SVSPs (Megale et al., 2018; Sekoguchi et al., 1986; Nikai et al., 1993) all acting to cause vasodilation. There is also evidence that SVMPs in puff adder venom may contribute to venom-induced hypotension through generation of the vasodilator angiotensin 1-7 (Paixão-Cavalcante et al., 2015). As well as their haemotoxic effects, these proteases are likely to be responsible for initiating the processes that lead to local tissue damage following puff adder bites, including swelling, blistering, inflammation and necrosis, the combined effects of which often result in severe pain and can result in long term morbidity. However, while SVMPs have been implicated in causing direct cytotoxicity (Bartlett et al., 2025), there have been no detailed studies of the puff adder proteins that might be responsible for this local pathology.

The abundance and variety of SVMPs and SVSPs in B. arietans venom has been confirmed by proteome analysis (Casewell et al., 2014; Fasoli et al., 2010). Along with the various studies on individual proteins, these proteomic studies have provided some insight into the composition of puff adder venom, but a complete description of the protease complement, and its regional variations – particularly since considerable variation in venom composition has previously been reported from this species (Currier et al., 2010; Dawson et al., 2024) - is required to better understand the diverse pathological effects of the venom and identify therapeutic targets. The SVSP content of puff adders has been the subject of a separate study (Wilkinson et al., 2025). Here, we describe the purification of, and the structural and functional characterisation of SVMPs that we have isolated from a variety of puff adder venoms (Nigeria, Tanzania, Ghana, Kenya, Eswatini and Saudi Arabia). The study was prompted by our observation of differences in content and bioactivities of venom from Nigerian and Tanzanian snakes (Dawson et al., 2024) and proteases isolated from these two form the bulk of this work, since transcriptomes are available for both. This study identifies that puff adder SVMPs are more complex that previously understood, both in terms of their composition and substrate-specificity and we discuss how this range of venom proteolytic activities may link to the pathology of human envenoming and to the required efficacy of treatment.

Materials and methods

Venom

Bitis arietans venoms were extracted from Nigerian (five snakes, named NGA B below), Tanzanian (four) and Eswatini (two) specimens presently maintained within the herpetarium at the Centre for Snakebite Research and Interventions at the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine (LSTM). Five Kenyan B. arietans venoms were provided by the Kenyan Snakebite Research and Intervention Centre, Kenyan Institute of Primate Research (KIPRE), Nairobi, Kenya. Samples from Namibian B. arietans were attained with permission from the National Commission on Research, Science and Technology (permit number RPIV025042023). Individual BitAriNAM001 was wild caught near Walvis Bay, while BitAriNAM002 and BitAriNAM003 were sourced from Windhoek and loaned from the collection of the National Museum of Namibia.

Venoms from individual specimens were labelled, immediately frozen at −20OC and later lyophilised for long term storage at 2 – 8 °C. To deliver the volume of venom required for the multiple analyses described below, venom extractions were performed on multiple occasions from each snake, except for the Namibian venoms which were collected in the field and desiccated before storage at 4OC prior to lyophilisation. The other venoms used in this study were taken from stocks held at LSTM; these are either from snakes previously kept in the herpetarium (Ghana and Nigerian, the latter named NGA A herein) or samples taken from wild-caught animals over many years and supplied to LSTM. All venoms were collected prior to or accordance with the Nagoya protocol. Immediately prior to biochemical analysis, the venoms were reconstituted in PBS (50 mM sodium phosphate, 0.15 M NaCl pH 7.2). For the chromatographic separations, the venoms were resuspended sodium phosphate buffers at the pH relevant to the method used for the first step in the procedure (see section 2.3).

Reagents

All reagents used were of analytical reagent grade and, unless otherwise specified, were purchased from Merck Life Science, Watford, UK or Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, UK.

Protein purification

All chromatography was carried out using either an AKTA LC system (Cytiva) or a Vanquish HPLC system (Thermo Fisher). Chromatography buffers were freshly prepared and vacuum filtered (0.2 μm) immediately prior to use.

Size exclusion chromatography of whole venom

The initial step in all isolation procedures was a separation of whole venom on a size exclusion chromatography (SEC) column. The buffers used for the SEC separation were chosen to facilitate the transfer to the second chromatography step (cation or anion exchange) without the need for buffer exchange. Thus, for SVMP PI isolation, 20 mg of freeze-dried venom was resuspended in 1.5 mL ice-cold PB5.2 (50 mM sodium phosphate pH 5.2) and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was immediately loaded onto a 120 mL column of Superdex 200HR equilibrated in PB5.2. For SVMP PIII isolation, all steps were identical but the PB5.2 buffer was replaced with 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.5 (TB8.5). In both cases the column was operated at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and 2 mL fractions were collected after the void volume. Elution was monitored at 214 and 280 nm. SDS-PAGE analysis and protease assays were carried out on all protein-containing fractions to determine which to select for the second stage of isolation.

SVMP PI purification

Using general protease assays, the bulk of the EDTA-inhibited proteolytic activities were found in the middle-eluting peaks of the SEC separations associated with prominent bands on SDS-PAGE at 20-30 kDa. These SVMP-containing fractions (2-3 mg of total protein) were applied to either a 1 mL HiRes Capto S or a 4.7 mL Capto S column equilibrated in PB5.2. Elution was performed using a 25-column volume (CV) gradient of 0 - 0.25 M NaCl in PB5.2. The flow rate was 0.6 – 1.0 mL/min. The unbound material was retained and 1 mL fractions were collected from the start of the NaCl gradient. Elution was monitored at 214 and 280 nm. Protease assays were carried out on the main peaks and SDS-PAGE and RP-HPLC was used to determine purity.

SVMP PIII purification

The SVMP PIIIs, including the prominent 68 kDa protein visible in some Kenyan B. arietans venoms were found in the first peak following the SEC separation of the venoms in TB8.5. The main SEC fractions containing the 50-70 kDa proteins visible on SDS-PAGE were applied directly to a 1 mL Mono Q column equilibrated in the same buffer. Bound proteins were eluted from the column using a 20 CV gradient of 0-0.3 M NaCl in TB8.5. The column was operated at 0.6 mL/min and 1.0 mL fractions were collected. Elution was monitored at 214 and 280 nm.

Analytical methods

SDS-PAGE

Samples were prepared for reducing SDS-PAGE analysis by adding sample buffer to a final concentration of 2% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol and then heating at 85 °C for 5 min. Electrophoresis was performed on 4-20% acrylamide gels (BioRad TGX) using a Tris-glycine buffer system, followed by staining with Coomassie Blue R250, colloidal Coomassie (ThermoFisher), silver-staining (Chevallet et al., 2006) or visualisation using a stain-free system (BioRad). Non-reducing gels were performed by omitting the mercaptoethanol and heat treatment. The SVMP PIII isolated from Kenyan B. arietans venom required a different method of preparation for non-reducing gels in order to retain its oligomeric structure: thus, NP-40 was added to a final concentration of 0.5% before adding sample buffer containing 0.5% (final) SDS. Molecular weights of purified proteins were calculated using a calibration curve prepared from PageRuler markers (Thermo Fisher) run on the same gel (4-20% TGX, BioRad).

Analytical SEC

For native size analysis of the Kenyan SVMP PIII, a 24 mL Superdex 200 SEC column was set up on an AKTA LC system (Cytiva) and equilibrated in PBS (25 mM sodium phosphate, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 7.2). The column was operated at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and elution was monitored at 280 nm. Fifty μL of sample was loaded. The column was calibrated by running 50 μL of BioRad SEC standard under the same conditions.

Deglycosylation of isolated proteins

In preparation for deglycosylation, a 20 μL sample was denatured by heating for 5 min at 85 °C following the addition of 2.0 μL 1% SDS and 0.5 μL 1 M DTT. After cooling to room temperature,1.7 μL of 10% NP-40 was added and then 0.5 μL PNGase F (at the supplied concentration) was added to start the reaction. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 2 h. Where deglycosylation was carried out under native conditions for gelatin zymograms, the denaturing step was omitted.

Mass spectrometric analysis

Intact mass determination

Protein samples were made to 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and subjected to RP-HPLC-MS experiments performed on a Thermo Vanquish HPLC system (Thermo, Germering, Germany) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo, Bremen, Germany). RP-HPLC separation was carried out on a Hypersil Gold C18 column (175 Å pore size, 100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle size) with the column oven set to 50 °C and the autosampler maintained at 6 °C. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min and the proteins/peptides were eluted with a gradient of 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water (buffer A) and 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (ACN) (buffer B): 2–40% B for 0–10 min, 40–80% B for 10–12 min, 80% B for 12–15 min, before returning to 2% B at 15–15.1 min and re-equilibrating at 2% B for 15.1–20 min. ESI settings were as follows: sheath gas, 5 arbitrary units (Arb); auxiliary gas, 2 Arb; spray voltage, 3.5 kV; ion transfer tube temperature, 275 °C; mass tolerance, 15 ppm. The Orbitrap full scan acquisition parameters were: resolution, 120,000 (at m/z 200); scan range, m/z 200–2000; radio frequency lens (%) set to 70, polarity positive with the automatic gain control (AGC) target set to standard.

MS analysis of tryptic peptides

Purified protein (20 μg) was denatured and reduced at 50 °C in 8 M urea/0.1 M Tris-Cl (pH 7.8), 5 mM dithiothreitol and subsequently alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min. Trypsin was then added at an enzyme/substrate ratio of 1:20 and digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped by the addition of TFA to 1% (v/v) and the reaction mix was then directly analysed by RP-HLPC-MS as in section 2.3.6.1.

MS/MS analysis of tryptic peptides

In preparation for this, the relevant proteins were desalted on a RP-HPLC column, as above. These were then dried in a centrifugal evaporator, 20 μL H2O was added and then re-dried. These proteins were resuspended in 8 M urea/0.1 M Tris-Cl (pH 8.5), reduced with 5 mM TCEP (Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine) for 20 min and alkylated with 50 mM 2-chloroacetamide for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Samples were diluted 4-fold with 100 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.5) and digested with trypsin at an enzyme/substrate ratio of 1:20 overnight at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by addition of FA, and digested peptides were loaded on to Evotips and analysed directly using an Evosep One liquid chromatography system (Evosep Biosystems, Denmark) coupled with timsTOF SCP-mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Peptides were separated on a 75 μm i.d. × 15 cm separation column packed with 1.9 μm C18 beads (Evosep Biosystems, Denmark) and over a predetermined 44-min gradient. Buffer A was 0.1% FA in water and buffer B was 0.1% FA in acetonitrile. Instrument control and data acquisition were performed using Compass Hystar (version 6.0) with the timsTOF SCP operating in data-dependent acquisition mode.

Fragmentation spectra were searched against an in-house B. arietans venom gland derived protein sequence database sourced from Nigerian and Tanzanian specimens (see Dawson et al., 2024) using Mascot (Perkins et al., 1999). Reverse decoys and contaminants were included in the search database. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was selected as a fixed modification, oxidation of methionine was selected as a variable modification. The precursor-ion mass tolerance and fragment-ion mass tolerance were set at 10 ppm and 0.04 Da, respectively, and up to 2 missed tryptic cleavages were allowed. Mascot files were parsed into Scaffold (version 5.0.1, Proteome Software, Inc.) for validation at a protein-level false discovery rate (FDR) of <1%.

General protease assays

To distinguish between metalloprotease and serine protease activities, the ability of the purified proteases to degrade casein and insulin B was determined in the presence of EDTA (metalloprotease inhibitor). Insulin B degradation can also give an indication of the degree of specificity of action of the protease as evidenced by the number of peptide products observed on HPLC following digestion. Key SVMPs were also tested against the fluorogenic substrate ES010 since this is routinely used in our laboratory for high-throughput bioassays.

Casein degradation with SDS-PAGE analysis

Venom or protein samples were incubated in TBSC (25 mM Tris-Cl, 0.5 M NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4) with beta casein at a ratio of 30:1 (w/w) casein:sample protein; using a final concentration of 1.0 mg/mL casein. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 2 h. SDS-PAGE sample buffer was added (final concentration 2% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol) to stop the reaction. This was then heated at 85 °C for 5 min. The extent of casein degradation was assessed using SDS-PAGE (see 2.3.4.1). In EDTA inhibition experiments, EDTA was added to the protein sample to a final concentration of 5 mM and incubated for 15 min prior to the addition of casein to start the reaction.

Insulin B digestion with RP-HPLC and MS analysis

Purified SVMPs were incubated in TBSC with the substrate peptide insulin B chain (with oxidised cysteines) at a ratio of 30:1 (w/w) peptide:SVMP, using a final concentration of 0.02 mg/mL insulin B. Where inhibitors were used, these were pre-incubated with the protein sample as in section 2.3.7.1. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 90 min and the reaction was stopped by the addition of TFA to 1%. An aliquot containing the equivalent of 1 μg insulin B was analysed by RP-HPLC using a Biobasic C4 column (2.1 × 150 mm). The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min and the separation was carried with the following gradient of acetonitrile in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid; 0-36%/40 min; 36-70%/3 min. Elution was monitored at 214 and 280 nm. For the concomitant MS analysis the digest was desalted using C18 ZipTips and manually injected into an ESI-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (see 2.3.6.1 for details).

Fluorogenic substrate assay

This assay, which kinetically measures the cleavage of a quenched fluorogenic substrate (ES010, R&D Biosystems) by metalloproteases was performed as previously described (Albulescu et al., 2020). Reactions were set up in a 384-well plate (Greiner) and consisted of 15 μL of pure SVMP PI isoforms at 0.05 – 0.2 mg/mL in PBS, and 75 μL of substrate (final concentration 10 μM) and were run in triplicate. Data was collected on a Clariostar (BMG Labtech) instrument at an excitation wavelength of 320 nm and emission wavelength of 405 nm at 25 °C for 1 h. The slope of the reaction between 0 and 2 min was calculated for each sample, the average background slope (PBS-only samples) was then subtracted, and specific activity expressed as ΔFluorescence/time(min)/pmole protein.

Functional protease assays

Five assays were carried out to determine the abilities of the isolated proteases to degrade proteins that may be of functional and pathological significance. Degradation of gelatin (predominantly collagen I) or laminin-rich basement membrane material are possible indicators of the ability to cause tissue damage. Potential coagulopathic activities were measured by the ability to degrade the key coagulation factors, fibrinogen, prothrombin and factor X. For the latter two substrates, specific proteolytic action required to generate the active forms (thrombin and factor Xa) was determined. Finally, the proteases were tested for their ability to act specifically on precursor peptides of the angiotensin pathway.

Gelatin zymogram assay

The gels (10% acrylamide) for this assay were prepared using the method of Laemlli (1970) with the adaptations of Fling and Gregerson (1986). Gelatin was prepared by briefly heating a 20 mg/mL solution at 50 °C, then adding this to the gel mix immediately prior to casting, such that the final gelatin concentration was 2 mg/mL (0.2% w/w). Venom or protein samples were then prepared for the zymogram assay by adding standard SDS-PAGE sample buffer, but with no reductant and a final SDS concentration of 1.6% (w/v). These were not heated. Molecular weight markers were run under standard reducing conditions, but with an empty lane between them and the sample lanes. Following electrophoresis, the method of Toth et al. (2012) was used to visualise gelatinase activity. Thus, the gel was rinsed, with shaking, in 2.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 30 min, then washed (3 × 10 min) with distilled water to remove the detergent. The buffer used for development was 50 mM Tris-Cl, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 0.02 % (v/v) Brij 100, pH 7.8. The gel was washed for 10 min in this buffer, then this was replaced with fresh development buffer and incubated overnight at 37 °C. To visualise gelatin degradation zones, the gel was stained for 60 min with Coomassie Blue R250 and destained until clear bands were visible against a blue background.

Digestion of basement membrane proteins

Basement membrane material (Geltrex, Thermo Fisher) was diluted with PBS to 10% of its supplied concentration (10-18 mg/mL) and stored in aliquots at −20 °C. For the assay, a fresh aliquot was carefully thawed out and immediately placed on ice. Digestions were set up with basement membrane material:protein sample at 30:1 (w/w) and performed at 37 °C for various time periods between 2 min and 4 h. Following this, SDS-PAGE sample buffer was added and the sample heated at 85 °C to stop the reaction. The extent of basement membrane protein degradation was then assessed using SDS-PAGE (section 2.3.4.1).

Digestion of plasma proteins (prothrombin, fibrinogen and factor X)

In both the prothrombin and fibrinogen degradation assays, these two substrate proteins were used in the assay at a final concentration of 1.0 mg/mL in TBSC and venom/pure protein samples were added at a ratio of 30:1 substrate:protein sample to start the reaction. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 60 min. For the factor X activation, the final concentrations in the assay were 50 μg/mL factor X and 2.0 (3.5 for PIII) μg/mL purified SVMP. RVV-X was used as a positive control in this assay: this was purified from Sri Lankan D. russelli according to the methods of Khin et al. (2017). This assay was carried out for 120 min at 37 °C. In all cases, EDTA was added (final concentration 5 mM) and incubated for 5 min to stop the reaction. SDS-PAGE sample buffer was then added and the sample was heated at 85 °C for 5 min. The extent of substrate protein degradation was then assessed using SDS-PAGE (section 2.3.4.1).

Angiotensinogen and angiotensin I cleavage assays

Purified SVMPs were incubated in TBSC with the peptides angiotensinogen or angiotensin I at a ratio of 30:1 (w/w) peptide:SVMP, using a final concentration of 0.02 mg/mL peptide. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 90 min and the reaction was stopped by the addition of TFA to 1%. An aliquot containing the equivalent of 1 μg substrate peptide was analysed by both RP-HPLC with UV detection and RP-HPLC-MS using the same methods as in section 2.3.6.1.

Results

Comparison of the regional B. arietans venoms

Electrophoretic analysis of Nigerian (NGA B) and Tanzanian (TZA) venoms from individual snakes held at LSTM, Kenyan (KEN) venoms held at K-SRIC, and Ghanaian (GHA) venoms from historical stocks held at LSTM showed differences in their protein profile, but particularly so the prominent bands in an otherwise clear 20 – 30 kDa region on the gel (Fig. 1A). TZA and GHA venoms possessed a sharp band at 21 kDa, whereas KEN and NGA B venoms had, instead, bands at 26 and 30 kDa respectively.

SDS-PAGE analysis of the venoms from individual B. arietans snakes A. West and East Africa. The venoms shown, samples from two different snakes from that region, are 1, 2: GHA; 3, 4: KEN; 5, 6: NGA B; 7, 8: TZA. B. Nigeria, Namibia, South Africa and Saudi Arabia. The venoms shown, two samples of each, are 9, 10: Nigeria (NGA A); 11-13: Namibia (NAM 001, 002, 003); 14: South Africa (ZAF); 15: Saudi Arabia (SDA). Both gels are 4-20% acrylamide (BioRad) and were run under reducing conditions. Eight μg of whole venom was loaded per lane and the gel and was stained using Coomassie Blue R250. The molecular weights of the markers (lane M, Thermo Page Ruler for gel A, Promega Broad Range for gel B) are indicated in kDa.

In the higher molecular weight region of the gel, there was a low level of the multiple protein bands in the 50-60 kDa PIII SVMP range. This region was particularly faint in the TZA venoms. One exception to this was a very prominent protein at 68 kDa that was seen in some of the KEN venoms (visible in Fig. 1, lane 3, snake 015) including one captive-bred KEN snake held at LSTM (not shown).

These patterns were consistently observed in venoms from a wider selection of animals (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, two stock Nigerian venoms from snakes previously housed at LSTM (NGA A) contained the 21 kDa protein rather than the 30 kDa band observed in the venom of Nigerian snakes (NGA B) presently held at LSTM. Within the other venoms analysed for Fig. 1B, a Saudi Arabian (SDA), a South African (ZAF) and three Namibian venoms (NAM) contained the 30 kDa form. One of the Namibian venoms possesses a noticeably large amount of this protein (Fig. 1B, lane 11). This gel also emphasises the low abundance in puff adder venoms of protein bands in the 50-65 kDa PIII SVMP range, though the Namibian venoms do appear to have more than can be seen in others. Suspecting that the prominent 21-30 kDa and 68 kDa protein bands were SVMPs, we set out to isolate them and test their activities towards key substrates.

Isolation of the SVMPs

The chromatography steps described here for the key venoms (TZA and NGA B) were performed multiple times, each time using venom from one individual snake or one batch of stock venom. The examples used here are for 20-30 kDa SVMPs isolated from Tanzanian, Eswatini and Nigerian (B) venoms obtained from LSTM-held snakes, from Kenyan venom provided by K-SRIC, and from Ghanian, Nigerian (A) and Saudi Arabian historical venoms. Due to their low abundance, only small quantities of SVMP PIIIs were isolatable from some of the venoms and, therefore, only the prominent 68 kDa protein in the Kenyan venom is described in detail here. The chromatography traces and associated SDS-PAGE analyses can be found in the supplementary file.

The suspected small SVMPs observed at 20-30 kDa on SDS PAGE of the various venoms were typically purified using SEC followed by cation exchange chromatography. An example trace for the SEC separation of the 21 kDa from Tanzanian (TZA) venoms can be seen in Fig. S1. The 21 kDa band, as seen in Fig. 1A, lanes 7 and 8, was found in the last of the 5 protein-containing peaks, 1-5. Further analysis (results not shown) revealed that peak 7 is adenosine and peak 6 is mostly the tripeptide SVMP inhibitor. The 21 kDa protein was fairly pure at this stage but was further purified using cation exchange chromatography (Fig. S2). The protein eluted in two peaks, the second of which contained the greater amount of the protein.

Fig. S3 shows a typical SEC separation of a Nigerian (NGA B) venom containing the 30 kDa protein, quite different in profile to that of the TZA venoms. The 30 kDa NGA (B) protein was found in the first half of the split peak numbered 2. The proteins in these fractions were subject to cation exchange chromatography (Fig. S4). Following this, the 30 kDa protein was found to be pure in both of the prominent peaks eluting at 11-17 mL. The irregular shapes of these peaks suggest that it exists in multiple forms.

Because the 26 kDa protein seen in the Kenyan (KEN) venoms has a similar native molecular weight to the abundant multiple forms of CLP dimers found in puff adders, SEC was not effective as part of the isolation procedure. It was therefore isolated from the venom using two steps of cation exchange (result for the second, high resolution, step is shown in Fig. S5).

Using the same isolation methods as described above, small quantities of the 21 and 30 kDa forms of the SVMPs were also isolated from some of the venoms used for the SDS-PAGE analysis in Fig. 1B. A 21 kDa form was isolated from stock Ghana (GHA) venom (Fig. S6) and a 21 kDa form was also isolated using just SEC of venom from an Eswatini (ESW) snake kept at LSTM (Fig. S7). Stock venoms from Nigerian snakes previously housed at LSTM (NGA A) all contained a 21 kDa form (see Fig. 1B, lanes, 5-6) and this was isolated from one of these (Fig. S8).

The 68 kDa putative PIII SVMP observed in one of the KEN venoms (snake 015, Fig. 1A, lane 3) was also isolated using a combination of SEC and ion exchange chromatography. SEC of the venom resulted in a large peak eluting at the start of the separation which predominantly contained the 68 kDa protein (Fig. S10). The fractions containing this protein were subject to anion exchange chromatography resulting in two distinct peaks (Fig. S11), the first of which contained the 68 kDa protein at a high degree of purity (Inset Fig. S11, lane 23). The second peak contained a protein with the α/β subunit pattern expected of a CLP, presumably a high molecular weight form (e.g. tetrameric) as commonly found in many other vipers (Arlinghaus and Eble, 2012).

Biochemical characterisation of the SVMPs

Protease class determination and activity towards a matrix metalloprotease substrate

Using casein degradation assays carried out in the presence or absence of EDTA, the 21 kDa (TZA), 26 kDa (KEN), 68 kDa (KEN) and 30 kDa (NGA B) proteases were confirmed to be metalloproteases (Fig. 2).

Determination of N-glycosylation status of the isolated SVMPs

The four main SVMPs used in this study were subjected to de-glycosylation with PNGase F (Fig. 3). The 21 kDa (TZA) protein appears to be non-glycosylated as there was no change in mobility on SDS-PAGE following PNGase F treatment. In contrast, the 30 kDa (NGA B) protein shifted in molecular weight by ∼9 kDa, which suggest the presence of three N-glycans (assuming 2.5-3 kDa per glycan), but a time-course de-glycosylation indicated only one intermediate (result not shown) and that it therefore possesses two N-glycans. De-glycosylation of the 26 kDa (KEN) protein caused a mobility shift on SDS-PAGE of ∼5 kDa and, taking into account the results for the 30 kDa form, is most likely to possess just one N-glycan. Following de-glycosylation, all three of the smaller 21-30 kDa proteins ran at the same molecular weight (∼21 kDa). When PNGase F treatment of the 26 and 30 kDa proteins was carried out under native conditions (in the absence of DTT, SDS and NP40), the proteins became completely insoluble (result not shown). PNGase F treatment of the 68 Da KEN SVMP showed that it was likely to possess three N-glycans, based on the ∼15 kDa shift in molecular weight that was observed (Fig. 3). The deglycosylated form has the expected molecular weight (∼47 kDa) for the protein-only segment of a mature PIII SVMP.

Identification of the TZA and NGA SVMPs: matching to known transcripts

Tryptic peptides were derived from the 21 kDa (TZA) and 30 kDa (NGA B) proteins and were subjected to MS/MS analysis to aid matching to presently known SVMP transcripts. In a separate analysis, the TZA form also underwent tryptic peptide mass matching by LC-MS. Two sequences to which the 21 kDa (TZA) and 30 kDa (NGA B) proteins matched (PP950538 and PP950451 respectively), found in recently determined transcriptomes from the respective snakes (Dawson et al., 2024), are shown in Fig. 4. These are both metalloprotease domains of an SVMP PII. The transcripts for these two proteins were top of their respective list (all proteins) for transcripts per million, reflecting the prominence of the proteins in the venom as visualised by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1A). As well as the PP950538 sequence, the 21 kDa (TZA) protein also matched well to the PI domain of a homologous SVMP PII B. arietans transcript BAR00042 identified in an earlier study from our laboratory (Currier et al., 2012). The latter is 95% identical to PP950538, with the bulk of the differences located in the N-terminus and the last 5 residues, and there are no differences in and around the active site. The two are shown in comparison in Fig. 4. The conclusion from this analysis is that the 21 kDa TZA and 30 kDa NGA B proteins are both PI SVMPs, derived from an SVMP PII transcript. No disintegrin-derived peptides were found in the MS/MS analyses for either protein.

Peptide bond specificity of the isolated SVMPs

Degradation of the B chain of insulin is traditionally used to determine the cleavage specificity of SVMPs (e.g. Yamakawa et al., 1995). Although all three PI SVMPs were able to fully digest insulin B chain within 90 min, the patterns of digestion for the three are different suggesting differences in their peptide bond specificities (Fig. 5). The 30 kDa NGA SVMP appears to have cleaved insulin B at just one site, generating two products. These two products, plus a third were seen with the 26 kDa KEN SVMP and 5-6 peptides were produced by cleavage with the 21 kDa SVMP. Unlike the PI SVMP forms, the 68 kDa PIII SVMP isolated from KEN 015 venom was unable to cleave any bonds in insulin B (bottom segment in Fig. 5).

Activity of the isolated proteases in the functional assays

Preliminary to this work, we had found pronounced differences in the ability of venoms from the main snakes used here to degrade substrate proteins traditionally used to indicate potential coagulopathic and tissue-degrading activities of snake venoms. Those TZA and GHA venoms were far more potent than the NGA A and KEN venoms in their degradative action against fibrinogen, prothrombin, factor X and basement membrane proteins. On the other hand, all the NGA venoms (NGA A and B) used here, as well as those from ESW and GHA, possessed a strong 40-50 kDa gelatinase activity that was absent in all of the TZA and KEN venoms, and the one ZAF venom in our possession. In a related study (Wilkinson et al., 2025) we showed this activity to be due to a novel form of serine protease. The exception to the latter was a very active, high-molecular weight (∼140 kDa) gelatinase observed in some KEN venoms. Using the purified proteases, we were able to identify the factors responsible for these differences.

Plasma proteins: fibrinogen, prothrombin and factor X degradation

The results of these assays mirrored that of the protease assays described in section 3.3; the 21 kDa SVMPs were far more potent than the 30 kDa glycosylated forms. All of the isolated SVMPs were able to digest fibrinogen to some degree (Fig. 9A). Complete degradation of both alpha and beta sub-units was observed when fibrinogen was incubated with each of the four 21 kDa forms (GHA, NGA A, ESW and TZA), and in the case of the GHA protein, there was even significant loss of the gamma subunit. In contrast the two 30 kDa glycosylated forms (SDA and NGA B) appeared to have only alpha-fibrinogenase activity, with possibly a little beta subunit degradation by the NGA (B) form. The smaller 26 kDa glycosylated form from KEN venom was as active towards fibrinogen as some of the 21 kDa forms.

Gelatinase activity

Using a zymogram assay, none of the purified 21 – 30 kDa SVMPs showed any gelatinase activity. In the KEN venoms, however, a high-molecular weight (∼140 kDa), EDTA-inhibited, gelatinase activity was identified that wasn't present in any of other venoms used in this study. The dimeric PIII SVMP isolated from the KEN venom 015 (section 3.2) was shown to be responsible for this gelatinase activity (Fig. 12). Under the electrophoresis conditions used in this zymogram assay, NP40 pretreatment is needed to maintain the dimer form (as demonstrated in section 3.3.4). In lane 1, run in the absence of NP40, the clear band at 140 kDa is due to a small amount of remnant active dimer, but the bulk has broken down to an inactive monomer under these conditions, visible as a Coomassie-stained band at around 55 kDa. When run after prior treatment with NP-40 (Fig. 12, lane 2) however, all of the PIII SVMP remained in the fully active 140 kDa dimeric form. It is clearly only active in its dimer form. Its N-glycans are not essential for the gelatinase activity of the PIII SVMP. Generation of angiotensin 1-7 by the PI SVMPs

A previous study of B. arietans venom (Paixão-Cavalcante et al., 2015) had shown that whole venom could convert angiotensin I to the vasodilator angiotensin 1-7. Using a simple assay with RP-HPLC-UV analysis, the action of the main PI SVMPs (TZA 21 kDa, NGA B 30 kDa) on angiotensin I resulted in the generation of two products (Fig. 13 shows the result for the TZA 21 kDa SVMP). RP-HPLC-MS analysis showed that the first eluting peak was angiotensin 1-7 and the second was the tripeptide FHL. When using the 21 kDa TZA PI SVMP in the same manner towards the angiotensin I precursor angiotensinogen, this protease was able to cleave at two sites in the latter (P-F, H-L), in the process generating the vasodilatory peptide angiotensin 1-7 as a major product (results not shown).

Discussion

Our study on puff adder venoms has revealed the presence of an interesting set of SVMPs: a PI form, present in high abundance in either a glycosylated or a non-glycosylated form, and a gelatinolytic SVMP PIII with a novel dimeric structure. These have been isolated and characterised. Measurements of their activities against major extracellular matrix proteins, key coagulation proteins, and peptides of the angiotensin pathway point to a possible role in the pathology of puff adder envenoming.

In the TZA and NGA venoms studied here for which transcripts are available, we demonstrated that the predominant form of SVMP in both is a PI which is processed from an SVMP PII transcript, following the scheme of Fox and Serrano (2008). The 21 kDa and 30 kDa PI SVMPs are ∼70% identical in amino acid sequence, but a BlastP search demonstrated little homology with other venom proteases, and with no matches to any non-B. arietans proteins scoring higher than 52%. As well as the clear visibility on SDS-PAGE of crude venoms, evidence for the abundance of SVMP PI proteins within puff adder venoms is very clear in the proteomic analysis in Currier et al. (2012) and Casewell et al. (2014, Supplement), though these proteins were not identified at the time, and also in the transcriptome data of Dawson et al., 2024. It is not unreasonable to speculate that the equivalent proteins isolated from the KEN, GHA, ESW and SDA venoms and those observed on SDS-PAGE in other venoms are also of this class. Although the full length PII SVMP protein (metalloprotease + disintegrin) and disintegrin domains derived from processing of these have often been isolated and structured, particularly from New World Bothrops and Trimeresurus spp., PI proteins from PII SVMP transcripts have not been identified in many other venoms (Olaoba et al., 2020). The most studied of these has probably been atrolysin E of C. atrox (Hite et al., 1992; Shimokawa et al., 1996) which, like the 21 kDa PI SVMP isolated here, has been shown to degrade extracellular matrix components (Baramova et al., 1989).

The N-glycosylation observed in the three PI SVMPs isolated from KEN, SDA and NGA venoms is an unusual feature for this class of SVMP. These were found to all have the ∼21 kDa protein section but with one (26 kDa form) or two (30 kDa form) N-glycans. Although this feature of these puff adder SVMPs is not unique, glycosylated PI SVMPs have rarely been observed. The only one that has been thoroughly documented is rhodostoxin, an PI found in Calloselasma rhodosotoma (Chung et al., 1996) and which is also derived from an SVMP PII transcript. It was found to have two N-glycans, one of which is at an equivalent site to one of those in the 30 kDa (NGA B) SVMP PI (see Fig. 4; PPPKYFSNCS, compared with PPSKYFSNCS in rhodostoxin). In a subsequent study to determine the role of the rhodostoxin N-glycans, Tan et al. (1997) found that removal of the N-glycans did not affect its haemorrhagic activity. Unfortunately, we were not able to investigate the role that these N-glycans may play in the bioactivities of the puff adder PI SVMPs because the proteins lost solubility following removal of the N-glycans with PNGase F.

Of the two forms of arietans PI, the one which will garner most interest, from a functional, pathological and therapeutic point of view, is the very potent 21 kDa non-glycosylated form found in TZA, GHA and ESW venoms and also in some NGA venoms. Based on its identical molecular weight and two-peak elution pattern on cation exchange chromatography (Fig. S2), this is very similar in character to the broad specificity ‘Protease A’ isolated from puff adder venom by Van Der Walt and Joubert (1971, 1972). It is difficult to judge the abundance of Protein A in the venoms they used, but we have clearly shown that, as well as being very bioactive, the 21 kDa SVMP that we have isolated here is the most abundant single protein in the venoms which have it. For these reasons, we made a concerted effort to fully characterise this protein.

Mass spectrometric analysis of tryptic peptides from the 21 kDa SVMP PI matched to a PII sequence (PP950538) in the recently obtained TZA puff adder transcriptome (Dawson et al., 2024). The only other SVMP PII sequence found in this set of transcripts was identical but for a K to N substitution at the penultimate residue. The tryptic peptides were better matched, however, to transcript BAR00042 from earlier work in our laboratory (Currier et al., 2012). In a subsequent study on a range of African vipers including B. arietans (Casewell et al., 2014), a very prominent 21 kDa protein was observed following RP-HPLC of a batched puff adder venom and subject to in-gel digestion and LC-MS/MS. This also identified a match to BAR00042 peptides, though the PP950538 sequence wasn't available at that time. There are also good matches between the sequences shown here for the 21 kDa SVMP PI and those of Edman-sequenced tryptic peptides from the aforementioned Protease A (Strydom et al., 1986). Neither of the PP950538 and BAR00042 sequences have a predicted mass (23,902 and 23,792.5 respectively, assuming 4 disulphide bonds) that exactly matches either of the experimental values obtained by LC-MS (23,293.9 and 23,341.7 Da). This is not a major concern, however, since it is likely this protein will have been subject to various modifications (natural and artefactual) during its time through synthesis, venom extraction, drying, storage and then the isolation procedures. In addition, the N- and C-termini of the proteins in Fig. 4 are assumed from the results of earlier Edman sequencing of PII derived PI SVMPs (see de Araujo et al., 1997) and may differ from this by one or two residues. Taking all this into account, and the heterogeneity seen in the elution peaks on high-resolution cation exchange columns, it is likely that there is some variation in the primary structures of these PI SVMPs, even within the same animal. Thus, the snakes from which the 21 kDa SVMP PI were isolated may have different PII SVMP transcripts to either of those presented in Fig. 4. Because it is a potential therapeutic target, there will be a need to generate recombinant versions of this protein (indeed, this is already in hand with collaborators): either of the PP950538 and BAR00042 sequences will suffice for this purpose and they are identical at the critical Zn-binding active site, and in long stretches either side of it.

The other 21 kDa forms isolated from GHA, ESW and NGA A venoms were just as potent, more so in some cases, than that from TZA venoms, so despite any concerns over heterogeneity of the latter, whatever its source the 21 kDa SVMP PI is undoubtedly a highly active venom protease with a broad specificity. They act strongly and rapidly against all the proteins used as substrates in this study. In a concomitant study (Bartlett et al., 2025) we also found that the TZA SVMP PI was also highly cytotoxic against HaCat cells, much more so than the 30 kDa NGA B form (IC50 2.7 and 16.2 μg/mL resp.) and more so than established cytotoxins such as the N. nigricollis 3FTx cytotoxins 1, 3 and 4 (IC50 21-64 μg/mL, Bartlett et al., 2024). If these figures are re-calculated in molar terms, the TZA SVMP PI was 30-fold more potent in this respect than the most cytotoxic N. nigricollis 3FTx. What is particularly striking about the 21 kDa SVMP PI was the speed with which it acted against the proteins in the basement membrane extract (Fig. 11B), causing significant laminin degradation within minutes of exposure. Among the doubts over using this particular assay to determine potential haemorrhagic bioactivities of proteases are concerns on the duration of the assays and their clinical relevance, with the proteases typically showing their effects only after several hours in this in vitro assay (Escalante et al., 2006), much longer than the time taken to observe haemorrhage in a clinical setting. That is not the case for the 21 kDa SVMP, which acts within minutes.

Considering the ∼70% similarity in primary structure between the 30 kDa and 21 kDa PI SVMPs, the reason for the far greater proteolytic activity of the latter is of some biochemical interest, and having both proteins allows for a comparative analysis. One possibility is that the difference may be due to the presence of the two N-glycans in the 30 kDa form; these are large, exposed structures which would be expected to hinder full access to larger substrates. Unfortunately, we couldn't compare the activities of the 21 kDa SVMP PI with a deglycosylated version of the 30 kDa form, because the latter was insoluble. The intermediate activity observed throughout for the 26 kDa KEN SVMP PI, which appears to have just one N-glycan, does, however, support the idea that the presence of the N-glycans may interfere with protease:substrate interactions.

Since the specific activity towards the small ES010 substrate (Sec. 3.3.1) is much greater in the 21 kDa than in the 30 kDa SVMP PI, it is likely that the differences in activity between the two forms also resides in the primary structure at and around the active site. Indeed, there are structural differences at the Zn-binding sites of the 21 and 30 kDa PI SVMPs (HEMGHNLGIRH and HEIGHNLGINH respectively, see Fig. 4). Using insulin B chain as a proteolytic substrate to determine peptide bond specificity, we showed that the NGA, KEN and TZA SVMP PI cleaved at the H-L, A-L and F-F bonds in this peptide, as is commonly seen with haemhorragic SVMPs (Yamakawa et al., 1995). In addition to these sites, however, the TZA 21 kDa form was able to cleave C-terminal to glycine residues; this may be sufficient to explain the extra potency seen with this protease. Glycine is a very common amino acid, particularly so in the β-turns critical to tertiary structure of many proteins. Interestingly, analysis of the venom gland transcriptomes (Dawson et al., 2024) showed regional differences in the tripeptide SVMP inhibitor (SVMPi) expressed by the puff adders (Wagstaff et al., 2008). The NGA venom from which the 30 kDa SVMP PI was derived has pELW in the relevant transcriptome, whereas the TZA venom transcriptome predicts an SVMPi with the sequence pEVW. An aliphatic residue in the middle position of an SVMPi is unusual in itself, but also, as far as we are aware, this is the first example of sequence variation of SVMPi peptides within the same species. This existence of a different SVMPi structure for each form of SVMP PI supports the notion that there are key differences at the substrate-binding sites of the 21 and 30 kDa PI SVMPs.

Although they are not as dominant on SDS-PAGE as they are in other viper venoms such as Echis spp., PIII SVMPs at around 60-65 kDa very small quantities were isolated from TZA, NGA, GHA and ESW venoms (results not shown). The low abundance of PIII SVMPs in puff adders has also been identified in previous proteomics and transcriptomic studies (Currier et al., 2012, Casewell et al., 2014; Juarez et al., 2006; Dawson et al., 2024). That said, a prominent PIII SVMP was observed in the venom of some KEN puff adders (e.g. snake 015, SDS-PAGE, Fig. 1A, lane 3). Because of its abundance in the venom, this PIII SVMP was easily isolated and, interestingly, it was not active against insulin B, a commonly used substrate for venom SVMPs (Yamakawa et al., 1995). Although it showed some ability to degrade fibrinogen, as most venom proteases seem to do, it showed very little activity against basement membrane proteins and prothrombin and none against factor X. However, the purified protein was found to be responsible for the strong gelatin-degrading activity of the Kenyan venom. It is unlikely that this protein is confined to Kenyan snakes: a high molecular weight gelatinase activity was observed in 1 of 12 Nigerian B. arietans tested venoms in an earlier study (Currier et al., 2010) and this correlated with a pronounced ∼65 kDa band seen after western blotting with an SVMP antibody. Paixão-Cavalcante et al. (2015) also observed a high molecular weight gelatinase activity in a mix of puff adder venoms from Guinea, São Tomé, Angola and Mozambique. The variable presence of this PIII SVMP gelatinase in puff adder venoms suggests that it is a protein that is locally specific and only expressed under certain circumstances.

This Kenyan PIII SVMP is of some biochemical interest since it possesses a novel oligomeric structure. It appears at around 68 kDa on reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE, but analytical SEC and non-reducing PAGE in the presence of NP40 showed that it exists naturally as a dimer, and it is active only when in this form. Each monomer was also found to possess 3 N-glycans which were shown to be unnecessary for its gelatinase activity but, as was the case for the glycosylated SVMP PI, appear to be important in maintaining solubility. Interestingly, the dimer is clearly not held together through disulphide bonds, as would be the case it if were an SVMP of the PIII-c class and, as such, is a novel form of PIII SVMP, not presently accounted for in the SVMP classification system (Fox and Serrano, 2008). It may be that this form of PIII SVMP is peculiar to B. arietans, but it is possible that this form does exist in other PIII SVMP-rich vipers and because of the ease with which it can revert to monomer form during electrophoretic analysis, it may not have been characterised as a dimer. In many respects, this KEN PIII SVMP compares with the 68 and 75 kDa B. arietans SVMPs (BHRa and BHRb) characterised by (Omori-Satoh Ap et al., 1995; Yamakawa et al., 1995). It is difficult to judge from their results what the native molecular weights might be, nor their abundance in the venom, but these were able to degrade gelatins formed from a variety of collagens. It is likely that these BHRs (venom source unknown), our Kenyan gelatinase PIII and that observed in Currier et al. (2010), are all regional variants of the same protein.

The principal aim of this study was a comprehensive characterisation of the SVMP content of puff adder venoms, partly prompted by the distinct differences we observed following SDS-PAGE analysis of venoms from various geographical regions. This is a preliminary biochemical study but presents sufficient evidence that these proteases should be subjected to further in vitro and in vivo studies to determine potential roles in the pathology of puff adder envenoming. The exemplar for this would be the extensive characterisation that has been carried out on the Bothrops SVMPs in relation to haemorrhagic activity (Gutiérrez et al., 2016a, 2016b) and blister formation at and around the bite site (Jiménez et al., 2008; Gutiérrez et al., 2018). Further studies should also address a potential coagulopathic role for these SVMPs, something that is well established in other vipers. At this stage we can at least suggest that the inability of puff adder SVMPs to cleave prothrombin or factor X in the precise manner needed to activate them means that the venom is unlikely to cause the consumptive coagulopathy that is seen in Echis spp. due to prothrombin activating SVMPs (Kini, 2005), and, indeed, this is not an observed feature of puff adder envenoming. It is possible, however, that the observed proteolytic action of the puff adder PI SVMPs on clotting factors contributes to an overall anticoagulant outcome upon envenoming.

Should further study confirm that these SVMPs are responsible for some of the pathological features puff adder envenoming, then what becomes a key issue is the contrast between the extensive degradation of key proteins by the 21 kDa forms and the almost negligible effects of the glycosylated forms. Compounding this marked difference in proteolytic activity is the variability in the levels of the glycosylated forms in the venoms that possess them. This is evident in Fig. 1B, particularly in the three Namibian venoms, but we also observed a wide variation in its levels in the venom of Nigerian (B) snakes; with one specimen having virtually no 30 kDa SVMP PI visible on SDS-PAGE (the venoms in Fig. 1A lanes, 5 and 6 were the two with the greatest amount). In contrast, in all those venoms that contained the 21 kDa PI SVMP, its level was markedly consistent, snake to snake and region to region. We have not presented the results of any biological function assays on the purified SVMPs, but in an extensive study of the coagulotoxicity of venoms from Bitis spp., Youngman et al. (2019), described pronounced regional variations in the anticoagulant activity of B. arietans venoms. They found that Tanzanian venoms were far more potent than those of West African (precise region not specified) snakes and more than that from nearby Kenya venom. Given the differences in the fibrinogen-degrading activity of the glycosylated and non-glycosylated forms of the SVMP PI, it is tempting to speculate that the variations measured by Youngman et al. were due to differences in the types and levels of SVMP PI present in the venoms used. With a range of venoms and pure SVMPs at our disposal, we are in a position to study this potential correlation in future. Whether the geographic variation in the presence and quantities of different SVMP PI in puff adder venoms is related to the pronounced phylogeographic structure in this species (Barlow et al., 2013) remains to be ascertained but would also be an interesting follow-up research question.

Of the components identified in puff adder venom that are likely to cause the hypotension that is commonly seen in puff adder bite victims (see Introduction), the rapid action of adenosine is most likely a mechanism for prey incapacitation. The protein components proposed to have a hypotensive action (BPP, VEGF and kallikrein-like SVSPs) will act in the longer term, however. Their vasodilatory actions will impede platelet adhesion and aggregation, thus enhancing the anticoagulant actions of the CLPs and SVMPs in the venom. No precise role for SVMPs in this action has been described previously, but the study of Paixão-Cavalcante et al. (2015) showed that puff adder venom could convert angiotensin I to the vasodilatory peptide angiotensin 1-7. This activity was inhibited by EDTA suggesting that an SVMP was responsible for this activity. We have demonstrated that this is due to the PI SVMPs, which have the ability to specifically generate angiotensin 1-7 from its precursor peptide angiotensin I, and also from the precursor to that peptide, angiotensinogen. Further study would be necessary to determine if this ability to generate angiotensin 1-7 is physiologically relevant. Even if so, it is clearly just one contributory factor in this respect, but it does the support the idea that puff adder venoms possess a cocktail of vasodilatory molecules, which between them may overlap chronologically in their action and thus help to maintain a hypotensive state over a long period of time, (Peterfi et al., 2019).

This diversity in composition and substrate specificities of the puff adder venom SVMPs perhaps explains why our understanding of the pathology of human envenoming is so incomplete and geographically variant. However, this venom protein - human pathology relationship only becomes more complex when the variability of the action of the serine proteases in puff adder venoms (see Wilkinson et al., 2025) is also considered.

Conclusion

The results presented here highlight the significant regional variations in the SVMP bioactivities of puff adder venoms. Variation was evident even within a single region, particularly the presence of the SVMP PIII gelatinase and the finding that NGA (B) venoms from snakes presently held at LSTM possessed the glycosylated 30 kDa SVMP PI whereas stocks of NGA (A) venom used in a previous study contained the more potent non-glycosylated 21 kDa form. Considering this, it is perhaps not surprising that such a variety of clinical manifestations are observed following puff adder envenoming (Tianyi et al., 2025). Considering the well-documented role of SVMP activity in venom action, it is reasonable to suggest that the severity of the reaction to puff adder envenoming may be related to whether the venom contains either the 21 kDa or the 30 kDa SVMP PI, with the 26 kDa Kenyan form perhaps being intermediate in this respect and future studies will focus on this. This diversity in protease activity means that the well-documented issues with developing targeted therapeutics may become a particular problem for puff adder bites and, thus, this study illustrates the need for puff adder venoms from multiple geographic origins to ensure that conventional antivenom is effective across Africa. The work presented here is a good starting point to address these issues – by identifying the key venom SVMPs, in providing methods for their isolation prior for use for in vivo studies or as immunogens for generation of toxin-specific antibodies, and as the basis for experimental testing of small molecule therapeutics.
