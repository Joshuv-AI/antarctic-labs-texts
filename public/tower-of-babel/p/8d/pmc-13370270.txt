Synergistic adsorption mechanisms in a multifunctional BAC-GO-rGO hybrid nanostructure for the enhanced removal of Ni2+ and methylene blue
Authors: Sankalpa Perera, Sanduni Dabare, Imalka Munaweera, Saranga Diyabalanage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising levels of industrial effluent containing heavy metals, including Ni2+ and cationic dyes such as methylene blue (MB), pose significant threats in terms of toxicity, stability, and bioaccumulation in ecological food chains. This study focuses on developing an efficient ternary BAC-GO-rGO composite with an optimized weight ratio of 80 : 15 : 5 for activated carbon, graphene oxide (GO), and reduced graphene oxide (rGO), respectively. The composite is synthesized by integrating bamboo-derived activated carbon with graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide to overcome the limitations of these carbonaceous materials when used individually. In this process, the use of a wet mixing method enabled the development of a hybrid nanostructure that could effectively utilize multi-mechanism adsorption via electrostatic interaction, π–π stacking, and surface complexation. According to batch experiments, the maximum removal efficiencies of ternary BAC-GO-rGO composite for Ni2+ (5 mg L−1) and MB (100 mg L−1) reached up to 97.35% and 86.41%, respectively. From the kinetic studies, the pseudo-second order kinetic model was found to be the best fit model for all the conditions studied, suggesting that chemisorption is the rate-limiting step. Equilibrium studies agreed with the Langmuir isotherm model and showed monolayer adsorption onto a homogeneous surface, giving values for the maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) of 22.88 mg g−1 for Ni2+ and 357.142 mg g−1 for MB. In general, these results demonstrate the potential of the BAC-GO-rGO composite as an efficient and versatile system for the sustainable treatment of complex industrial wastewaters with both organic and inorganic pollutants. Overall, the BAC-GO-rGO composite demonstrates efficient removal of MB and Ni2+ ions with good regeneration stability over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles, indicating its potential for sustainable wastewater treatment applications.

Introduction

The development of industry and technology at a rapid pace significantly increases the generation of wastewater, with dyes and heavy metals, posing a serious threat to ecosystems and human health.1,2 Harmful substances like heavy metals, pesticides, toxins, and dyes have been directly released into water bodies, via industrial emission into waterways, by both point sources (directly emitted by industries and non-point sources (indirectly emitted by industries), which threaten the existing water resources.3 As the current global economy shows a demand for production output across various industries, this industrial growth also increases the environmental challenges, particularly concerning the heavy metals and organic dyes.2,4

Heavy metals are considered as a major threat to the environment due to their stability and mobility.3,5 Although these are required in trace amounts for normal biological functions, excessive concentrations can be harmful to both animals and plants.6,7 Nickel is used in industries such as silver refineries, electroplating, zinc-based casting industries, storage battery industries for catalysts, alloys, and refineries due to its exemplary electrical, chemical, and thermal properties.4 Exposure to nickel exceeding permissible limits can lead to health issues in humans, such as headaches, diarrhea, and vomiting. Prolonged exposure may cause cancer of the lungs, nose, and bones, dermatitis (nickel itch), rapid respiration, cyanosis, extreme weakness, and DNA damage.4,6,8

In addition to heavy metal contamination, the discharge of organic dyes into aquatic water bodies poses a major threat to the environment as the usage of organic dyes increases across industries, ranging from textiles to solar cell manufacturing.2,7,9 Methylene blue (MB) is a cationic dye, which allows MB to be easily adsorbed onto various materials. Therefore, the dye has been used in industries such as textiles, paper, printing, and leather processing, resulting in the presence of MB in industrial wastewater.9 The release of MB rich wastewater into aquatic water bodies can pose a significant risk to a wide range of aquatic organisms, including fish, algae, and invertebrates, mainly by disrupting respiratory and metabolic functions. Furthermore, in terms of human health, exposure to MB can cause eye irritation, and prolonged or high-dose exposure might lead to methemoglobinemia, a condition where the blood oxygen level is low.9

Wastewater treatment is essential for disposing of public and industrial waste, reducing future risks to public health and the environment.7 Conventional wastewater treatment methods can be categorized into three sections: physical, chemical, and biological. Screening, flow equalization, sedimentation, and filtration are considered as physical treatment methods, while adsorption, disinfection, and dichlorination are considered as chemical treatment methods. Biological treatment methods include trickle filters, activated sludge process, anaerobic digestion, aerated lagoon, rotating biological contactors, and biological nutrient removal. Wastewater treatment is usually a three-step process that includes primary, secondary, and tertiary treatment. Primary treatment removes coarse solids through sedimentation, screening, and other processes. The secondary treatment step removes remaining residual dissolved organic matter by microbial oxygenation.

Despite their widespread use, primary and secondary treatment processes are often insufficient to effectively remove persistent contaminants such as heavy metals and organic dyes. These pollutants are usually present in dissolved complex forms, which allows them to pass through conventional treatment technologies. As a result, advanced technologies such as reverse osmosis, distillation, electrodialysis, foam separation, ultrafiltration, ion exchange, and photocatalytic processes are used to remove heavy metal ions and organic dyes.2 However, these treatment methods are associated with high capital investment, elevated operational and maintenance costs, and increased energy consumption, restricting their widespread implementation throughout industries.2,7

Therefore, using adsorption to remove heavy metals and dyes has emerged as a cost-effective and sustainable approach in wastewater treatment.2,7 It involves the attachment of the heavy metal ions or dye molecules onto the surface of the solid materials, which are known as adsorbents. Numerous adsorbents have been explored for the removal of nickel ions and MB molecules, such as activated carbon, biochar, and metal organic frameworks, etc.5,7,9 Activated carbon (AC) has been widely used as an adsorbent in wastewater treatment due to its high specific surface area, well-developed micro and meso pore structure, and broad adsorption capability towards a wide range of contaminants. The porous carbon framework of activated carbon provides abundant adsorption sites, enabling effective uptake of heavy metal ions and dye molecules through physical adsorption, electrostatic interactions, and surface complexation.10–13

However, the adsorption behavior of AC is non-selective, and its performance is often compromised in complex water systems containing multiple coexisting contaminants. In such multi-component environments, competition for active sites and limited surface functionality restrict the ability of single-component adsorbents to achieve efficient, simultaneous removal of different classes of pollutants. These limitations highlight the necessity of advanced composite materials in which complementary functionalities are integrated within a single adsorbent to enhance performance against diverse contaminants.2,14

Graphene oxide (GO) has been explored as a promising functional material for wastewater treatment due to its high surface area and abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl moieties.3,14,15 These functional groups provide electrostatic interaction sites for nickel ions and MB molecules, thereby increasing adsorption capacity compared to conventional carbon-based adsorbents.14,15 Despite its high affinity for contaminants, the application of GO is often limited by its tendency to restack, which reduces accessible surface area and adsorption. Furthermore, the mechanical stability and recyclability of GO remain limited when it is applied in isolation. GO sheets tend to exhibit structural fragility and are difficult to recover and reuse efficiently after application, which restricts their long-term practical performance. These drawbacks indicate that GO is more effectively utilized when incorporated into a structurally stable porous support matrix. Such integration enhances its mechanical integrity, facilitates easier handling and recovery, and improves its suitability for repeated use in practical applications.16

Moreover, to enhance the composite's effectiveness for organic dye molecule removal, reduced graphene oxide (rGO) obtained by partial reduction of graphene oxide was incorporated. rGO exhibits restored sp2 domains and defect-rich structure, which promotes π–π interactions with organic molecules. In addition, structural defects in rGO can serve as active adsorption sites, further enhancing dye uptake.17,18 However, rGO suffers from aggregation and limited dispersibility in aqueous systems, which can restrict its adsorption efficiency. As with GO, incorporating rGO into a supporting matrix allows to fully exploit its adsorption potential by improving its dispersion stability, preventing sheet restacking, and enhancing the accessibility of active adsorption sites.

Thus, the integration of GO and rGO with AC represents an effective strategy to overcome the inherent limitations of individual adsorbent components while retaining their characteristics. The hierarchical porous structure of activated carbon acts as a robust mechanical scaffold that facilitates the uniform dispersion of GO and rGO. This structural support helps prevent restacking and aggregation of the graphene-based nanosheets, thereby maintaining their accessible surface area and enhancing their performance in adsorption-related applications.12,13 Within this composite framework, GO provides abundant oxygen-containing functional groups that facilitate strong interactions with metal ions, whereas rGO introduces defect-rich sp2 carbon domains14 that enhance π–π interactions with aromatic organic dye molecules.19,20 Concurrently, activated carbon maintains high surface area, structural integrity, and efficient mass-transport pathways, ensuring effective access to active adsorption sites.15 This synergistic integration enables the development of multifunctional composite adsorbents that address the challenges of efficiently removing diverse pollutant classes from complex wastewater systems.14 Therefore, in this study, a highly efficient ternary BAC-GO-rGO composite was developed, characterized, and evaluated for the effective removal of nickel ions and MB dye through kinetic investigations. Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were used as received without further purification. Raw bamboo biomass (Bambusa vulgaris) was locally sourced from naturally grown bamboo plants in Sri Lanka and used as the precursor material for activated carbon synthesis. Deionized water was used throughout all sample preparation procedures and experimental work.

Preparation of bamboo-activated carbon (BAC) from bamboo biomass

Fresh bamboo shoots were cut into pieces of approximately 15 cm × 1 cm, thoroughly washed with water, and were air-dried. Bamboo charcoal (BC) was prepared via low-temperature pyrolysis at 200–300 °C for 1 hour in a sealed container. The obtained BC was ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. For chemical activation, 30.00 g of potassium hydroxide (KOH) was dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water, after which 30.0 g of BC powder was gradually added under continuous stirring at 400 rpm for 1 hour. The resulting viscous slurry was dried at 110 °C for 6 hours. The dried mass was then crushed to a fine powder, transferred to ceramic crucibles, and thermally activated in a muffle furnace at 700 °C for 1 hour. The resulting bamboo-activated carbon (BAC) was sequentially washed with 0.1 M HCl and deionized water until a neutral pH was reached. Finally, the product was dried at 110 °C for 4 hours and stored for further characterization and application.21–24

Adsorption characteristic of bamboo activated carbon (BAC)

Iodine number

The iodine number is defined as the amount of iodine adsorbed (mg) per gram of activated carbon, and is commonly used as an indicator of the micropore volume (0–20 Å) of activated carbon. The iodine number of BAC was determined following the standard method reported in the literature,25 and the value was calculated using eqn (1).1

After adsorption, the suspension was filtered, and the remaining methylene blue concentration was determined by measuring the absorbance at 663 nm using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer. Synthesis of graphene oxide (GO)

GO was synthesized using modified Hummers' method. A 3.00 g portion of natural graphite powder was mixed with 1.50 g of NaNO3 in 70 mL of concentrated H2SO4 under constant stirring for 30 minutes. The reaction mixture was kept below 10 °C, and 9.00 g of KMnO4 was added in portions while stirring slowly for another 30 minutes. The mixture was then warmed to 35 °C and stirred for an additional 2 hours. Subsequently, the resulting slurry was diluted with 140 mL of deionized (DI) water, and the temperature was raised to 95 °C and maintained for 15 minutes. The reaction mixture was then allowed to cool down to room temperature. Thereafter, 420 mL of DI water was added, followed by the dropwise addition of 10 mL of 30% H2O2 under constant stirring, which changed the color from dark brown to yellow, indicating the formation of graphene oxide.

For purification, the obtained GO was thoroughly washed with a 1 : 10 (v/v) HCl : DI water solution, then repeatedly washed with DI water until a neutral pH was reached. The product was separated by centrifugation and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 72 hours, to obtain thin sheets of GO.26,27

Synthesis of reduced graphene oxide (rGO)

rGO was prepared by chemical reduction of graphene oxide using sodium borohydride. A 100.0 mg portion of graphene oxide was dispersed in 200 mL of deionized water by ultrasonication for 30 minutes to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Then, 30 mL of 0.1 M NaOH was added, and the suspension's pH was adjusted to approximately 12. The resulting dispersion was transferred to a conical flask and heated in an oil bath at 80 °C under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 1.00 g of sodium borohydride was gradually added in portions. The mixture was maintained at 85 °C with stirring for 1 hour and 30 minutes. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove residual reagents. Finally, the obtained product was oven-dried at 80 °C for 8 hours.28

Morphological and structural characterization of the activated carbon, graphene oxide, and reduced graphene oxide

The surface morphology of BAC, GO, and rGO was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) of the model Zeiss EVO LS10. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded using a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer equipped with a scintillation counter detector. The diffractometer was operated with Cu Kα radiation in continuous mode at 40 kV and 30 mA, and PXRD patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5° to 60° with a scan speed of 1° per minute. All Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Vertex 80 instrument over the range 400–4000 cm−1, using 100 scans at 4 cm−1 resolution. Raman spectra were recorded using a Renishaw inVia Raman spectrometer.

Preparation of BAC-GO-rGO composite suspension for the adsorption studies

The BAC-GO-rGO composite was prepared using a wet mixing method with a mass ratio of BAC (80 wt%), GO (15 wt%), and rGO (5 wt%). Graphene oxide was initially subjected to wet grinding with the dropwise addition of distilled water (pH = 7) to obtain a homogeneous and stable aqueous dispersion. The dispersion was transferred into a conical flask and magnetically stirred at 600 rpm at room temperature. BAC was separately ground into a fine powder and dispersed in a small volume of distilled water to form a uniform slurry, which was gradually added to the GO dispersion under continuous stirring. Subsequently, rGO was finely ground, dispersed in distilled water, and slowly introduced into the mixture following the same procedure. The resulting suspension was continuously stirred for 2 h to ensure uniform dispersion and interaction among the components. The prepared suspension was directly used for adsorption experiments involving the removal of Ni2+ and MB from aqueous solutions.15 Additionally, a portion of the resulting dispersion was dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C for 24 h, and the dried BAC-GO-rGO composite was subsequently used for point of zero charge determination. The dried BAC-GO-rGO composite was characterized using PXRD, FTIR, and SEM.

Determination of the point zero charge for the composite

The point of zero charge of the composite adsorbent was determined using the solid-addition method. A 50 mL volume of 0.01 N NaCl solution was transferred into a series of containers. The pH of the solutions was adjusted to the range 2–11 using 0.01 M HCl and 0.01 M NaOH. Then, 30.0 mg of BAC-GO-rGO composite was added to each container and placed in a constant-temperature water-bath shaker. After being kept for 24 hours to reach equilibrium, the solution pH values were measured. The final pH against initial pH was plotted, and pHpzc was determined as the pH at which the initial and final pH values were equal.11

Nickel and MB removal using BAC-GO-rGO composite-batch adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the removal performance of the BAC-GO-rGO composite for nickel ions (Ni2+) and MB from aqueous solutions. All experiments were carried out in 250 mL conical flasks, corresponding to an adsorbent (BAC-GO-rGO composite) dosage of 0.376 g L−1. The BAC-GO-rGO composite suspension was prepared as described in Section 2.6. The flasks were placed in a shaking water bath and agitated at 200 rpm to ensure uniform mixing and effective contact between the adsorbent and the solution. All the adsorption testing was carried out at 30 ± 2 °C and at a solution pH of approximately 7.0. Nickel ion solutions with initial concentrations of 5 mg L−1 and 10 mg L−1, and MB solutions with initial concentrations of 100 mg L−1 and 400 mg L−1, were used in the adsorption studies. The adsorption experiments were performed over a contact time range of 10 to 180 minutes. At predetermined time intervals, samples were withdrawn from different sets of sample containers, and the solid–liquid phases were separated by centrifugation and filtration prior to analysis. The residual concentration of Ni2+ in the aqueous solutions after adsorption was determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) (Atomic absorption spectrometer-Thermo Scientific iCE 3500), and the concentration of methylene blue in the aqueous solution was determined using UV-visible spectroscopy (PerkinElmer LAMBDA 365), at 663 nm wavelength.11,29

Effect of pH and temperature on the adsorption performance

Batch adsorption experiments for the investigation of pH and temperature effects were conducted as described previously. For the pH effect study, the solution pH was adjusted to the target value of pH 9 prior to the experiment using 0.01 M HCl and 0.01 M NaOH as appropriate, with all other experimental parameters maintained constant. For the temperature effect study, the water bath temperature was set to 60 °C, while the remaining experimental conditions were kept identical to those of the standard procedure. In both cases, a contact time of 90 minutes was used, as the percentage removal became nearly constant beyond this duration and the residual MB and Ni2+ concentrations in the supernatant were determined spectrophotometrically.

Reusability study

The reusability of the BAC-GO-rGO composite for methylene blue and nickel adsorption was evaluated through three consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles. Adsorption experiments were conducted under the optimized conditions (MB concentration = 100 mg L−1, Ni2+ = 5 mg L−1, adsorbent dosage = 0.376 g L−1, pH 7.0, (30 ± 2 °C, stirring speed = 200 rpm, and contact time = 90 min). After each adsorption cycle, the adsorbent was separated by centrifugation (or filtration) and the residual MB concentration and Ni2+ was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 663 nm.

For regeneration, the spent adsorbent was rinsed with distilled water and then stirred in 50 mL of ethanol for 1 hour at room temperature to remove the adsorbed dye. The regenerated adsorbent was washed multiple times with distilled water until the filtrate was colorless, then dried at 60 °C for 12 hours before being reused in the next adsorption cycle. This adsorption–desorption process was repeated three times, and regeneration efficiency was determined by the amount of adsorption capacity retained after each cycle. Ethanol has been recognized as an effective desorption medium for restoring methylene blue from graphene-based adsorbents.

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to investigate possible changes in surface functional groups during repeated adsorption–desorption cycles. FTIR spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 for regenerated composites. The surface morphology of the composite after the regeneration cycle was examined using SEM.

Results and discussion

This study focuses on the sustainable synthesis of BAC from bamboo biomass and the fabrication of a multifunctional composite by incorporating GO and rGO. The integration of these carbonaceous components was intended to combine the porous structure and adsorption performance of activated carbon, as reflected by its iodine value, methylene blue adsorption capacity, and set adsorption values, with the abundant oxygen-containing functional groups of GO and the structural contribution of rGO within the composite system. The fabricated BAC-GO-rGO composite was evaluated for its suitability in industrial wastewater treatment, with particular emphasis on the removal of the cationic pollutants.

The integration of GO and rGO into the BAC matrix was done to fabricate a composite with improved adsorption performance resulting from interaction mechanisms such as electrostatic attraction, π–π interactions, surface complexation, and pore filling effects. The following sections discuss the morphological, structural, and surface characteristics of the synthesized materials and correlate these properties with their adsorption behavior, kinetics, and removal efficiency toward MB and nickel ions.

Characterization of bamboo-activated carbon (BAC)

The adsorption properties of AC depend greatly on its surface chemistry and pore structure. The presence of surface functional groups governs the nature of interactions between the adsorbent and the adsorbate, while the porosity determines the accessibility of adsorption sites. Therefore, the adsorption capability of AC is largely dependent on the type of precursor material and the activation process employed during synthesis. In particular, the development of porosity is significantly affected by the choice of chemical activating agent, the impregnation ratio, and the activation temperature, which control pore formation.32

Potassium hydroxide (KOH) is used as a chemical activating agent to produce porous AC from biomass precursors, usually in a two-step activation process that involves the initial carbonization (pyrolysis) step followed by the chemical activation step. This two-step approach has been widely reported to be much more effective than the single-step activation process, owing to higher AC yield, enhanced pore development, and improved structural stability.32

During the initial pyrolysis step, the biomass precursor undergoes thermal decomposition, during which volatile gaseous compounds are removed, resulting in the formation of a rigid, aromatic-rich carbon framework. This pre-carbonized structure is crucial, as it provides mechanical stability and resistance to excessive burn-off during the chemical activation step. Marsh and Reinoso demonstrated that pre-carbonization prevents structural collapse during aggressive chemical activation treatments and improves pore controllability.33

During the chemical activation process with KOH, a series of redox and gasification reactions between KOH and the carbon matrix occur, which leads to extensive pore formation. These reactions generate metallic potassium, K2CO3, CO, and CO2. These reactions induce chemical etching and pore widening, resulting in a hierarchical pore structure. Otowa et al. (1993) reported that KOH activation is effective in the formation of micropores (<2 nm) in abundance. Higher activation temperature and higher KOH-to-precursor ratios contribute to mesopore formation, which is essential for adsorbing larger molecules such as organic dyes.34

Several studies have demonstrated that the two-step activation approach with KOH tends to produce activated carbons with higher surface areas and more developed pore structures than single-step activation. For example, Karakehya et al. (2023) demonstrated that activated carbons prepared from Lycopodium clavatum spore by a two-step process (pre-carbonization followed by KOH activation) showed significantly higher surface area and greater total pore volume than those prepared by direct one-step KOH activation, indicating that pre-carbonization facilitates more effective pore development during activation.35 In a similar comparison using orange peel as a precursor, two-stage KOH activation yielded an activated char (KAC-2) with a higher surface area compared to the one-step (KAC-1) and this translated into superior adsorption performance for phenol from aqueous solutions.36

The availability of both micropores and mesopores is critical for adsorption applications. Mesopores act as transport channels that facilitate rapid diffusion of adsorbates into interior adsorption sites, while micropores contribute predominantly to high adsorption capacity by providing a large surface area. This synergistic pore architecture has been shown to significantly enhance adsorption kinetics and efficiency for both cationic dyes and metal ions, as reported in studies involving MB and Ni2+ adsorption onto KOH-activated carbons.4,37

Therefore, the two-step KOH activation strategy adopted in this study is well supported by existing literature and provides a rational approach for producing AC with a tailored pore structure and enhanced adsorption performance, making it particularly suitable for wastewater treatment applications.

The development of pores in AC increases surface area and the number of active adsorption sites, which can be evaluated using the iodine number and the MB number. The iodine number is commonly used to assess the microporosity of AC, as it reflects the adsorption of iodine molecules within pores typically smaller than 20 Å. Iodine number generally ranges from 500 to 1200 mg g−1. Furthermore, the MB number provides insight into the pore-size distribution predominantly associated with mesopores.11

The BAC exhibited an iodine number of 761.78 mg g−1, which indicates a high surface area and a well-developed micropore network. The iodine number is widely used to characterize AC as an indicator of microporosity and adsorption capacity, with typical values for high-grade AC used in water treatment reported in the range of approximately 600–1100 mg g−1.38 The relatively high iodine number observed in this study can be attributed to the precursor's base activation. Bamboo's fibrous lignocellulosic anatomy promotes uniform carbonization and facilitates the development of micropores during chemical activation. In addition, the two-step activation process (pyrolysis followed by KOH activation) enhances pore development by allowing initial carbon framework formation prior to aggressive chemical etching, thereby increasing accessible internal surface area and microporosity.

The MB number is an important indicator of mesopores volumes of an adsorbent and its ability to trap medium and large molecules of pollutants.25,38–40 The measured MB number of the synthesized BAC is 233.96 mg g−1 which confirms the presence of a well-developed mesoporous network. This value is significantly higher than the minimum industrial standard of 120 mg g−139 and falls within the upper tier of the Japanese industrial standard (JIS K 1474) range of 180–230 mg g−1.38

In a comparative analysis with other adsorbents reported in the literature, the performance of milled activated carbon modified with 2.5% KMnO4 (206 mg g−1)38 and is almost identical to phosphate-vapor-functionalized coir-activated carbon (230 mg g−1).11 Although this value is lower than some commercial benchmarks, such as Filtrasorb 400 (319 mg g−1) or specialized KOH/CO2-activated bamboo-based carbons (454 mg g−1), it still remains within the effective range for practical wastewater treatment.38,40 A high MB number indicates a well-developed porous structure, particularly with sufficient mesoporosity, which can accommodate the relatively large molecular dimensions of MB (∼1.43 × 0.61 × 0.4 nm). This enhances diffusion into the pore network and promotes efficient adsorption and removal of the pollutant.37,40,41

Porosity development in BAC with chemical activation occurs through a series of reactions between KOH and the carbon matrix at high temperatures. In particular, the reactions involve temperatures in the range of 700–900 °C. First, solid KOH undergoes dehydration to produce potassium oxide (K2O) and water vapor (Reaction 1). Then water vapor further reacts with the carbon matrix via water–gas reactions to form CO and H2 (Reaction 2), and via the water–gas shift reaction to produce CO2 and more H2 (Reaction 3). This results in carbon loss and the nucleation of pores, forming the initial micropores.50

The produced CO2 combines with K2O to give potassium carbonate, K2CO3 (Reaction 4). At high temperatures, K2CO3 is thermodynamically unstable and starts decomposing into K2O and CO2 (Reaction 5). The carbonate formation/decomposition cycle leads to continuous production of CO2 inside the carbon matrix. Thereby leading to increased pressure within the matrix and pore enlargement. Moreover, CO2 continues to react with carbon, yielding additional gases (Reaction 6).24

With increasing activation temperature, K2O and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) get reduced by carbon to form potassium vapor (Reaction 7 and Reaction 8). This potassium is responsible for creating pores by inserting into the carbon layers, expanding them, and weakening carbon structures. Cooling followed by washing makes the potassium elements disappear, thereby leaving a micro- and mesopore network.51

In general, the synergistic effects between carbon gasification, carbonate decomposition, and potassium intercalation account for the extensive porosity formation in KOH-activated carbons. In addition, this process is known to be one of the most effective methods for producing ACs, which confirms the porous structure observed in the SEM analysis of the prepared BAC.52,53

In addition to improved pore formation, the gasification reactions that occur during the activation of KOH also cause considerable structural disorder in the carbon lattice, which can be investigated by PXRD. The PXRD pattern of the BAC (Fig. 3A) shows a strong and broad diffraction band located at 2θ ≈ 23°, related to the diffraction of the (002) plane of the graphitic carbon. It indicates that this consists of small and disordered graphitic regions instead of ordered graphite, as observed in previous research on AC chemically activated biomass.33

Weak and broad signals located at 2θ ≈ 43° refer to the (100) plane of the carbon layer, confirming the existence of turbostratic disorder of the graphitic regions.19 The presence of broad diffraction bands and the lack of well-defined peaks confirm the amorphous nature of the activated carbon produced due to carbon lattice disorder caused during both carbonization and chemical activation processes.33

Moreover, peak broadening implies the low degree of ordering of the graphene sheets and the formation of defects, resulting from gasification and intercalation reactions during the KOH activation process.50

Characterization of graphene oxide

Even though BAC exhibited a highly porous structure and an active surface, the adsorption performance can be enhanced by introducing additional oxygen-containing functional groups and two-dimensional carbon domains. Therefore, GO was incorporated with BAC as a component owing to its layered structure, high surface area, and abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups. This introduction enables the composite to engage in electrostatic interactions, surface complexation, and π–π interactions with heavy metal ions and organic dye molecules, thereby improving adsorption efficiency.3 Furthermore, GO addition improves the dispersion of the activated carbon and prevents the pore blocking, and maintains an open structure within the composite material.

To determine the structural and morphological features of graphene oxide and to understand its role in enhancing the adsorption behavior of the BAC-GO system, a detailed characterization of GO was carried out using analytical techniques.

SEM was used at different magnifications to characterize the surface morphology of GO. The SEM micrograph (Fig. 4A and B) of graphene oxide exhibits a characteristic two-dimensional sheet-like morphology with thin and wrinkled layers, which is a typical morphological feature of exfoliated graphene oxide. The sheets exhibit extensive folding and overlap, indicating successful exfoliation of GO into a few layers, as is commonly reported for chemically synthesized graphene oxide.57

SEM images of synthesized GO (A) 5000× magnification, (B) 10 000× magnification.

A large surface area is provided by the loosely stacked layered morphology of the GO, this structure also exposes the chemically active functional sites, which are favored in adsorption applications. This abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups enables the electrostatic interactions and surface complexation with heavy metal cations, along with π–π interactions with cationic dye molecules, which leads to enhanced performance in the adsorption.58

While the SEM analysis provides insight into the surface morphology and layered structure of GO, PXRD pattern was used to examine the crystalline structure and interlayer organization. The PXRD pattern of graphene oxide (Fig. 5A) exhibits a strong, sharp peak at approximately 2θ ≈ 11°, which corresponds to the (001) crystal plane. This peak characteristic of GO is attributed to increased interlayer spacing due to the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups. In addition to the main peak, a broad and less intense peak was observed around 2θ ≈ 25°, which may be associated with residual graphitic domains.59 Overall, the PXRD results confirm the successful formation of GO with interlayer spacing and disorder, consistent with the SEM observations and favorable for subsequent composite formation and adsorption applications.

The broad absorption band observed at 3200–3300 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups. The presence of sp2 hybridized CC stretching vibrations at 1500–1600 cm−1. A distinct peak at 1230–1320 cm−1 was attributed to C–O–C stretching vibrations of epoxide groups. The presence of these oxygen-containing functional groups confirms the successful formation of GO with a highly functionalized surface.60 Compared to activated carbon, graphene oxide exhibits significantly intense oxygen-related absorption bands, confirming extensive surface oxidation.61

Characterization of reduced graphene oxide (rGO)

While GO provides a defect-rich graphene framework, its electrical conductivity and graphitic order remain limited due to an excess of oxygen-containing functional groups. Therefore, in order to partially restore the conjugated sp2 carbon network, graphene oxide was chemically reduced to obtain reduced graphene oxide. The structural, morphological, and chemical characteristics were analyzed to understand the changes introduced by the reduction process.

The morphological changes induced by the reduction of GO were examined using SEM, as shown in Fig. 6, which reveals a crumpled and flake-like morphology composed of stacked and agglomerated graphene sheets. In contrast to thin GO sheets, the rGO sheets appear thicker and more compact due to the restoration of π–π interactions upon reduction. Despite increased stacking, the rGO sheets retain a wrinkled, folded surface, characteristic of reduced graphene oxide and resulting from lattice strain and residual defects left after reduction. Such structural features indicated the conversion of GO to rGO, while preserving a defect-rich architecture advantageous for surface interactions.62 The presence of wrinkles also prevents complete graphitic restacking, having accessible surface area and providing active sites beneficial for composite formation and adsorption applications.57

The PXRD pattern of the synthesized reduced graphene oxide (Fig. 7A) shows a broad peak around 2θ ≈ 25–26°, which corresponds to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon. This broad peak indicates that the π-conjugated structure of graphene has been restored considerably in the produced rGO. Determination of the point of zero charge of the BAC-GO-rGO composite

Point of zero charge (pHpzc) can be described as a pH value when the net surface charge of the adsorbent reaches to zero and is important in terms of controlling the adsorption characteristics, the surface chemistry of the adsorbent, and electrostatic interactions between the adsorbent surface and ionic species present in the solution. It is also necessary to know the pHpzc in order to conduct adsorption experiments on a specific adsorbent and understand the underlying mechanisms and the adsorbent's affinity to charged pollutants. In solutions with pH below the pHpzc, protonation of hydroxyl, and phenolic functional groups occurs, leading to a positive adsorbent surface charge. Conversely, in case of the latter situation, when the pH is higher than pHpzc, these functional groups undergo deprotonation, forming a negative adsorbent surface. This process plays an important role in adsorption efficiency, especially in the adsorption of ionic substances. Higher pH values than the pHpzc lead to negative charges on the BAC-GO-rGO composite, enhancing its ability to absorb cations via electrostatic attraction. On the other hand, in acidic environments (pH < pHpzc), the electrostatic repulsion of positively charged surface and adsorbate hinders the adsorption of cations. Such pH-dependent behavior is typical for carbon-containing and graphene-derived adsorbents.72

As shown in Fig. 10 the pHpzc value obtained for the BAC-GO-rGO composite is 5.63 ± 0.1. This specific value represents the pH at which the surface exhibits equal positive and negative charges, indicating the equilibrium between protonation and deprotonation of surface functional groups.

Determination of the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of the BAC-GO-rGO composite adsorbent.

At pH values below the pHpzc (pH < pHpzc), the final pH shifted upward relative to the initial values, which is driven by the protonation of the surface sites. For this reason, the adsorbent surface remains positively charged under acidic conditions. In contrast, at pH levels exceeding 5.6 (pH > 5.6), a downward shift in pH was observed, due to the deprotonation of the surface hydroxyl groups and the transfer of protons to the aqueous phase. This suggests that the composite will exhibit a higher affinity for cationic pollutants in alkaline conditions, where electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged adsorbent surface and cationic heavy metal ions and dye molecules.11

For the pH study, adsorption experiments were conducted at pH 7 and pH 9. This pH range was selected based on the adsorption behavior of the target pollutants and the chemical stability of the system. Extension to pH values significantly above 9 was not considered suitable for Ni2+ adsorption, as nickel ions are prone to form Ni(OH)2 precipitates under alkaline conditions (typically above pH ∼8–9). Under such conditions, the observed decrease in dissolved Ni2+ concentration would be dominated by precipitation rather than adsorption, thereby preventing an accurate assessment of the adsorbent performance. Similarly, strongly acidic conditions were not investigated in detail, as the measured pHpzc of the BAC-GO-rGO composite is approximately 5.6. Below this value, the adsorbent surface becomes positively charged, leading to electrostatic repulsion with both cationic methylene blue (MB+) and Ni2+ ions, which would significantly hinder adsorption efficiency.

Adsorption performance – removal efficiency

Following the physicochemical characterization study of the BAC-GO-rGO composite material, the adsorption potential of the composite material was assessed in removing Ni2+ (cationic heavy metal ions) and MB (organic dye). A comparison of the performance of the composite with that of BAC only with GO and rGO. Adsorption efficiencies, kinetics, and equilibrium studies were conducted using various concentrations and contact times to investigate the adsorption characteristics.

Nickel ion removal efficiency

Fig. 11 presents the removal efficiency of Ni2+ ions at an initial concentration of 5 mg L−1 for both BAC-GO-rGO and the BAC-only samples, which showed consistently lower removal efficiencies throughout the contact times, fluctuating between 20.20 ± 3.66% at 20 minutes and reaching the highest removal efficiency at 30 minutes, which is 47.64 ± 13.59%, eventually settling at 38.00 ± 0.69% at 120 minutes. The lack of a progressive trend in removal efficiency and the high standard deviation values indicate inconsistent adsorption behavior of the bare BAC. In contrast, the BAC-GO-rGO composite showed considerably high removal efficiencies starting at 72.87 ± 15.17% at 10 minutes, and followed by a steady increase up to a peak value of 97.35 ± 2.28% at 90 minutes, after which there is a slight decrease in removal efficiency to 91.53 ± 5.15% at 120 minutes, indicating attainment of equilibrium at 90 minutes.

Removal efficiency of Ni2+ at (A) 5 mg L−1 (B) 10 mg L−1 at 30 °C per 0.376 g L−1 of BAC-GO-rGO and BAC only at pH = 7.

The consistently low and irregular removal efficiency of the BAC only at Ni2+ ion removal can be explained based on the characteristics of the KOH activation mechanism, which is known to produce a microporous structure through chemical etching due to the activation mechanism, rather than introducing surface functional groups to the surface.50 Although the microporous structure offers a large surface area for adsorption, heavy metal ion adsorption occurs mainly via physical adsorption through pore filling and van der Waals forces.10 In comparison, higher removal efficiencies were shown by the BAC-GO-rGO composite, which is mainly due to the oxygen-functional groups present in the GO, such as hydroxyl, and epoxy groups that enable electrostatic interactions and complexation with the cationic Ni2+ ions.73,74 The increasing effectiveness of the composite for removal as contact time increases from 10 to 90 minutes is attributed to the gradual binding of Ni2+ to specific binding sites, while the relatively lower standard deviations compared to BAC only further emphasize the reliability and reproducibility of the composite in the adsorption process.

Fig. 11 further presents the removal efficiency of Ni2+ ions at an increased concentration of 10 mg L−1. The BAC-only sample demonstrated relatively higher removal efficiencies than those achieved at the 5 mg L−1 Ni2+ concentration, which ranged from 68.88 ± 0.32% to 78.70% over the contact times. However, the removal efficiency did not show a clear trend of increasing with increasing contact time, reaching 78.21 ± 2.75% at 120 minutes. Conversely, the BAC-GO-rGO composite showed removal efficiencies started from 23.74 ± 4.59% at 10 minutes, then increased sharply to 84.07 ± 4.15% after 20 minutes, and even higher to 92.56 ± 1.05% at 50 minutes. After that, the removal efficiency remained steady, then decreased slightly to 79.38 ± 7.75% at 120 minutes.

The rapid removal efficiency observed for the BAC-only samples within 10 minutes, reaching up to 78.70%, is a common feature of adsorption with BAC.10 This initial rapid step occurs due to the high availability of active sites readily accessible to Ni2+ ions.10,75 As these active sites become gradually occupied with physical adsorption.76 The process proceeds to a slow step of chemical binding to the carbon matrix.,10 the concentration gradient that arises due to the high initial nickel ion concentration acts as a driving force for the Ni2+ ions towards the surface of the activated carbon.74 This results in faster adsorption, leading to the occupation of all available surface sites.74,77 After this rapid uptake phase, the removal efficiency of the BAC-only samples reaches a plateau, suggesting that adsorption is limited by the lack of surface functional groups in the carbon matrix. Even though some chemisorption occurred due to the available minimal surface functional groups.49 At high temperatures, the KOH activation mechanism reduces functional groups via the pyrolysis of radical groups.22 As these functional groups are needed for the further removal of metal ions through complexation or ion exchange,10 The absence of functional groups depletes the available active sites, causing the system to plateau early and limiting removal efficiency.

In contrast, the rapid removal of the a BAC-only samples, a steady increase in the removal efficiency of the BAC-GO-rGO composite (from 23.74 ± 4.59% at 10 minutes to 92.56 ± 1.05% at 50 minutes) indicates that the adsorption of Ni2+ by the composite involves a more complex adsorption process.10 The higher Ni2+ removal observed at 10 mg L−1 compared to 5 mg L−1 can be explained by the increased concentration gradient, which enhances mass transfer and promotes more frequent collisions between Ni2+ ions and the adsorbent surface. At higher initial concentration, a greater fraction of the available adsorption sites on the BAC-GO-rGO composite becomes effectively utilized, leading to improved overall uptake.

This enhancement is further reinforced by the synergistic interaction between GO and reduced rGO within the composite. The oxygen-containing functional groups (such as –OH, –COOH, and –CO) on GO facilitate Ni2+ adsorption through electrostatic attraction and surface complexation, while the conjugated π-electron network of rGO contributes to structural stability and improved dispersion of the composite, preventing aggregation and increasing the accessibility of active sites. Together, these complementary features increase the availability of effective binding sites, which, under higher concentration conditions, results in more efficient Ni2+ uptake.73

Since materials with high adsorption capacity require a longer time to achieve equilibrium adsorption,74 it can be said that the higher number of active adsorption sites in composite is able to maintain its removal rate throughout the contact times, rather than BAC-only samples, which reached its maximum removal earlier. Since the concentration of Ni2+ ion concentration in 10 mg L−1 solution creates a relatively steeper concentration gradient, Ni2+ ions were able to overcome the mass transfer resistance and adsorb to inner binding sites.40

This behavior can be attributed to the rapid adsorption of Ni2+ onto BAC during the early contact period (10 min), where readily available surface sites are quickly occupied, leading to an initial approach toward site saturation. Under such conditions, the contribution of additional functional groups and structural features provided by the BAC-GO-rGO composite becomes less distinguishable within the short contact time. As a result, both materials show similar apparent removal efficiencies despite their structural differences.

The BAC-GO-rGO composite shows particularly strong performance at low initial Ni2+ concentrations (5 mg L−1), which is highly relevant to environmentally realistic dilute contamination conditions.78 In many natural and post-treatment water systems, heavy metal concentrations are typically present in the low ppm to sub-ppm range due to dilution, partial remediation, and environmental dispersion, where efficient removal becomes more challenging because of limited concentration driving force.7,74,79

Under these conditions, adsorbents with high surface affinity and abundant accessible functional groups are required for effective trace metal capture.3,74,80 The enhanced performance of the BAC-GO-rGO composite can be attributed to the synergistic contribution of GO and reduced rGO, where oxygen-containing functional groups promote strong Ni2+ binding through electrostatic attraction and surface complexation, while rGO improves dispersion and prevents aggregation, thereby increasing the accessibility of active adsorption sites.3,8,15,58,81

The significant improvement observed at 5 mg L−1 demonstrates that the composite is particularly effective in low-concentration regimes, where adsorption is governed by high-affinity surface interactions rather than bulk saturation effects.38,81 This makes the BAC-GO-rGO system especially suitable for polishing applications aimed at removing residual Ni2+ from already treated or diluted wastewater streams to meet stringent environmental quality requirements.3,39,58

Methylene blue removal efficiency

The results shown in Fig. 12 indicate that MB exhibits a gradual increase in adsorption compared to Ni2+, which shows rapid initial removal.37,73 This difference arises from the distinct adsorption mechanisms governing the two adsorbates.37,40,74 Ni2+, as a small, hydrated ion, rapidly interacts with readily available surface functional groups, leading to fast surface-controlled adsorption and early attainment of equilibrium.73,74

Removal efficiency of MB at (A) 100 mg L−1 (B) 400 mg L−1 at 30 °C per 0.376 g L−1 of BAC-GO-rGO at pH = 7.

In contrast, MB adsorption proceeds more gradually over time for both BAC and BAC-GO-rGO systems.37,82 As shown, BAC alone increases from 32.89 ± 1.61% at 10 min to 60.46 ± 6.06% at 120 min, reaching equilibrium thereafter, while the BAC-GO-rGO composite continues to increase from 48.48 ± 1.71% to 86.41 ± 5.44% at 180 min. This behavior is attributed to the larger molecular size of MB (1.43 × 0.61 × 0.40 nm),38,40 which requires diffusion into mesopores and internal adsorption sites, resulting in a more diffusion-controlled process.38,41,76

Additionally, MB adsorption is enhanced by π–π interactions between its aromatic structure and the graphitic domains of BAC-GO-rGO,3,19,83 as well as electrostatic interactions with oxygen-containing functional groups.37,84 These combined mechanisms contribute to the higher equilibrium adsorption capacity15,83 and the progressive uptake observed over time.37,38

The high iodine number (761.78 mg g−1) confirms a well-developed microporous structure of the adsorbent, which contributes significantly to its enhanced adsorption capacity and sustained uptake of MB through diffusion-controlled pore filling.21,34,38,85 The adsorbent is therefore predominantly microporous (<2 nm).11,38,85 However, the molecular size of MB (∼1.4 nm) lies at the upper limit of micropore accessibility, resulting in a mixed adsorption behavior involving surface adsorption, partial micropore filling, and diffusion into mesoporous regions.40,41 The MB number of the BAC (233.96 mg g−1) further confirms the existence of mesoporosity within the adsorbent structure. The MB number is widely used as an indicator of mesopore volume surface area because the molecular dimensions of MB are relatively larger than those of iodine.39 The hierarchical pore structure of the BAC-GO-rGO composite, including both micropores (typically < 2 nm) and mesopores (2–50 nm), has been confirmed by the coexistence of high iodine number (761.78 mg g−1) and large MB number.11,25

This dual porosity is particularly advantageous for the adsorption of distinct pollutants. Small ionic species, such as Ni2+, can easily access the high-energy binding sites of the micropore network, while larger organic molecules, such as MB, are hosted in the mesopore channels.10,11 In addition, the mesopores act as transport pathways, reducing the diffusion resistance and enabling the progressive uptake during the contact time.32,41 This is in accordance with a diffusion-controlled adsorption behavior, for which the developed mesoporous network ensures that the internal surface area remains accessible to bulky aromatic molecules.37,41

The BAC-only reaches an equilibrium plateau (∼60%) by 120 minutes, because the adsorption capability depends on the porous structure and high surface area for physical adsorption.52 In non-functionalized BAC, the removal efficiency is controlled and limited by the available active adsorption sites, which implies that once all these active sites are saturated, the driving force arising from the concentration gradient is not sufficient to achieve further adsorption resulting in stagnant removal efficiency as observed in 120 minutes and 180 minutes.37,75,82

The superior removal efficiency of the BAC-GO-rGO composite (reaching a maximum of 86.41 ± 5.44% at 180 minutes) could be explained by an increased density of active binding sites and a multi-step adsorption mechanism. The GO in the composite possesses numerous oxygenated functional groups, such as hydroxyl, and epoxy, which serve as specific binding sites for MB molecules via electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding.3,15,61,76 The conjugation in rGO promotes efficient π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of the MB and the graphene sheets.17,76,86 Functionalization enhances mesoporosity (2–50 nm pores), which act as “transport channels,” decreasing mass-transport resistance for larger molecules such as MB. Therefore, MB molecules reach the inner surface more effectively compared to bare BAC.32,37

The absence of a clear plateau for the BAC-GO-rGO composite at 180 min suggests that materials with higher adsorption capacity require a longer contact time to reach equilibrium. In contrast, the BAC-only samples exhibit rapid saturation, indicating a limited number of accessible adsorption sites. The composite, with a greater number of available and accessible active sites, continues to adsorb MB over the studied period, suggesting a higher theoretical adsorption capacity than BAC alone.74

Fig. 12 also shows the removal efficiency of MB at an increased initial concentration of 400 mg L−1 for both BAC-only and the BAC-GO-rGO composite over the 10 to 180 minutes time interval. It is apparent from the results that both BAC-only and the BAC-GO-rGO composite showed significantly lower removal efficiencies than their respective removal percentages at an initial concentration of 100 mg L−1. For BAC only, there was no progressive trend observed, and it showed an essentially stagnant profile, removal percentages varying between 16.61 ± 1.55% at 10 minutes to 21.57 ± 1.93% at 120 minutes, showing 20.81 ± 3.42% at 180 minutes, without any progressive increase in its value. The BAC-GO-rGO composite also showed a limited removal percentage, which was 29.25 ± 2.975% at 10 minutes, while reaching 33.15 ± 5.63% at 120 minutes, after which no significant increase in removal percentages was observed at 180 minutes, which indicated the attainment of equilibrium.

The considerably lower removal efficiency recorded for both materials at 400 mg L−1 compared to 100 mg L−1 can be directly linked to the increased number of MB molecules competing for the limited number of active adsorption sites.77,81,84 In the system, where the adsorbent dosage is maintained at a constant level, the increasing competition among the molecules results in saturation of the available sites20,72,84 At a concentration of 400 mg L−1, the abundance of MB molecules in the solution far exceeds the adsorption capacity of the two materials. Although the increasing concentration gradient generates a stronger driving force to overcome mass-transfer resistance, there are no vacant sites available.40,72,74,81

Stagnant and low removal efficiency of BAC only can when the concentration at 400 mg L−1 can be attributed to the limitations on surface chemistry of the BAC itself. Due to high-temperature pyrolysis during the activation process, the surface of the BAC exhibits a much lower density and a variety of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as hydroxyl group, which are necessary for efficient adsorption through chemical interactions.22,51,87 At increased dye concentrations, the limited available binding sites would be occupied within the first 10 minutes, whereas, due to the lack of functional groups required to facilitate ion exchange and complexation, BAC would reach capacity within a short contact time.19,22

Even though the BAC-GO-rGO composite was superior to BAC only during the entire contact time interval, its slight increase in removal efficiency from 10 to 180 minutes at 400 mg L−1 indicates its limitation at high initial concentrations. In particular, the π–π stacking effect of the rGO component and the electrostatic attraction of the GO component are very efficient at low concentrations but limited in handling the relatively higher concentrations of contaminants.15,20,37,76 This indicates that despite the improved surface functionalities due to the addition of graphene derivatives, the number of available active sites for the removal process eventually become saturated, indicating that even the high-performance multi-mechanism adsorbents have a functional ceiling when the adsorbate load dramatically exceeds the available adsorption capacity.20,81

Kinetic model selection and fitting for Ni2+ adsorption

As evident from the Table 2 it is clear that zeroth order, first-order, and second order reaction kinetic models all had poor fits in the Ni2+ 5 mg L−1 and 10 mg L−1 concentrations for both AC only and BAC-GO-rGO composite materials, with respective values ranging from low values of 0.0001 to 0.3259. This indicates that conventional reaction kinetic models cannot effectively predict adsorption on solid surfaces, as they are heterogeneous systems involving multiple processes such as diffusion and reactions.88,89 The pseudo-first-order (PFO) model also displayed low linearities with the corresponding R2 values being 0.3540 and 0.3847 for BAC and the BAC-GO-rGO at 5 mg L−1, and 0.1932 and 0.0947 at 10 mg L−1, respectively. Poor correlation further signifies that the PFO is not suitable to describe the adsorption process, as PFO often only fits experimental data during the initial stage of contact.74,88

In comparison, the pseudo-second-order (PSO) provided a better fit under both concentrations, with R2 values of 0.9052 and 0.9857 at 5 mg L−1 and 0.9939 and 0.917 at 10 mg L−1 for BAC only and BAC-GO-rGO composite (Fig. S1), respectively, indicating a high level of consistency between experimental results.10,88 The superior performance of the PSO model, which assumes that the reaction rate is directly proportional to the square of the number of available adsorption sites, indicates that the rate-limiting step is chemisorption.10,88 This process is based on valence forces in which electrons are shared between the Ni2+ ions and the active surface sites of the adsorbents.11,72,74

This result is highly consistent with the existence of surface functional groups in the activated carbon and the oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl, and epoxy) that were introduced by the GO.58,62 In addition, the π–electron system of rGO acts a Lewis base, forming electron donor–acceptor complexes with the Ni2+ ions.58 Together, these features enable the progressive uptake of the Ni2+, validating PSO fit for both BAC only and BAC-GO-rGO composite.8,10,50

Kinetic model selection and fitting methylene blue adsorption

The kinetic investigation of MB adsorption revealed a significantly different competitive environment compared with that for Ni2+, due to changes in the dye's molecular properties. At 100 mg L−1, the zeroth-order, first-order, and second-order models showed much higher R2 values in the BAC-GO-rGO composite (0.8431, 0.9223, 0.9303 respectively). This linear correlation is associated with the stepwise increase in MB adsorption over the contact time. Smaller metal ions, relatively larger molecules of MB, suffer from mass-transfer resistance, which slows diffusion into the pores of the adsorbent.37,38,40,77,90,91 The above characteristic is further reflected in the pseudo-first-order fit of both BAC and BAC-GO-rGO composite, which shows an R2 of 0.8732 and 0.9390, respectively, at 100 mg L−1. The above correlation is consistent with the PFO model assumption that the adsorption rate is proportional to the number of vacant sites during the initial diffusion-adsorption stages.7,81,84,92

Regardless of these higher R2 values, the PSO model consistently exhibits the highest R2 values for BAC only and the BAC-GO-rGO composite at 100 mg L−1 (0.9918 and 0.9962, respectively) and at 400 mg L−1 (0.9949 and 0.9638, respectively) (Fig. S2). These superior fits for the PSO model confirm that the rate-limiting step is controlled by a chemisorption process characterized by valence bonds resulting from electron exchange.7,19,72,76,77,81 According to the PSO kinetic model, the adsorption rate is primarily governed by the availability of adsorption sites and is proportional to the square of the difference between equilibrium and instantaneous adsorption capacities (qe − qt), rather than directly depending on the adsorbate concentration in solution.7,11,72,77

For the BAC-GO-rGO composite, continued uptake of the MB is enabled by electrostatic interactions between the cationic MB molecules and the negatively charged oxygenated functional groups (such as hydroxyl) introduced by GO,20,74,86,87 while the restored π-conjugated network of rGO provides strong π–π stacking interactions with the aromatic rings of the MB dye.3,36,37,58,86

For bare BAC despite its limited availability of functional groups, the rate-limiting step is chemisorption.7,11,72 The BAC surface is not chemically inert. The chemisorption process is carried out by residual heteroatoms (oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur) that are bonded to the edges of the graphene layers, thereby generating localized regions of surface basicity.32,49,91 Moreover, the π-electron system of the graphitic planes provides Lewis basic sites that facilitate electron donor–acceptor interactions with adsorbates. Allowing for specific chemical binding even in the lack of traditional functional groups. Additionally, the microporous network generated by KOH activation produces slit-shaped pores in which adsorption forces from opposite walls overlap, thereby enhancing the strength of interactions that can be characterized by chemical kinetics.79,90 According to the PSO model, the adsorption rate is proportional to the square of the available vacant sites, indicating that the bare AC aggressively fills its available capacity during the initial contact phase.10,31 However, because the total density of these specific binding sites is low compared to functionalized materials, the system reaches an equilibrium plateau much faster, as no further chemical mechanism exists to capture additional ions once these residual sites and micropores are saturated.10,75

Adsorption isotherm selection and fitting

To further analyze the adsorption behavior of the BAC-GO-rGO composite and calculate the maximum adsorption capacity, isotherm studies were conducted for both Ni2+ and MB. The experimental equilibrium data were fitted for both the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models, which are two of the commonly used isotherms in adsorption studies.

The results presented in the Table 6 showed a superior of the Langmuir isotherm model with R2 values of 0.9931 and 0.9994 for Ni2+ and MB (Fig. S3), respectively while the Freundlich model yielded a poor fit with much lower R2 values of 0.7758 for Ni2+ and 0.7136 for MB, showing that the Freundlich model was inadequate for use in the modeling the adsorption behavior of these pollutants on the composite material.29,36,54 The superior fit of the Langmuir isotherm indicates that adsorption occurs on a homogeneous surface with a limited number of binding sites, with no interaction between adsorbed molecules.7,31,72,78 This implies that this finding coincides with the chemisorption-dominated PSO analysis of kinetic data which indicates that the process depends upon chemical interactions between the Ni2+ and MB, such as valence forces through electron sharing or exchange, at surface binding sites.7,10,72,74 The strong correlation with the Langmuir adsorption model further indicates that the surface adsorption process is surface-mediated, which involves surface complexation and ion exchange with pollutants at surface binding sites.74 Once saturation is achieved through occupation of the surface binding sites, the composite reaches its limit.31,74

Effect of pH and temperature on the adsorption performance

At pH 7, the BAC and BAC-GO-rGO composite (Fig. 13) achieved MB removal efficiencies of 60.42 ± 9.425% and 86.41 ± 5.44%, respectively. When the pH was increased to 9, both materials showed lower MB removal efficiency, to 25.34 ± 2.2% and 65.02 ± 3.30% for the composite.94 Given that the measured pHpzc of the composite was a, the adsorbent carries a net negative charge at both pH values, and further deprotonation of surface functional groups at pH 9 would be expected to strengthen the electrostatic attraction toward the cationic MB.29,39,72 Since pH adjustment to 9 was achieved through NaOH addition, the solution at pH contained a considerable amount of Na+ counterions. High background ionic strength suppresses cation dye adsorption through compression of the electrical double layer at the negatively charged adsorbent.10 The comparatively smaller decline for the composite (86.41% to 65.02%) relative to BAC alone (60.42% to 25.34%) reflects the additional contribution of π–π interactions between MB and graphitic domains of GO and rGO.3,19,76

MB removal efficiency changes against increasing pH.

The effect of temperature on MB and Ni2+ removal by the BAC-GO-rGO composite (Fig. 14) was investigated at 30 °C and 60 °C. MB removal efficiency decreased from 86.41 ± 5.44% to 80.30 ± 4.48% over this range, indicating that adsorption of the dye onto the composite is exothermic. By Le Chatelier's principle, increasing the temperature on an exothermic equilibrium shifts the system towards desorption, reducing the dye retained at high temperature.95 At the molecular level, this can be attributed to the elevated kinetic energy of MB molecules at higher temperatures, which increases the rate of desorption relative to adsorption and reduces residence time on binding sites, weakening the π–π, hydrogen-bonding, and electrostatic interactions through which the MB interacts with the composite surface. This is consistent with the reports for other GO-based composite adsorbents, where MB uptake similarly proceeds through interactions that are weakened by increasing temperature (Fig. 14).95

Removal efficiency changes against increasing temperature for BAC-GO-rGO composite.

Ni2+ removal, in contrast, remained consistent over the two different temperatures, measuring at 97.34 ± 2.24% at 30 °C and 95.34 ± 1.31% at 60 °C. The overlapping standard deviations indicate that Ni2+ retention by the composite is largely insensitive to temperature, distinguishing its behavior from the exothermic process of MB.

This difference in thermal sensitivity is consistent with the synergistic, adsorption pathways for the BAC-GO-rGO composite. Ni2+ retention is expected to be dominated by electrostatic attraction and surface complexation with oxygen-containing functional groups of the GO and rGO components, forming comparatively strong interactions that are resistant to thermal disruption.96,97 MB, as a larger, planar molecule, relies on π–π stacking interactions with graphitic domains of GO and rGO, an interaction type that is more readily disrupted by elevated temperatures.96,97

Reusability

The removal efficiency of MB (Fig. 15) by the BAC-GO-rGO composite decreased progressively over three successive adsorption–desorption cycles, declining from 82 ± 0.78% in the 1st cycle to 48.88 ± 3.21% in the 2nd cycle and further to 27.22 ± 0.24% in the 3rd cycle. This gradual reduction in removal efficiency indicates the progressive saturation and partial blockage of active adsorption sites, including hydroxyl (–OH), and π-conjugated CC surface groups, by residual MB molecules that were not fully desorbed during regeneration. The decline is consistent with the FTIR observations (Fig. 16B), where attenuation of key functional group bands of MB across cycles confirmed the cumulative occupation of adsorption sites, thereby reducing the composite's overall dye uptake capacity with repeated use.98

The Ni2+ removal efficiency (Fig. 15) of the BAC-GO-rGO composite remained consistently high throughout three successive adsorption–desorption cycles, maintaining approximately 98% removal in each cycle. The absence of any noticeable decline in removal efficiency demonstrates the excellent regeneration capability and reusability of the composite. This stable performance suggests that the active adsorption sites remained readily available for Ni2+ binding even after repeated regeneration treatments. Furthermore, the regeneration process was sufficiently effective in removing previously adsorbed Ni2+ ions, thereby preventing significant site blockage or loss of adsorption capacity. The sustained adsorption efficiency across all three cycles indicates that the composite possesses high structural and chemical stability under repeated operational conditions. Therefore, the BAC-GO-rGO composite can be considered a highly reusable adsorbent for Ni2+ removal, with negligible deterioration in adsorption performance over multiple reuse cycles.99

Simultaneously, the FWHM increased from 1.15° to 2.51°, while the crystallite size decreased from 7.0 nm to 3.2 nm. The broadening of diffraction peaks indicates increased structural disorder, defect generation, and disruption of ordered stacking within the GO structure. Increased peak broadening and reduced crystallite size are commonly associated with the formation of defective and less ordered carbon domains.103,104

A notable structural change was observed after the third reuse cycle, where the GO diffraction peak shifted significantly to 2θ = 9.21°. Lower diffraction angles correspond to larger interlayer distances according to Bragg's law, indicating expansion of the layered GO structure. Similar low-angle shifts have been reported when molecules or functional groups become intercalated between GO layers, causing structural swelling and enlargement of the interlayer galleries.105,106

Furthermore, the decrease in FWHM from 2.51° to 1.30° and the corresponding increase in crystallite size to approximately 6.2 nm suggest partial structural reorganization of the expanded layers. Rather than continuous degradation, the material appears to undergo rearrangement into a new layered configuration during repeated reuse cycles. However, further investigation is needed to confirm this.

FTIR analysis (Fig. 16B) provided additional evidence for interactions between MB molecules and the carbonaceous framework of the composite.

The band located at approximately 1637 cm−1 in the fresh composite gradually shifted to lower wavenumbers during repeated adsorption cycles, reaching 1630 cm−1 after the third cycle. This band is generally associated with CC stretching vibrations of graphitic domains and can also contain contributions from adsorbed water molecules.107

The gradual red shift (1637 → 1630 cm−1) indicates modifications in the electronic environment of the carbon framework. Such shifts are commonly attributed to strong interactions between aromatic MB dye molecules and graphene-based materials through π–π stacking interactions. The aromatic rings of methylene blue can interact with the delocalized π-electron system of GO/rGO and activated carbon, altering the vibrational energy of the CC bonds.

The systematic nature of the shift across successive cycles suggests progressive interaction between retained dye molecules and the composite surface. The observed red shift may also be enhanced by hydrogen bonding interactions involving oxygen-containing functional groups present on GO sheets. The absence of characteristic MB bands in the FTIR spectra may indicate that the adsorbed MB molecules are predominantly confined within the pores of the activated carbon, leading to signals below the detection limit of the technique.

Repeated exposure to capillary forces during consecutive wetting and drying steps caused the flexible GO/rGO sheets to undergo localized restacking and aggregation, resulting in sheet compaction and reduced accessible surface area (Fig. 17).108,109 This physical restructuring transformed the highly exfoliated morphology into denser clustered domains.110,111 Although the activated carbon scaffold retained its overall structural integrity throughout the reuse cycles, the gradual decrease in MB removal efficiency can be attributed to reduced surface accessibility and progressive occupation of adsorption sites within the pore network.112,113

Previous studies on Ni2+-loaded graphene oxide systems have reported the appearance of new bands in the 950–1020 cm−1 region following nickel adsorption, attributing them to interactions between Ni2+ ions and oxygen-containing functional groups on the adsorbent surface. These bands are generally associated with Ni-induced modifications of C–O, C–O–C, and hydroxyl groups, indicating the formation of surface complexes during adsorption.115

The second cycle exhibited a distinct absorption band at approximately 1095 cm−1, while the third cycle showed splitting of the original band near 1121 cm−1 into two bands centered around 1121 and 1095 cm−1. Such peak splitting is commonly associated with the development of different local chemical environments around oxygen-containing functional groups. Repeated adsorption and desorption may cause slight rearrangement of surface functional groups and generate multiple coordination environments for Ni2+, resulting in the appearance of overlapping vibrational modes. The absence of significant shifts in the major bands suggests that these changes are localized surface phenomena rather than evidence of structural degradation.114,117

Furthermore, the slight shift of the hydroxyl stretching band from 3416 cm−1 in the initial composite to approximately 3452 cm−1 after repeated reuse suggests changes in hydrogen-bonding interactions on the adsorbent surface. Similar FTIR shifts have been reported following metal-ion adsorption on graphene oxide materials and are generally attributed to coordination interactions between metal ions and surface oxygen functionalities.115 The FTIR results demonstrate that the BAC-GO-rGO composite maintained its chemical integrity after repeated Ni2+ adsorption–desorption cycles. The emergence of minor additional bands and peak splitting in the fingerprint region indicates surface complexation between Ni2+ ions and oxygen-containing functional groups, while the preservation of the principal absorption bands confirms the excellent structural stability and reusability of the composite. This finding is consistent with the unchanged Ni2+ removal efficiency observed over three consecutive cycles.

Overall, the BAC-GO-rGO composite demonstrates efficient removal of MB and Ni2+ ions, along with good regeneration performance over three adsorption–desorption cycles. Future studies could focus on extending the cycle number, evaluating long-term structural stability, and investigating performance under real wastewater conditions.

Conclusion

The study successfully synthesized and characterized the GO, rGO, and bamboo activated carbon. Comprehensive characterization confirmed the activated carbon with an iodine number of 761.78 mg g−1, also with an excellent porous network confirmed by the SEM images. Successful oxidation of graphite into graphene oxide and successful reduction of graphene oxide into reduced graphene oxide were characterized using PXRD, and the SEM images of BAC, GO, and rGO were used to analyze the surface morphological characteristics of the components. Availability of the functional groups was analyzed with FTIR.

The adsorption performance of the BAC-GO-rGO composite over the plain BAC demonstrated clear superiority in all tested concentrations of both Ni2+ and MB. For Ni2+ composite achieved a peak removal efficiency of 97.35% at 5 mg L−1, and the composite managed to maintain the higher removal efficiency at 92.56% even when the concentration of the Ni2+ ions is increased to 10 mg L−1, in both cases outperforming the plain activated carbon. In the removal of MB, the composite achieved a maximum efficiency of 86.41% at 100 mg L−1, relative to the approximately 60% removal efficiency achieved by plain BAC. Although at 400 mg L−1 both materials faced saturation limits, the composite managed to achieve 33.15% peak removal efficiency compared to the 21.57% achieved by the AC.

Kinetic studies confirm that the pseudo-second-order (PSO) model provided the best fit for all experimental conditions, with R2 values exceeding 0.90 for both Ni2+ and MB. This implies that the rate-determining step for these adsorption processes is chemisorption, which involves the adsorption between adsorbate and adsorbent with valence forces involving electron sharing or exchange between the cationic pollutants and the binding sites. This process is supported by the synergistic effects of the GO and rGO components in the composite, which enabled the electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking, and surface complexation. Furthermore, the Langmuir isotherm model accurately described that the adsorption follows monolayer adsorption onto the homogeneous surfaces of both plain activated carbon and composite, with calculated maximum adsorption capacities of 22.88 mg g−1 for Ni2+ and 357.14 mg g−1 for MB.

The BAC-GO-rGO composite exhibits efficient removal of MB and Ni2+ ions with good regeneration performance over three adsorption–desorption cycles, highlighting its potential for wastewater treatment applications.

Overall, the BAC-GO-rGO composite exhibited superior performance as an adsorbent, outperforming the conventional activated carbon via better chemical interactions and a multi-level adsorption technique. However, despite the superior performance, one of the major limitations of the composite is reaching the saturation limit under higher initial concentrations of pollutants. Future work should focus on improving the regeneration efficiency and adsorption capacity of the composite, as well as optimizing its pore structure and surface functionality to extend its applicability to a wider range of contaminant concentrations and real wastewater systems.
