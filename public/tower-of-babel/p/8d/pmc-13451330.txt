Development of an Oxford nanopore sequencing technology-based whole genome sequencing method for Plasmodium falciparum to support malaria molecular surveillance
Authors: Catherine Bakari, Aurel Holzschuh, Misago D Seth, Rashid A Madebe, Dativa Pereus, Celine I Mandara, Pierre Schneeberger, Jonathan J Juliano, Jeffrey A Bailey, Deus S Ishengoma, Christian Nsanzabana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole genome sequencing (WGS) is critical for malaria molecular surveillance (MMS). While short-read platforms have been widely used for Plasmodium falciparum genomics; they have limitations in resolving repetitive regions and structural variation in this highly complex genome. Long-read technologies, such as those developed by Oxford nanopore technologies (ONT), offer complementary capabilities and may be particularly suitable for low-resource settings. We optimized an ONT-based WGS protocol for P. falciparum from DBS and whole blood samples. Laboratory strains (3D7, HB3, and Dd2) were mixed with whole blood to create mock infections and dried blood spots (DBS). DNA was extracted using Qiagen or Tween-Chelex 100, and parasite DNA was enriched using McrBC/MspJI digestion, NEBNext microbiome DNA enrichment Kit (NMDEK), and selective whole genome amplification (sWGA). Parasite and human DNA levels were quantified by multiplex qPCR. Sequencing was performed on ONT (R10.4.1 flow cell). The protocol was validated with? clinical samples from a therapeutic efficacy study. Tween-Chelex 100 had a higher DNA yield compared to Qiagen kit (2–4 Ct value difference), though Qiagen extractions gave longer reads (median 2791 vs. 2252 bp). NMDEK effectively depleted human DNA by increasing the Ct value of β-tubulin human gene by ~ 10 cycles, while sWGA increased parasite DNA. Combined enrichment yielded > 75% parasite DNA at 100 p/µl and > 90% genome coverage. WGS of mock and clinical samples achieved median read lengths > 2 kb, > 30× depth, and 99.8% accuracy. Whole blood outperformed DBS in depth and coverage. ONT WGS showed high concordance with Sanger sequencing and detected additional mutations and structural variants, including pfmdr1 and pfgch1 copy number variations. The optimized ONT-WGS protocol enabled accurate, high-coverage sequencing from whole blood and DBS. It provides a practical option for generating long-read data with relatively rapid turnaround and modest infrastructure requirements, supporting its application for MMS in malaria-endemic settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46451-8.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

The progress towards reducing malaria incidence and mortality rate by at least 90% by 2030, according to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) global technical strategy (GTS) of 20151, has stalled, particularly in countries with high malaria burden2. Several factors contribute to this stagnation, including biological threats such as antimalarial drug resistance, diagnostic resistance resulting from Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2/3 (pfhrp2/3) gene deletions, invasive mosquito species, and increasing insecticide resistance3. Other non-biological challenges include climate change, weak health systems, and limited funding for malaria control and research as well as innovation3.

Malaria molecular surveillance (MMS) has the potential to complement routine surveillance which was recommended by WHO through the GTS to make malaria surveillance a core intervention to provide evidence to guide implementation and tracking progress of malaria control and elimination strategies4.

MMS can provide valuable insights on the status, patterns, and trends of the emergence and spreading of antimalarial, diagnostic, and vector resistance. It also helps assess the impact of interventions on parasite genomic diversity4–7 and complements classic epidemiological data to provide more accurate trends and estimates of transmission intensities4–6. Recent reports indicate that more countries have the capacity to generate pathogen genomics data, a growth largely driven by the initiatives launched during the Covid-19 pandemic8–10. However, further efforts are needed to strengthen the capacity for good-quality data generation and analysis to support real-time MMS and decision-making.

As MMS expands, there is growing recognition of the need to move beyond single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) detection and to include broader forms of genomic variations, such as structural variants (SVs). SVs have been increasingly recognized as important contributors to certain phenotypic traits in P. falciparum. For example, copy number variation (CNV) in P. falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 gene (pfmdr1) is associated to mefloquine resistance; CNVs in plasmepsin 2 and 3 genes (pfpm2/3) can cause reduced susceptibility to piperaquine11,12 and the GTP cyclohydrolase 1 gene (pfgch1) have been associated with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine resistance13. Deletions in pfhrp2/3, which are crucial for HRP2-based rapid diagnostic tests, are linked to diagnostic resistance, posing a threat to accurate detection of Plasmodium falciparum parasites before treatment with antimalarial drugs14,15. Furthermore, SVs in vaccine target genes, such as csp, which encodes the primary target of the RTS, S and R21 malaria vaccines, could influence vaccine efficacy16,17. Beyond variant detection, whole genome sequencing (WGS) enables high-resolution analysis of parasite population structure, transmission dynamics, and evolutionary patterns18,19.

Second-generation sequencing (SGS) platforms, primarily based on short reads, have been widely used to generate data for genomic surveillance of various pathogens, including malaria parasites20. This technology can be used for targeted or WGS, offers high-throughput analyses, and provides sequencing data with high accuracy21. However, the short reads (between 75 and 300 bases) pose challenges for genome mapping and de novo assembly, particularly in organisms with complex genomic architectures such as P. falciparum. The P. falciparum genome is rich in low-complexity regions (poly-A tracts and short tandem repeats), large insertions and deletions (indels), and highly duplicated gene families, such as the var, rifin, and stevor gene families, which share extensive sequence similarity. These features often cause short reads to align to multiple regions22. This complexity reduces the ability to accurately construct the parasite’s whole genome and limits the detection of SV23.

Long-read sequencing (LRS) generates long stretches of DNA spanning tens of kilobases, improving the ability to uniquely map to reference genomes, resolve complex genomic regions, and accurately genotype SV24–27. Although this technology has been associated with high sequencing error rates in the past, recent advancements in sequencing chemistry and analysis software have significantly improved its accuracy. For instance, PacBio with HiFi reads can now achieve 99.9% accuracy, and Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) with Kit 14 chemistry achieves > 99% accuracy28. Among the platforms, ONT is particularly well-suited for malaria-endemic low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to its affordability, scalability, portability, and ease of implementation in resource-limited settings29–33.

For P. falciparum, WGS can play an important role in MMS by providing the most comprehensive genomic information.For example, WGS of P. falciparum can support the discovery of new mutations associated with antimalarial drug resistance or vaccine escape. Indeed, WGS can detect genetic variations across the entire genome, including SNPs, iindels, and SV. Leveraging the advantages of WGS with ONT may not only enhance our understanding of drug and diagnostic resistance but also generate actionable insights to guide malaria control strategies and inform public health policies, especially in malaria-endemic regions.

To date, most of LRS-based WGS studies have been limited to laboratory-adapted strains cultured in vitro34,35, primarily due to the technical challenges of obtaining sufficient high-quality parasite DNA from clinical samples with minimal host contamination. A limited number of studies have employed LRS for specific genomic targets in clinical isolates. For example, a PacBio-based analysis of var genes36 and ONT focused on targeted sequencing of specific loci such as drug resistance genes37,38, pfhrp2/3 genes, and other polymorphic markers39. However, ONT-based WGS of clinical P. falciparum samples, particularly from minimally processed field-collected materials, remains largely unexplored. When attempted, such efforts have often relied on leukocyte-depleted blood, limiting their scalability and application in low-resource settings. WGS of clinical malaria samples is further hindered by several factors: the high abundance of host DNA, which accounts for over 80% of the total DNA40 2013); low parasite densities; and limited amounts of blood from dried blood spots (DBS), with a volume of ~ 30 to 50 µl. Moreover, DNA yields from DBS are substantially lower compared to whole blood41. To address these limitations and improve parasite DNA yield, several enrichment methods have been developed. These include selective whole genome amplification (sWGA)42 and enzyme cleavage methods (MspJI and McrBC)43,44. Despite their utility, the compatibility and performance of these ONT-based WGS remain insufficiently characterized. We have tested these methods and developed an optimised P. falciparum enrichment and WGS protocol using ONT to support MMS in resource-limited countries.

Methods and materials

Parasite culture and mock samples

Three laboratory strains (3D7, HB3, and Dd2), obtained from the Malaria Research and Reference Resource Center (MRA-102, MRA-155, and MRA-150, USA), were used for this study. Briefly, parasite cultures were maintained in 4.5 mL of 1640 Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium (Invitrogen, Paisley, UK) in 500 per µl of uninfected red blood cells. The cultures were kept at 5% haemotocrit at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5% Albumax, 25 mM HEPES, 100 µM hypoxanthine, 24 mM sodium bicarbonate, and gassed with a mix of 4% CO₂, 3% O₂, and 93%45. Parasite cultures were synchronized using 5% D-sorbitol treatment to selectively lyse mature-stages of the parasites, allowing the enrichment of ring-stage parasites and ensuring a synchronized population before harvesting as previously described45.

To obtain a range of parasite densities (10,000, 1,000, and 100 parasites/µl of blood) often reported in malaria patients or asymptomatic carriers46,47, mock samples were prepared by mixing synchronized ring-stage laboratory strains with uninfected human whole blood. To account for polyclonality, mixtures of different strains were prepared in varying ratios with the aim of capturing the frequency of minor alleles ranging from 10% to 40%. Seven mixtures were prepared with either two strains of 3D7 and Dd2 at the ratios of 8:2, 2:8, 6:4, and 4:6, respectively, or three strains of 3D7, HB3, and Dd2 at the ratios of 6:3:1, 3:1:6, and 1:6:3, respectively. In all mixtures, the total parasite concentration was 10,000 p/µl, with the lowest individual strain concentration being 1,000 p/µl. The prepared samples were either used directly for DNA extraction or spotted on filter papers to prepare dried blood spots (DBS). For DBS, 50 µl was blotted on two different filter papers, QIAcard FTA® Cards (Whatman, GE Healthcare UK Limited, Amersham Place, UK) and 3MM CHR Whatman (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA).

Clinical samples

Fourteen samples were selected from a Therapeutic Efficacy Study (TES) conducted in Karagwe District, Kagera Region, Northwestern Tanzania, from April to September 2022, were selected. This study was conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical and regulatory guidelines. Prior informed consent was obtained from all participants, and samples were collected from individuals who consented to participate in the study. Details of these samples have been published elsewhere48. All laboratory procedures were performed following standard operating procedures. The samples had varying levels of parasite densities, ranging from 144 p/µl to 82,000 p/µl, and had previously been sequenced by Sanger sequencing for two genes: pfmdr1 (region 1: codon positions 86 and 184; region 2: codon positions 1034, 1042, and 1246) and the propeller domain of the pfk13 gene (codon positions 430–726)48.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from parasite culture and mock samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 µl of sample was used for extraction, and DNA was eluted in 100 µl of elution buffer. For DBS, three 6 mm diameter pieces were cut from each sample using a single-hole paper punch, and genomic DNA was extracted using Tween-Chelex 100 (Bio-Rad Laboratories AG, Switzerland) as previously described44, or with the QIAamp DNA Blood mini kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions49. The extracted DNA was eluted in 100 µl of elution buffer with the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit or 150 µl for DBS samples processed using Tween-Chelex 100. To remove potential PCR inhibitors, the extracted DNA was purified using 1.8X Ampure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Australia) and stored at 4 °C before use.

DNA enrichment

Enrichment of parasite DNA was performed using a two-step process. The first step focused on the depletion of human DNA using one of the following methods according to the manufacturer’s instructions: (a) McrBC enzyme (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA: used at the time of this study but no longer commercially available), (b) MspJI enzyme (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA, or c) NEBNext® Microbiome DNA enrichment Kits (NMDEK) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) with minor modification for DBS samples. The second step consisted of sWGA using the 6 A and 10AD primer sets with modified dNTPs (70% AT; 30% GC)44. The amplified DNA was purified using 1.8X Ampure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Lane Cove N2W, Australia) and further treated with T7 endonuclease I to remove the secondary structures formed after sWGA.

qPCR

To assess the efficiency of the enrichment process, a multiplex qPCR assay targeting three genes was used. For all qPCR reactions, 2 µl of purified DNA eluate was used per reaction, without normalization for input blood or elution volume. The genes included the P. falciparum lactate dehydrogenase (pfldh), a single-copy gene of P. falciparum; the single-copy human β-tubulin or human RNaseP (HsRNaseP); and the Plasmodium species multiple-copy 18 S rRNA gene (Pspp 18 S) (Supplementary Table 1). Sequencing platforms

Assay development and initial optimization were conducted using ONT with MinION Mk1C platform. For the rest of the study, high-throughput sequencing and data generation were performed on the ONT PromethION platform. The assay is platform-agnostic within the ONT ecosystem and is compatible with multiple ONT devices, allowing implementation according to throughput requirements.

Library preparation, nanopore sequencing, and data analysis

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat #SQK-NBD 114.24, ONT, UK). Sequencing was performed for 72 h using R10.4.1 flow cells (FLO-MIN114, ONT) on PromethION P2 Solo (ONT, Oxford, UK) with default parameters. Base calling was performed with Dorado v0.7.0 using the super-accurate model (highest accuracy). Sequencing data were processed on a high-performance computing cluster equipped with NVIDIA GPUs for demultiplexing, and trimming of sequence reads was performed with Dorado v0.7.0. Summary statistics of read length and quality were generated using Nanoplot version 1.42.0. Trimmed reads were filtered using Filtlong v0.2.1 with parameters --keep_percent 90 and --min_length 1000, then competitively mapped to the concatenated P. falciparum 3D7 (PlasmoDB v67) and human (GRCh38) reference genomes using Minimap2 v2.28 with the map-ont preset to minimize false-positive mapping. Samtools V1.759 utilities were used to sort and index the BAM files. Coverage statistics and proportions of parasite and human DNA were determined using samtools for coverage and depth. Samtools view was used to clean the resulting BAM files, excluding alignments marked as unmapped and excluding alignments with the non-primary alignment using flags 4 and 256. The cleaned BAM files were used for variant calling using Clair3 for SNPs and Sniffles2 for structural variations, including indels. Functional annotation of the resulting SNPs was performed using SnpEff V4.1l62. A schematic representation of this pipeline for processing ONT data is shown in Fig. 2.

Bioinformatics pipeline for the analysis of WGS data generated using Oxford nanopore technology.

For SV detection, manual visualization was performed by computing for depth of coverage using samtools depth and per-base coverage by extraction for gene loci of interest and their flanking regions. BED files defined gene boundaries ± upstream/downstream 10 kb regions.

Results

Optimization of DNA extraction and enrichment methods using different samples

Extraction from DBS samples using Tween-Chelex-100 yielded higher DNA concentrations compared to Qiagen kit, with Ct values being 2–4 cycles lower (Fig. 3). Whole blood samples showed 4–7 cycle lower Ct values than DBS, reflecting greater input volume (Fig. 3). Across all sample types and parasite densities, Tween-Chelex-100 extraction produced significantly lower Ct values compared to Qiagen (Wilcoxon test value = 0.031). Median Ct differences ranged from 2 to 5 cycles in favor of Chelex (Fig. 3). However, Qiagen extraction produced significantly longer sequencing reads (median range 2,341–3,847 bp compared to 1,905–2,842 bp for Tween-Chelex; p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷) and more consistent N50 lengths (Fig. 4).

Performance of different extraction methods based on qPCR results of Pan-Plasmodium 18 S rRNA and Homo sapiens RNaseP sequence genes. (A) Pspp18S and (B) HRNaseP.

Read length for sequencing data across different extraction methods.

Host DNA depletion resulting from enzymatic digestion (McrBC, MsPJI) was confirmed by gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 1), and for both enzymatic digestion and NMDEK, by qPCR (Fig. 5). McrBC treatment modestly increased Ct values for both parasite and host DNA. NMDEK, in contrast, substantially depleted human DNA (β-tubulin Ct value increased from 22.1 to 30.4) while causing only a modest reduction in parasite DNA (pfldh Ct increase from 22.7 to 26.7), leading to its selection for downstream use.

Enrichment efficiency of parasite DNA based on analysis of Ct values from qPCR amplification of culture parasites.

After sequencing, the proportion of reads mapping to the parasite genome relative to human DNA varied by treatment. NMDEK treatment resulted in 0.50 parasite DNA (10000 p/µl and 100 p/µl). In contrast, sWGA treatment resulted in 0.50 at 100 p/µl and 0.92 at 10,000 p/µl. Combining NMDEK and sWGA increased the proportion to 0.96 at 10,000 p/µl and 0.80 at 100 p/µl, representing a 1.5-fold increase in relative abundance compared to either method alone. This enabled robust sequencing (e.g., > 10× mean coverage at 100 p/µl and > 30× at 10,000 p/µl, with > 80–96% chromosome coverage; Fig. 6). NMDEK performance was further validated across parasite strains (3D7, HB3, and Dd2) and sample types. It consistently depleted human DNA, increasing β-tubulin Ct values by 6–11 cycles, while preserving parasite DNA (Pspp18S Ct remained stable across parasitemia and sample types). Subsequent enrichment of parasite DNA using sWGA dramatically increased parasite DNA yield, reducing Pspp18S Ct values by up to 16.3 cycles, even at 100 p/µl. These trends were consistent across all tested sample types, including DBS made on FTA cards and 3MM filter papers (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Comparison of different parasite DNA enrichment efficiencies from mock samples at different parasite densities.

In the 14 clinical samples, parasite DNA was consistently less abundant than human DNA, particularly at lower parasitemia. This was reflected in qPCR Ct values: human β-tubulin Ct values (22.9–24.0) were lower than parasite pfldh (21.0–29.4), indicating higher initial human DNA content. Correspondingly, the parasite DNA fraction (pfldh / [pfldh + β-tubulin]) decreased with parasitemia, from a mean of 0.829 at ≥ 100,000 p/µl to 0.069 at < 1,000 p/µl (Supplementary Fig. 3). Following NMDEK enrichment, human DNA was substantially depleted, with β-tubulin Ct values increasing from a median of 23.0 (range: 22.3–24.6) to 32.9 (range: 30.1–34.7), reflecting ~ 10-cycle reduction. After sWGA, human DNA was nearly undetectable (Ct > 40 in most samples; Fig. 7b). In contrast, parasite DNA levels remained stable after NMDEK treatment (pfldh median Ct: 23.3–24.1; range: 21.6–32.0), indicating minimal loss (Fig. 7a, c). sWGA further enriched parasite DNA, reducing pfldh Ct to a median of 21.5 (range: 19.0–23.7), with similar trends observed for Pspp18S. Three low-parasitemia samples (T0908_D28 [144 p/µl], T0952_D14 [660 p/µl], and T0952_D0 [1044 p/µl]) were outliers, showing higher residual human DNA even after both NMDEK and sWGA.

Parasite DNA enrichment efficiency from 14 clinical samples based on Ct values from qPCR results showing amplification of parasite genes (Pfldh and Pspp18S). The level of significance is shown as follows: * p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, *** p ≤ 0.0001, ns = no significant difference. A = Pfldh gene, B = human beta tubulin gene, and C = Pssp 18 S gene.

Optimization of sequencing using P. falciparum laboratory strains and mock samples

WGS was initially performed separately for each of the three well-characterized laboratory strains (3D7, HB3, and Dd2) serving as internal controls for data quality and variant validation. This was done with genomic DNA extracted from high-density parasite cultures without enrichment (3D7, HB3, and Dd2, with 225,648 p/µl, 299,992 p/µl, and 626,288 p/µl, respectively). The sequencing produced an average of 1,048,084 reads per sample, with post-filtering median read lengths of 4.4 to 7.5 kb and N50 values up to 11.9 kb. Read quality was high, with median Phred scores of 24.8 to 25.5 (approximately 99.7% base accuracy). Over 99.9% of the reads mapped to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference genome, with minimal DNA contamination (< 0.06%) (supplementary Table 2). The mean per-base sequencing depth across the nuclear genome was 158x. Coverage uniformity was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) and the interquartile range (IQR) of per-base coverage. The CV was 105%, indicating substantial variability in depth across positions. The IQR ranged from 118x to 165x, showing that most genomic regions were well covered across the P. falciparum chromosomes for all three laboratory strains, except the mitochondrial and apicoplast genomes (Fig. 8). The 3D7 strain had 100.0% genome coverage (Fig. 9), while Dd2 and HB3 strains exhibited coverage ranging from 96.0% to 99.0% across all 14 P. falciparum chromosomes when mapped to the 3D7 reference genome. Mapping both the Dd2 and HB3 strains to their corresponding (Dd2 and HB3) reference genomes, respectively, resulted in 100.0% coverage across all 14 chromosomes, except chromosome 2 of the HB3 strain, which had a coverage of 96.0% (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The assay successfully detected all known drug resistance-associated mutations due to SNP for the three laboratory strains sequenced. The 3D7 strain had no mutation linked to known drug resistance, except the Pfhdps A437 mutation. In contrast, the HB3 strain carried the A437G mutation in pfdhps along with three additional mutations, which were in the pfdhfr with one mutation (S108N on chromosome 4) and two mutations in the pfmdr1 gene (Y184F and N1042D on chromosome 5). The Dd2 strain displayed a total of 17 mutations across multiple genes, including four mutations in pfdhfr (C51I, C59R, and S108N), one mutation in pfmdr1 (N86Y), six mutations in pfcrt (K76T, A220S, Q271E, N326S, I356T, and R371I on chromosome 7), three mutations in pfdhps (S436F and A613S), one mutation in kelch10 (P623T on chromosome 10), one mutation in PPH (V1157L on chromosome 10), two mutations in pffd (D193Y and T236I on chromosome 13), and one mutation in the pfmdr2 gene (T484I on chromosome 14) (Fig. 10). Sequencing depth across chromosomes 5 (5:929,384–988,747), 8 (8:1,290,239-1,387,982), 12 (12:973,000-978,000), and 13 (13:2,769,916-2,844,785) was used to infer three known structural variations (SVs): pfmdr1 and pfgch1 copy number variation, and pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 gene deletions, respectively. The Dd2 strain exhibited a clear pfmdr1 duplication, with a mean read depth over the gene region at 318×, representing a 1.37-fold increase relative to its flanking regions (233×). In contrast, pfhrp2 coverage in Dd2 dropped to 84.3×, compared to 104× in the flanking sequence, indicating a 0.81-fold decrease, suggesting gene deletion consistent with its known profile. The HB3 strain, known to carry a deletion in pfhrp3, showed a gene region depth of 169× versus 211× in its flanks, reflecting a 20% drop in coverage. No evidence of pfhrp2 deletion was observed in HB3, which showed a moderate increase in pfhrp2 depth (154×) compared to its flanks (138×). A slight increase in pfmdr1 coverage (165× vs. 154×) suggested no strong evidence of duplication. Regarding the pfgch1 locus on chromosome 12, no clear evidence of amplification was observed based on read depth comparisons. In Dd2, the mean coverage over the gene was 156× versus 141× in flanking regions (fold change: 1.11); HB3 showed 237× vs. 232× (fold change: 1.02); and 3D7 showed 141× vs. 135× (fold change: 1.04). These small changes are consistent with expected variability in single-copy regions (Fig. 11). Additionally, analysis with Sniffles identified copy number variations (CNVs) in the pfgch1 gene in 3D7 (position 974230 − 97605) and the HB3 strain (position 942449–1103340) and in the pfmdr1 gene only in Dd2 strain (position). However, Sniffles did not detect the pfhrp2 gene deletion in Dd2 strain (position 888289–970239) or the pfhrp3 gene deletion in the HB3 strain. We are currently exploring other tools to detect the deletions.

WGS of mixed laboratory strains and mock samples enriched by NMDEK and sWGA resulted in a median quality score of 27 (99.8% base accuracy). The average median read length was 2.0 kb for Qiagen and 1.6 kb for Tween-Chelex 100 extractions, respectively. The estimated N50 length averaged 3.8 kb for Qiagen and 3.0 kb for Chelex-100. After filtering for reads with lengths < 1 kb, the median read length shifted to 2.8 kb for Qiagen and 2.1 kb for Tween-Chelex 100 (Supplementary Table 3). The sequencing meandepth varied widely depending on levels of parasitemia. High parasitemia (10,000 p/µl) resulted in mean depths exceeding 50x, leading to consistently high coverage of all chromosomes for all strains and with minimal variations among the sample types (Supplementary Fig. 6). In contrast, low parasitemia (100 p/µl) yielded significantly lower mean depths, often below 10x for 3 MM and FTA, with reduced and more variable coverage levels. Among the DBS, FTA cards generally performed better than 3MM filter papers, particularly at intermediate parasitemia (1,000 p/µl), where FTA samples sometimes approached whole blood in performance (Figure xx).

For DBS samples on 3MM filter papers and FTA cards, mapping percentages were consistent across extraction methods. Higher parasite densities resulted in a greater proportion of reads mapping to the P. falciparum genome, while lower densities showed a corresponding decrease. At 10,000 p/µl, over 86.0% of reads mapped to the parasite reference genome for both extraction methods. At 1,000 p/µl, mapping percentages dropped to 60.0% for Tween-Chelex 100 extraction and just over 46.0% for Qiagen extraction. At the lowest parasite density of 100 p/µl, mapping rates fell below 28.0% for both methods. DNA extracted from whole blood using the Qiagen method also showed slightly lower mapping percentages at 10,000 p/µl (80.9%) compared to DBS samples at the same density. This proportion declined further to 54.0% at 1,000 p/µl and 36.0% at 100 p/µl (Fig. 12). Despite these declines, chromosome-wide coverage for whole blood samples remained above 90% even at 100 p/µl. In contrast, coverage for FTA card and 3MM filter paper samples at the same parasite density dropped to below 50.0%. Notably, extraction methods did not have an impact on coverage or mean depth, as in both datasets, coverage exceeded 85% across all parasite densities, with near-complete genome representation (> 95%) observed at 1,000 and 10,000 p/µl regardless of extraction method. At the lowest parasite density (100 p/µl), variations in coverage were observed between sample types, with FTA generally outperforming 3MM. Mean sequencing depth followed similar trends but showed greater variability. At all parasite densities, higher depth was associated with improved coverage, particularly at the lower parasitemia level. While Qiagen-extracted samples showed higher median depth than Chelex-extracted ones, this trend likely reflects differences in sequencing run parameters (such as number of pores in the flow cell loading) rather than extraction efficiency alone and therefore should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of extraction protocols. The best results were obtained for samples with higher parasite densities (> 1,000/µl), regardless of the sample type (Fig. 10). In mock samples, detection of mutations was strongly associated with the relative abundance of each strain. In the 6:3:1 (3D7:HB3:Dd2) mixture, 14 of 21 mutations were detected, including complete detection of all four HB3-specific mutations (at ratio 3) and partial detection of 10 out of 17 Dd2-specific mutations (at ratio 1). In the 3:1:6 mixture, where HB3 was the least abundant strain, 18 of 21 mutations were detected; only one of the four HB3-specific mutations was recovered, while all 17 Dd2-associated mutations (at ratio 6) were detected. In the 1:6:3 mixture, where HB3 was the dominant strain, all 21 mutations were identified, including full recovery of both HB3- and Dd2-specific variants. Across all mixtures tested, mutations from the dominant strain were consistently and reliably detected. In contrast, detection of minority-strain mutations was incomplete and varied according to their proportional representation in the sample. In mixtures where a single strain predominated, such as 6:4:0, 4:6:0, 8:2:0, and 2:8:0, detection of strain-specific mutations was complete, indicating high sensitivity when only two strains are present in the sample. In intermediate-ratio mixtures, including 1:6:3, 3:1:6, and 6:3:1, not all mutations from all three strains were generally recovered, suggesting a detection threshold of approximately 20 to 30% relative abundance. Notably, in 3:1:6, where HB3 was present at a 1 ratio, only a single HB3 mutation was detected. Similarly, in 6:3:1, Dd2 was at a 1 ratio and showed partial recovery, underscoring a sensitivity limit for minor populations in complex mixtures (Fig. 13).

In mock samples containing a single strain, the same SVs were detected on chromosomes 5, 8, and 13 as in cultured laboratory strains sequenced without enrichment. In mixed-strain samples, pfmdr1 copy number and hrp2/3 gene deletion variations were readily detected when carried by the majority strain. The sample with 6:3:1, when Dd2 was the least abundant strain, showed a marked reduction in pfhrp2 coverage (19.9× vs. 37.1× flank, fold change: 0.54), suggesting a potential deletion. In contrast, pfhrp3 displayed an unusually high fold change (3.15), likely indicating amplification, given a lower flank coverage (6.58×). pfmdr1 also showed reduced coverage (fold change: 0.74), raising the possibility of partial deletion. pfgch1 showed near-equal depth (69.6× vs. 72.9×), not supporting duplication. In contrast, in a sample with a 3:1:6 ratio, where Dd2 strain was a majority clone (ratio of 6), it showed the strongest signal for pfhrp2 deletion (fold change: 0.41, 59% drop) and a moderate increase in pfmdr1 coverage (77.8× vs. 59.5×; fold change: 1.31), suggesting copy number gain. pfhrp3 also showed increased gene coverage (fold change: 1.60), potentially reflecting duplication. pfgch1 exhibited a fold change of 1.20, which may suggest mild amplification. The sample with a 1:6:3 ratio displayed no significant deviation in pfgch1 or pfmdr1 (fold changes close to 1.0), indicating likely single-copy status. pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 showed reduced gene coverage (fold changes: 0.72 and 0.78), suggesting partial deletions. However, Sniffle detected duplication in pfgch1 sample with a 6:3:1 ratio, where the 3D7 strain was present in a 6 ratio (Fig. 14).

Sequencing clinical samples of P. falciparum

The median base quality score of the 14 samples sequenced was over 27, corresponding to 99.8% base accuracy. The median read length was 1.6 kb, and the average N50 length was 2.9 kb. After filtering out reads shorter than 1 kb, the median read length increased to 2.4 kb, and the N50 length averaged 3.5 kb. Chromosome-wide coverage varied with parasite density, with over 90%, 80%, 70%, and 30% at 82,421 p/µl, 2,196 p/µl, 660 p/µl, and 144 p/µl, respectively. Sequencing depth was below 50x for the samples with low parasite densities (< 1000 p/µl) and above 50x for moderate (1000 to 15000 p/µl) and very high parasite densities (> 100,000 p/µl) (Supplementary Fig. 7). The proportion of reads mapping to the P. falciparum genome was highly dependent on parasite densities. At higher parasite density (> 20,000 p/µl), more than 95.0% of reads mapped to the P. falciparum genome. At the parasite density between 500 and 20,000 p/µl, approximately 80.0% of the reads mapped to the P. falciparum genome, while at very low parasite densities (< 500 p/µl), the proportion of reads mapping to the reference genome dropped further to 30.0% (Table 1). Of the clinical samples, 43% (6/14) exhibited polyclonal infections, with complexity of infection (COI) values ranging from 1 to 2. Sequencing results from Sanger and ONT were largely consistent for pfk13 and pfmdr1 gene mutations, although some discrepancies were observed. Complete sequencing data for both genes were obtained from all 14 samples using both Sanger and ONT methods and used to assess concordance between the two platforms. Among these, 11 samples (78.6%) showed full agreement in pfk13 mutation profiles between Sanger and ONT. Two samples with high parasite densities (82,421 p/µl and 60,000 p/µl) displayed discordant results: ONT detected pfk13 mutations, whereas Sanger did not identify any SNPs; likely the mutations were carried by the minority clones in the samples, as both samples had COI of > 1. In contrast, one low-density sample (600 p/µl) showed the opposite pattern: Sanger detected mutations, while ONT did not identify any SNPs. For the pfmdr1 gene, sequencing results for the detections of SNPs at codons N86Y, Y184F, and D1246Y were completely concordant between Sanger and ONT (Fig. 15). Moreover, ONT sequencing identified several additional mutations in genes not targeted by Sanger, including pfatp6-E431K, pfatp6-S769N, pfdhfr-C59R, pfdhfr-N51I, pfdhfr-S108N, pfdhfr-S108T, pfdhfr-I164L, pfdhps-A437G, pfdhps-K540E, pffd-D193Y, pfSec14-N615D, pfcrt-A220S, pfPF3D7-1451200-N71N, and pfPF3D7-1322700-T236I (Supplementary Table 4). SV analysis using Sniffles identified 11 duplication events in seven samples. These duplications were detected on chromosomes Pf3D7_05_v3 and Pf3D7_12_v3, corresponding to the pfmdr1 and pfgch1 loci, respectively. Duplications involving the pfmdr1 locus (Pf3D7_05_v3) were identified in T0936_D0, T0936_D28, T0950_D0, and T0982_D21, with sizes ranging from 1033 bp to 3117 bp, and positions spanning ~ 884,900 to ~ 947,000 bp. Duplications on Pf3D7_12_v3, affecting the Pfgch1 locus, were found in samples T0908_D0, T0944_D0, T0944_D21, and T0936_D28, with event sizes ranging from 467 bp to 2362 bp. These duplications were clustered around positions 973,800 to 984,700 bp (Supplementary Table 5). Analysis of deletion events is underway.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and optimized an assay for WGS of P. falciparum using ONT, addressing key technical challenges in generating high-quality long-read genomic data from clinical samples. A major outcome of this study is the successful implementation of a novel parasite enrichment method that reduces human DNA content, as shown by a ten-cycle increase in Ct values across samples with varying parasite densities. This significantly improved sequencing depth and genome coverage, particularly in complex regions of the parasite genome that are often underrepresented in datasets generated by short-read sequencing. By systematically comparing DNA extraction methods, sample types, and enrichment strategies, our approach enabled more comprehensive genomic analysis of P. falciparum. The method has high potential for supporting the ongoing efforts based on MMS to monitor parasite evolution, drug resistance, and transmission dynamics even in resource-constrained settings which are endemic to malaria.

Similar to other WGS protocols, comparison of extraction methods highlighted the critical influence of DNA preparation on the performance of long-read sequencing methods50. Of the DNA extraction methods, Qiagen yielded consistently longer and more uniform reads than the Tween-Chelex 100 method, as evidenced by significantly higher median read lengths and N50 values. These results are consistent with previous studies that emphasized the importance of DNA purity and integrity for maximizing read length, particularly for platforms such as Oxford Nanopore and PacBio50. The relatively shorter reads generated from samples extracted using Tween-Chelex 100 could possibly be due to vigorous vortexing steps during DNA extraction, which may have potentially increased DNA fragmentation or residual impurities that inhibit the efficiency of downstream processes51. While Tween-Chelex-based methods are appealing due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness52, our findings indicate that Qiagen provides superior read lengths for applications where this is critical, such as detection of SVs. Our findings suggest that for applications requiring longer reads and sequencing accuracy, column-based DNA isolation and purification methods like Qiagen offer a superior alternative51. However, in resource-limited settings or when working with DBS, DNA extraction using Tween-Chelex may still offer practical benefits, though with some compromise in sequencing output. Our results further demonstrated that genome coverage improves with increasing sequencing depth, especially at low parasite densities. Both Qiagen and Chelex extraction protocols enabled high-quality sequencing across a range of sample types and parasite concentrations. However, Qiagen-extracted samples showed higher sequencing depth, and this may likely be due to technical differences between sequencing runs rather than intrinsic differences in extraction efficiency alone. This distinction is critical, because sequencing depth is determined by multiple variables, including the number of pores in the flow cell, sample multiplexing, and pore occupancy.

We evaluated the enzyme-based enrichment method using McrBC and MspJI in combination with sWGA to improve P. falciparum DNA recovery from clinical samples. Both enzymes reduced total human DNA yield, but they also reduced parasite DNA, likely due to shared recognition motifs in human and parasite genomes. However, McrBC achieved superior performance, with over 90% genome coverage at 1000 p/µl, compared to MspJI, which had less than 80% genome coverage. However, the most effective strategy combined NMDEK for human DNA depletion with sWGA for parasite amplification, which offered a highly effective approach for enriching parasite DNA even from clinical samples with a wide range of parasitemia. It resulted in the highest proportion of read mapping to the parasite genome, with consistent chromosome-wide coverage, particularly at relatively high parasite densities, above 1000 p/µl, achieving > 30× mean depth and > 96% coverage across the 14 chromosomes. The combined method resulted in parasite DNA comprising up to 96% of the total DNA in the samples with high parasitemia (10,000 p/µl) and 80% in samples with low parasitemia (100 p/µl), a significant improvement over either method alone. Importantly, this enrichment translated into high sequencing performance.

Our study used an integrated assay, combining NMDEK and sWGA, and it was identified as the most effective approach across sample types. Previous studies have explored alternative methods for parasite DNA enrichment. For example, leukocyte depletion53 is highly efficient but requires freshly collected blood, limiting its practicality in low-resource settings as well as its use in large-scale MMS projects such as those that have been recently launched in Africa29,30,54. Enzyme-based enrichment using MspJI and McrBC has also been investigated43,44, and McrBC was reported to increase the depth of genomic coverage in low-density samples44. Similarly, Oyola et al. reported a four- to seven-fold parasite enrichment using MspJI without sWGA, along with an increase in reads mapping to the parasite genome40. In contrast, Shah et al. observed a reduction in both human and parasite DNA before sWGA when MspJI was used43. A key difference between these studies lies in the starting material: Oyola et al. used whole blood, whereas Shah et al. used DBS. Although McrBc could have served as an alternative, its utility is limited due to its discontinuation by the manufacturer. Dual enrichment provides the greatest advantage at low parasitemia. While dual enrichment entails additional reagents and procedural steps, its benefits are most pronounced at low parasitemia, where host DNA otherwise dominates sequencing reads. In our analyses, single enrichment methods, such as sWGA alone, resulted in the loss of approximately 50–60% of reads to host DNA, whereas dual enrichment reduced discarded reads to ~ 10–20%, substantially improving effective sequencing yield and genome coverage. At higher parasitemia, the added benefit is modest, but for low-parasitemia samples which represent many of infections in sub-Saharan Africa55. Dual enrichment markedly enhances sequencing efficiency, justifying the additional cost and complexity relative to usable data.

In the current study, using ONT WGS without sWGA resulted in median read lengths exceeding 5 kb with laboratory strains, allowing for unique mapping to the P. falciparum reference genome. However, in both mock and clinical samples, the median read length dropped to approximately 2 kb. This reduction was primarily attributed to the sWGA step, which is essential for enriching parasite DNA in whole blood samples containing human DNA42. Unfortunately, sWGA introduces secondary structures that can block the ONT nanopores, requiring enzymatic cleavage to remove these structures, resulting in a shortening of the read lengths. Despite this, the approach still produced reads longer than 1 kb after filtering for short fragments in both mock and clinical samples. Notably, 50% of sequenced reads were around 3 kb, which is substantially longer than those typically obtained through second-generation short-read sequencing. Moreover, our assay yielded not only longer reads but also a higher proportion of reads mapping to the P. falciparum genome and improved coverage of the parasite genome compared to previous studies42,44. For instance, Oyola et al. used sWGA for SGS, while Teyssier et al. integrated McrBC with sWGA and SGS42,44. In contrast, the current approach leveraged long-read sequencing for greater resolution. With further optimization, particularly through improved strategies to remove secondary structures introduced by sWGA and refining enrichment techniques for low-parasite density samples, this assay has the potential to become the most effective method for long-read WGS of malaria parasites using DBS and can significantly support MMS in resource-limited countries.

Despite high coverage and depth for all laboratory strains, the 3D7 strain showed over 99.9% genome coverage with uniform distribution across all chromosomes. In contrast, the HB3 and Dd2 strains had lower coverage (over 96%) but exhibited notable variability across chromosomes, which was potentially and largely due to the use of 3D7 as the reference genome, resulting in the introduction of bias in comparative coverage analyses. The accuracy of long-read sequencing using ONT could be further improved through a de novo assembly approach56,57, and this will need to be further pursued to fully achieve the benefits of this method. For mock and clinical samples, genome coverage and sequencing depth were strongly influenced by parasite density. Samples with high parasite densities (> 10,000 p/µL) gave high DNA concentrations for sequencing, as seen by the coverage, which was above 90% among samples with high parasite densities and coverage below 50% in samples with less than 100 p/µL. Additionally, sequencing depth was uneven, likely due to amplification biases introduced by sWGA primers.

The findings of this study indicate that, as expected, the detection of SNPs and SVs was more accurate in monoclonal samples (pure cultures and mock samples with single strains) than in polyclonal samples. However, even in polyclonal samples, such as mixtures of laboratory strains, a larger proportion of minority clones was still detected. In contrast, SV detection in polyclonal samples depended primarily on sequencing depth. Clinical samples are primarily polyclonal, with studies from different geographical regions reporting high prevalence rates: over 77% of samples with polyclonal infections in Zambia58, 56% in Kenya59, and over 50% in Tanzania60,61. This suggests that SNPs will most likely be detected with high confidence in polyclonal clinical samples, while detection of SV will remain underreported, indicating that more efforts will be needed to improve the detection of these important variants in clinical samples.

Clinical samples showed high concordance between Sanger and ONT WGS for detecting SNPs in pfk13 and pfmdr1. However, in two samples with high parasite densities (82,421 p/µl and 60,000 p/µl), ONT detected the 561 H mutation, which was not detected by Sanger sequencing. This discrepancy likely reflects the presence of minority clones harboring the mutation at frequencies below the detection threshold of Sanger sequencing, yet detectable by ONT due to its higher sequencing depth and sensitivity. Furthermore, ONT could detect certain variants even at low parasite densities (< 1,000 p/µl), as demonstrated in mock samples. This highlights the influence of parasite density on the sensitivity of the ONT WGS protocol and underscores the need for further optimization for low-density samples. Discordant SNPs detection between ONT and Sanger sequencing in low-density samples is likely due to low read coverage and mapping challenges in AT-rich or repetitive regions. While ONT long reads generally provide high sensitivity, regions with sparse coverage may fail to meet variant calling thresholds, leading to false negatives. Evaluating multiple SNP callers can improve confidence, but low coverage remains a key limiting factor in low-parasitemia samples. This underscores the importance of optimizing sequencing depth and read quality when applying ONT WGS to clinical samples with low parasite densities.

Identification of SVs in key drug resistance genes underscores the added value of long-read sequencing technologies for comprehensive genomic surveillance. Duplications in the pfmdr1 gene was observed in three clinical samples but had never been reported in Tanzania, and these are of particular concern, as pfmdr1 amplification has been associated with reduced susceptibility to multiple antimalarial drugs, including mefloquine, lumefantrine, and artemisinin derivatives62,63. Similarly, duplications affecting the gch1 gene, also detected for the first time in three samples, have been linked to increased expression of gch1, which plays a central role in folate biosynthesis and is known to modulate the parasite’s response to antifolate drugs such as sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine. The presence of these duplications in multiple samples, including one (T0944_D21) with co-amplification of both mdr1 and gch1, suggests that structural variations may arise and persist under selection pressure caused by antimalarial drugs. These findings highlight the high potential of long-read sequencing and structural variant calling tools (e.g., Sniffles) to reveal clinically relevant genomic alterations not captured by conventional SNP-focused surveillance approaches. Continued monitoring of SVs in resistance-associated loci is warranted to track emerging patterns of drug resistance and inform treatment policy.

The assay developed here provides a cost-effective approach (~ USD 100 per sample) for generating high-quality whole-genome data from low parasite input, with a high proportion of reads mapping to the Plasmodium genome. It is compatible with multiple Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platforms, from the portable MinION to the higher-throughput GridION and PromethION, allowing flexibility and scalability across laboratories. Estimated per-sample costs reflect consumables and library preparation only, as ONT instruments are typically provided under lease or placement models rather than purchased outright, making reagents the primary cost driver. Once instrumentation is in place, per-sample costs are broadly comparable across platforms. Lower-throughput devices may require longer runs or reduced multiplexing, while higher-throughput devices reduce turnaround time. While ONT WGS offers flexibility and scalability, computational requirements represent an additional consideration for laboratories in resource-limited settings. Data storage per clinical sample ranged from ~ 3–6 gigabytes (GB) for raw reads, with processed files adding ~ 1–2 GB per sample. Basecalling using Dorado accelerated with a graphics processing unit (GPU), and downstream alignment and variant calling relied on scripted workflows without graphical user interface support. Smaller laboratories may use local workstations for small batches, while larger datasets may require high-performance computing clusters, potentially impacting turnaround time and personnel requirements. These computational considerations were not included in per-sample consumable cost estimates (~$100/sample) but should be factored into planning for adoption. Continued development of optimized, lightweight pipelines and GPU-enabled basecalling tools may help reduce the computational burden, improving the feasibility of ONT WGS for malaria genomic surveillance in resource-limited settings.

Given the current cost structure, ONT-based WGS is best positioned as a selective whole-genome sequencing framework, in which a limited proportion of samples with predefined features of interest are sequenced to guide targeted MMS efforts. In addition to MMS, ONT sequencing capabilities for WGS can facilitate studies of other pathogens, including bacteria64,65 and viruses66,67 2020), leading to strengthened surveillance of infectious pathogens, particularly those of high public health importance such as tuberculosis and human immunodeficiency virus, as well as improved detection and response to outbreaks.

While this study focused on P. falciparum samples from Africa, the ONT-based WGS assay and enrichment strategies we optimized are broadly applicable. The combination of human DNA depletion and selective parasite amplification can be adapted for use in other geographic regions, including Asia, and for other Plasmodium species such as P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi. Species-specific adjustments, particularly in primer design for sWGA, may be required to accommodate differences in genome composition and AT content, but the overall workflow including DNA extraction, long-read sequencing, and variant analysis remains largely transferable. This flexibility suggests that the method could support global MMS efforts, enabling comparative studies across regions and species while maintaining high-resolution detection of SNPs, structural variants, and drug-resistance markers.

Limitations and future directions

While the integrated NMDEK and sWGA approach showed strong performance, challenges remain in SV detection in polyclonal infections and very low-parasitemia samples. The sWGA-induced read shortening and amplification biases need further refinement. De novo assembly methods and improved parasite-specific enrichment tools will enhance resolution and reduce mapping bias. Continued innovation is needed to fully harness the potential of ONT with WGS and targeted amplicon sequencing for enhanced MMS for supporting the ongoing elimination strategies in different endemic countries.

Conclusion

WGS with ONT using the combination of NMDEK and sWGA enrichment methods provided a promising protocol with broad applicability to different sample types (whole blood and DBS). This approach yielded a high proportion of reads mapping to the P. falciparum genome, producing high-quality data with improved mapping accuracy, coverage, and length. Additionally, it provided a scalable solution costing about $100 per sample and with a rapid turnaround time of five to six days, from DNA extraction to final results. The protocol workflow demonstrated sensitivity in the detection of both known SNPs and SVs in laboratory strains, though the results may be influenced by factors such as parasite densities and COI. Furthermore, the protocol showed agreement with Sanger sequencing for drug resistance markers such as pfmdr1 and pfk13 while detecting additional mutations relevant to drug resistance. This protocol workflow has great potential for large-scale MMS initiatives in malaria-endemic countries.
