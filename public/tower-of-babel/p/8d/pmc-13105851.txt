Development of a pediatric immune cell atlas and characterization of CD4+ T cells in food allergy
Authors: Natalie M Vance, Kirsi M Järvinen, Juilee Thakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe immune system undergoes maturation throughout early life, shaped partly by environmental exposures. As a result, pediatric immune responses can be implicated in many diseases including food allergy (FA). This study leveraged publicly available single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from eight clinical studies analyzing peripheral blood mononuclear cells to develop a pediatric single‐cell reference atlas from 57 individuals. The atlas was used to investigate cellular heterogeneity between healthy children, characterize CD4+ T cells, and CD4+ T cell sub‐type specific responses associated with age and allergy. Seven CD4+ T cell subclusters were identified and annotated, a unique subset of cytotoxic Th2‐like cells was enriched for both peanut‐ and egg‐allergic gene signatures. Cluster‐specific age‐associated signatures were identified using multi‐variate regression models revealing enrichment of metabolic pathways during early‐life. FA gene signatures were identified using bulk and scRNA‐seq data and were compared across studies using the developed reference. Finally, Random Forest models were developed to investigate the predictive role of innate immune cells in CD4+ T cell regulation revealing TLR/NOD pathway activities in monocytes associated with gut‐ and skin‐homing Th2 cells. Thus, this study provides a robust single‐cell reference atlas of healthy children for pediatric research and further demonstrates three applications of the atlas through cellular mapping and identification of gene signatures in the context of FA.Keywords: cytotoxic Th2‐like cells, pediatric food allergy, random forest modeling, single‐cell pediatric reference atlas

Key message

This study presents a single‐cell pediatric reference atlas of healthy children throughout early life (birth to 16 years) using single‐cell RNA‐sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data collected from blood samples. This reference atlas was further subdivided to investigate CD4+ T cells specifically, as these cell subsets regulate immune responses. This study shows three applications of the CD4+ T cell reference. First, the reference is used to find age‐associated gene signatures which are primarily identified in naïve and T regulatory cells. Second, this study identified a unique cell subset, cytotoxic Th2‐like cells, that are enriched for genes highly expressed in children with peanut or egg allergy, suggesting a potential role for these cells in directing food allergies. Third, this study investigates the role of monocytes in regulating gut‐ and skin‐homing capacity of CD4+ T cells. To discern the importance of cytotoxic Th2‐like cells in the development of allergic disease, this cell population should be further validated and investigated experimentally.

INTRODUCTION

The immune system is constantly developing after birth and throughout early life. Numerous factors influence the trajectory of one's immune development such as the age of introduction of food, vaccination, exposure to pathogens, and microbial interactions within one's environment. 1 Consequently, immune development determines the response in multiple diseases, including allergic disease. 2 Globally, the incidence of pediatric allergy has risen, most notably in westernized countries, where it is estimated one in five children have an allergic disease. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Sensitization to food allergens often occurs early in life, when the immune system is rapidly developing. 7 , 8 , 9 Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) can capture both innate and adaptive immune cells in circulation, providing an opportunity to study cellular heterogeneity and interactions. However, pediatric research is often limited by difficulty in obtaining adequate sample sizes as well as restrictions in collecting ample sample volume, particularly blood. 10 To facilitate pediatric research it is critical to establish a healthy immune reference across multiple studies. Here, a comprehensive meta‐analysis of publicly available data from scRNA‐seq of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected from healthy children aged 0 to 16 years is presented. Furthermore, CD4+ T cell types are analyzed in depth because they play critical regulatory roles in immune system development.

The reference developed here can be used as a control in pediatric studies or to investigate the heterogeneity across a healthy pediatric cohort. A potential application is to investigate food allergies. Since the reference is developed with participants from urban populations, one can expect the elicitation of immune mechanisms underlying allergic disease. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Indeed, several studies have shown a protective farm life effect driven by regulation of CD4+ T cell subpopulations and innate immune cell sensing. 17 , 18 In this study gene signatures of CD4+ T cells from food‐allergic children at the bulk and single‐cell levels are mapped to the pediatric reference atlas. Additionally, given recent work that indicates innate immune training and regulation by monocytes as important contributors to the development of allergic diseases, 19 , 20 , 21 this study investigated the potential regulatory role of monocytes in CD4+ T cell composition with a focus on gut‐ and skin‐homing sub‐populations as these are the main routes of food allergen exposure and sensitization. 7 , 8 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Overall, this study provides a robust, comprehensive single‐cell pediatric reference of PBMCs from a healthy cohort of children across multiple clinical studies that has broad applicability for future pediatric research.

METHODS

Data curation

The pediatric reference was developed using publicly available scRNA‐seq data published in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Exact search terms and settings used were: (((“Homo sapiens”[Organism] AND “expression profiling by high throughput sequencing”[DataSet Type]) AND ((“pediatrics”[MeSH Terms] OR pediatric[All Fields]) OR (“child”[MeSH Terms] OR child[All Fields]))) AND (healthy[All Fields] OR (“control groups”[MeSH Terms] OR control[All Fields]))) AND 10X [All Fields]. Studies that contained scRNA‐seq data of PBMCs processed with 10X Genomics from individuals aged 0 to 16 years recruited as control subjects were included. Studies were excluded if subjects were not clearly identified as healthy controls, were over 16 years of age, or lacked sequencing data of PBMCs. Data pre‐processing

Raw count matrices were imported into R 34 (version 4.2.1) and pre‐processed with Seurat 35 (version 5.1.0). Seurat objects per sample were merged, log10 normalized, and scaled. Cells with 250–5000 features and less than 15% expression of mitochondrial genes were retained, ensuring doublets and dead or dying cells were filtered out. Following these quality control metrics, the pediatric reference contained 347,172 cells from 57 pediatric individuals across eight studies. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

Imputation of biological sex

Biological sex was imputed and/or assessed based on the expression of non‐homologous Y genes. 26 non‐homologous Y genes were expressed in the pediatric reference; of these, four highly expressed genes (DDX3Y, RPS4Y1, EIF1AY, and SRY) were selected for imputation (see Appendix S1).

Integration and clustering of scRNA‐seq data

To correct for technical variation between studies, integration of scRNA‐seq data was performed using Harmony. 36 Integration quality was assessed using both cluster and integration Local Inverse Simpson's Index (cLISI and iLISI) scores. 36 Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for dimensionality reduction and the first 15 principal components (PCs) were used for cell clustering. Cells were clustered at increasing resolutions from 0.1 to 1 and cellular distribution between clusters at varying resolutions was assessed using the Clustree package. 37 A final resolution of 0.1 was applied to avoid overclustering of the data and resulted in 12 clusters. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat 35 and were used alongside canonical markers and the automated annotation method scType 38 to annotate clusters in the PBMC pediatric reference (Table S1).

Isolation of T cells

Naïve and memory CD3+ T cell clusters were selected from the pediatric reference, log10 normalized, and scaled. Reciprocal PCA (RPCA) was applied to integrate the data and integration quality was assessed using cLISI and iLISI scores using the lisi 36 package in R. PCA was applied for dimensionality reduction and the first 30 PCs were used for cell clustering. Cells were again clustered at increasing resolutions from 0.1 to 1 and resolution was evaluated using Clustree. 37 A final resolution of 0.25 was applied, resulting in ten distinct clusters. scType, DEGs (average log2FC >1 and pct.1 >0.5), and canonical T cell markers were used for cluster annotation (Tables S2 and S3). Two clusters expressing high levels of CD8A and CD8B were identified as CD8+ T cells and excluded from downstream analysis. A separate cluster was identified as platelets by scType and was also removed, resulting in seven CD4+ T cell clusters containing 132,221 total cells from 57 pediatric individuals.

Identifying differentially expressed genes across age

Individuals were grouped by age as follows: early life (0–2 years), young child (2–5 years), child (6–11 years), and adolescent (12–16 years). To identify differentially expressed genes across age in CD4+ T cell clusters, scRNA‐seq data was normalized and scaled on all genes before pseudobulk analysis whereby counts were summed per subject within each cluster. Housekeeping genes were removed, and linear regression was performed to identify genes associated with age group upon controlling for study and biological sex. Pairwise comparisons for all age groups were calculated and adjusted with Tukey's HSD test; age‐associated genes were selected (adjusted p‐value <.1) for pathway analysis. Over‐representation analysis (ORA) was performed using clusterProfiler 39 with T cell immune‐related and metabolic Reactome pathways. 40

Food allergy gene set curation

Two publicly available bulk RNA‐sequencing datasets of food‐allergic children were obtained from the NCBI GEO. Bajzik et al. (GSE196495) contains data for memory effector T cells sorted from PBMCs collected from ten peanut‐allergic individuals aged 10 to 12 years. 41 Martino et al. (GSE114065) contains data for naïve CD4+ T cells sorted from PBMCs collected from 21 egg‐allergic individuals aged 11 months to 4 years. 42 Gene sets specific to peanut and egg allergies were derived from each of these studies individually. The egg‐specific food allergy (FA) gene signature was derived by selecting the top 200 upregulated DEGs between the 21 egg‐allergic individuals and 44 non‐allergic, age‐matched controls; DEGs were identified by the authors using the limma package in R. 42 , 43 Bajzik et al. lacks non‐allergic, age‐matched control samples; as such, differential expression analysis could not be performed for these samples. To derive the peanut‐specific FA gene signature, PCA was performed on the normalized count data and the top two PCs which explained 50% variance were selected. 200 genes were selected, proportional to the variance explained, resulting in roughly 160 genes from PC1 (43% variance) and 40 genes from PC2 (10% variance). Peanut and egg allergy signatures were functionally characterized via pathway analysis using the Reactome database. 40 To assess enrichment of these two FA‐specific gene signatures in the CD4+ T cell reference, the single‐cell pathway score (scPS) method was applied. 44 scPS scores for each gene signature were divided into quartiles with Q1 containing the lowest scores and Q4 containing the highest scores. Additional deconvolution with full bulk RNA‐seq gene signatures from egg‐ and peanut‐allergic individuals was performed with CIBERSORT, 45 using the CD4+ T cell reference as the signature matrix (see Appendix S1).

Mapping CD4+ T cells from food‐allergic individuals to the reference

Two scRNA‐seq datasets were mapped to the CD4+ T cell reference. Monian et al. (GSE158667) was obtained from the NCBI GEO and contains data for memory CD4+ T cells sorted from PBMCs collected from six peanut‐allergic individuals aged 8–16 years. 46 Pizzarello et al. was obtained from our prior study and contains data for CD4 + CRTH2+ T cells sorted from PBMCs collected from two egg‐allergic individuals aged 1 year. 47 (See Appendix S1 for details on cell sorting procedures). Single cells from each of the FA scRNA‐seq datasets were mapped to the CD4+ T cell reference using the FindTransferAnchors and MapQuery functions within Seurat. 35

Random Forest modeling of monocyte and CD4+ T cell associations

Random Forest regression models were built using the randomForest package 48 (version 4.7–1.2) in R whereby monocyte gene set scores, calculated using scPS, 44 were used to predict the average gene expression of homing markers in gut‐ and skin‐homing cells in each CD4+ T cell cluster. Cells co‐expressing ITGA4 and ITGB7 or CCR9 were classified as gut‐homing 7 , 8 , 22 and cells expressing CCR4, CCR10, or SELPLG were classified as skin‐homing. 23 , 24 , 25 Leave one out cross validation was applied for each cluster whereby the Random Forest model was trained on data from 56 of the 57 subjects and tested on the data from the subject that was left out. This process was repeated for all subjects and model performance was evaluated as an average of all iterations, providing an unbiased approach to model validation. Importance scores were calculated and ranked for each monocyte pathway during each validation fold, and average rank was assessed across clusters. Tuning of parameters ntree and mtry was performed using Grid Search resulting in ntree = 500 and mtry = 9 for the gut‐homing models and ntree = 500 and mtry = 5 for the skin‐homing models.

RESULTS

Development of a pediatric PBMC reference atlas

A comprehensive, pediatric scRNA‐seq PBMC reference was curated using healthy subjects from eight publicly available studies (Table 1). Samples were classified by the age groups neonate, infant, young child, child, or adolescent (Figure 1A). For samples where biological sex was not reported, sex was imputed using the proportion of cells expressing genes specific to the Y chromosome. This method revealed all samples from one study (GSE214865) had misreported biological sex in the NCBI GEO (Figure S1A). For downstream analysis, imputed biological sex was used (Figure S1B). A total of 347,172 cells from 57 individuals were integrated and clustered, resulting in 12 clusters that were annotated using a combination of the automated annotation method scType and manual annotation with canonical cell markers and DEGs, identifying major immune populations including T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells (Figure 1B,C, Figures S2 and S3A). Integration quality was assessed using cLISI and iLISI scores. The mean and median cLISI scores for the 12 PBMC clusters were 1.048 and 1.000, respectively, indicating the cells within each cluster share similar expression patterns and are the same cell type. Mean (2.489) and median (2.469) iLISI scores for the PBMC clusters indicate that cells from the eight different studies are well integrated while still preserving biological variability (Figure 1D,E). Additionally, cells from all studies were represented in the major cluster, indicating successful integration of the data (Figure S4). The resulting reference represents the major circulating immune cells in a cohort of healthy children from birth through adolescence.

Furthermore, to investigate CD4+ T cell heterogeneity, CD3+ T cells were isolated from the PBMC reference and clustered in ten distinct clusters. Clusters 1 and 3 contained CD8+ T cells and were removed for downstream analysis (Figure S5A,B and Appendix S1). Additionally, cluster 7 was identified as a platelet cluster and was removed (Figure S5A,B and Appendix S1). RPCA integration ensured CD4+ T cells were integrated across study, age, and imputed biological sex (Figure S6A–C). cLISI mean (1.0464) and median (1.000) scores for the CD4+ T cell reference demonstrate strong clustering of cell types whereas mean (2.649) and median (2.601) iLISI scores demonstrate good integration of CD4+ T cells across the eight different studies (Figure 2D,E). CD4+ T cell clusters were annotated as described in the methods, identifying traditional CD4+ T cell subtypes (Th1, Th2, Treg, etc.) as well as a small, unique cluster of cytotoxic Th2‐like cells defined by expression of GATA3, PRF1, and GZMB (Figure 2A,C and Figure S3B). All CD4+ T cell subclusters were present across age groups with naïve CD4+ T cells representing the greatest proportion of cells in each age group (Figure 2B). As expected, the young child, child, and adolescent groups had higher proportions of differentiated CD4+ T cells compared to the neonates and infants, particularly Th2 and Th17‐like cells (p < .001, chi‐squared test) (Figure 2B). Taken together, scRNA‐seq data of PBMCs was integrated across eight studies with seven CD4+ T cell subclusters identified for downstream analysis.

Age‐associated CD4+ T cell gene signatures

To explore the functional capacity of CD4+ T cells across age, DEGs were identified within each cluster between four age groups. After filtering out study‐ and sex‐associated genes (Figure S7A–G), age‐associated gene sets were identified within each cluster, with the early life age group showing the largest signature across clusters. The top age‐associated genes for the naïve CD4+ T cell and Treg clusters were chosen for pathway analysis as these displayed the most prominent age‐specific responses (Figure 3A,B). Interestingly, pathways enriched in early life tend to only be enriched within that age group whereas pathways enriched in the young child, child, and adolescent groups are enriched in at least two of the later ages (Figure 3C,D). This suggests a shift in immune function after the first 2 years of life to more sustained immune signatures throughout childhood, consistent with what has been shown in the literature. 49 , 50 The enriched pathways across older age groups in naïve CD4+ T cell and Treg clusters reflect a more generalized immune response (chemokine binding, immunoregulatory interactions, interleukin‐10 signaling) whereas early life naïve CD4+ T cells are enriched for more metabolic pathways like glucose metabolism (Figure 3C). In addition to glucose metabolism pathways, early life Tregs show enrichment of a variety of immune mediated pathways including several related to IL‐12 family signaling (Figure 3D). Together, these results support a shift in immune signatures after the first 2 years of life.

Monocyte functional pathways are associated with homing properties of CD4+ T cells

Recent work has found evidence that innate immune training and regulation by monocytes may play a key role in development of allergic disease. 19 , 20 , 21 The gut and skin are two important sites of allergen sensitization and cellular homing to these sites is well characterized by expression of ITGA4, ITGB7, and CCR9 for the gut, and CCR4, CCR10, and CLA for the skin. 7 , 8 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Among the CD4+ T cell clusters, the Th2 and cytotoxic Th2‐like clusters exhibited the greatest relative proportions of both gut‐ and skin‐homing cells (Figure 4C,D). To further analyze monocyte mediated regulation of CD4+ T cells, Random Forest models were built to predict the homing features of each of the CD4+ T cell clusters using monocyte functional pathway scores (Figure 4A,B, Figure S8A,B, Table S4). The monocyte functional pathways better predicted expression of homing markers in naïve CD4+ T cells, Th2 cells, Th17‐like cells, cytotoxic Th2‐like cells, and effector/memory CD4+ T cells (R 2 > .5). Gut‐homing potential of CD4+ T cell subtypes was most highly associated with TLR signaling cascades, particularly TLR 3 and NOD signaling (Figure 4C). Notably, CD16hi monocyte pathways had higher importance scores when assessing gut‐homing expression among Th2 cells compared to other CD4+ T cell clusters, suggesting an association of non‐classical CD16hi monocytes with Th2 cell homing to the gut (Figure 4C). Skin‐homing potential of CD4+ T cell subtypes was also highly associated with TLR and NOD signaling pathways, as well as Rap1 signaling (Figure 4D). In contrast to gut‐homing, CD16hi monocytes seem to have little association with skin‐homing of Th2 cells (Figure 4D). Additionally, the complement cascade is associated with Th2 and naïve cells whereas co‐stimulation by the CD28 family molecules is associated with skin‐homing of cytotoxic Th2‐like cells alone (Figure 4D). Taken together, several associations between monocyte functional pathways and gut‐ or skin‐homing CD4+ T cells emerged, suggesting that monocyte capacity to regulate gut‐ and skin‐homing properties of CD4+ T cells is dependent on monocyte cell type and functionality.

Food allergy‐specific CD4 response signatures are enriched in cytotoxic Th2‐like cells

To explore the role of these CD4+ T cells in FA, enrichment of egg and peanut allergy gene signatures was assessed among the clusters (Tables S5 and S6). Both the egg and peanut allergy signatures derived from bulk RNA‐seq studies were enriched in the cytotoxic Th2‐like cluster (Figure 5A,B and Figure S9A,B). To investigate this further, single cells from egg‐ and peanut‐allergic individuals (Table S7) were mapped to the CD4+ T cell reference. Memory CD4+ T cells from peanut‐allergic individuals (age 8–16 years, n = 6) mapped predominantly to Th2 cells whereas CD4 + CRTH2+ T cells from egg‐allergic individuals (age 1 year, n = 2) mapped mostly to the naïve CD4+ T cell cluster (Figure 5C–F). While the sample size of food allergic children is limited, a small fraction of cells from both peanut‐ and egg‐allergic individuals map to the cytotoxic Th2‐like cluster implicating their presence across multiple food allergies and across ages.

Given the number of cells from food‐allergic children that mapped to the naïve cluster, this study sought to investigate these cells further. Previous work has identified Th2 cell subsets, Th2A and Th2B cells, among allergic individuals, specifically young children with FA. 47 , 52 To investigate whether naïve CD4+ T cells could be Th2 precursors, markers of Th2, Th2A, and Th2B cells were assessed for expression in the naïve cluster. Indeed, among naïve CD4+ T cells in both FA studies, there were subsets of cells that express the transcription factor GATA3 as well as the IL‐4 receptor, suggesting these naïve cells might be poised to differentiate to Th2 cells (Figure S10A–C).

DISCUSSION

This study developed a comprehensive pediatric single‐cell reference atlas of PBMCs by performing meta‐analysis of eight clinical studies. The atlas is used to investigate cellular heterogeneity among healthy and food allergic children. This work shows that CD4+ T cell clusters exhibit age‐specific gene signatures throughout early life and suggests that certain monocyte pathways are associated with gut‐ and skin‐homing features of CD4+ T cells. Additionally, a unique cytotoxic Th2‐like cluster of cells was identified and evaluated in the context of peanut and egg food allergies. The identification of cytotoxic Th2‐like cells is intriguing as to their role in FA. While cytotoxicity is a trait typically attributed to CD8+ T cells, cytotoxic CD4+ T cells have been described in the context of chronic viral infection, autoimmune disease, and cancer. 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 The literature describes several markers that have been used to describe cytotoxic CD4+ T cells, but have consistently found co‐expression of perforin and granzyme B among these cells, suggesting these to be hallmark features of cytotoxic CD4+ T cells. 62 This aligns with the cytotoxic Th2‐like cluster described here. Future work is required to investigate the role of cytotoxic CD4+ T cells in pediatric immune development and allergic disease.

The pediatric reference atlas, developed using publicly available data, has far‐reaching utility as a control dataset in pediatric research, given the protected status of children as a vulnerable population 10 and the difficulties surrounding blood collection from minors. This study presents three applications of the CD4+ T cell reference. First, the age‐associated gene signatures of CD4+ T cell clusters align with what is known about immune development throughout early life; as children age and are exposed to a variety of environmental antigens, their immune systems begin establishing compartments of differentiated and memory cells. 49 , 50 Second, mapping of food allergy bulk and scRNA‐seq data reveals cell‐type specific differences using the robust cross‐study reference. This is particularly critical to compare different scRNA‐seq studies that have smaller sample sizes. The findings are interesting given the sorting strategy and the known Th2‐bias in early life that would suggest cells from the young egg‐allergic children would map to the Th2 cluster, like the older peanut‐allergic children do. 49 , 50 Further probing of cells mapping to the naïve CD4+ T cell cluster suggests that among the naïve compartment of CD4+ T cells in food‐allergic children there are subsets of cells which are poised to differentiate to a Th2 phenotype such as Th2A 52 and Th2B44 cells, which are implicated in allergic disease. Third, when assessing the potential role of monocytes in homing of CD4+ T cells, CD14loCD16hi monocytes stand out as predictors of gut‐homing Th2 cells. A previous study found that CD14loCD16hi monocytes can differentiate to CD103 + RALDH2 + TCF4+ dendritic cells capable of producing retinoic acid, a key component of imprinting CD4+ T cells with gut‐homing potential. 63

In conclusion, this study leveraged publicly available data to develop a single‐cell pediatric PBMC reference atlas with broad applicability and has demonstrated its use in three applications.
