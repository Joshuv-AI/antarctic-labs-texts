Three methods for fair ranking of multiple protected items
Authors: Massimo Melucci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Three approaches to fair ranking in retrieval systems are compared in this paper: mPFR, which is based on the theory of preferences and eigensystems; cRR, which is a simple‘ ’round robin” method; and mMLP, which is based on linear programming. In order to increase fairness without sacrificing retrieval effectiveness, the techniques post-process the rankings that a retrieval system sends back to users. The findings demonstrate that when it comes to protecting elements, mPFR and cRR accomplish the same level of effectiveness and fairness. Despite being computationally more costly than the latter, the former’s mathematical architecture enables the ranking of reordering techniques at various levels of complexity, while mMLP might not be practical for datasets that are too big. Therefore, the choice between these methods often hinges on the specific use case and dataset size, where trade-offs between computational efficiency and desired fairness come into play. Future research could explore optimizing these techniques further to enhance their applicability across diverse scenarios, ensuring that both fairness and effectiveness are maintained.

Introduction

Users access information items such as scientific papers and webpages by means of queries sent to and processed by retrieval or recommender systems. Then, the systems send the retrieved item rankings back to the users by non-increasing measure of relevance of the items to the users’ information needs to maximize retrieval effectiveness.

However, fairness should at the same time be guaranteed, i.e., the providers of the retrieved items should be exposed to the users without being penalized because of their membership in minority groups. Without such protection, for example, the scientific papers authored by researchers affiliated with lesser-known organizations might be penalized because of insufficient bibliographic indexes. As a consequence, another ranking based on the membership of protected groups exists to make protected items more easily accessible than unprotected items, thus leading to an apparent counterposition of fairness to effectiveness.

Usually, research on fair information access focuses on one protected group and then on one unprotected group, e.g., “female” and “male,” thus providing experimental results regarding binary settings; for example, the authors of academic papers may be associated with organizations of either developed or developing countries. One complication derives from multiple protected items. This case may be due to the membership of items in two or more protected groups of one or more protections. In this case, the item providers may be grouped according to a criterion such as gender, and there might be items referring to two or more minority groups such as “female” and “non-binary.” Similarly, the item providers may be grouped according to two or more criteria, such as gender and geographic location, and there might be items referring to two or more minority groups, such as “female” and “developing countries.”

Starting with the observation that a ranking is the result of a series of preferences based on usefulness, relevance, and protection criteria, this paper describes preference eigensystems for fair ranking when a criterion has multiple protected groups. The paper focuses on fair Information Retrieval (IR), so the items are documents. Effectiveness refers to a system’s ability to retrieve only relevant documents, while fairness refers to the groups of document authors.

We introduce the Preference Fair Ranking with Multiple Protected Groups (mPFR) method, which is based on pairwise preferences. The method involves computing the principal eigenvector of a matrix, which is defined as a linear combination of two or more matrices. In the case of two major requirements, such as retrieval effectiveness and fairness, one represents the preferences for utility or relevance, while the other represents the preferences for item security. A series of experiments compared the retrieval effectiveness and fairness of the rankings provided by the mPFR with that of two well-known algorithms for fair rankings. The results demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method. To this aim, the paper illustrates the method by using matrices and eigensystems thereof and reports on the comparison of three methods for fair ranking:Preference fair ranking with multiple protected groups (mPFR),Mixed linear program for multiple protected groups (mMLP), andCorrected round Robin (cRR).The methods post-processed the rankings sent by a retrieval system back to the users. Post-processing consisted of item reranking by using a combination of relevance and fairness. In this paper, all three methods have been extending the corresponding versions that were designed for binary settings and were reported in the relevant literature. The experiments that have been carried out with large public test collections showed that the three methods are effective in improving fairness without compromising retrieval effectiveness. mPFR and cRR are superior to mMLP. Both methods achieve the same degree of fairness and effectiveness when prioritizing protected elements. mPFR is computationally more expensive than cRR, but its mathematical architecture allows for ranking reordering methods at different complexity levels.

Related work

The literature on fair information access discusses the duality between users and providers in information item production, retrieval, and ranking. Users need information, while providers need exposure equal to other providers. A survey on information access is provided in1. Usually, fair provider exposure is often excluded by retrieval and recommender systems; for example, gender and economic development are often ignored in ranking systems, whereas only the user’s information needs satisfaction is searched. In very recent years, the contributions to the literature have been less intense than in the past. Consequently, we mention works mostly published in the late 10s and early 20s. Recent interest in fairness in retrieval and recommender systems has been fueled by similar interest in other areas of information access. Numerous scientific papers have been published on this topic, with the visibility of goods and services becoming more acute in recommender systems. Ranking in IR can hinder the simultaneous maximization of retrieval effectiveness and fairness. The third edition of SWIRL highlighted the need to consider fairness issues, with many considerations found in its technical report. Baeza-Yates’ article and conferences like FAccT focused on fairness and related issues. Recent research attempts aim to take stock of the research results obtained so far.2–4

There are two types of fairness, as explained in5: group fairness and individual fairness, which influence ranking effectiveness. Individual fairness is achieved when similar individuals are treated equally, while maximal group fairness occurs when the items belong to the same groups. As with the distinction between classification and clustering, an important distinction between group fairness and individual fairness is that in the former, group labels are predefined and items are labeled a priori, whereas in the latter, clusters of similar items are constructed unsupervisedly and items are added to them based on distance functions.

This work discusses group fairness, which ensures equal visibility for different author groups. Without group fairness, minority authors may be disadvantaged, while majority group documents may be promoted higher. The other types of fairness were addressed in6–8, for example. Another relevant work is described in9 and10, yet it is only partially relevant to this paper, since it focuses on individual fairness, whereas this paper is focused on group fairness. The interaction between group fairness, individual fairness, and retrieval effectiveness was investigated in11. The methodology to study that interaction consisted of an operator whose matrix represents the difference of two operators—an operator that represents relevance and one that represents fairness or its counterpart called “bias”. The method described in11 aims to maximize the quadratic form of that difference operator. However, the definition of the matrices was outside the main scope. In this paper we utilize pairwise preferences to implement them.

Methods for fair ranking can be classified into heuristic or greedy approaches and mathematical optimization-based approaches like the linear program for fairness of exposure in rankings. The search for fair information access has often been based on optimization, which involves a function to be maximized or minimized and one or more constraints, all expressed mathematically as linear equations; in this regard, see7,8,12–20. In other works, emphasis has been given to the measurement of errors or deviations and related adjustments from what is considered fair, as, for example, done in21–28.

Background

FA*IR

The FA*IR algorithm has been one of the earliest methods for fair ranking. It first splits the ranking into one ranking of protected items (p) and another ranking of unprotected items (q). Then, the method fills a new ranking u by taking one item from either p or q at a time, i.e., in a “round robin” way. Finally, FA*IR adds the item from p to u if the expected number of protected items at the next rank of u is not less than the number of protected items already added to u. If the latter is not less than the former, the most likely relevant, but not yet added item is added to u. The expected number of protected items up to each rank is the main evidence to obtain the group fairness of a ranking. FA*IR compares the number of protected items up to every rank with the expected number of protected items if they were picked at random according to Bernoulli variables. The details are in8.

As the original FA*IR deals with binary criteria, a multi-protected group version of FA*IR was reported in7. It is based on a data structure called “multinomial tree,” i.e., a tree of which a node for each rank stores the distribution of frequency of items expected for every protected group; therefore, a multinomial tree is an m-ary tree where m is the number of groups. Moreover, the method is based on the calculation of the inverse multinomial function to decide whether a ranking is fair and fill the nodes of a multinomial tree.

Unfortunately, the multi-protected group version of FA*IR is impractical for the common sizes of IR, as those considered in this work. First, the complexity of the calculation of the inverse multinomial function is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^m)$$\end{document}, where n is the length of a ranking. Second, the complexity of the calculation of a multinomial tree is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(m^n)$$\end{document}. The experiments that are reported in “Experiments” considered dozens of groups and up to one hundred items per ranking. In general, the overall complexity of the multi-protected group version of FA*IR makes its implementation and experimentation unfeasible, since the number of groups may be in O(10) and the number of items in a ranking may be in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(10^2)$$\end{document}.

Mixed linear program for multiple protected groups (mMLP)

A representative work in optimization is the linear program for fairness of exposure in rankings18, which maximizes the utility of a ranking under fairness constraints; we call it mMLP. A similar, independently published work was also proposed in12 using integer linear programming with some heuristics to deal with computational issues due to the large number of constraints and variables. We omit to report it since the approach is of the same kind as the one described in18.

With mMLP, the utility function is a bilinear form on the real vector space, yet the role of the independent variable is played by the matrix of the bilinear form. As a consequence, the utility function has \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n^2$$\end{document} independent variables and 2n parameters given by the utility vector and the attention model. Attention refers to the distribution of probability to a group.

As the matrix of the bilinear form has to be a doubly stochastic matrix, there are 2n constraints such that both the sum of the row elements and the sum of the column elements have to be all one. Moreover, there are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n^2$$\end{document} constraints stating that each element of the doubly stochastic matrix has to be non-negative and not greater than one. Finally, fairness is expressed by other constraints in terms of bilinear forms.

In mathematical terms, mMLP can be summarized as follows: Let n be the number of items as well as the number of ranks. The mapping between items and ranks is a bijection. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i,j}$$\end{document} be the probability that i is mapped to j. As the mapping is a bijection, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \times n$$\end{document} matrix is doubly stochastic, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{i=1}^n p_{i,j} = \sum _{j=1}^n p_{i,j} = 1\ .$$\end{document} Moreover, the attention model x and the utility vector u are given; therefore, the expected utility \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u'Px$$\end{document} is the target function of which the maximal point, which is a double stochastic matrix, is searched. As the problem is expressed as a linear program, the fairness constraint is expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f'Pg= h$$\end{document}. The vector g represents the importance of each position, e.g., position bias or attention model x. The vector f is a vector that encodes the degree of membership of an item to one group with respect to the membership to the other groups.

The fairness constraint could be of three types: Disparate Impact (DI), Demographic Parity (DP), or Disparate Treatment (DT). When the relevance distributions of the two groups are significantly different, DP may result in a significant loss of utility, but the ranking of the items has no bearing on fairness. DI mandates that each group’s exposure be commensurate with their average utility. According to DP, each group’s projected clickthrough rate is directly correlated with its average utility. The clickthrough rate indicates how valuable or relevant an item is in the eyes of the user.

Preference fair ranking (PFR)

The single protected group-based version of PFR was introduced in29. In this section, a summary is reported. PFR can be used to compare n items based on two criteria, such as relevance and protected group membership. Consider a set of n items scored according to both criteria. The degree of preference is computed for each pair of items and for each criterion. PFR combines the preferences with respect to a criterion in a matrix. Given a criterion, each matrix entry is the degree of preference for one item over another item according to the criterion. In cases of two criteria, such as relevance and group membership, two matrices will linearly be combined into one matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q \in \mathbb {R}^{n \times n}$$\end{document}.

Using PFR, an observer starts from an item at random and ends at another item with a probability depending on the degree of preference. The Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees that the unique, positive eigenvector x of Q with eigenvalue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} exists, ensuring the maximum total measure of preference of items over items.30

Preference fair ranking with multiple protected groups

In this section, Preference Fair Ranking with Multiple Protected Groups (mPFR) is explained in three parts: first, the method is introduced in “Method”; then, the case of two items and two groups is analyzed in “The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 2$$\end{document} case”; finally, the Pareto optimality is discussed in “Pareto optimality”.

Method

Each criterion determines a certain number of groups, such as the groups of relevant items and that of non-relevant items as for relevance. As for gender, the items can be included in the male group, female group, or the non-binary gender group. The protected groups are usually meant to refer to one criterion; for example, if the criterion is gender, the protected groups are female and non-binary, as addressed by Preference Fair Ranking (PFR).

In general, the protected groups may refer to more than one criterion. Therefore, Q should aggregate different preference criteria and multiple protected groups for each criterion. In this way, three or more preference matrices are provided, and the preference matrix Q is the sum thereof. In this paper, we consider an extension of PFR, called mPFR, such that the items should be ranked by relevance and by degree of membership to two or more protected groups of different criteria.

Consider two criteria, e.g. relevance and some other criteria, thus yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m-p$$\end{document} unprotected groups and p protected groups. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{i,j}$$\end{document} measures the extent to which i is more useful than j and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{i,j}(k)$$\end{document} similarly measures the extent to which i is more protected than j according to the k-th criterion.

The matrices A, B(1), ..., B(p) may be obtained as follows for each query. A query is given as input to a retrieval system, which in turn returns a list of documents ranked by decreasing utility score. The document scores and the group membership scores of the retrieved documents are converted to ranks where n is given to the top-ranked document and 1 to the bottom-ranked document. The ties are replaced with the arithmetic mean, e.g., 2, 1, 2, 4 became 2.5, 1, 2.5, 4. Pareto optimality

The main eigenvector of Q is the preference distribution over the n items induced by a hypothetical random browser that moves from one item to another according to the degree of preferences with respect to utility or relevance as measured by a and group protection as measured by b. The main eigenvector of Q induces the best ranking, i.e., the most preferred item should be ranked first and so on. Suppose each item i provides some utility \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_i$$\end{document} to the user and some protection \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_i$$\end{document} to the item’s groups. The expected utility is thus\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u'v = u_1v_1+\cdots +u_nv_n \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and the expected group protection is thus\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} t'v = t_1v_1+\cdots +t_nv_n. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Suppose \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w \in [0,1]^n$$\end{document} is an alternative preference distribution over the n items that induces an alternative ranking. The main eigenvector of Q provides the Pareto optimality if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u'v> u'w \wedge t'v \ge t'w) \vee (u'v \ge u'w \wedge t'v > t'w)$$\end{document}. In the event of Information Retrieval (IR), u is the array of the user’s judgments on item relevance, whereas t is the array of assessments of protection of the item’s group. In such a context, the Pareto optimality can hardly be obtained because ofthe context-sensitiveness of relevance, andthe intrinsic, potential incompatibility between relevance and protection.The context-sensitiveness of relevance depends on the user and his/her own information needs. To a certain extent, protection assessment might also be context dependent; for example, an ethnic group may be perceived as a minority in a certain age and will not in a future age. The context-sensitiveness of relevance might be estimated by using data sources that may reflect context to some extent; for example, click-through data can be used to this end. Even if relevance or protection were less “dynamic” than they are, the intrinsic, potential incompatibility between relevance and protection would arise.

Because of the intrinsic, potential incompatibility between relevance and protection, a ranking induced by v can be better than that induced by w on the basis of relevance judgments, and at the same time it can be worse on the basis of protection assessments. In this paper, the approach to minimize the incompatibility between relevance and protection and the consequent trade-off between the measures of effectiveness and fairness is both of experimental (“Experiments”) and methodological nature (this section).

Conclusions

Based on the experimental results performed with the 2021 and 2022 Text Retrieval Conference (TREC) Fair track datasets, it can be stated that Preference Fair Ranking with Multiple Protected Groups (mPFR), Corrected Round Robin (cRR), and Mixed Linear Program for Multiple Protected Groups (mMLP) are effective in improving fairness without compromising retrieval effectiveness. Since the three methods tested in this work are believed to be representative of the class of methods available, the experimental results suggest that using one of these methods is a cost-effective strategy to improve the fairness of rankings.

In particular, Preference Fair Ranking with Multiple Protected Groups (mPFR) and Corrected Round Robin (cRR) have been shown to be superior in terms of fairness to Multiple Protected Groups (mMLP) both in terms of Average Fairness (AF) and truncated Gini mutability index (tG ). Moreover, both mPFR and cRR achieve the same degree of fairness and effectiveness when the priority given to the protected elements is raised to the maximum possible degree, i.e., the mPFR’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} and the cRR’s r.

As said, mMLP is infeasible with multiple groups. Because of eigenvector calculation and matrix density, mPFR is computationally more expensive than cRR; consequently, cRR can be efficient thanks to the O(n) complexity. However, mPFR is based on a mathematical model, i.e., a linear matrix combination that allows the design of ranking reordering methods at different degrees of complexity, such as those in which different item protection criteria or relevance-based criteria are mixed.

There is a question about the experimental data regarding the stability of the effectiveness measures, namely Average Precision (AP) and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG), when the fairness measures change. An examination of the latter showed that the ratio of relevant to protected documents and the ratio of protected to relevant documents are relatively high for many searches. To put it another way, it cannot be claimed that protected documents are treated differently during the judgment stage in terms of their applicability. Generally speaking, test sets that allow the researchers to assess if fairness reranking techniques are as effective as they look are required when it is known beforehand that such discrimination occurs.

The limitations of the illustrated and tested methods suggest investigating approximation methods for mMLP or adaptive hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of mPFR and cRR. Perhaps the use of approximation methods proposed in the linear programming literature will make mMLP both feasible and effective. Furthermore, more research should be conducted to determine whether the three methods allow for the re-ranking of different documents and what characteristics these documents possess.
