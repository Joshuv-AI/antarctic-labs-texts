Restrictive State Opioid Treatment Program Regulations Constrain Local Access To Methadone Maintenance Treatment
Authors: J. Travis Donahoe, Noa Krawczyk, Julie M. Donohue, Dylan Nagy, Paul J. Joudrey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
State regulations governing opioid treatment programs (OTPs) vary widely in their restrictiveness, but how they affect geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment for opioid use disorder remains poorly understood. In this study of comprehensive data on methadone shipments to OTPs in 2019, we found that ZIP codes just across the border in states with low restrictiveness in OTP regulations had more than twice the OTP density and methadone shipments compared with nearby and otherwise similar ZIP codes in states with high restrictiveness. These findings suggest that restrictive state OTP regulations constrain geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment and that easing such restrictions is a viable strategy to expand access.

Despite a sharp decline relative to 2023, more than 80,000 people died of a drug overdose in the US in 2024, with the vast majority of these deaths involving opioids.1 Despite the proven effectiveness of medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) in reducing overdose mortality,2 a significant treatment gap persists, with most people with OUD not receiving these lifesaving medications.3 Access is particularly limited for methadone maintenance treatment, which is restricted to federally certified opioid treatment programs (OTPs) under the Narcotic Addict Treatment Act of 1974. Well-known shortages of OTPs and supervised dosing requirements leave many people without a nearby facility and having to routinely travel long distances for care.4–6 More than 50 percent of patients who start methadone maintenance treatment discontinue it in the first six months,7,8 with burdensome requirements reported as a common reason for discontinuation.9

Beyond federal regulations, state-level regulations may further limit access. Some states impose barriers to opening new OTPs, including OTP-specific certificate-of-need laws, which require legal documentation of need for a new OTP before opening; zoning restrictions on where OTPs can be located; and additional licensing requirements.10 Others impose mandates that may worsen the experience of patients receiving care, such as setting minimum counseling requirements, limiting take-home dose eligibility, and mandating frequent urine drug screenings.10 Although these state-level regulations may contribute to the difficulty of receiving methadone maintenance treatment in the US, their relationship with access remains poorly understood.

To examine how state OTP regulations facilitate or impede access to methadone maintenance treatment, this study assessed the relationship between state OTP regulations and geographic access to this treatment by combining an existing policy typology11 with comprehensive facility-level data on methadone shipments to all OTPs in the US in 2019. Using a regression discontinuity design, we estimated changes in OTP availability and methadone shipments to OTPs for ZIP codes just inside the border of states with low restrictiveness compared with otherwise similar ZIP codes just across the border of states with high restrictiveness. This allowed us to isolate the relationship between states’ OTP restrictiveness and geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment and to account for potential unobservable confounders across states, such as differences in need.

Study Data And Methods

We built a cross-sectional data set on state OTP regulations and ZIP code–level methadone maintenance treatment access in 2019 in several steps that we describe below. Additional details are in online appendix A1.12

OTP RESTRICTIVENESS

We categorized states as having low or high OTP restrictiveness on the basis of a policy typology established in prior research.11 The typology used latent class analysis to classify states into three groups of regulatory models, based on eleven policies that were described in a Pew Charitable Trusts review of policies in effect in 2021,10 present in at least 10 percent of states, and which prior literature identifies as presenting barriers to patient experience (defined as regulations affecting “how patients receive care”) or access to care (defined as regulations affecting “the ease with which patients access care”)11 (policy details are in appendix exhibit A2).12 Further details are in appendix A112 and the original study documents.10,11 The typology identified three classes of regulatory models: high restrictiveness on patient experience and low restrictiveness on access to service, medium restrictiveness on patient experience and high restrictiveness on access to service, and low restrictiveness on both patient experience and access to service.11 In our main analysis, we classified states as having high restrictiveness if the typology identified them as having either high patient experience restrictiveness or high access restrictiveness (appendix exhibit A3).12

OUTCOMES

To measure access to methadone maintenance treatment at the five-digit ZIP code level, we used facility-level methadone shipments data from the Automation of Reports and Consolidated Orders System in 2019 (the most recent year available). These data, released as part of multidistrict opioid litigation,13 provide a comprehensive record of opioid shipments to all endpoints (including OTPs) in the US. In total, the records for 2019 encompassed methadone shipments to 1,611 unique OTPs (appendix A1).12 We used these data to construct two measures of ZIP code–level access to methadone maintenance treatment: an indicator for having any OTP, and the quantity of methadone shipments to OTPs (in grams).

ZIP CODE CHARACTERISTICS AND OTHER STATE POLICY DATA

We obtained ZIP code characteristics (population age sixteen or older, racial and ethnic composition, employment rate, median income, educational attainment, and health insurance coverage rate), using American Community Survey five-year estimates (2019–23) from the Census Bureau.14 We also obtained data on state Medicaid expansion status, coverage of methadone maintenance treatment within Medicaid, and other opioid-related policies from KFF and the State Policy and Politics Database,15–17 (see appendix A1 for more details).12

DISTANCE TO STATE BORDERS WITH OTP REGULATION DIFFERENCES

Using the Census Bureau’s Cartographic Boundary Files,18 we calculated the minimum distance (in miles) between the center of each ZIP code (approximated using ZIP Code Tabulation Areas) and the border of a state with a difference in OTP restrictiveness on either side of the state border (that is, high versus low restrictiveness or vice versa) (appendix exhibit A4).12 Distances were negative for states with high restrictiveness and positive for states with low restrictiveness. We used this as our running variable in a regression discontinuity framework, as described below.

DATA SET

We created a combined ZIP code–level data set with all of the variables described above. We excluded ZIP codes outside the continental US (Alaska and Hawaii), those in Wyoming (which had no OTPs or state OTP policies as of 2019),10,11 those that overlapped state boundaries, and those that lacked American Community Survey data. This resulted in a final data set of 31,290 ZIP code–level observations in 2019.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

To estimate the relationship between state OTP regulation restrictiveness and OTP availability and methadone shipments, we used a regression discontinuity design that took advantage of differences in OTP restrictiveness across state borders. We compared outcomes for ZIP codes just inside states with low restrictiveness with outcomes for ZIP codes that were just across the border in states with high restrictiveness. This approach helped isolate the relationship between restrictiveness and outcomes, as other factors that might influence OTP availability and methadone shipments (such as underlying treatment need) were unlikely to change sharply at state boundaries. To support this assumption, we tested whether ZIP code characteristics were different across borders with different state regulation restrictiveness.

To implement this analysis, we estimated linear regressions among all ZIP codes within a predetermined bandwidth from state borders where OTP regulations were different, with two key parameters: a continuous variable for miles to the state border where OTP regulations were different (the running variable), which we allowed to have a different coefficient in low- and high-restrictiveness states; and a binary variable for being in a state with low restrictiveness (the jump variable). We also controlled for two state policies that could have confounded our estimates by being correlated with OTP restrictiveness and treatment need: state Medicaid expansion status and coverage of methadone maintenance treatment within Medicaid.

The jump variable was the coefficient of interest and captured changes in local OTP availability and methadone shipments in ZIP codes just inside low-restrictiveness states relative to ZIP codes just across the border in high-restrictiveness states. It incorporated regulatory influences on the overall number of OTPs, as well as on where OTPs chose to locate. The continuous (running) variable captured whether this change dissipated with distance, as would be expected if high restrictiveness influenced where OTPs located.19

Our primary specification used a data-driven algorithm20 to determine included ZIP codes, based on distance from state borders where OTP regulations were different (known as the bandwidth). The optimal bandwidth was 82.09 miles, encompassing 11,833 ZIP codes. We also used a triangular kernel to place greater weight on the ZIP codes that were closer to the border where regulations were different.21 Standard errors were clustered on states, which was the level of treatment assignment.22 In addition, we reported robust 95% confidence intervals that achieved correct coverage under data-driven approaches to bandwidth selection.20

ROBUSTNESS AND EXTENSIONS

We performed a range of robustness checks. First, we estimated models with varying bandwidths from 20 to 200 miles of borders with regulation differences (in increments of 10) and separately with triangular, Epanechnikov, and uniform weighting.21 Second, we examined results for the number of OTPs and grams of methadone shipments per 100,000 residents, weighting ZIP codes by population size. Our primary analysis used non-population-adjusted metrics, as ZIP code population poorly reflects the population served by a typical OTP.4

Third, we examined how other state opioid-related policies23 varied with OTP restrictions and whether results were robust to controlling for them. Fourth, we examined robustness to controlling for restrictions not captured in the typology we used: requirements that facilities remain open after typical business hours and patient capacity limits.24 Fifth, we performed a state-level analysis that examined differences in the number of OTPs and quantity of methadone shipments per 100,000 residents, OUD prevalence (from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health),3 and the number of OTPs and quantity of methadone shipments per 1,000 residents with OUD in states with high versus low restrictiveness.

Last, to examine how different types of regulations might be differentially related to changes in our study outcomes, we separately estimated two changes. First, we estimated changes for ZIP codes in states with low patient experience and access restrictiveness relative to ZIP codes in states with high patient experience restrictiveness and low access restrictiveness. Second, we estimated changes for ZIP codes in states with low patient experience and access restrictiveness relative to ZIP codes in states with high access restrictiveness and medium patient experience restrictiveness (appendix exhibit A5).12

LIMITATIONS

This study had several limitations. First, although we used a strong study design and adjusted for policies that we believed were most likely to be potential confounders, there is still a possibility that other unaccounted-for state policies or unobservable differences in treatment need across ZIP codes could have biased our results. We were unable to demonstrate whether treatment need was similar across state borders because of a lack of data on OUD prevalence at the ZIP code level. Second, we did not isolate the association between specific restrictions and study outcomes, as regulations were highly correlated within states,11 making it difficult to disentangle their individual impacts using cross-sectional data. Further research using longitudinal data and studying policy changes is needed to shed light on the role of specific restrictions.

Third, our primary analysis did not consider OTP regulations that were not included in the typology that we used to classify states.11 Other OTP regulations that are correlated with restrictive regulatory models could also contribute to limited access. Finally, we were not able to examine changes in state regulations or how current federal OTP regulations, which have changed significantly since the COVID-19 pandemic,25 may affect research into our study question.

Study Results

ZIP CODE SIMILARITY ACROSS STATE BORDERS

Exhibit 1 presents regression discontinuity estimates of differences in ZIP code characteristics for ZIP codes that were just inside states with low restrictiveness relative to ZIP codes just across the border in states with high restrictiveness. We did not find any large or statistically significant differences in population size, racial and ethnic composition, employment rates, income, educational attainment, or health insurance coverage rates between these ZIP codes. This suggests that ZIP codes on either side of the border where state OTP regulations were different were otherwise similar to one another, supporting the internal validity of our study design.

CHANGES IN OTP AVAILABILITY AND METHADONE SHIPMENTS

Exhibit 2 presents the relationship between the probability that a ZIP code had any OTP and the distance to the border of a state where OTP regulations were more or less restrictive among all ZIP codes within eighty-two miles of these state borders. In states with high restrictiveness, approximately 3.4 percent of ZIP codes had any OTP in 2019. This probability was unchanging with distance to the low-restrictiveness state border. However, immediately across the border of states with low restrictiveness, the probability of having any OTP was much higher. This increase declined with distance from the state border. Results were similar for total grams of methadone shipments to OTPs (exhibit 3).

In the regression discontinuity models, we found that the probability of having any OTP was 4.3 (robust 95% CI = 1.4, 7.6) percentage points higher for ZIP codes just inside states with low restrictiveness relative to ZIP codes just across the border in states with high restrictiveness (exhibit 4). This was equivalent to a 126 percent increase over the mean in states with high restrictiveness. Similarly, average annual methadone shipments to OTPs were 386.9 (robust 95% CI = 94.2, 743.8) grams higher just across the border (exhibit 4), equivalent to a 104 percent increase.

We also found that the probability of having any OTP and methadone shipments declined with distance from the border in states with low restrictiveness. However, these declines were not statistically significant (exhibit 4). Finally, we found that having expanded Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act was associated with higher probabilities of having an OTP and methadone shipments to OTPs but that Medicaid coverage of methadone maintenance treatment was not (exhibit 4).

ROBUSTNESS AND EXTENSIONS

Appendix exhibits A6 and A7 present results from regressions that estimated changes in OTP availability and methadone shipments at the border of states with low restrictiveness with different bandwidths and weighting strategies.12 Point estimates were stable across these specifications. Appendix exhibit A8 presents results using population-adjusted outcomes, which were similar to our main results.12 Appendix exhibit A9 shows that states with high restrictiveness also tended to have higher opioid prescribing restrictiveness.12 However, results were robust to including these additional policies as covariates (appendix exhibit A10).12 Appendix exhibit A11 shows that results were robust to controlling for state requirements that OTPs remain open outside of typical business hours and requirements for patient capacity limits,12 which were not included in the typology11 that we used to classify states in our primary analysis.

At the overall state level, appendix exhibit A12 shows that rates of OTPs and methadone shipments per 100,000 residents were similar for states with high versus low restrictiveness.12 However, OUD prevalence was lower in states with low restrictiveness. Rates of OTPs and methadone shipments per 1,000 residents with OUD were higher in states with low restrictiveness.

Appendix exhibit A13 presents regression discontinuity estimates separately for different types of OTP restrictiveness.12 We found similar changes in the probability of a ZIP code having an OTP and methadone shipments to OTPs, irrespective of whether the change was from high patient experience restrictiveness to low restrictiveness or from high access restrictiveness to low restrictiveness.

Discussion

In this study, we found that geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment, measured as the probability of having any OTP and methadone shipments to OTPs, more than doubled when crossing the border from a state with high restrictiveness to a neighboring state with low restrictiveness. The magnitudes imply an equivalent of eight to thirty-five more people receiving methadone maintenance treatment annually at doses of 30–120 mg per day in the average ZIP code just inside states with low restrictiveness. Similar patterns emerged for restrictions on patient experience and restrictions on access to services.

These results may reflect state restrictions limiting the opening and continued operation of OTPs, as well as regulatory avoidance—that is, OTPs strategically locating across the border in less regulated states. Our finding that the effect dissipated with distance from the border is consistent with regulatory avoidance, although the pattern was not statistically significant. At the same time, substantial differences in OTPs and methadone shipments per 1,000 residents with OUD across states with different regulatory models as a whole suggests influences on the opening and closing of facilities as well. Further research is needed to understand the contribution of each mechanism.

Overall, our findings suggest that restrictive state OTP regulations significantly reduce geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment, forcing patients to travel farther for care that is unavailable locally or forgo treatment. These burdens may be especially acute for Medicaid enrollees, as coverage for out-of-state care can be limited or administratively difficult to obtain.26 Although historically restrictive federal OTP regulations are known to drive shortages of methadone access in the US,6,27 our study provides new empirical evidence that state-level regulations may exacerbate this issue. This highlights a key area where states could pursue reforms to expand access and address the opioid epidemic locally.

Our research aligns with prior research that shows that there is significant variability in methadone maintenance treatment access across states.28,29 We identified state OTP regulations as a key potential driver of this variation. Going forward, it will be essential to evaluate how state-level differences in OTP restrictions and geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment evolve with 2024 federal reforms that aim to facilitate more person-centered care (such as allowance for more frequent take-homes, higher initial dosing, and reduced counseling)27 and reimbursement for OTP care within Medicare.30 State differences in access to methadone maintenance treatment may widen in the setting of reduced federal restrictions. States with low OTP restrictiveness may reap the benefits of recently reformed federal regulations and further improve access, whereas states with high restrictiveness may lag unless they update their regulations. Some states have rescinded recently expanded federal take-home dosing flexibilities,31 highlighting the important role that state policies could play in determining the success of recent federal reforms.

Our findings also shed light on the troubling success of policies that often explicitly aim to restrict access to methadone maintenance treatment, such as the moratorium on OTPs in West Virginia,10 despite methadone maintenance treatment’s established, lifesaving effects for people with OUD.2 An important question is the extent to which these regulations arise from public resistance to methadone maintenance treatment or private interests, and how to approach reforming them. There is a long-standing history of local resistance to opening OTPs in the US that is partly attributable to stigma.32 Many OTPs are also private, for-profit entities, with roughly one-third owned by private equity firms.33 These entities may favor maintaining barriers to new methadone maintenance treatment entrants. Another important consideration is whether ending the federal prohibition on methadone maintenance treatment provision outside of OTPs, as proposed by the Modernizing Opioid Treatment Access Act of 2023, could circumvent state and federal OTP restrictions by integrating methadone maintenance treatment into existing outpatient care.

Taken together, our findings highlight that addressing restrictive state OTP regulations is a critical component of efforts to modernize and expand access to methadone maintenance treatment. This evidence makes a clear case for policy makers to critically review and update state OTP regulations so that they are aligned with the best available medical and public health guidance and are positioned to fully leverage recent federal OTP reforms.

Conclusion

Thousands of people in the US with OUD continue to go without effective medications and to die from opioid overdoses each year. Expanding geographic access to methadone maintenance treatment is a promising strategy for addressing this issue. Our finding that ZIP codes just across the border of states with less restrictive OTP regulations have more than double the probability of having an OTP and methadone shipments underscores the critical importance of reforming state policies to expand geographic methadone maintenance treatment access and address the opioid crisis.
