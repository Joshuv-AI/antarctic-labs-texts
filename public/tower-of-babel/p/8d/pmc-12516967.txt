Musculoskeletal disorders among spousal caregivers of individuals with chronic motor disabilities: a cross-sectional study in Tunisia
Authors: Ikram Haddada, Ameni Ameur, Soumaya El Arem, Aymen Hadj Salah, Aymen Fekih, Meriem Kacem, Issam Khalifa, Noura Belhadj, Mouna Sghir, Wassia Kessomtini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine the prevalence and identify the independent factors associated with musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among spousal caregivers of individuals with chronic motor disabilities.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThe sole tertiary-level Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Department serving its region in Tunisia.ParticipantsA consecutive sample of 142 spousal caregivers (response rate 85%) was recruited between January and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were being the primary spouse caregiver for an individual with a chronic motor disability (>6 months). Exclusion criteria included caregivers with pre-existing chronic musculoskeletal or rheumatic diseases or an inability to complete the questionnaire.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the prevalence of MSDs, assessed by the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire.Secondary outcomes included pain intensity (visual analogue scale (VAS)) and the identification of factors independently associated with MSDs. Associated factors (eg, caregiver burden by Zarit Burden Scale, fatigue by Checklist Individual Strength) were analysed using multivariate logistic regression.ResultsThe prevalence of at least one MSD was 72.5% (103/142). Chronic low back pain (LBP) was the most common MSD (54.2%). Among affected caregivers, the mean pain intensity was 56.9±18.2 mm on the VAS, with 28.2% reporting severe pain (VAS≥70 mm). Multivariate regression analysis identified four independent predictors of MSDs: providing care for≥8 hours per day (adjusted OR (AOR)=10.06, 95% CI 1.24 to 81.38), a high caregiver burden (AOR=4.15, 95% CI 1.07 to 16.06), severe caregiver fatigue (AOR=3.10, 95% CI 1.24 to 7.72) and caring for an obese partner (AOR=3.07, 95% CI 1.00 to 9.40).ConclusionsThe high prevalence of MSDs, particularly chronic LBP, among spousal caregivers highlights the significant physical strain associated with this role. The strong association with prolonged daily caregiving, high burden and fatigue identifies key modifiable risk factors. These findings suggest that the health of spousal caregivers is often overlooked, which could impact their long-term well-being and the quality of care they provide. Implementing targeted support interventions, such as ergonomic training and respite care, should be considered.Keywords: Chronic Pain, Family, Primary Health Care, Caregivers, Back pain, Musculoskeletal disorders

Introduction

Caregiving encompasses a multifaceted spectrum of support, encompassing both physical and emotional assistance for individuals grappling with illness or disability. This crucial role can be broadly categorised into two distinct modalities: formal care, delivered by trained healthcare professionals within a clinical setting, and informal care, typically provided by unpaid individuals, such as family members or close friends.1 2

In low- and middle-income countries, access to formal care services often faces significant limitations due to a confluence of factors, including insufficient healthcare infrastructure, limited availability of specialised facilities, substantial geographical distances and the prohibitive cost of care.3 This pronounced gap in formal care services inevitably amplifies the burden borne by informal caregivers, particularly within vulnerable communities.3

Societal norms frequently dictate that wives assume the primary role of caregiver for their ailing husbands.4 This phenomenon is particularly pronounced following a motor disability, where spousal caregivers shoulder the primary responsibility for their partners' well-being. These responsibilities extend beyond emotional support, encompassing a multitude of physically demanding tasks.5

Spousal caregivers are frequently involved in assisting with mobility, transfers, personal hygiene and other essential daily activities.6 These tasks often necessitate physically demanding actions, such as lifting, transferring and assisting with dressing, which can exert considerable strain on the caregiver’s own physical well-being.7

Unlike professional caregivers who typically benefit from defined roles and established support systems, spousal caregivers often confront an almost limitless scope of responsibilities. The constant demands of caregiving, particularly the physically demanding tasks, coupled with chronic time constraints and limited access to respite care, significantly elevate the risk of developing musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among spousal caregivers. MSDs, such as low back pain, can have a profound impact on their physical and emotional well-being.8,10

The development of MSDs, characterised by joint disorders, chronic pain and functional limitations, can significantly exacerbate physical and emotional suffering among spousal caregivers.8 Furthermore, the combination of fatigue and exhaustion can lead to a decline in the quality of care provided to their spouses.11 Weakened both physically and emotionally, caregivers may be compelled to reduce the intensity or frequency of care, potentially jeopardising their partner’s well-being.12

Despite extensive research on the physical and mental health challenges faced by caregivers of individuals with physical disabilities, and a growing body of literature on chronic pain conditions and their impact on individuals' lives, the prevalence and risk factors for MSDs among spousal caregivers of individuals with mobility impairments remain understudied. This research gap motivated our study, which aims to determine the prevalence of MSDs and associated factors among spousal caregivers of persons with chronic motor disabilities.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, focusing on spousal caregivers of patients with chronic motor disabilities. The study period was 11 months (from August 2023 to June 2024).

Study setting

This study took place at a specialised Physical Medicine Department in Tunisia, a North African country with extremely limited national resources in this medical field.13 The centre where the research was conducted is the only specialised physical medicine and rehabilitation facility serving its region. As such, it is the sole referral centre for rehabilitation in the area, providing comprehensive care for patients with motor disabilities.

Participants

The study included spouses who were the primary caregivers for their husbands with chronic motor disabilities (>6 months). Exclusion criteria included: (1) spousal caregivers unable to complete the questionnaire; (2) spousal caregivers providing care for another individual with special healthcare needs and (3) spousal caregivers with a pre-existing history of chronic illnesses, such as rheumatic diseases, diabetes, fibromyalgia, musculoskeletal diseases or any psychiatric disorder prior to their husband’s motor impairment.

Sample size estimation and sampling method

The calculation of the minimum sample size was based on the following formula: n=(Z2×p×(1−p))I2, where: n=required sample size; p=population proportion; Z=confidence level (1.96 for a 95% confidence level) and I=margin of error (7% or 0.07).

Using an estimated population proportion of 82.8% (caregivers of stroke survivors with MSDs) from a previous similar study,8 the total sample size required for the present study was estimated to be 112.

Participant recruitment was carried out using a consecutive sampling approach. This method was chosen to minimise selection bias and to obtain a clinically representative sample of the caregiver population accessing our tertiary care facility during the study period. Throughout the active data collection period (January–June 2024), all eligible spousal caregivers who accompanied their husbands with chronic motor disabilities to routine follow-up appointments at the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation were systematically informed about the study and invited to participate. This sampling strategy strengthens the internal validity of our study for the specific context of our referral centre.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Taher Sfar Mahdia Hospital (Approval No. CEM-2024-01-06, dated 6 January 2024).

We confirm that active data collection involving human participants commenced only after ethical approval was received. The overall project timeline (August 2023–June 2024) included preparatory phases, such as study design, tool development and staff training, which took place prior to January 2024.

During their husbands’ routine clinical visits, eligible spousal caregivers were verbally informed about the study’s purpose, procedures (including questionnaires and time requirements) and its voluntary, anonymous nature by a treating physician. They also received a written information sheet. After clarification of their rights—including the freedom to decline participation without impacting their husbands’ care and the right to withdraw at any time—written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with standard ethical guidelines.

Instrument

The study collected data on husbands with chronic motor disabilities, including their age, body mass index (BMI) and the underlying conditions causing their impairments. Additionally, sociodemographic data were gathered from the spousal caregivers, including age, BMI, number of children, educational level, occupation, type of residence and socioeconomic status, which was determined using the 2015 criteria of the National Institute of Statistics of Tunisia.14

The functional independence measure (FIM)15 16 assesses the level of assistance required by husbands with motor disabilities in performing the basic activities of daily living (ADLs). The FIM includes 18 basic ADLs, such as self-care (eg, eating, grooming, bathing, dressing and toileting), sphincter control, transfers, locomotion, communication and social cognition.

Each item on the FIM is rated on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (total assistance; the patient provides less than 25% of effort to complete each task) to 7 (complete independence; the patient completes each task in a timely and safe manner). The sum of all item scores yields a total score, ranging from 18 to 126, with higher scores signifying greater independence. According to the FIM scoring guidelines, the total score is categorised as follows: 18–36 (total assistance), 37–44 (maximal assistance), 45–62 (moderate assistance), 63–79 (minimal assistance), 80–99 (supervision), 100–123 (modified independence) and 124–126 (complete independence). In this study, we adopted the cut-off value of 80, as proposed by Buia et al,17 to define independence, considering individuals with an FIM score≥80 as independent.

Caregiving characteristics

Caregiving characteristics included daily hours of care, total caregiving duration, caregiver burden and fatigue.

Caregiver burden was assessed using the Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale (ZCBS).18 The ZCBS is an instrument designed to quantify the stress associated with providing family care. It comprises 22 items that explore various aspects of the caregiver’s health, social life, personal life, financial situation, emotional well-being and interpersonal relationships. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always). The total score, derived from the sum of all item scores, ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating greater caregiver burden. According to the ZCBS scoring guidelines,19 scores are categorised as follows: 0–20 (no care burden), 21–40 (light care burden), 41–60 (moderate care burden) and 61–88 (heavy care burden).

Caregiver fatigue was assessed using the multidimensional checklist individual strength (CIS).20 The CIS is a self-report measure comprising 20 statements rated on a seven-point Likert scale. It evaluates fatigue across four dimensions: subjective experience of fatigue, reduced motivation, reduced activity and reduced concentration. The total CIS score, calculated by summing scores across all dimensions, ranges from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating greater fatigue, lower motivation, reduced activity and poorer concentration. According to the CIS scoring guidelines,21 scores are categorised as follows: ≤26 (no fatigue), 27–34 (mild fatigue) and ≥35 (severe fatigue).

Musculoskeletal disorders

MSDs among spousal caregivers were identified using the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ), a rigorously validated international instrument. Caregivers self-administered the NMQ, indicating on a body map any areas experiencing ache, pain or discomfort (neck, shoulders, elbows, hands, wrists, upper back, lower back, hips, thighs, knees, ankles and feet).22 23

For each reported MSD, the duration of symptoms was recorded. Pain intensity was measured using the visual analogue scale (VAS), a 0–100 mm scale where 0 represents no pain and 100 represents the worst imaginable pain. Pain intensity was categorised as minimal (VAS<30 mm), moderate (VAS 30–70 mm) or severe (VAS≥70 mm).24

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (V.26), with categorical variables presented as frequencies (percentages) and continuous variables as means±SDs (normality verified by Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). Group comparisons used Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests (two tailed) as appropriate. To identify independent MSD risk factors, we first performed univariate screening (p<0.2 threshold), followed by multivariate binary logistic regression with backward elimination (using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), which balances model fit and complexity, with final retention at p<0.05). Model validity was ensured by maintaining≥10 events per variable, confirming the absence of multicollinearity (all variance inflation factors (VIFs) <5, indicating no problematic correlations between predictors) and demonstrating adequate fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test), with results reported as adjusted ORs (AORs) (95% CIs) and two-tailed p values<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

The study achieved a response rate of 85.0%, with 142 spousal caregivers agreeing to participate out of 167 eligible individuals. The average age of participants was 58.76±11.35 years (range: 30–85 years). Their average BMI was 26.5±4.2 kg/m² (range: 18.8–41 kg/m²). A significant proportion (23.2%) was classified as obese (BMI≥30 kg/m²) and 55.7% had a primary education or less. Musculoskeletal disorders

In this cohort of 142 spousal caregivers, 103 (72.5%) reported suffering from at least one MSD. Among those with MSDs, 40 (28.2% of the total sample) mentioned a single musculoskeletal pain, while 63 (44.4% of the total sample) reported having two or more MSDs. The most commonly affected regions were the lower back (54.2%), shoulder (25.3%) and neck (19.7%), as presented in figure 1.

The average duration of musculoskeletal symptoms was 3.6±2.3 years, ranging from 1 to 10 years. In 22.5% of cases (32 caregivers), these pains had been present for at least 5 years. Discussion

Our study primarily focused on female spousal caregivers, a deliberate choice rooted in the unique sociocultural context of our research setting. Here, deep-seated religious and social norms largely assign women the primary responsibility for caring for family members, particularly ailing spouses. This often leads wives to perceive caregiving as an obligatory role rather than a voluntary choice. This local pattern mirrors global trends, where women constitute the majority of informal caregivers worldwide.4 Evidence suggests that they frequently form stronger emotional bonds with care recipients, make greater personal sacrifices and are less inclined to seek external assistance.4

Regardless of cultural background, spousal caregivers typically dedicate more time and undertake more demanding tasks due to the unique marital bond.4 This phenomenon is especially pronounced when caring for a partner with a motor disability, as these caregivers assume primary responsibility for their partners’ well-being. Such roles extend beyond emotional support, encompassing a multitude of physically demanding tasks.5 The constant nature of these physically strenuous caregiving demands, compounded by chronic time constraints and limited access to respite care, significantly elevates the risk of MSDs among spousal caregivers. In this cohort, a significant proportion of spousal caregivers (72.5%) reported at least one MSD. Notably, while 28.2% reported a single musculoskeletal pain, a significant proportion (44.4%) experienced two or more MSDs. Univariate analysis revealed significant associations between MSDs and caregiver age, occupational status, obesity and functional status of the person with a disability, hours of care, burden and fatigue. Multivariate analysis confirmed that obesity of the person with a disability, hours of care, burden and caregiver fatigue were the primary determinants of MSD onset.

MSDs, encompassing a diverse range of conditions, pose a significant public health challenge. With increasing life expectancy, the socioeconomic burden of MSDs is steadily growing. Furthermore, the disability associated with these conditions is on the rise globally, significantly impacting individuals’ ability to engage in both professional and daily activities.25 26

While occupational overuse is a significant contributor to MSDs,27 it is crucial to acknowledge their multifactorial nature. Factors beyond occupational demands, including ageing, psychosocial factors and demands of domestic and leisure activities, also play a crucial role in their development.28 Notably, the literature emphasises the potential impact of caregiving for a relative with a motor disability on the development and maintenance of musculoskeletal problems. Managing the daily challenges associated with a person’s motor deficits, such as assistance with ADLs like bathing, dressing, eating and mobility, can significantly strain caregivers physically and increase their risk of developing MSDs.29 30

Caregiving for a spouse with a motor disability often involves physically demanding tasks, such as lifting, transferring and assisting with dressing, frequently performed with improper posture.30 Research by Ramezani et al31 found that family caregivers adopting postures, such as prolonged forward bending (eg, while pushing a wheelchair) or repetitive spinal flexion, exhibit a significantly higher risk of developing MSDs, with a 7.67-fold increased risk compared with the general population. As a consequence, these caregivers experience chronic musculoskeletal overstrain and overload due to the repetitive nature of these demanding tasks performed in awkward postures. This physical strain can lead to a variety of MSDs, including back pain, tendinitis and joint pain. Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of MSDs among spousal caregivers, with Darragh et al10 and Vincent-Onabajo et al8 reporting prevalence rates of 94% and 82.2%, respectively. In line with these findings, our study observed a high prevalence of MSDs, with 72.5% of the 142 spousal caregivers reporting at least one MSD.

As researchers increasingly focus on the physical well-being of caregivers, MSDs, particularly chronic low back pain (CLBP), have emerged as significant concerns.32 CLBP appears to be the most common MSD among caregivers of individuals with motor disabilities. Previous studies have reported the high rates of CLBP among this population, with Vincent-Onabajo et al8 finding it in 72.2% of cases within their study. Similarly, Abdullahi et al29 and Yalcinkaya et al12 reported CLBP prevalence rates of 53.9% and 82.8% among family caregivers, respectively. Our findings align with the existing literature, with CLBP emerging as the most prevalent MSD in our cohort (54.2%), followed by shoulder (25.3%) and neck (19.7%) complaints.

The physically demanding tasks associated with caring for individuals with motor disabilities, such as frequent lifting, transferring and providing personal care, expose caregivers to a heightened risk of developing MSDs. These demanding activities, often performed in awkward postures and for extended periods, particularly when caring for individuals with severe functional impairments, contribute significantly to this risk.12 31 33 Our findings confirm this relationship, revealing a significant association between the care recipient’s functional status and the development of MSDs in the caregiver spouse (p=0.021).

Caring for a spouse with both a physical disability and obesity presents significant physical challenges for caregivers, significantly increasing their risk of developing MSDs, particularly in the lower back. The combination of limited mobility and excess weight in the care recipient necessitates frequent lifting, transferring and providing personal care, often in awkward positions, exacerbating the physical demands on caregivers.34 35 Our findings support this, demonstrating that the obesity of care recipients is a significant factor contributing to the development of MSDs in their caregivers (p=0.005).

MSDs are complex conditions influenced by a multitude of factors, including demographic, psychological, sociocultural and occupational variables.36 Age is a significant risk factor for developing MSDs.37 38 As individuals age, physiological changes, such as decreased bone density, muscle mass and joint cartilage, along with reduced flexibility, increase susceptibility to these conditions.37 38 The prevalence of osteoarthritis also increases with age, further exacerbating joint pain and stiffness.39 Our study found a significant association between the age of spousal caregivers and the onset of MSDs (p<0.001), supporting the established link between ageing and the development of these conditions.

In addition to demographic factors, Nguyen et al36 highlighted the role of occupational factors, such as job dissatisfaction, heavy workload and workplace stress, in the development and persistence of MSDs. Our study corroborates these findings, demonstrating a significant association between the professional status of spousal caregivers and the development of MSDs (p=0.002).

The significant physical and emotional demands of caring for a spouse with motor disabilities can have a profound impact on caregivers’ well-being. Due to multiple disabilities and loss of autonomy, individuals with motor impairments often require extensive assistance from their spouses for basic ADLs.40 41 This constant caregiving role can lead to physical and emotional exhaustion.42

While previous research has examined these issues among mothers of children with disabilities,43 the specific impact of caregiving on spousal fatigue and exhaustion has been relatively unexplored. In our study, 34.5% of spouses perceived their caregiving burden as severe, and 71.1% reported high levels of fatigue and exhaustion. This fatigue, weakness and exhaustion can make it difficult for spouses to cope with daily challenges, increasing their anxiety, fear and risk of developing various health problems.44 Indeed, in addition to autoimmune diseases, headaches and gastrointestinal problems, fatigue and burnout in caregivers have been linked to the development of musculoskeletal pain.45

Our findings support these observations, demonstrating significant associations among high levels of caregiver fatigue (CIS) (p<0.001), caregiver burden (ZCBS) (p<0.001) and the number of hours spent providing care (p<0.001), and the onset of MSDs among spousal caregivers.

Despite the significant physical and emotional burden of caring for a spouse with physical disabilities, and growing awareness of chronic pain, few studies have investigated MSDs among caregivers—a gap our research addresses. However, our study has several limitations. First, the single-centre design may limit the generalisability of our findings. Second, while the inclusion of patients with diverse neurological conditions reflects the real-world clinical practice, it may obscure disorder-specific risk factors for MSDs.

Furthermore, the generalisability of our findings must be considered within the specific context of our study setting. This research was conducted in the sole tertiary-level Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Department serving its region in Tunisia. While this allowed us to capture a wide spectrum of severe cases referred for specialised care, certain factors may affect external validity. First, the urban location of our centre may under-represent caregivers from rural areas who face different access-to-care challenges. Second, socioeconomic barriers influencing referral to a tertiary centre may limit the representation of all socioeconomic groups. Finally, cultural norms specific to North Africa that shape family dynamics and caregiving roles may mean that our findings are not directly generalisable to other cultural contexts.

Furthermore, in line with national ethical guidelines and standard demographic practices in Tunisia, our study did not collect data on racial or ethnic groups. Tunisia has a highly homogeneous population,46 and such categorisations are not used in national census data or medical research. Therefore, while our findings are robust for the sociodemographic context studied, they cannot inform on potential associations between race/ethnicity and MSD risk in caregivers, which may be a relevant factor in more heterogeneous populations.

Notwithstanding these limitations for generalisability, the internal validity of our findings is strengthened by the consecutive sampling method. This approach ensured a clinically representative sample of the target population accessing specialised rehabilitation services in this setting, thereby accurately reflecting real-world caregiver profiles in a resource-limited context.

Methodologically, the discrepancy between univariate and multivariate analyses underscores the distinction between identifying potential associations and isolating independent predictors. Variables significant in univariate screening (eg, caregiver age and husband’s functional status) lost independence in the multivariate model, likely due to mediation effects. For instance, the association between advanced caregiver age and MSDs appears to be largely mediated by longer daily care duration, suggesting that age increases risk primarily through greater exposure to physical strain. Furthermore, the overlapping variance explained by strong psychosocial predictors (eg, care burden, fatigue) may have diminished the unique contribution of demographic factors.

The observed imprecision in certain effect estimates, such as the wide CI for prolonged caregiving, primarily reflects the limited number of participants in high-exposure categories (eg, only 25.4% provided≥8 hours per day of care) and natural outcome variability. While our sample size was sufficient to detect the strong, primary associations reported (adhering to the>10 events-per-variable rule and achieving statistical significance), it may have been underpowered to detect smaller effects, such as the modest association with husband’s obesity. Consequently, while the identified associations are robust, the point estimates of their magnitude should be considered preliminary and require verification in larger, dedicated cohorts.

Despite these considerations, our analytical approach was conservative, employing multivariate modelling with backward elimination based on the AIC to balance fit and complexity. The absence of multicollinearity (all VIFs<5) and the model’s adequate fit further support the robustness of the final independent risk factors identified.

Conclusion

The findings of this study reveal a high prevalence of MSDs, particularly CLBP, among spousal caregivers of individuals with motor disabilities in Tunisia. The strong and independent associations observed with prolonged daily caregiving, high caregiver burden and severe fatigue suggest that the physical and emotional strain of this role may represent a significant risk factor for caregiver health. Despite their critical role, our results indicate that the well-being of these caregivers is often overlooked.

These findings point to the potential need for a multifaceted support strategy. Consideration should be given to initiatives aimed at mitigating this risk, such as integrating caregiver health assessments into clinical practice, providing ergonomic training and access to assistive devices and facilitating respite care options. Future efforts involving healthcare providers, policymakers and community support could be valuable to safeguard the long-term health of caregivers and, by extension, the quality of care they are able to provide.
