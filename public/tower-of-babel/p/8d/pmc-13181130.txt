Functional Difficulty Among Women Aged 18–49 Years in 22 Low-and Middle-Income Countries: Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys, 2017–2022
Authors: Md Sabbir Ahmed Mayen, Salwa Nawsheen Nisha, Sumya Afrin, Tanvir Ahammed, Muhammad Abdul Baker Chowdhury, Md Jamal Uddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with functional difficulty often experience activity limitations due to their increased vulnerability compared to men. Although prevalence estimates exist, cross-country evidence on determinants in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains limited. This study estimated the pooled prevalence and identified factors associated with functional difficulty among women of reproductive age in LMICs.MethodsWe analyzed data from the most recent Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys conducted between 2017 and 2022 in 22 LMICs. The outcome variable was functional difficulty among women aged 18–49 years. Survey-weighted pooled prevalence estimates were calculated. A multivariable multilevel logistic regression model was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of functional difficulty was 5.15%, ranging from 1.33% in Sierra Leone to 13.39% in the Central African Republic. Higher odds of difficulty were observed among women of higher reproductive age (42–49 years; AOR = 4.75), those with no/pre-primary/primary education (AOR = 1.95), urban residents (AOR = 1.60), and women never in union (AOR = 1.34). Additional risk factors included exposure to intimate partner violence (AOR = 1.14), high parity (≥ 7 children; AOR = 1.44), unimproved sanitation (AOR = 1.17), low life satisfaction (AOR = 2.09), and lack of mobile phone ownership (AOR = 1.33). Regionally, women residing in African countries had higher odds of difficulty (AOR = 2.24) than those in Asia.ConclusionsFunctional difficulty affects one in 20 women of reproductive age in LMICs, with substantial cross-country variation. The burden is patterned by sociodemographic, reproductive, psychosocial, household, and digital access factors, highlighting persistent inequalities and the need for targeted, equity-focused interventions to improve women’s functioning and well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00551-w.Keywords: Functional difficulty, Low- and middle-income countries, Multivariable multilevel logistic regression, Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), Pooled analysis, Women

Introduction

Functional difficulty, as defined by the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health, involves impairments in body structure or function, activity limitations, and restrictions in social participation [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 15% of the global population lives with some degree of functional difficulty, an increase from around 10% in the 1970s, with 2–4% experiencing severe difficulty [2].

Globally, functional difficulty disproportionately affects women and older adults. Prevalence is higher among older populations in lower-income countries (43.4%) compared with higher-income countries (29.5%), and among women (19.2%) than men (12%) [2]. Evidence from the Global Burden of Disease study further indicates that, across all ages, women bear a greater burden of functional impairment than men, with approximately 60% higher prevalence and an 11% higher likelihood of moderate or severe difficulty, partly attributable to higher age-specific incidence and longer life expectancy [2, 3]. Even among adults aged 15–49 years, the prevalence of functional difficulty remains slightly higher among women (2.8%) than men (2.6%) [3].

Evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) highlights both higher prevalence and considerable cross-country variation. Analysis of World Health Survey data (2002–2004) from 49 countries reported a higher prevalence in LMICs (15.1%) than in high-income countries (10.8%), with women, particularly older women, being more affected [4]. Consistently, the Disability Initiative 2022 reported higher prevalence among women than men in Bangladesh (8.9% vs. 7.0%), Malawi (12.4% vs. 8.9%), Suriname (18.1% vs. 13.6%), and Tonga (13.1% vs. 12.1%) [5]. Country-specific studies further document substantial heterogeneity, with 14.1% of women reporting functional difficulty in Lesotho and more than half of older women in Ghana (53.7%) experiencing at least one functional limitation [6, 7]. Similar patterns have been observed in Kiribati and Pakistan [8, 9].

Despite international, national, and regional frameworks promoting the inclusion and empowerment of women and girls with functional difficulty, many continue to face restricted employment opportunities, inaccessible transport, inadequate workplace accommodations, and discrimination, which limit their participation in education, income generation, and broader development processes [10–13]. They also frequently experience stigma, prejudice, and social exclusion [3]. Moreover, maternal functional difficulty has been associated with an increased risk of functional difficulty among children [14].

To our knowledge, no recent study has performed a pooled multilevel analysis of risk factors for functional difficulty among women of reproductive age in LMICs. This study, therefore, estimates the pooled prevalence, examines the sociodemographic characteristics of women aged 18–49 who reported functional difficulty, compares them with women without difficulty, and identifies associated factors.

Methods

Study Population & Data Source

We used nationally representative data from Round 6 of UNICEF’s Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), which employs a standard two-stage sampling design. Full sampling details are available elsewhere [15]. MICS-6 was selected because it was the first round to include the Adult Functioning Module.

Data were initially drawn from 37 LMICs, but 15 countries were excluded due to missing key variables on women’s functional difficulty, leaving 22 countries in the final analysis. The Adult Functioning Module is designed for adults aged 18–49, consistent with SDG indicators and excluding older adults due to age-related complications. Therefore, only women aged 18–49 were included, and those with missing functional difficulty responses were removed. Of 369,771 women initially merged, 58,439 were excluded, resulting in a final pooled sample of 311,332 women. The merging, inclusion, and exclusion process for each country is illustrated in Supplementary Material 1: Fig. 1.

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable was a binary indicator of functional difficulty, measured using the Adult Functioning Module of the MICS-6, based on the Washington Group (WG) short set of questions. This module assesses six functional domains: seeing, walking, hearing, remembering, communication, and self-care [16]. Respondents reported difficulty in each domain as no difficulty, some difficulty, a lot of difficulty, or cannot do at all. Women reporting “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in at least one domain were classified as having functional difficulty, while those reporting “no difficulty” or “some difficulty” in all domains were classified as not having functional difficulty [17, 18].

Explanatory Variables

Explanatory variables were grouped into individual- and community-level factors based on prior literature [9, 19]. Individual-level variables included mother’s age, education, marital status, wealth index, media exposure, intimate partner violence (IPV), household size, number of children, age at marriage, recent live birth, water source, handwashing facilities, sanitation, life satisfaction, characteristics of the household head (age, sex, education), mobile phone ownership, and home internet access. Community-level variables included urban/rural residence and world region [20]. Variable definitions and recoding are summarized in (see Supplementary Material 1: Table 1).

Data Pooling Procedures and Statistical Analysis

Data from 22 LMICs were pooled following established guidelines for combining complex survey data [21]. Within each country, women’s and household files were merged, and variables were reviewed, recoded, and harmonized to ensure cross-country comparability. A country identifier was added before appending all datasets (unweighted n = 311,332). Internal consistency was assessed using descriptive statistics and cross-tabulations (see Supplementary Material 2). A full description of the pooling procedures is provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata variables were applied using the svyset command in STATA to account for the complex sample design and restore representativeness. Missing observations were excluded. Pooled prevalence estimates were obtained using random-effects meta-analysis and presented with forest plots. Given the hierarchical structure of the MICS data, with women nested within clusters and countries, multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted with fixed effects for factors and random effects for clusters and countries to address survey heterogeneity. Because cluster-level weights were unavailable in public MICS datasets, household- and cluster-level weights were approximated using a published power allocation method (α = 0.50) [22]. Univariable analyses produced crude odds ratios (COR), and variables with p < 0.25 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model; those with p < 0.05 (Wald test) were retained in the final model. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (Additional File 1: Table 2), and model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. 2).

Model comparison was conducted using Akaike’s information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, deviance, and the likelihood ratio test. The multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model demonstrated better overall performance than the standard logistic regression model across all comparison measures (Supplementary Material 1: Table 3). All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 15 MP.

Results

Study Population Characteristics and Descriptive Findings

The weighted sample included 311,873 women aged 18–49 years, of whom 4.6% (n = 14,356) reported functional difficulty (Table 1). The median age was 30 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 24–38).

Across all six functional domains, rural women consistently reported higher levels of difficulty, especially in the most severe category (“cannot do at all”), compared with urban women (Fig. 1) . Functional difficulty increased with age, reaching 9.8% among women aged 42–49 (Table 2). Prevalence was higher among women with no/pre-primary/primary education (5.7%), those in poor households (5.2%), and women exposed to IPV (5.2%). Smaller households (1–5 members) also showed higher prevalence (4.7%). Improved water, sanitation, and availability of water and soap for handwashing were associated with lower prevalence, whereas open defecation showed a higher prevalence of 6.4%. Women satisfied with life had a lower prevalence (3.6%), as did those with mobile phones or household internet access. Regionally, prevalence was highest in Asia (5.7%) and lowest in both Africa and Oceania/South America (3.6%).

Pooled Prevalence

The pooled prevalence of women’s functional difficulty across 37 LMICs was 5.34% (95% CI: 4.58–6.11) (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. 3). By income level, prevalence was 4.94% (95% CI: 3.51–6.36) in upper-middle-income, 5.13% (95% CI: 4.19–6.06) in lower-middle-income, and 6.44% (95% CI: 4.01–8.87) in low-income countries.

For the 22 countries included in the main analysis, the pooled prevalence was 5.15% (95% CI: 4.33–5.98) (Fig. 2). Sierra Leone had the lowest prevalence (1.33%), while the Central African Republic had the highest (13.39%).

Factors Associated with Functional Difficulty among Women of Reproductive Age

Women aged 42–49 years had 4.75 times (AOR = 4.75, 95% CI: 4.03–5.62) higher odds of functional difficulty than those aged 18–25 years. Compared with women with higher education, those with no/pre-primary/primary and secondary/vocational education had 1.95 times (AOR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.64–2.34) and 1.47 times (AOR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.24–1.73) higher odds, respectively. Women never in a union, urban residents, and those exposed to IPV had 1.34 times (AOR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.18–1.53), 1.60 times (AOR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.41–1.82), and 1.14 times (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.04–1.26), higher odds, respectively (Table 2).

Women living in households with 6–15 and 1–5 members had 1.42 times (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.03–1.94) and 1.63 times (AOR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.18–2.24) higher odds, respectively, than those in households with 16–50 members. Women with more than seven children had 1.44 times (AOR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.02–2.04) higher odds than those with no children. Marriage before age 18 was associated with 1.29 times (AOR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.03–1.61) higher odds, while having no live birth in the past two years was associated with 1.36 times (AOR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.24–1.49) higher odds. Unimproved sanitation was associated with 1.17 times (AOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.02–1.34) higher odds. Neutral and unhappy life satisfaction were associated with 1.34 times (AOR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.22–1.48) and 2.09 times (AOR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.85–2.38) higher odds, respectively. Living in male-headed households (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.02–1.27) and not using a mobile phone (AOR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.20–1.48) were also associated with increased odds. Regionally, women in Africa (AOR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.96–2.55) and Oceania/South America (AOR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.15–1.77) had higher odds than those in Asia.

Conversely, women in households with heads aged 26–33 (AOR = 0.74), 34–41 (AOR = 0.78), and 42–49 years (AOR = 0.80) had lower odds compared with those in households headed by individuals aged 15–23 years. Lack of home internet access was also associated with 0.89 times (AOR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80-1.00) lower odds of functional difficulty.

Discussion

Our findings from 22 LMICs show that one in 20 women of reproductive age experienced functional difficulty, with more than a 10-fold difference in prevalence between the Central African Republic (highest) and Sierra Leone (lowest). Such variation across countries of similar economic status underscores cross-country inequalities in women’s functional health. Consistent with global estimates, women in low-income countries experienced a higher burden than those in upper-middle-income countries, likely due to limited healthcare and rehabilitation capacity, poor access to assistive devices, and weaker social protection systems, which can lead to earlier onset and more rapid progression of functional limitations [2, 23–31].

Our study identified several risk factors associated with women’s functional difficulty. Increasing age was associated with higher odds of difficulty, likely reflecting age-related physiological decline and a greater burden of chronic conditions [19, 32]. Education showed a protective association; higher education attainment was associated with lower odds, possibly through improved health literacy and healthcare utilization [33, 34]. At the same time, women with difficulty may face barriers to education due to inaccessible learning environments [35, 36]. Women never in a union also had higher odds of difficulty, possibly related to stigma [37]. Previous studies suggest that different impairment types are unevenly distributed across marital groups, with mental impairments more common among divorced women and visual impairments among widowed women [38]. Interestingly, urban women were more likely to report difficulty than rural women. Despite better healthcare access, urban environments may contribute to higher risks through sedentary lifestyles, pollution, psychosocial stress, and age-related chronic disease [39]. Previous studies report mixed urban–rural patterns, with some showing higher prevalence in rural areas and others finding higher visual, hearing, and limb difficulties in urban areas [40–44].

Reproductive and household factors were significantly associated with women’s functional health. Higher parity and early marriage were linked to increased odds, potentially reflecting cumulative physical strain, constrained autonomy, vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections, and psychological distress related to early childbearing and child loss [45–49]. Women who had not given birth in the past two years were more likely to report difficulty, consistent with evidence linking functional limitations to obstetric complications that may affect fertility [50]. Unimproved sanitation further exacerbated risk through increased exposure to infections and physical hazards [51].

Psychosocial conditions were also relevant. Women with functional difficulty reported lower life satisfaction, in line with evidence linking functional limitations to depression, stigma, psychological burden, and increased vulnerability to suicidal behavior [52, 53]. Exposure to IPV also showed a strong association with functional difficulty, suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship. Women with limitations are more vulnerable to abuse due to dependency and social isolation, while violence itself causes long-term physical and psychological impairment [54–58]. However, in many LMICs, support services are often fragmented and inaccessible to women with functional difficulty [59–61].

Digital access disparities were evident. Consistent with international data showing large disability-related gaps in smartphone ownership (80% in Nigeria, 73% in Bangladesh, and 63% in Kenya), women with functional difficulty were less likely to own or use a mobile phone, possibly due to high device costs, limited awareness of accessibility features, and low digital literacy [62]. Conversely, the observed association between internet use and functional difficulty may reflect reverse causality, as women with impairments increasingly rely on digital platforms for health information and social support [63]. Regionally, we observed a higher prevalence of functional difficulty among women in Africa than in Asia, consistent with WHO estimates [2].

A key strength of this study is the use of large, nationally representative pooled data, which enabled robust cross-country comparisons. Additionally, the multilevel analytical approach accounted for clustering and between-country variation. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; some clinical variables were unavailable due to the use of secondary data, and functional difficulty was self-reported, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias.

In summary, women with functional difficulty face substantial challenges in LMICs, with higher risk observed among older women of reproductive age, those with lower educational attainment, women not in a union, urban residents, and those exposed to IPV. Reproductive history, household conditions, sanitation, digital access, and life satisfaction were also associated with functional difficulty. Policy responses should prioritize inclusive education, accessible reproductive and maternal health services, and strengthened protection against IPV, alongside investments in sanitation and digital inclusion to address inequalities in women’s functional health in LMICs.
