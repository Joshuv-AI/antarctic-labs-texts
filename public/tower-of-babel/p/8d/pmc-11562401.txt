Polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis after a primary cesarean section in a low-risk patient: A case report and literature review
Authors: Nathalie Chamseddine, Hanan Aghar, Zeinab Haidar, Ghadir Aoud, Amir Ibrahim, Ghina Ghazeeri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceNecrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare life-threatening soft tissue infection, with only few obstetrical cases reported in the literature.Case presentationWe report a case of a previously healthy female who developed polymicrobial NF on day 4 post an uncomplicated primary cesarean delivery, who presented initially with fever, chills, and abdominal pain.Clinical discussionThe patient underwent a life-saving debridement of surgical wound with subsequent positive wound culture with polymicrobial growth and pathology suggestive of necrotizing fasciitis. Patient recovered in the intensive care unit with smooth postoperative course.ConclusionEarly diagnosis and presumed aggressive intervention with early surgical debridement and supportive therapy were key factors favoring the good prognosis seen in this patient.

Introduction

We discuss here a case of necrotizing fasciitis after a primary cesarean delivery in a low-risk previously healthy patient performed at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC).

Literature review

Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs), particularly necrotizing fasciitis (NF) are rare, yet rapidly progressing life-threatening soft tissue infections. Divided initially into two microbiologic categories, NF is mainly caused by group A streptococcus (GAS), gram positive or gram-negative pathogens, and to a lesser extent fungi [1]. While type I is mainly polymicrobial and type II is monomicrobial, a third type (type III) of aggressive NF caused mainly by marine Vibrio species has been recently described in the literature [2]. Very few cases of NF caused by fungal infections have been described in the literature. To our knowledge, there are no such reported cases following obstetrical surgeries [3].

With a mortality rate of up to 70 %, timely diagnosis and intervention are crucial in the management of NF [4]. NF affects 0.4 in every 100,000 people per year in the United States [5]. However, only a few cases were reported after a cesarean section over the last two decades [5].

The majority of NF cases develop in patients with pre-existing comorbidities, such as diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, renal or liver disease, malignancy among others.

Case discussion

This is a case of a 28-year-old, previously healthy female who underwent a primary low transverse cesarean delivery for failure of induction of labor at 40 weeks and 1 day of gestation.

The patient had no pertinent medical or surgical history, with a borderline BMI of 26 kg/m2 at the time of delivery. She delivered a healthy female infant. Operative and postpartum courses were uncomplicated, and the patient left on day 2 post-operatively in clinical and hemodynamic stable conditions. On day 4 post-operatively, she presented to our emergency department (ED) with a fever reaching 38.5 °C, chills, and burning pain at the incision site that was not relieved by pain medications.

Upon presentation, patient was afebrile. 2 h later, she developed a fever of 38.5 °C. Persistent sinus tachycardia reaching 160 bpm was noted throughout her stay in the ED despite several antipyretics and pain medication administration.

On physical exam, the abdomen was soft and distended with diffuse tenderness over all four quadrants. No crepitus was noted. Inspection of the surgical wound revealed mild erythema surrounding the wound. Laboratory workup upon presentation showed a white blood cell count of 2400/cu.mm, with a neutrophil count of 77 %, a lymphocyte count of 17 %, procalcitonin of 1.41 ng/mL, C-reactive protein of 234 mg/L, and a normal basic chemistry laboratory testing.

In the ED, patient received a shot of Amikacin, and broad-spectrum antibiotics with Vancomycin and Tazocin were initiated. A Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis was done and showed fat stranding at the surgical site with small amount of fluid and gas pockets extending superiorly and laterally along the anterior abdominal wall subcutaneous tissues and muscles, with no evidence of abscess, findings related to post-operative changes (Fig. 2). Right after completing the CT scan, the patient deteriorated clinically with marked lethargy and worsening abdominal pain out-of-proportion to physical exam so a decision for a life-saving exploration with possible debridement was made.Fig. 2CT scan with contrast showing gas pockets along the anterior abdominal wall (shown in asterisk).Fig. 2

Upon exploration of the previous scar, a significant amount of purulent foul smelling typical “greyish dirty dishwater like” discharge was noted and drained. The abdominal fascia was found to be partially necrotic. Debridement was done by plastic surgery team until reaching viable tissue. The hysterotomy site along with the rectus muscles were explored and were unremarkable. Copious irrigation with normal saline, hydrogen peroxide and gentamycin was performed [7]. Two Jackson-Pratt (JP) drains were inserted. An intra-operative diagnosis of an early necrotizing fasciitis was established. The wound was then closed with polydioxanone (PDS) suture in an interrupted fashion. The reason why we opted for closure of the wound is mainly because of early detection and low suspicion for the need of further exploration.

Intra-operative cultures grew unusual organisms: Entorococcous faecium and Streptococcus Anginosus susceptible to Vancomycin. The wound culture taken in the ED grew Pseudomonas stutzeri. Final pathology confirmed the diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis. Post-debridement, she was maintained on Vancomycin, Meropenem and Clindamycin.

Daily dressing change with hydrogen peroxide and normal saline was performed. On day 6 post debridement, the right JP drain was removed. The patient left the hospital on day 9 post-surgical debridement in stable conditions with the left JP drain in place draining 25 mL of serous fluid. She was discharged on Ciprofloxacin and Augmentin for 5 additional days.

The sutures were removed on day 19 post-op. Fucidin and mebo cream were advised. Patient recovered fully on day 20 post-op. She was doing serial laboratory work up with a notable decrease in inflammatory markers.

Discussion

Necrotizing fasciitis is a rare, challenging post operative complication, with only few cases reported in the literature pertaining to the obstetrical field [8]. A PubMed search identified 13 reported cases from 1990 till 2018 with a search of “necrotizing fasciitis following caesarean delivery” [9]. To our knowledge, the exact number comprising all necrotizing fasciitis following an obstetrical procedure remains unavailable. In fact, most NF cases reported in obstetrical and gynecological settings are among comorbid patients with obesity and diabetes mellitus, and hypertension among other conditions [3,[10], [11], [12]]. In the obstetrical scene specifically, a longitudinal cohort study found an association between NF and pregnancy complications such as preterm labor and gestational diabetes for at least five years after delivery [13]. Other specialties have reported cases of similar gravity. For example, Gilardi et al. reported a case of NF after breast augmentation mastopexy combined with Brazilian Butt Lift in a healthy patient eventually necessitating extensive debridement [3].

NF is a rapidly spreading, life-threatening condition involving all the layers of the tissue compartments as well as deeper tissues in the pelvis [14]. Caused by “flesh-eating bacteria”, it is mainly categorized based on the microbiology and the evidence of gas in the tissues. Type I is polymicrobial caused by aerobic and/or anaerobic bacteria, mostly affecting adults with comorbidities, whereas type II is usually monomicrobial caused mainly by beta-hemolytic streptococci (GAS), occurring in healthy individuals with no underlying comorbidities [15,16]. Evident risk factors of NF include major trauma, immunosuppression, HIV, malnutrition, uncontrolled diabetes, malignancy, and obesity among others. In women, pregnancy, childbirth or even pregnancy loss can be risk factors. There are some cases as ours with identified NF among patients devoid of identifiable risk factors [17].

Necrotizing fasciitis can occur in immunocompromised individuals, including those with rheumatic diseases, especially those on treatment (corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs). A review of 18 cases from the literature supports this association and underscores the need for heightened clinical suspicion in these patients. The clinical symptoms in these patients can be subtle, adding more to the challenges in diagnosis NF [13]. Although our patient was previously healthy, a strong family history of rheumatologic diseases in her first-degree relatives was noted; hence a possible underlying non-manifesting rheumatologic was hypothesized. However, after recovery, the negative tests for both rheumatologic serologies as well as for HIV likely ruled out these conditions.

NF presents as an acute and rapidly deteriorating condition. Therefore, diagnosis and intervention are time critical. The most common clinical manifestations are fever, erythema, edema, pain out-of-proportion to physical examination, crepitus and/or systemic toxicity and end-organ failure. NF is a clinical diagnosis and even a slight suspicion should prompt adequate and aggressive management. Although diagnosis should not solely rely on imaging or laboratory testing, radiographic plain film imaging may demonstrate gas in the muscles and superficial fat in 35 % of the cases [18]. In our case, although the radiographic evidence of small amount of fluid and gas were suggestive of post-surgical changes, exploration was still highly advised given the patient's hemodynamic instability. Laboratory workups showed non-specific signs of infection, with leukocytosis, hyponatremia, elevated inflammatory markers (c-reactive protein, procalcitonin). An elevation in serum creatinine kinase or liver enzyme (mainly aspartate aminotransferases) is helpful in distinguishing deep infection from superficial cellulitis [19].

Due to its challenging diagnosis, a Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing fasciitis (LRINEC) score has been developed in 2004. The score comprises of six laboratory parameters: CRP, hemoglobin level, total leukocyte count, serum sodium, serum creatinine and blood glucose level [20]. A score < 6 is interpreted as low risk for NF, whereas >8 is strongly predictive of the disease. However, this score is not highly specific and should not be relied on solely for diagnosis. In our case, the patient had a LRINEC score of 5.

The mainstay treatment of NF is early and aggressive surgical debridement along with broad spectrum antibiotics.

It is worth mentioning that the vast majority of cases with NF following cesarean section reported in the literature had a more severe and aggressive presentation than our patient, whether by laboratory testing, clinically or by findings on CT scan. Although most of NF cases following cesarean section necessitated several serial debridement surgeries or open wound management, we strongly believe that the early presentation to the ED and the early intervention in our patient favored a positive outcome compared to the cases previously described in the literature.
