Beyond the algorithm: reframing biomarker-guided antibiotic stewardship with behavioural insights
Authors: Patrycja Sleboda, Hailey Warren, Michael K Mansour
Published: 2026

Antibiotic resistance is a significant threat to public health, and infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria are becoming increasingly common. A major driver of resistance is inappropriate use of antimicrobials, often without a clear indication.1,2 One of the most common indications for antibiotic prescription is lower respiratory tract infections. Due to delays or a lack of pathogen identification and non-specific clinical and radiographic findings, clinicians are left without definitive evidence of the need for antibiotics, leading to their overuse.3 In the setting of diagnostic uncertainty, clinicians empirically prescribe antibacterial treatment, a pattern that reflects both rational risk management and well-documented cognitive biases.

To address this uncertainty, several novel biomarkers have been studied to distinguish between viral and bacterial aetiologies, thereby improving targeted antibiotic decisions. Procalcitonin (PCT), a pro-inflammatory cytokine that is below 0.1 ng/mL in healthy individuals and upregulated in response to endotoxemia, has demonstrated clinically meaningful sensitivity and specificity in predicting bacterial infections. RCTs comparing PCT-guided antibiotic algorithms to standard management show a significant reduction in antibiotic exposure without increasing mortality or treatment failure.4–6 Despite this body of evidence for PCT use, decisions to initiate or discontinue antibiotics are not driven solely by biomarker values. Rather, they are embedded within social, organizational and psychological contexts that shape how information is interpreted and acted upon. Consistent with this viewpoint, a large prospective multicentre trial conducted in the USA did not demonstrate a reduction in antibiotic use with PCT-guided management alone, despite robust prior evidence.7 The authors noted that effective implementation of PCT may require additional educational and structural support.7

Thus, antibiotic stewardship is not solely a technical challenge of improving diagnostics but also a behavioural one. Understanding how clinicians interpret, trust and respond to biomarker-based recommendations, particularly within complex team environments, may be essential to realizing the full potential of PCT-guided stewardship.

Traditional stewardship approaches, including clinical guidelines, education and the use of audit-and-feedback, have shown modest and variable effects on prescribing behaviour.8,9 PCT-guided stewardship offers a timely and individualized strategy that enables earlier discontinuation of antibiotics in patients with pneumonia. Importantly, it also aligns naturally with principles from behavioural economics, which offer complementary tools to enhance stewardship efforts without restricting clinician autonomy.

Specifically, PCT-guided treatment recommendations supported by expert review can leverage clinician trust—a key determinant of risk perception and medical decision-making10,11 and support clinical judgments that are often complex and not purely rational. Thus, incorporating PCT into stewardship programmes through guided interpretation by a trusted governing body, such as an antimicrobial stewardship team, may therefore increase adherence and, as suggested by prior work, reduce unnecessary antibiotic use.12

When designing a PCT-guided stewardship intervention, several factors warrant consideration, including the type of recommendation and its presentation, the framing of stewardship messages, team structure and peer comparison.

Adherence appears to vary by recommendation type, as found in an internal study at the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH), a major teaching hospital in Boston, Massachusetts (MGH IRB 2019P000362). B·R·A·H·M·S PCT was assayed on a COBAS Roche platform located in the core lab with a turnaround time of 2 h. In this study, medical team providers followed an ASP recommendation to continue antibiotics based on PCT at 97.3% (93.3, 98.9; 144/148) of cases and completed a defined, shorter treatment duration at 96.5% (88.1, 99.0; 55/57) concordance. The recommendation to stop antibiotics after the first two PCT < 0.25 ng/mL (N = 113) was only followed in about half of the cases (47.8%; 38.8, 56.9), and a second recommendation to stop antibiotics on the subsequent day resulted in cessation of antimicrobials in 1 out of 7 patients (13.8%; 5.5, 30.6; 4/29). These data demonstrate a known asymmetry bias. Specifically, the recommendations to continue antibiotics or complete a predefined treatment course are often readily accepted, whereas advice to discontinue therapy, even when supported by repeatedly low PCT values and clinical stability, may encounter greater resistance. This asymmetry likely reflects classical status quo bias and loss aversion,13 whereby stopping antibiotics may feel riskier than continuing them, despite evidence to the contrary. These observations suggest the need to examine adherence by recommendation type and to optimize how recommendations are presented. Behavioural economics offers several promising approaches, including the use of ‘nudges’ to guide decision-making while preserving clinician autonomy. Framing effects, that is, presenting outcomes as gains rather than losses, are known to influence clinical decisions.14–17 Similarly, PCT-guided recommendations could emphasize either the harms of antibiotic overuse or the safety and benefits of discontinuing unnecessary therapy to improve adherence and decision quality.

Team structure is another underappreciated determinant of stewardship effectiveness. Clinical decisions in hospital settings are frequently made by teams rather than individuals, and research in behavioural science suggests that group decision-making can both mitigate and amplify cognitive bias.18,19 Clinical team structure may differ in how they respond to stewardship recommendations, with larger, multi-clinician teams appearing more likely to adhere to and follow guidance than smaller teams or individual hospitalists. In fact, larger teams may diffuse individual responsibility and facilitate acceptance of external recommendations, whereas smaller teams or individual clinicians may rely more on personal experience and availability heuristics.20–24 Variation in adherence across team structures observed in prior stewardship initiatives raises important questions. Are larger teams more likely to follow stewardship recommendations because they engage in more deliberative, data-driven decision-making? Or does conformity to perceived consensus, sometimes described as groupthink, play a role?25 Although evidence generally suggests that groups outperform individuals in complex decision-making tasks,19,26 understanding the mechanisms underlying these effects is critical for optimizing stewardship design.

Clinical decisions are shaped not only by data but also by contextual, psychological, social and emotional factors.27 Interventions tailored to individuals or smaller teams may therefore benefit from communication strategies that emphasize the effectiveness of PCT-guided recommendations or leverage social influence. Social norm feedback, for example, has been shown to reduce inappropriate antibiotic prescribing by highlighting how an individual’s behaviour compares with that of peers.9,28 Building on this approach, stewardship systems could incorporate peer comparison or consensus cues (e.g. ‘Most clinicians discontinue antibiotics at this PCT level’) to encourage adherence.

Overall, stewardship strategies that fail to account for team context risk uneven uptake. A one-size-fits-all approach may succeed in some clinical settings while proving ineffective in others.

In summary, biomarkers such as procalcitonin provide powerful, evidence-based guidance for antibiotic stewardship, but their impact depends critically on how clinicians engage with them. Integrating behavioural insights, including trust-based communication, strategic framing, social comparison and attention to team structure, offers a promising path to improving antibiotic decision-making while preserving clinician autonomy.29 Moving beyond the algorithm to address the human factors that underlie prescribing behaviour is essential to reducing unnecessary antibiotic use and combating antimicrobial resistance in everyday clinical practice.
