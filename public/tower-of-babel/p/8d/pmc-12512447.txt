A meta-analysis of efficacy and safety of parietal pleurectomy versus pleural abrasion in treating spontaneous pneumothorax
Authors: Kai Yang, Chen Wang, Qi Wang, Yongzhi Liu, Wei Cao, Jinlong Zhang, Haochi Li, Dacheng Jin, Yunjiu Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous pneumothorax is a common benign disease. Its treatment aims to restore the collapsed lungs, relieve symptoms, and prevent recurrence. Currently, video-assisted thoracoscopic bullectomy combined with parietal pleural fixation has become the mainstream treatment method. However, the choice between parietal pleurectomy and pleural abrasion remains a subject of continuous debate. Consequently, we conducted a meta-analysis to assess the differences in perioperative efficacy and complications between the two surgical methods combined with bullectomy.MethodsRelevant literatures were searched in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and The Cochrane Library from the establishment date of each database to February 1, 2025. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the literatures were screened. The meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.3 and Stata 16MP software. The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing the risk of bias and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale were employed to evaluate the quality of the included studies.ResultsA total of 15 literatures were included, involving 2732 patients. The meta-analysis results showed that the postoperative pain degree of the two groups was similar [MD = 0.32, 95%CI (−0.13 ~ 0.77), P = 0.17]. However, the pleural abrasion group had shorter operation time [MD = 15.87, 95%CI (11.30 ~ 20.44), P < 0.0001], less intraoperative blood loss [MD = 14.62, 95%CI (8.58 ~ 20.66), P < 0.0001], less total postoperative drainage volume [MD = 67.82, 95%CI (10.26 ~ 125.38), P=0.02], shorter postoperative drainage time [MD = 0.38, 95%CI (0.05 ~ 0.72), P༝0.02], and shorter hospital stay [MD = 0.25, 95%CI (0.04 ~ 0.46), P༝0.02] compared with the parietal pleurectomy group, and the differences were statistically significant. In terms of safety, the parietal pleurectomy group would increase the incidence of postoperative hemorrhage [OR = 3.99, 95%CI (1.49 ~ 10.65), P = 0.006], but there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of postoperative atelectasis (P = 0.47), pulmonary leakage (P = 0.95) and pulmonary infection (P = 0.05). In addition, the parietal pleurectomy group could effectively reduce the long-term recurrence rate of patients [OR = 0.56, 95%CI (0.41 ~ 0.77), P = 0.0003], and the difference was statistically significant.ConclusionsFor patients with spontaneous pneumothorax and bullae, parietal pleurectomy inevitably increases the perioperative burden and prolongs the hospital stay of patients, but can effectively prevent postoperative recurrence. The two surgical methods have similar safety, but parietal pleurectomy will increase the risk of postoperative hemorrhage. Clinically, the optimal treatment plan should be selected based on the individual characteristics of patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03157-4.

Background

Spontaneous pneumothorax (SP) is a prevalent thoracic condition that occurs due to the rupture of lung tissue or visceral pleura in the absence of external trauma, leading to gas accumulation in the pleural cavity and subsequent lung compression and collapse. This often manifests as acute symptoms such as chest tightness, chest pain, and dyspnea [1]. Among the contributing factors, the presence of pulmonary bullae plays a significant role in the development of spontaneous pneumothorax. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and congenital pulmonary dysplasia can compromise the structural integrity and functional capacity of lung tissue, reducing the elasticity of the bulla walls. During respiration, the intra-bullous pressure progressively increases, causing expansion and eventual formation of bullae [2]. In patients with these underlying conditions, sudden increases in intrapulmonary pressure, such as those caused by severe coughing, forceful defecation, or strenuous physical activity, may result in the rupture of excessively thinned bulla walls, thereby triggering spontaneous pneumothorax [3].

For patients with spontaneous pneumothorax accompanied by pulmonary bullae, early closed thoracic drainage or aspiration can provide rapid symptom relief, however, the long-term recurrence rate remains relatively high. Therefore, thoracoscopic wedge resection of the lung to remove the diseased pulmonary bullae is the main treatment option. This procedure has progressively evolved from traditional three-port surgery to single-port thoracoscopic surgery, which not only reduces postoperative pain but also decreases the incidence of complications [4, 5]. Combining bullae resection with parietal pleurodesis can effectively prevent disease recurrence [6, 7]. The clinical implementation schemes of parietal pleural fixation are diverse, including mechanical methods [8](parietal pleurectomy, pleural abrasion), chemical methods [9](slipstone powder spraying, sclerosing agent injection), and biological methods [10](autologous blood fixation), etc. Among these, mechanical pleurodesis induces pleural adhesion through physical stimulation, offering a gentler mechanism of action compared to chemical methods. This results in a significantly lower incidence of postoperative adverse reactions and foreign body granulomas. In comparison to biological methods, mechanical pleurodesis demonstrates lower operational costs and superior efficacy [11]. Despite its proven efficacy and safety, leading to widespread clinical adoption, controversies persist regarding specific fixation techniques, particularly concerning the complications associated with parietal pleurectomy and the recurrence rate following pleural abrasion.

Therefore, in this study, we performed a comprehensive meta-analysis to evaluate the perioperative efficacy and complications associated with two types of parietal pleurodesis following thoracoscopic bullae resection combined with either pleural abrasion or parietal pleurectomy for the treatment of spontaneous pneumothorax with bullae.

Methods

Design

This meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and was registered on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251007503).

Criteria for literature inclusion and exclusion and observation indicators

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants: patients diagnosed with primary spontaneous pneumothorax and pulmonary bullae via imaging examination, who met the surgical indications for thoracoscopic bullae resection and pleurodesis; (2) Intervention: the experimental group underwent parietal pleurectomy in addition to the standard procedure, while the control group underwent pleural abrasion. The surgical techniques for both groups were described in detail; (3) Study design: all included studies were prospective or retrospective cohort studies, or randomized controlled trials.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients who received conservative treatment, underwent thoracotomy, or experienced secondary pneumothorax; (2) Studies comparing intervention measures that did not involve mechanical pleurodesis; (3) Literature types such as reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and experiential summaries; (4) Publications from which useful data could not be extracted or the full text was inaccessible.

Observation indicators: (1) Perioperative data encompassed operation duration, intraoperative blood loss volume, postoperative pain scores, total postoperative drainage volume, postoperative drainage duration, and length of hospital stay; (2) Postoperative complications comprised pulmonary air leakage, atelectasis, postoperative hemorrhage, pulmonary infection, and postoperative recurrence.

Literature search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and The Cochrane Library from their inception to February 1, 2025. The search strategy involved combining MeSH terms with free-text keywords. The MeSH terms included “spontaneous pneumothorax,” “pleurodesis,” “parietal pleurectomy,” “pleural abrasion,” and related concepts. The complete search string used for the literature search in PubMed is provided in Appendix.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two thoracic surgeons independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the literature for initial screening. The articles that met the inclusion criteria were further evaluated by reading the full texts and subsequently included based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through consensus discussions. Data extraction from the included literature encompassed the following: (1) Basic information: author details, study duration, patient demographics (gender and average age), sample size, and lesion location; (2) Outcome measures: operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage time, total postoperative drainage volume, postoperative pain scores, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and long-term recurrence rates.

Literature quality evaluation

The quality of the included literature was systematically assessed. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk-of-bias assessment tool was utilized, which evaluates potential biases in random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. Non-randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), a validated instrument consisting of eight items with a maximum score of 9. The NOS evaluates three main aspects: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of outcomes. Studies scoring ≥ 7 were classified as high-quality, those scoring 5–6 as moderate-quality, and those scoring < 5 as low-quality.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.3 was employed to conduct the meta-analysis of all extracted data, and the Q-statistic test was utilized to assess the heterogeneity among studies. In cases where no significant heterogeneity was observed (P ≥ 0.1 and I²<50%), a fixed-effects model was applied for combined analysis. Conversely, if significant heterogeneity was detected, a random-effects model was adopted for the analysis. For binary variables, odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, while for continuous variables, mean differences (MD) along with 95% CIs were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P value less than 0.05. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the one-at-a-time elimination method to determine the source of heterogeneity. Additionally, a funnel plot and Egger’s test were employed to assess the presence of publication bias in the included studies.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 907 articles were identified through database searches, including PubMed (n = 257), Web of Science (n = 273), Embase (n = 356), and The Cochrane Library (n= 21). Following an initial review of titles and abstracts, 496 articles were excluded. The remaining 59 articles underwent full-text screening, during which 34 articles were excluded due to ineligible patients or interventions, and 10 articles were excluded because the required data could not be extracted. Ultimately, 15 articles were included in the analysis [12–26]. The literature screening process and results are presented in Fig. 1.

Meta-analysis results

Operation time

Seven articles included in the analysis reported the operation times for both groups [14, 19–24]. Following a heterogeneity test (I2 = 71%, P = 0.002), a random-effects model was selected for data synthesis. The results indicated that the parietal pleurectomy group had a significantly longer operation time [MD = 15.87, 95%CI (11.30 ~ 20.44)], with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.00001), as depicted in Fig. 3.

Intraoperative blood loss

Intraoperative blood loss was reported in three studies [14, 21, 24]. Following a heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.71), a fixed-effect model was selected for analysis. The results indicated that the parietal pleurectomy group experienced significantly greater intraoperative blood loss [MD = 14.62, 95%CI (8.58 ~ 20.66)], with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.00001), as depicted in Fig. 4.

Postoperative pain score

Three studies utilized a visual analogue scale to quantify the intensity of postoperative pain [20, 21, 24]. Following the heterogeneity test (I2 = 81%, P = 0.005), a random-effects model was selected for analysis. The findings indicated that the intensity of postoperative pain was comparable between the two groups [MD = 0.32, 95%CI (−0.13 ~ 0.77)], with no statistically significant difference observed (P = 0.17). Postoperative pulmonary leakage

Six studies reported postoperative pulmonary leakage [14, 15, 20, 21, 23, 24]. Following the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.79), a fixed-effect model was selected for analysis. Two articles reported postoperative pulmonary infections [21, 25]. Following the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.51), a fixed-effect model was selected for analysis. Postoperative hemorrhage

Six studies reported postoperative hemorrhage [15, 19–21, 24, 25]. Following the heterogeneity test (I2 = 0%, P = 0.80), a fixed-effect model was selected for analysis. The results demonstrated that the incidence of postoperative hemorrhage in the pleural abrasion group was significantly lower than that in the parietal pleurectomy group, with statistical significance [OR = 3.99, 95%CI(1.49 ~ 10.65), P = 0.006]. This suggests that parietal pleurectomy may increase the risk of postoperative hemorrhage in patients, as illustrated in Fig. 12.

Postoperative recurrence status

Fifteen studies were included in the analysis, which reported on the long-term recurrence rates among patients [12–26]. Following a heterogeneity test (I2 = 22%, P = 0.21), the fixed-effects model was selected for analysis. The results demonstrated that the long-term recurrence rate in the pleural abrasion group was significantly higher compared to the parietal pleurectomy group, with a statistically significant difference [OR = 0.56, 95%CI(0.41 ~ 0.77), P = 0.0003]. Second, we hypothesized that the extent of pleurotomy (e.g., “partial parietal pleurotomy” versus “extensive parietal pleurotomy”) might serve as a critical factor influencing our findings. Among the 15 studies included in the analysis, 12 explicitly described partial pleurotomy performed above the fifth intercostal space, whereas the remaining three did not specify the surgical extent. These three studies only documented long-term patient recurrence and demonstrated no significant changes in results upon their exclusion. However, due to inadequate descriptions of the surgical scope in the original studies, it was not feasible to perform a subgroup analysis of relevant indicators.

Subgroup analysis

Due to the significant heterogeneity in the surgical duration, postoperative pain score, postoperative drainage time, and total postoperative drainage volume among the studies, in order to minimize the influence of combining data from different study types on the overall analysis results, the above data were classified and analyzed in subgroups based on retrospective studies and randomized controlled trials. The results showed that compared with the combined analysis results, after subgroup analysis based on study types, the heterogeneity of each outcome indicator did not change significantly, indicating that combining data from different study types did not affect the overall analysis results.

Sensitivity analysis

For observation indicators exhibiting high heterogeneity (I²>50%) in this study, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the source of heterogeneity. The findings indicated that the heterogeneity in operation time, drainage time, and VAS pain scores might primarily stem from the study by Jin-Shing Chen (2012). Unlike other studies, this particular investigation exclusively included patients with multiple (> 3) or unidentifiable bullae. This variation in patient baseline characteristics introduced systematic bias into these measures; upon excluding this study, the heterogeneity was substantially reduced while the combined effect size remained robust. Similarly, the study by Adel K. Ayed (2000) was identified as the primary source of heterogeneity for the total postoperative drainage volume index. The reported drainage volumes for both groups in this study were relatively low, with no significant intergroup difference, leading to a marked reduction in heterogeneity upon exclusion. For the remaining observation indicators, the results exhibited no significant changes following the exclusion of any single study, thereby affirming the reliability of the conclusions drawn in this study. Publication bias

For the outcome indicators with a large number of included studies (length of hospital stay and long-term recurrence), Review Manager was utilized to generate funnel plots for assessing potential publication bias. The results indicated that the included studies were symmetrically distributed on both sides of the funnel plot (Fig. 15), suggesting an absence of significant publication bias. Additionally, the Egger linear regression test was conducted using Stata software, and the findings were consistent: neither the length of hospital stay (P = 0.348) nor long-term recurrence (P = 0.494) exhibited evidence of substantial publication bias.

Discussion

Spontaneous pneumothorax represents one of the most common emergencies in thoracic surgery. The primary objective of its treatment is to eliminate lung air leakage, promote lung re-expansion, and minimize the risk of recurrence. While early closed thoracic drainage can significantly alleviate symptoms due to its ease of operation, reduced pain, and patient acceptance, it is associated with a high recurrence rate and cannot provide a definitive cure [27]. Consequently, surgical intervention remains the principal treatment modality for these patients. With advancements in minimally invasive techniques, thoracoscopic bullae resection has emerged as a method that substantially reduces surgical trauma and postoperative complications, gradually supplanting traditional thoracotomy as the standard procedure for spontaneous pneumothorax patients with pulmonary bullae [28]. Despite the advantages of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), 5–10% of patients may still experience recurrence of spontaneous pneumothorax. This recurrence can be effectively mitigated by combining VATS with parietal pleurodesis following bullae resection [29, 30].

Mechanical pleurodesis and chemical pleurodesis are the most frequently employed fixation methods in clinical practice. Chemical pleurodesis is simpler to perform and serves as an alternative option for patients with poor surgical tolerance. By administering talc, erythromycin, bleomycin, or other agents, aseptic allergic pleurisy can be induced, leading to adhesion between the parietal and visceral pleurae [27]. While certain meta-analyses have demonstrated that chemical pleurodesis offers advantages in terms of recurrence rate [31], some studies indicate that this method may elicit more severe reactions, thereby increasing the risk of side effects such as chest pain and fever. Additionally, its therapeutic efficacy is influenced by factors including the patient’s condition, injection technique, and drug selection and dosage [32]. Mechanical pleurodesis is particularly suited for patients with recurrent or complex pneumothorax. This procedure involves physically stimulating the pleural surface to induce bleeding and exudation, thereby triggering a localized inflammatory response and fibrin deposition, which promotes adhesion between the visceral and parietal pleurae to achieve fixation. Currently, numerous factors influence the choice between these two fixation methods, and no definitive recommendations exist. Further high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to address this issue [33].

This study focuses on comparing mechanical pleural fixation methods, primarily parietal pleurectomy and pleural abrasion. These two techniques exhibit certain differences in terms of treatment efficacy and safety. Caecilia Ng et al. [25] reported no significant difference in postoperative complications between parietal pleurectomy and pleural abrasion but noted that parietal pleurectom effectively reduced patient recurrence rates (8/34 vs. 1/73,P< 0.001). Conversely, Jaewon Chang et al. [34]concluded that pleural abrasion reduces perioperative burden and shortens hospital stay compared to pleurectomy without affecting the pneumothorax recurrence rate. Furthermore, Chen et al. [24] conducted a detailed comparison of these two surgical approaches, revealing that parietal pleurectomy had longer operation times (average 81.4 min vs. 55.8 min,P < 0.001), greater intraoperative blood loss (average 29.4 mL vs. 13.2 mL, P = 0.025), and higher postoperative drainage volumes (average 287.4 mL vs. 195.8 mL, P = 0.040). However, due to the local application of minocycline after pleural abrasion in this study, which may synergistically enhance chemical pleural fixation, both groups demonstrated comparable outcomes regarding drainage duration, hospital stay length, postoperative complications, and recurrence rates.

Our study demonstrated that the pleural abrasion group exhibited a significant advantage in perioperative indicators. Compared to pleural abrasion, patients undergoing parietal pleurotomy exhibit significantly longer operation times, postoperative drainage durations, and hospitalization periods. Additionally, intraoperative blood loss and total drainage volumes are notably increased. Our findings are highly congruent with the research reported by Ocakcioglu [21]. Although we observed that parietal pleurectomy might increase the degree of postoperative pain in patients, this difference did not reach statistical significance due to the limited sample size and routine postoperative analgesia administration. A previous study by Ling [35] focused on the number of patients experiencing severe postoperative chest pain rather than pain scores, which could partially explain the discrepancy between their findings and ours. pleural abrasion is a relatively straightforward procedure to perform, requiring only coarse sandpaper to stimulate the pleural surface until punctate bleeding occurs. Given its minimally invasive nature, the operation time is shorter and blood loss is reduced. Conversely, parietal pleurectomy involves systematic dissection of the parietal pleura, making it a more complex and extensive procedure that may lead to intraoperative bleeding and prolonged operation times. Additionally, the greater destruction of the parietal pleura during pleurotomy, exposure of intercostal nerve endings, and loss of pleural integrity may exacerbate postoperative pain in patients. Larger incisions can also increase postoperative exudate, necessitating extended drainage periods and indirectly contributing to longer hospital stays. The differences in perioperative indicators between these two fixation methods suggest that pleural abrasion may be more advantageous for patients prioritizing rapid recovery.

Although there was considerable heterogeneity in some perioperative observation indicators, subgroup analysis indicated that the study type was not a contributing factor to this heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that two specific studies were the primary sources of increased heterogeneity. Excluding these studies significantly reduced heterogeneity without altering the direction of the combined effect size or statistical significance. Additionally, we considered lesion size and the extent of intraoperative pleural fixation as potential causes of heterogeneity. However, due to insufficient detailed descriptions of volume or extent in the original studies, it was not feasible to conduct subgroup analyses based on these factors. It is important to note that across all included studies, perioperative indicators in the pleural friction group were consistently superior to those in the pleurotomy group. The variation in indicator data sizes among studies may primarily be attributed to differences in surgeon experience. Some medical centers have relatively recent developments in related techniques, placing surgeons in the ascending phase of their learning curves. This may result in prolonged operation times, which subsequently influence subsequent perioperative indicators, representing another potential source of heterogeneity.

Most clinicians prefer pleural abrasion over pleurotomy. In addition to being a simpler procedure with less trauma, the critical factor is that pleural abrasion does not lead to severe postoperative complications. A retrospective study involving 208 patients [20]demonstrated that pleural abrasion and parietal pleurectomy had comparable rates of pulmonary air leakage; however, the incidence of acute postoperative hemorrhage after parietal pleurectomy was significantly higher (7.4% vs. 0.9%), which aligns with the findings of this study. In a systematic review of randomized controlled trials published by Ling [35] in 2015, the authors compared the efficacy of pleural abrasion with other fixation methods. The results indicated no significant difference in the postoperative recurrence rate between pleural abrasion and parietal pleurectomy (P = 0.821), but pleurectomy resulted in more pronounced postoperative pain and a higher frequency of complications such as postoperative bleeding. Furthermore, this study highlights that combining pleural abrasion with chemical fixation does not further reduce the risk of recurrence but may increase the likelihood of acute chest pain. Regarding postoperative complications, this study corroborates our conclusions, reinforcing the notion that extensive dissection of the parietal pleurectomy is prone to damage intercostal and subpleural vessels, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative hemorrhage. Although we reached opposite conclusions regarding the rate of distant recurrence, which may be an effect of the small number of original studies included in the study and the wide variation in follow-up time on the robustness of the conclusions.

In the evaluation of pleural fixation efficacy, the long-term recurrence rate serves as the most critical observation index. However, due to limitations in the included studies, two prior meta-analyses indicated that although parietal pleurectomy demonstrated a trend toward reducing the risk of long-term recurrence, this difference did not achieve statistical significance [34, 35]. A retrospective cohort study conducted by Chang et al. [14]reported that the overall incidence of postoperative complications was comparable between the two groups (6.7% vs. 5.8%). Notably, 8.6% (3 cases) of patients in the pleural abrasion group experienced ipsilateral recurrence, whereas no recurrences were observed in the parietal pleurectomy group. Given the limited sample size in this study, the recurrence rate failed to reach statistical significance. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that both surgical approaches exhibit similar postoperative safety profiles, with parietal pleurectomy potentially offering advantages in long-term recurrence prevention, consistent with the conclusions drawn in our study. We analyzed data from 15 studies reporting this index, revealing that the long-term recurrence rate was significantly higher in the pleural abrasion group compared to the parietal pleurectomy group. Patients undergoing pleurotomy were found to effectively reduce their risk of long-term recurrence. It has been suggested that parietal pleurectomy leads to a lower rate of postoperative recurrence in patients by completely removing the wall pleura, allowing the visceral pleura to come into direct contact with the chest wall and form stronger fibrous adhesions [36].

The findings of this study indicate that for patients with spontaneous pneumothorax and pulmonary bullae, parietal pleurectomy imposes an additional perioperative burden. This is primarily manifested in the intricate and complex nature of the surgical procedure, which prolongs operation time. Additionally, the extensive resection wound leads to increased intraoperative blood loss, thereby affecting postoperative drainage volume and extending the duration of catheterization and hospitalization. However, the level of postoperative pain does not significantly differ between the two groups. Regarding postoperative complications, the incidence of atelectasis, air leakage, and pulmonary infection following parietal pleurectomy is comparable to that after pleural abrasion; however, parietal pleurectomy increases the risk of postoperative hemorrhage. Therefore, gentle manipulation is required during the operation to avoid excessive traction during pleural resection. Postoperatively, thorough hemostasis should be ensured, and the patient’s vital signs and thoracic drainage output should be closely monitored. Six studies have addressed the management of acute postoperative hemorrhage following parietal pleurectomy. All patients achieved recovery through medical hemostasis or secondary surgery, with no fatalities reported. Notably, compared to prior meta-analyses, by incorporating a larger sample size, we have demonstrated for the first time that parietal pleurotomy holds clinical significance in preventing pneumothorax recurrence.

The selection of pleural fixation should involve a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s individual condition and disease severity. For patients with persistent air leakage, ineffective conservative treatment, poor lung function, or underlying diseases, pleural abrasion, which offers the advantages of minimal surgical trauma and rapid postoperative recovery, may be considered as a preferred treatment option. This is particularly relevant for elderly patients or those with coagulopathy, as reducing bleeding and pain can effectively minimize the risk of postoperative complications. In cases of recurrent pneumothorax, giant bullae, or multiple small bullae, parietal pleurectomy offers a more thorough approach, which is crucial in preventing postoperative recurrence.

There are several limitations in this study that warrant acknowledgment: (1) There was substantial heterogeneity in perioperative-related indicators. While the sensitivity analysis identified two studies as the primary sources of heterogeneity, potential factors such as surgeons’ experience and technical differences could not be disregarded; (2) Inconsistencies in the definition of late recurrence and follow-up duration across studies could not be resolved, potentially leading to evaluation bias in recurrence rates and undermining the robustness of the conclusions; (3) Several original studies incorporated into the analysis did not provide detailed reports on the extent of parietal pleurectomy, thus restricting in-depth analysis of associated indicators; (4) The restricted number of studies included in certain observational indicators might potentially reduce the statistical power and reliability of the conclusions; (5) This study included a total of 12 retrospective studies, and its findings require further validation through large-scale, high-quality, multi-center randomized controlled trials in the future.

Conclusions

In summary, the pleural abrasion technique demonstrates more advantages in perioperative indicators, making it suitable for patients pursuing rapid rehabilitation or those at risk of intolerance to surgery. Although parietal pleurectomy imposes a heavier perioperative burden, it effectively reduces the risk of postoperative recurrence, particularly benefiting patients with recurrent or complex pneumothorax. The incidence of postoperative complications is comparable between the two procedures. Despite an increased risk of postoperative hemorrhage associated with parietal pleurectomy, patients can recover following active treatment.
