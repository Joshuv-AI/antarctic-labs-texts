Constructing large-scale benchmark datasets for the hierarchical hub location problem using geospatial information: a case study on populous Indian cities
Authors: Arup Kumar Bhattacharjee, Anirban Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces an innovative approach for constructing large-scale, realistic benchmark geospatial datasets specifically designed for the single-allocation hierarchical hub median problem, a key variant of the hierarchical hub location problem. Addressing the lack of publicly available large-scale datasets in location science, we have provided a detailed step-by-step methodology to generate benchmark datasets. Also, we have provided datasets of various granularities based on Kolkata and Mumbai, two of India’s most populous metropolitan cities as a ready reference. The datasets are built using actual building-level geographic data acquired from OpenStreetMap and QGIS. Benchmark datasets are solved for the single-allocation hierarchical hub median problem formulation using IBM ILOG CPLEX to provide exact solutions, supporting robust empirical validation. The datasets are fully reproducible, scalable, and adaptable to various hierarchical hub location problem formulations and urban planning scenarios. By making these resources publicly available, this work fills a significant research gap and offers a foundational platform for algorithmic benchmarking and future methodological innovations in hierarchical hub location studies.

Introduction

The field of location science encompasses various problems aimed at determining the optimal spatial configuration of service systems1. Hierarchical hub location problem (HHLP), frequently used in transportation and communication networks is one of the key problem domains in the field of location science. Furthermore, single-allocation hub median problem (SA-H-MP) is a sub-domain of HHLP which organizes a set of nodes into demand nodes, hub nodes and central hub nodes2,3. Although various mathematical formulations and solution strategies for HHLP and its variants are available, this problem area lacks access to large-scale, real-world benchmark datasets that can enrich this research domain. Research in this sub-domain of location science mostly depends on small synthetic datasets, so their relevance to real-world environments is limited.

Several classical benchmark datasets, most notably the civil aviation board (CAB), Turkish network (TN), and Australian post (AP) datasets have played a pivotal role for research in the domain of hub location problem (HLP)4–6. These datasets have been extensively used to validate algorithmic formulations and solution strategies across various subdomains. However, despite their prominence, they suffer from critical limitations that restrict their suitability for HHLP. Specifically, these datasets were not originally designed to support n-tier hub structures, and their relatively small, static, and aggregated nature limits scalability and realism, especially in urban settings. Furthermore, their national-level granularity offers limited applicability to city-level urban planning challenges such as zoning, emergency logistics, or smart service delivery. This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a scalable, reproducible, and urban geospatial benchmark dataset based on real building-level data from Indian cities–to support empirical research in broader HHLP formulations.

The SA-H-MP model, formally introduced by Yaman7, consists of demand nodes, hub nodes (intermediate facilities) and central hub nodes (top-tier hubs), which are arranged together to form an n-tier network8. The primary objective of SA-H-MP is to determine the optimal placement of hubs and the central hubs to minimize the overall routing cost between any two demand nodes. SA-H-MP uses a mixed-integer model where demand nodes are either directly assigned to a hub or a central hub node and each hub node is assigned to a single central hub node2. As a result, in SA-H-MP each lower layer node is connected to at most a single upper layer node. At this juncture, we would like to mention that terms such as demand node, hub node and central node may vary across different literature, but these terminologies align with widely accepted naming conventions in HHLP models. For a comprehensive understanding of the mathematical formulation of SA-H-M problems, please refer to the “Appendix”. The mathematical formulation outlines the intricacies of the SA-H-MP network, facilitating a precise representation of its three-layer structure.

It must be noted that this paper does not propose a new algorithmic solution for HHLP rather it presents a novel approach for constructing benchmark datasets for SA-H-MP using real-time data extracted from OpenStreetMap and Quantum geographic information system (QGIS). Based on the proposed methodology two standard datasets are provided with this paper. Datasets are prepared based on two of India’s largest cities—Kolkata and Mumbai, to demonstrate their scalability and realism. The resulting datasets, along with their exact solution for the SA-H-MP problem formulation are made publicly available to support future research in HHLP and related areas.

By providing a standardized and realistic benchmark dataset construction framework using geospatial information, our work aims to address a critical gap in the HHLP literature and facilitate more robust and comparable datasets for future research. Despite the progress made in the SA-H-MP domain, many studies have focused on networks with no more than 200 nodes, limiting their representation of real-world scenarios. This limitation underscores the need for a real-time data repository dedicated to research purposes, offering valuable assistance to the community. Due to the unavailability of datasets for the most populous cities in the world, we have created datasets specifically for Kolkata and Mumbai, which are two highly populated metropolises in India. The methodology described in this article could serve as a model for generating additional datasets comparable to these new datasets.

It may be found that several works on HHLP include computational studies and instance generation, but most of them restrict themselves to small, synthetic or aggregated datasets. On the contrary, our work offers a systematic step-by-step approach for developing authentic, large-scale benchmark datasets specifically designed for SA-H-MP utilizing actual metropolitan geospatial information. This study generates benchmark instances using comprehensive building-level data fetched using OpenStreetMap and QGIS. The resultant datasets are scalable, reproducible and specifically tailored for HHLP models. By publicly releasing both the data and exact solutions obtained using IBM®ILOG®CPLEX®, we aim to fill a critical gap in the literature and support rigorous empirical research in this field.

This model involves the identification of a suitable set of service providers, namely, the central hub and hub nodes, from which services can be effectively dispersed to demand nodes. The spatial arrangement is designed to align with the demand generated by customers, encompassing a set of demand points indicating customer locations and service centers estimating the demand for services generated by customers. This work targets a significant need in the HHLP literature by providing a methodology for dataset generation and enabling future studies on model building and algorithmic improvements in this domain. The proposed approaches and datasets are meant to facilitate not just conventional HHLP research but may also be used to explore case-specific constraints including urban density, zoning rules particularly in the framework of populous Indian cities.

A combination of QGIS and OpenStreetMap was utilized to construct our extensive datasets, which are employed for computing the optimal or exact solution for the SA-H-MP model9,10. To the best of our knowledge, this study marks the initial effort to generate large datasets using QGIS and OpenStreetMap for SA-H-MP. This dataset is designed to address not only the SA-H-MP model but also for other HHLP models.

Literature review

Although the HLP originally came with an idea to increase efficiency in transportation and telecommunications networks, in recent years, its domain has evolved from the conventional hub-and-spoke network design to complex designs with typical real-life constraints. O’Kelly proposed the basic hub location architecture where hubs act as connecting nodes to reduce overall transportation costs by grouping nodes within a network11. Similarly, Campbell and subsequently Alumur and Kara, proposed several models including p-hub median, p-hub center and hub covering problems to solve more realistic challenges in this domain12,13.

In recent years, many thorough studies have helped improve this discipline even further. Farahani et al. offer a detailed study of HLP models, their classifications and practical uses and also emphasize the need for real-time datasets in bridging the gap between theory and practical implementation14. Contreras and O’Kelly, in their work, offer a consolidated overview of hub location modeling in the Location Science handbook, combining both classical and modern perspectives15. More recently, Alumur et al. discuss the paradigm shift of HLP models to more contemporary modeling trends and highlight the necessity for realistic and scalable data instances for empirical validation16. Therefore, these studies draw attention towards growing consensus on the need for high-quality real-world publicly available datasets to assess the efficacy and robustness of HLPs. Our contribution in this paper follows this same objective to provide a methodology to produce large-scale benchmark datasets created especially for HHLP models, thereby augmenting the empirical backbone of this field.

Reviews by Sahin and Sural and Davari et al. offer a closer understanding of HHLP models and their real-world applications17,18. Based on this foundation, HHLP expands from its basic two-level hub-spoke architecture to a multiple layer architecture usually arranged into central hub and hub nodes. This multi-tier architecture accurately simulates large-scale real-time systems like postal services and airline operations. To target a specific n-tier problem domain under HHLP, we have worked with SA-H-MP, which was formally introduced by Yaman7.

Benchmark datasets such as CAB, TN, and AP have formed the empirical foundation for a wide range of HLP studies. These datasets were constructed based on legacy transport networks or postal routes and are well-suited for classical hub-spoke architectures7,13. However, their utility is limited in hierarchical, urban, or modern geospatial contexts. Notably, they lack the structural resolution to model layered hierarchies found in SA-H-MP and similar formulations. Moreover, the fixed size and scope of these datasets hinder their adaptability to scenarios requiring fine-grained zoning, or real-time demographic mapping. Several recent reviews have emphasized the need for more realistic and scalable benchmark datasets that reflect evolving real-world constraints14,15. Our proposed Indian Urban Dataset (IUD) directly responds to this call by providing a high-resolution, city-based dataset using actual building distributions from Kolkata and Mumbai, enabling more accurate modeling and algorithmic validation in HHLP studies.

Since this article deals with HHLP datasets, so we need to highlight some popular benchmark datasets frequently used in this domain. Several benchmark datasets have historically supported research in HLPs, notably the CAB, TN and AP datasets7,13,19. For both classical and hierarchical hub location problems, these datasets have served as standards in many studies. However, these datasets are typically based on legacy transport networks or synthetic constructions and do not reflect the urban density and complexities seen in rapidly growing cities, particularly in developing countries like India. Our work addresses this gap by creating new large-scale real-world datasets based on actual building distributions in metropolitan cities like Kolkata and Mumbai.

Datasets

The datasets presented in this study are prepared based on the geographical layouts of Kolkata and Mumbai, two major cities in India. This choice is justified by the significance of these metropolises, representing diverse urban landscapes in the eastern and western regions of the country. Kolkata, as the capital of West Bengal, stands as a populous cultural hub, while Mumbai, the capital of Maharashtra, is renowned as India’s financial center. Analyzing these cities provides valuable insights into spatial configurations that can be applied to varied urban settings, making them suitable benchmarks for the research community.

Kolkata stands as one of the most populous metropolis within the Republic of India encompassing a land area measuring 1480 square kilometers and is inhabited by a population of 4,496,694 individuals20. We have also acquired data regarding Mumbai, another highly renowned metropolis in India21 and generated the datasets for both cities. Mumbai is located on the coast of the Konkan and features a significant natural harbor. Mumbai serves as the financial and commercial hub of India. Service systems often encompass expansive areas, necessitating substantial data for effective design and quality evaluation. Customers are typically represented by a set of demand points, providing a spatial depiction of their distribution. In scenarios where a service system is designed to cater to the entire public, the assumption is that all citizens are customers and the demand for services correlates with the population. However, if the system targets a specific subset of the population (e.g., children of a certain age), deriving demand solely from data related to the entire population may yield misleading results22.

In this study, we establish a correlation between consumer demand and the density of buildings within a city, positing that an increase in building density corresponds to a higher number of potential customers in the area. Considering each structure in an urban region as a potential demand point, the total number of demand points tends to be considerable when modeling urban service networks. Using QGIS, we identified a total of 192,158, and 74,274 structures in Kolkata and Mumbai respectively. The entire set has been partitioned into multiple sub-units, ranging from 100 units to 2000 units. The units under consideration can be conceptualised as zones and the well-known K-means clustering algorithm has been employed to construct these zones. The following section provides a comparison between our datasets and several well-established benchmarks in the literature, highlighting the distinct characteristics and contributions of the IUD.

Methods

In this section, the process of generating benchmark datasets is described in detail. To solve the SA-H-MP problem, the dataset must consist of two matrices. One matrix should contain the transportation costs between all pairs of nodes, while the other matrix should represent the frequency of communication between each pair of nodes in the form of weight. The steps to generate datasets are as follows. Below mentioned steps are illustrated in Fig. 2. The cost and weight matrices generated in Steps 3 and 5, respectively, are used to solve SA-H-MP. In this experiment, buildings are treated as multipolygons and their locations are then transformed from multipolygon to point coordinates using the Polygon Centroids tool of QGIS. This tool places a point at the geometric centroid of the polygon preserving the attributes of the polygon (Fig. 2a).Applying clustering technique for preparing centroid matrix for each zone The well-known K-means clustering technique is applied to divide all buildings into several zones (say X). The considered number of zones is 100, 300, 400, 500, 600, 800, 900, 1000, and 2000. This approach produces a centroid matrix reflecting the centroid of all these X zones. For instance, the presence of 100 zones indicates that the city’s buildings are categorised into 100 clusters. The centroid of each zone corresponds to a specific point or building, which may serve as a demand node, hub, or central hub for the entire zone (Fig. 2b).Cost matrix generation By setting the diagonal elements to zero, a symmetric matrix is produced that contains the distance between each pair of centroids of each of the X zones. The Euclidean distance between every pair of vectors in the centroid matrix is computed. This distance matrix works as the cost matrix. Applications and usage of the dataset

The benchmark datasets developed in this study serve as a fundamental resource for researchers working on the HHLP domain, particularly SA-H-MP. Below, we outline several potential applications of this dataset: Benchmarking optimization algorithms: Researchers may utilize the provided datasets to evaluate the efficacy of pre-existing novel algorithms designed for addressing various HHLPs. The exact solutions generated by IBM ILOG CPLEX provides robust baseline references for evaluating the quality of solutions and the efficiency of algorithms.Scalability and robustness testing: With these available instances ranging from 100 to 2000 zones, users can evaluate how well their proposed algorithms may work with varying problem size and complexity. This is especially beneficial for evaluating the performance of the algorithms on real-world geospatial datasets.Urban service system design: Since these large-scale datasets are based on actual metropolitan layouts, therefore, they can be utilized in practical studies, such as emergency services, logistics routing etc.Cross-model experimentation: Although the datasets were built using the SA-H-MP model in mind, it is adaptable to various other HHLP models as well. Researchers working on models with additional constraints like budget, coverage etc. may customize this spatial dataset for their experiments.Educational and training resource: The complete dataset along with supporting files (shapefiles, matrices, and exact solutions) serves as a real-world case study for teaching location science and operation research in academic settings.

Conclusion

HHLP and its various classifications lack datasets that could be utilized to test the mathematical models proposed by the researchers. This study presents a methodology for creating benchmark datasets with real geographic and demographic data, addressing the unavailability of large-scale real-world datasets in the domain of HHLP. Using the proposed methodology, we have created datasets based on Kolkata and Mumbai, two of India’s most populous cities, utilizing building-level data. Instances are generated ranging from 100 to 2000 zones and all these datasets and their exact solutions are made available in the public domain for the researcher community. Our approach aims to function as a data repository and as a methodological framework that can be used for generating datasets for other geographical locations as well. This work does not propose new algorithms, constraints, or models, rather, it addresses a long-existing issue of dataset scarcity in the field of location science. This paper has provided a detailed methodology for dataset generation and some benchmark datasets to assess existing or new algorithms in the domain of HHLPs. Nevertheless, this study did not consider the covered area of buildings when mapping the population with buildings i.e., we have assigned equal weight to all the buildings, which may not truly reflect the actual situation. In a metropolis such as Kolkata or Mumbai, a high-rise building may accommodate a greater population density compared to numerous smaller buildings. These intriguing and demanding research problems can be explored in the future.
