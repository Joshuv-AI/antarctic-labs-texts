Novel immunohistochemical assay utilizing the INSM1 (SP493) antibody demonstrates high specificity and sensitivity in detecting primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasms
Authors: Katerina Dvorak, Alexander Gomez, Rafael Sainz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAbout 15–20% of lung neoplasms are of neuroendocrine origin. In addition to evaluation of morphological features, these neoplasms can be identified by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using antibodies against neuroendocrine markers such as synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and neural cell adhesion molecule, CD56. Recent studies have shown that insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) is increasingly used as a diagnostic marker for neoplasms of neuroendocrine origin, as it is highly sensitive and specific. The primary focus of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of a recently developed assay that uses a new INSM1 (SP493) antibody for detecting primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasms and to compare this IHC assay with currently used neuroendocrine markers. MethodTissue microarrays containing 391 lung neoplasms were stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody, INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, CONFIRM anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody on BenchMark ULTRA instruments using OptiView IHC DAB Detection Kit or ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit using manufacturer’s recommended protocols. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for each biomarker. ResultsThe sensitivity and specificity of the new assay using INSM1 (SP493) antibody for detecting primary lung neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation were 96.8% and 92.3%, respectively. Comparable results were seen for the lung neoplastic cases stained with INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, yielding a sensitivity of 94.9% and specificity of 94.1%. In contrast, the anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody showed considerably lower sensitivities. The sensitivity was 78.7% for the anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody and 85.4% for the CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody. However, the specificities of anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody were comparable with the specificity of the INSM1 (SP493) antibody [91.7% for anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, 90.4% for CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody]. The performance of the anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody was suboptimal, with a sensitivity of 82.9% and a specificity of 87.6%.ConclusionsOverall, our data demonstrate that the IHC assay using the newly developed INSM1 (SP493) antibody is highly specific and sensitive for identifying primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasms.

Introduction

Lung neoplasms are the most common cause of mortality related to malignant neoplasms in the world. According to a recent review, 2,480,301 new cases of lung neoplasms were reported in 2022, and 1,817,172 patients died with this disease [1]. Lung neoplasms are broadly classified into three main categories: epithelial neoplasms, lung neuroendocrine neoplasms, and mesenchymal neoplasms [2].

While epithelial neoplasms are the most common type, accounting for about 85% of lung neoplasms, a significant subset exhibits neuroendocrine differentiation [3]. Currently, the World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes four major types of lung neuroendocrine neoplasms; these include small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), typical carcinoid, and atypical carcinoid [2]. SCLC is an aggressive and rapidly growing tumor that accounts for about 15% of lung neoplasm [4, 5]. LCNEC is a relatively rare aggressive neoplasm with an incidence rate of 0.3%–3%, that shares many aggressive features with SCLC [6]. Carcinoid tumors are also relatively uncommon, representing only 1–2% of all lung neoplasms [7]. Typical carcinoids generally behave indolently, while atypical carcinoids demonstrate more aggressive growth and a higher propensity for metastasis. Mesenchymal lung neoplasms are also very rare neoplasms arising from mesenchymal tissues present in the lung, including muscle, bone, cartilage, and fibrous tissues [8]. Overall, it is essential to accurately diagnose the specific type of lung neoplasm, as the treatment strategies and prognoses significantly vary.

The diagnosis of neuroendocrine neoplasms of the lung requires integration of histologic features with immunohistochemical confirmation of neuroendocrine differentiation [7]. Morphologically, these neoplasms exhibit characteristic organoid nesting, trabecular or rosette-like growth patterns, and finely granular chromatin [9]. However, morphology alone may be insufficient, particularly in poorly differentiated neoplasms or small biopsy specimens. For this reason, immunohistochemical markers are widely employed [10]. Synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56 are the most commonly used markers to establish neuroendocrine differentiation⁸. Each, however, has limitations that reduce diagnostic certainty. Synaptophysin is highly sensitive but not specific, as it may be expressed in a range of non-neuroendocrine neoplasms [10]. Chromogranin A is relatively specific, but its expression is frequently reduced or absent in poorly differentiated carcinomas [9, 11]. CD56 is a sensitive marker but suffers from poor specificity, as it is expressed in lymphomas, certain sarcomas, and some non-neuroendocrine carcinomas. These limitations underscore the challenge of reliably diagnosing pulmonary neuroendocrine neoplasms, especially in limited tissue samples.

To address these challenges, novel diagnostic markers have been sought. Insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) has recently emerged as a highly promising biomarker for neuroendocrine neoplasms. INSM1 is a zinc-finger transcription factor that plays a central role in embryonic neuroendocrine differentiation and is normally expressed in tissues such as pancreatic islet cells, adrenal medulla, and selected regions of the nervous system [12]. Importantly, INSM1 is expressed in a wide range of neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation, including pulmonary carcinoids, SCLC, and LCNEC. Multiple studies have now demonstrated that INSM1 exhibits both high sensitivity and high specificity for neuroendocrine neoplasms, surpassing traditional markers in diagnostic accuracy [12, 13]. Unlike chromogranin A or synaptophysin, INSM1 staining is typically robust even in poorly differentiated neoplasms, making it a particularly valuable tool in challenging diagnostic settings. Recent studies suggest that INSM1 is sensitive and specific enough to serve as a standalone first-line marker of neuroendocrine differentiation [10].

Roche Tissue Diagnostics developed a new IHC assay using INSM1 (SP493) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody that is intended for laboratory use in the qualitative detection of INSM1 protein by light microscopy in sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. This new INSM1 (SP493) assay is available for the BenchMark ULTRA/ULTRA PLUS and BenchMark GX instruments with OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit and ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit. INSM1 (SP493) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody is provided as a ready-to-use product formulated at a fixed antibody concentration of 5 µg/ml.

The primary goal of this study was to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the newly developed INSM1 (SP493) assay in detecting primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasms. Additionally, the performance of this new INSM1 (SP493) antibody was compared with the performance of synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56, and also with a different INSM1 antibody clone, INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody from Cell Marque.

Methods

Material

INSM1 (SP493) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 790–7247), CONFIRM anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 790–4407), anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) Primary Antibody (P/N 760–2519), anti-p40 (SP225) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 790–7219), anti-Thyroid Transcription Factor-1 (SP141) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 790–4756), Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig (P/N 760–4795) and Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (MOPC-211) [Negative Control (Monoclonal), P/N 760–2014)] were obtained from Roche Tissue Diagnostics (RTD). Napsin A (MRQ-60) Mouse Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 760–4867) and CD56 (MRQ-42) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody (P/N 760–4596) were acquired from Cell Marque, Inc. INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody (P/N 475R-98) was purchased from Sigma. OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (P/N 760 − 700, RTD) and ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit (P/N 760 − 500, RTD) were used to detect individual protein expression. Hematoxylin II (RTD, P/N 790–2208) and Bluing Reagent (P/N 760–2037, RTD) were used for counterstaining.

Tissue specimen

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lung neoplastic and non-neoplastic cases were purchased from Pantomics Inc. (LUC962–48 cases, LUC1504–75 cases, LUC1021-102 cases), US Biolab (LC818b − 80 cases), and Biocore USA (B-120Lun-1–118 cases). One section from each TMA was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to confirm the diagnosis initially assigned to each case by the vendor pathologist. Overall, these TMAs contained 425 cores from 423 cases. Out of these 425 cores, 391 cores were from lung neoplasms and 34 cores were from non-malignant cases. The study was performed using pre-constructed tissue microarrays (TMAs). Therefore, case selection was predetermined, and no additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied.

Immunohistochemistry

INSM1 (SP493) antibody

For the development of the INSM1 (SP493) assay, a broad range of staining parameters and different antibody diluents were evaluated to establish optimal assay staining conditions. Additionally, sections from Tour of Tumor and Tour of Body tissue microarrays from Pantomics Inc., containing 30 different normal tissue types and 29 neoplasms, were used to confirm the optimal staining conditions. As expected, the staining patterns was observed in the normal or neoplastic tissues corresponded with previously published data; for example, high expression of INSM1 was detected in pancreatic islet cells, pituitary gland, and small cell lung carcinoma. These microarrays were crucial in assessing the precise staining and subcellular localization of the INSM1 protein while monitoring for potential off-target staining. Following assay optimization, sections from acquired TMAs containing lung neoplasms were baked for 30 min at 60 °C as recommended by the vendor and stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody using the optimized protocol on BenchMark ULTRA with OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit. Additionally, one INSM1-positive pancreas case was stained in each run to monitor the proper functioning of the instrument. Furthermore, to measure the level of non-specific staining, the sections from each TMA were stained with Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig. After completion of the staining run, slides were dehydrated according to the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit method sheet and coverslipped.

Other neuroendocrine markers

The sections from the same TMAs were also stained with the currently used commercially available antibodies that detect neuroendocrine differentiation. The protocols from respective Method Sheets were used for staining TMAs on the BenchMark ULTRA. These include anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody, and CONFIRM anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody. The same protocol used by Nordic Immunohistochemical Quality Control (NordiQC) was used for staining all TMAs with the INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody. All protocols used in the study are shown in Table 1. Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig or Negative Control (Monoclonal) were used to evaluate the level of non-specific staining. Similar to the slides stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody assay, the slides were dehydrated and coverslipped after the completion of the staining run.

Additional markers

Additional markers were used to stain TMAs to verify the diagnosis of several cases, where the initial diagnosis was uncertain. These included anti-p40 (SP225) antibody for confirmation of squamous cell carcinoma diagnosis, anti-Thyroid transcription factor-1 (SP141) antibody [anti-TTF1 (SP141) antibody], and Napsin A (MRQ-60) antibody for confirmation of lung adenocarcinoma diagnosis. The protocols from individual Method Sheets were used for staining TMAs on BenchMark ULTRA with OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit. To measure the level of non-specific background signal, one slide was also stained with Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig or with Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (MOPC-211) using the same protocol as the corresponding marker.

Scoring

All evaluable cases stained with different antibodies were scored by a pathologist as positive or negative for each marker. All cases were scored independently without consideration of results for other markers. Positivity was determined based on the presence of nuclear staining for INSM1, cytoplasmic staining for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, and cytoplasmic/membranous staining for CD56. Additionally, background staining was also evaluated. The case was considered positive if the specific signal was higher than the background signal. It was expected that lung neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation would show a positive signal when stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody, INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody, and anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, while squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, other lung neoplasms, and normal lungs would be negative.

Analysis

Sensitivity measures a test’s ability to correctly identify positive cases, while specificity measures a test’s ability to correctly identify negative cases. Assuming that the diagnosis assigned to each case by the vendors’ pathologists and then confirmed by the RTD pathologist is accurate, the sensitivity of each biomarker was calculated as a proportion of the cases that were correctly identified by IHC as neuroendocrine lung neoplasms among all lung neoplastic cases that were scored as positive. Specificity of each biomarker was calculated as a proportion of the cases that were correctly identified by IHC as non-neuroendocrine lung neoplasms among all lung malignant cases that were scored as negative. In addition, accuracy was also calculated using data from all five TMAs stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody and INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody.

Results

Five lung tissue microarrays containing 425 malignant, non-malignant, and normal cores from 423 cases were stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody, INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody according to the manufacturer’s instructions on BenchMark ULTRA with OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit or ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit. Out of these 425 cores, 34 cores were from normal or non-malignant tissues. These cases were negative for all neuroendocrine markers evaluated in this study except for two cases of normal lung that were scored as positive when stained with anti-Chromogranin A (KL2H10) antibody. In these two cases, endothelial cells, nerves, and cilia of the bronchial epithelium showed a positive signal. Normal cases were not included in the final data analysis.

All malignant cases were first classified based on H&E morphology by the vendor’s pathologists and RTD pathologists. However, the vendor’s pathologist scored seven cases as SCLC; but this diagnosis was not confirmed by RTD pathologists based on cellular morphology, or these cases exhibited suboptimal tissue preservation, or minimal tumor in the core. Therefore, these cases were excluded from the analysis. Finally, several cores were not evaluated by the pathologist due to extensive necrosis, or the core was missing from the tissue array.

Additionally, seven cases that were diagnosed as SCLC (6 cases) or atypical carcinoid (1 case) were negative when stained with all five different antibodies, raising the possibility that these cases are not of neuroendocrine origin. Therefore, these cases were re-evaluated and stained using anti-TTF1 (SP141) antibody, Napsin A (MRQ-60) antibody, and anti-p40 (SP225) antibody. The data indicated that 4 out of 7 cases were positive for p40 expression, but negative for TTF1 and Napsin A, suggesting that the correct diagnosis of these cases is squamous cell carcinoma. Two cases were strongly positive for TTF1 and Napsin A, supporting a lung adenocarcinoma diagnosis. Only one case was negative when stained with all three antibodies, suggesting that this case is truly of neuroendocrine origin. The corrected diagnosis of these cases was included in the final data analysis.

The results from this study show that the majority of lung neoplastic cases stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody showed correct positive/negative status based on the diagnosis given to these cases by RTD and vendor pathologists (Table 2). Background staining was acceptable in all evaluable cases. Similar results were obtained for these cases stained with INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody (Table 2). Importantly, the cases stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody showed stronger staining intensities and a distinct, crisp, nuclear staining pattern with no background staining when compared with the same cases stained with INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody. The staining pattern of synaptophysin is also cytoplasmic.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the newly developed INSM1 (SP493) assay and compare its performance with traditional markers currently used for diagnosing lung neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation.

Initially, the INSM1 (SP493) antibody assay underwent extensive protocol optimization for use with the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit on the BenchMark ULTRA platform. Following that, 391 lung neoplastic cases with a variety of diagnoses were stained with five different antibodies on Benchmark ULTRA with OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit or ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and evaluated as positive/negative for the individual markers. These included INSM1 (SP493) antibody, INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody, anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for each marker. Overall, the data from this study indicate that the newly developed assay using INSM1 (SP493) antibody is highly sensitive and specific for identifying primary lung neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation (Table 2).

The results of our study are concordant with most previously published reports. For example, Yan et al. evaluated 106 lung neoplastic cases and found that INSM1 sensitivity and specificity were 95% and 100%, respectively [14]. Sakakibara et al. evaluated 141 lung neoplasms; the data from this study showed the INSM1 (A-8) antibody sensitivity and specificity of 92% and 94% [15]. Another study by Mukhopadhyay et al. that evaluated 345 cases reported 95% sensitivity and 98% specificity of INSM1 for neuroendocrine lung neoplasms [16]. In contrast to our study, Kriegsmann et al. published a study showing low INSM1 sensitivity (76%) but high specificity (99%) [17]. However, compared to our study, these published studies used different INSM1 antibody clones.

Though several studies have reported the expression of INSM1 in a subset of lung SCC cases [9]. The INSM1 expression in SCC may indicate some degree of neuroendocrine differentiation and can complicate the differential diagnosis, especially when distinguishing between neuroendocrine neoplasms like SCLC and lung SCC. Our data also confirmed this observation. In our study, 10/95 (10.5%) cases of lung squamous cell carcinoma showed positivity when stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody (Table 3; Fig. 1). However, the INSM1 signal was typically weak, with only a small fraction of cells expressing the marker. Summary of data

Importantly, the data from this study also showed comparable sensitivity and specificity of two different INSM1 antibody clones, INSM1 (SP493) antibody and INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody. The sensitivity and specificity of INSM1 (MRQ-70) antibody were 94.9% (149/157) and 94.1% (207/220), respectively, and the accuracy was 94.4% (356/377).

In contrast, in this study, the sensitivities of three traditional neuroendocrine markers were considerably lower, [i.e., 82.9% (126/152) for anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, 78.7% (118/150) for anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody, and 85.4% (123/144) for CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody]. The specificities of anti-Chromogranin A (LK2H10) antibody and CD56 (MRQ-42) antibody, anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, were comparable to INSM1 (SP493) antibody, while the specificities of anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody were lower (Table 2). For example, 17.3% adenocarcinoma cases showed a positive signal when stained with anti-Synaptophysin (SP11) antibody, while only 2.1% of lung adenocarcinoma cases showed a positive signal when stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody (Fig. 1).

Importantly, all three currently used neuroendocrine markers are expressed in the cytoplasm and/or on the membrane, while INSM1 is a transcription factor with nuclear expression (Fig. 1). The distinct nuclear staining pattern is easier to interpret and less prone to background artifacts. These attributes are particularly advantageous, especially in small biopsies where tissue availability is limited. In routine practice, the differential diagnosis of primary pulmonary neuroendocrine neoplasms relies on careful morphologic evaluation in combination with immunohistochemical profiling. Our results indicate that INSM1 (SP493) antibody provides robust nuclear staining across primary lung neuroendocrine neoplasms, complementing conventional markers such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and CD56. However, correlation with H&E morphology and other markers, such as TTF1, p40, or Napsin A, remains essential, as INSM1 expression should be interpreted within the context of tumor architecture and cytologic features. Another advantage of this assay is the relatively rapid turnaround time; when performed on BenchMark ULTRA instruments, the IHC assay for INSM1 can be completed in approximately 3 h, ensuring a standardized and efficient workflow. This integrated approach reinforces diagnostic confidence and helps distinguish true neuroendocrine neoplasms.

Several limitations of this study deserve consideration. First, instead of whole tissue sections, TMAs were used in the study to evaluate the expression of the neuroendocrine markers in lung neoplasms, which may not be representative of the whole tumor. Second, this study evaluated only primary lung neoplasms and not tumors that had metastasized to the lungs, which represents a limitation of our analysis. Third, although we observed variation in the INSM1 staining intensity among different cases, the correlation between staining intensity and the degree of neuroendocrine differentiation was not systematically assessed. This study also did not evaluate the relationship between pTNM staging and INSM1 expression, as staging data were not available for all cases. Finally, the TMAs contained no LCNEC cases to assess INSM (SP493) antibody performance. LCNEC is a rare subtype of lung neuroendocrine neoplasms accounting for only 0.3–3.0% of all lung neoplasms. However, our additional internal data showed the presence of INSM1 in the biopsies of LCNEC stained with INSM1 (SP493) antibody. This finding is consistent with the study by Sakakibara et al., which reported INSM1 expression in LCNEC [15]. Future studies incorporating LCNEC cases, metastatic tumors, whole tissue sections, and analyses of INSM1 staining intensity in relation to the degree of neuroendocrine differentiation and pTNM staging are warranted to further validate these findings.

Overall, this study confirmed that the new IHC assay with INSM1 (SP493) antibody is highly specific and sensitive in detecting primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasms. Our findings support the growing evidence that INSM1 (SP493) antibody represents a valuable addition to the diagnostic toolbox for neuroendocrine neoplasms. Nevertheless, we recognize that established markers remain useful, particularly in confirming neuroendocrine morphology and capturing the biological heterogeneity of these neoplasms. Therefore, the integration of INSM1 into a broader immunohistochemical panel may represent the most effective approach in routine practice.
