Effects of ultrasound combined with pH-shift assisted glycosylation modification on the structural and functional properties of by-product proteins extracted from shrimp heads
Authors: Yufeng Li, Jinsong Wang, Yi Wu, Shuxian Guo, Lihong Zhu, Xiaoyan Ou, Tong Li, Yong Zhao, Jing Jing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA protein isolated from the heads of M. rosenbergii shrimp (MHPs), a shrimp by-product, possesses high nutritional value, but its low solubility limits its application. This study examined the effects of ultrasound coupled with the pH-shift assisted glycosylation modification of MHPs on the physicochemical properties and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) loading capacities of the proteins. The results indicated that ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation effectively enhanced the glycosylation degree of MHPs to 48.3 %. MHPs processed by this method exhibited looser spatial structures, higher thermal denaturation temperatures (98.5 °C) and lower interfacial tension, which facilitated the formation of a denser, more ordered network at the oil/water interface. These favorable changes effectively improved the solubility, oil/water adsorption capacity and emulsifying performance of MHPs. Notably, the MHPs modified using ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation had a higher EGCG loading capacity, and the prepared MHP-EGCG complex exhibited an excellent antioxidant capacity.Keywords: Proteins, Ultrasound, Glycosylation, Loading capacity

Introduction

The shrimp (Macrobrachium rosenbergii) is an economically significant aquatic species with an annual production of approximately 250,000 tons (Wu et al., 2021). The whole shrimp contains 25–26 % protein, 1–2 % lipid, 2 % ash and 69–71 % water, which provides a large amount of high-quality animal protein for humans (Rasid, Brown, Pratoomyot, Monroig, & Shinn, 2017). However, the head of shrimp is often treated as a by-product during industrial processing and consumption. In reality, the head of shrimp also contains high levels of nutrients, including protein, fat and carbohydrates. Among them, the shrimp head proteins (MHPs) contain all of the essential amino acids (EAAs) for humans and have an EAA content of 41.09 %, which is significantly higher than some commonly consumed plant proteins (Wu et al., 2021). However, MHPs are less soluble than other common animal and plant proteins, which greatly limits their application in food (Bohrer, 2017).

The Maillard reaction between proteins and reducing sugars is a promising method to enhance protein solubility and functional properties (Li, Xu, Xu, Zeng, & Zhou, 2022; Liu et al., 2020). It is generally considered environmentally friendly and safe because it does not require other chemical crosslinking agents (such as propylene glycol, lauric acid monoglyceride, etc) and can occur under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, time and humidity (Liu, Wang, Hu, Cai, & Qin, 2019). Lactose is a typical disaccharide, composed of glucose and galactose, which provides energy and essential nutrients in the human metabolism. Additionally, lactose promotes the growth of lactic acid bacteria in the human intestine, brain development in infants and young children and the absorption of calcium by the human body. More importantly, previous studies have found that lactose coupled with proteins through the Maillard reaction can improve the physicochemical and functional properties of proteins (Liu et al., 2019). However, traditional wet heat glycosylation technology has inherent limitations such as long reaction cycles and low glycosylation grafting efficiency, which requires the development of technical methods to improve the degree of protein glycosylation.

Ultrasound is a safe, environmentally friendly food processing technology that has been widely adopted in the food industry (Li et al., 2024). The energy generated by ultrasonic cavitation effectively destroys protein structure and accelerates high-speed particle collisions, thereby effectively promoting the binding of proteins and reducing sugars. Therefore, ultrasound is increasingly being used to improve the functional properties of proteins (Chen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). However, ultrasound has a limited effect on protein structure and cannot expose the active groups encapsulated inside the insoluble protein, which significantly constrains the efficiency of protein glycosylation grafting. Consequently, it is necessary to explore other technologies that can enhance the efficiency of MHPs glycosylation and improve its functional characteristics using the synergistic effect of ultrasound.

Another simple, highly efficient method for improving protein solubility and functional properties is pH-shift treatment (Zeng et al., 2024). When proteins are exposed to extremely acidic or alkaline pH conditions far from their isoelectric points, the increase in electrostatic repulsion can cause the partial unfolding of the protein molecules. Subsequently, once the pH of the solution is adjusted back to 7, the protein structure folds called the ‘molten globule state’ (Tan et al., 2021). This unfolding process involves the release of functional groups encapsulated within the protein, effectively enhancing its ability to bind with sugar molecules. Based on previous research, it is hypothesized that the use of ultrasound combined with pH-shift treatment would effectively alter the spatial structure of MHPs, improve their glycosylation efficiency, and thus effectively enhance their physicochemical properties (solubility, emulsifying performance, and loading capacity, etc.).

Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a major bioactive constituent of green tea, is recognized for its significant health-promoting effects, including potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and potential anti-cancer properties (Li et al., 2022). However, EGCG is easy to degrade, and higher pH, oxygen concentration, and temperature can promote its degradation (Wang et al., 2012). Previous research has demonstrated that proteins can form soluble complexes with EGCG, improving its stability and preserving its antioxidant capacity (Feng et al., 2021). Due to these characteristics, EGCG was selected as a model compound in this study to evaluate the loading capacity of glycosylated-modified MHPs.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment on MHP glycosylation by analyzing changes in their physicochemical, foaming, and emulsifying properties. Additionally, we investigated the loading capacity of the modified MHPs for EGCG. These results are expected to improve the functional characteristics of the MHPs through ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation treatment with lactose, thereby expanding the potential uses for shrimp by-products in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Shrimp were purchased from local supermarkets (Shanghai, China), lactose and EGCG (99 %) were obtained from Aladdin Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and the rest of the reagents used in this study were analytical grade.

Glycosylation treatment

The MHPs were extracted from the by-products of M. rosenbergii heads using our previously established method (Wu et al., 2021). The MHPs solution was mixed with a lactose solution in a ratio of 1:1 (w/w), and sodium azide (0.01 %, w/w) was added to prevent microbial growth. The MHPs-lactose mixture was stirred magnetically for 2 h at room temperature.

For samples treated with ultrasound-assisted glycosylation, the pH value of the MHPs-lactose mixture solution was adjusted to 7.0, followed by ultrasound treatment at 20 kHz (300 W, 70 % amplitude) for either 10 or 20 min, respectively (on time: 5 s, off time: 5 s). For samples treated with ultrasound combined with pH-shift assisted glycosylation, the pH of the MHPs-lactose mixture was adjusted to 11 using 1 M NaOH, followed by sonication at 300 W for either 10 or 20 min (on time: 5 s, off time: 5 s). Subsequently, the pH of all samples was adjusted to 7.0 using HCl (1 M). After stirring for 24 h, all samples were dialyzed using a dialysis bag (8–14 kDa) to remove unreacted lactose. Finally, the glycosylated protein sample was freeze-dried.

The experiment included the untreated protein samples (control), protein samples treated with lactose glycosylation (U-0), protein samples prepared with ultrasound-assisted glycosylation treatment for 10 min (U-10) and 20 min (U-20) and protein samples prepared with the ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation for 10 min (U + pH -10) and 20 min (U + pH -20).

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The FT-IR analysis of MHPs was conducted following the method outlined by Wang et al. (2012). MHPs were combined with KBr at a ratio of 1:100. The samples were scanned 64 times within the range of 500 to 4000 cm−1 using an FT-IR spectrometer. PeakFit software 4.12 (Chicago, IL, USA) was used to analyse secondary structure components from the amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1), and peak area to different secondary structures was calculated.

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

The fluorescence spectrum of the protein sample was determined using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The protein solution in the cuvette (0.2 mg/mL) was excited at 280 nm, with a scanning range of 290–450 nm and a slit width of 5 nm. The experiment was repeated 5 times and results were recorded.

The surface hydrophobicity (H0) analysis

The H0 of protein samples was assessed using 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) as a fluorescence probe. Five microliters of the protein sample (2 mg/mL) were mixed with 20 μL ANS (8.0 mM). The fluorescence intensity of the sample at 364 nm (excitation wavelength) and 475 nm (emission wavelength) was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The initial slope of the fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration curve represents the H0 of the sample.

Protein size and zeta potential measurements

The droplet size was measured using a Mastersizer 3000 (Malvern Instruments, U.K.). Deionized water was used as the dispersant. The zeta potential of protein were measured by Zetasizer Nano ZS90.

Microstructure observations

The microstructure of MHPs was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) following the approach of Zeng et al. (2024). The freeze-dried MHPs samples were coated with gold using a sputtering coating machine. Subsequently, the coated samples were observed using an SEM at a voltage of 30 kV.

The MHPs (0.5 g) were mixed with 10 mL phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and stirred for 1 h to ensure complete dissolution. The concentration of MHPs in the supernatant was determined by the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method, with bovine serum protein as the standard. The solubility of proteins was is calculated using the protein content in the supernatant/total protein content.

Interface tension analysis

The interfacial tension of MHPs was assessed using a drop tensiometer following the methods established by Li, Xu, et al. (2022). Prior to conducting the measurements, a roughened platinum plate was meticulously cleaned using deionized water, followed by flame sterilization to ensure a clean surface. A volume of 10 mL of the samples (0.5 wt% concentration) was carefully dispensed into a beaker with a diameter of 20 mm to prepare the aqueous phase. An automated sampler was then employed to introduce a droplet of sunflower seed oil into the protein solution, enabling the measurement of interfacial tension (25 °C).

Quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM-D) analysis

The adsorption behavior of MHPs on oil surfaces was investigated using a QCM-D machine (model E4). Before the experiment, 200 μL drop of a solution containing 0.5 % w/v sunflower seed oil in chloroform was applied to the sensor, which was then spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 90 s to ensure uniform droplet fraction and form a stable oil film. After that, the sensor was left to dry overnight at 37 °C. To ensure a stable baseline for frequency (f) and dissipation (D) measurements, each experiment was pre-equilibrated with water for 15 min. Protein solutions obtained from different glycosylation treatments (0.5 %, w/v) were introduced at the flow rate of 80 μL/min for adsorption measurement. The loosely bound or unbound protein samples were flushed away with Millipore water after 60 min (Wei et al., 2020).

Load performance analysis

The preparation of protein EGCG complex followed the previous research method with slight modifications (Liu et al., 2019). The MHPs treated with U + pH -10 assisted glycosylation were dissolved in 0.1 mol/L PBS (pH 7.0) and stirred at 4 °C for 12 h. Then the above solution was mixed with EGCG solutions of varying concentrations (0.01–2.0 mmol/L) and stirred continuously until an opalescent suspension formed, indicating the formation of the MHPs-EGCG complex.

Determination of antioxidant activity

The MHPs-EGCG complex solution and free EGCG solution were stored in the dark at 4 °C for 7 d, with a small amount extracted daily for antioxidant activity testing. The ability of the samples to scavenge free radicals was assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazine (DPPH−). In brief, 0.05 mL of the sample were mixed with 2.45 mL of DPPH− solution (0.06 mg/mL), and then incubated for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, the absorbance of the sample at 517 nm was recorded using a spectrophotometer, and the DPPH− clearance ability of sample was calculated based on the standard curve. The reduction activity of the sample was evaluated using ferric ion reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) according to previous research methods (Manesa, Kebede, Dube, & Nindi, 2020), 0.05 mL of samples were mixed with 2.45 mL of Fe3+ reagent, and then incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, the absorbance at 593 nm was recorded by a spectrophotometer.

Cell culture and enzyme activity analysis

When HepG-2 cells grew to a density of 80–90 % in the cell culture bottle, discarded the culture supernatant and rinsed with PBS. 1 mL of 0.25 % trypsin was added in the above sample to digest the cells, then gently blew and mixed the cells. The complete culture medium was added in the sample, and then continued to culture in a cell incubator for 24 h. Results and discussion

Degree of glycosylation

Glycosylation is a condensation reaction between the reducing sugar and the amino group on the protein side chain. The degree of glycosylation (DG) of proteins can be quantified by analyzing changes in the content of binding amino groups (Lu, Chi, & Chi, 2024). As shown in Fig. 1A, the DG in ultrasound-treated MHPs was significantly higher than the control group (28.2 %). This enhancement can be explained by ultrasound's ability to promote protein unfolding, which exposes nucleophilic residues (lysine, histidine and arginine) in accessible areas and therefore increases opportunities for glycosylation (Li et al., 2020; Tang, Yao, Xia, Cheong, & Tu, 2024). Notably, the MHPs treated with ultrasound combined with pH-shift had the highest grafting rate, regardless of treatment time (U + pH -10 or U + pH -20). The method of ultrasound combined with pH-shift glycosylation treatment was more effective at increasing the rate of DG (48.3 %) than ultrasound alone (p < 0.05). This phenomenon was attributed to the unfolding of protein molecules at pH values far above the isoelectric point of the protein, which exposed more amino groups and effectively enhanced the glycosylation reaction (Li et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022). Similarly, ultrasound combined with pH-shift glycosylation treatment improved the DG of pea protein isolates (Jiang et al., 2022).

Structural characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) is widely used to evaluate structural changes in proteins (Kano & Shiraki, 2023; Zhou et al., 2024). Fig. 1B showed the infrared spectrum of MHPs in different treatment groups. All samples exhibited three typical absorption peaks in the amide I band (1700–1600 cm−1), amide II band (1550–1500 cm−1) and hydroxyl group (3700–3200 cm−1). In detail, the amide I bands of glycosylated samples shifted from 1653.43 cm−1 to 1653.72 cm−1 compared with the native proteins. The red-shift of MHPs increased significantly after treatment with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation, likely due to the increased degree of glycosylation that promotes the formation of amide bonds and alters protein structure (Cheng et al., 2022). Additionally, the MHPs in the ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment group had the widest absorption peak at the hydroxyl group compared to the other treatment groups. The increased hydroxyl content can likely be explained by an increase in sugar chains added to protein chains.

Infrared spectroscopy analysis of amide I (1600–1700 cm−1) was used to examine secondary structural changes in the MHPs, which changed slightly after glycosylation treatment (Table 1). In untreated MHPs, random coil content was 24.5 %, α-helix was 19.2 %, β-turn was 16.5 % and β-sheet was 39.8 %. The α-helix and β-sheet content in glycated proteins decreased significantly, while the β-turn and random coil content increased notably, which suggests that lactose undergoes a binding reaction with the amino groups in the α-helix and β-sheet regions. Moreover, α-helix content is associated with the degree of protein grafting; as α-helix content decreases, the degree of grafting increases. The MHPs treated with ultrasound combined with pH-shift assisted glycosylation had lower α-helix content (14.1 % vs. 19.2 % in the control) and higher random coil content (31.2 % vs. 24.5 % in the control). These findings indicate that ultrasound-assisted glycosylation results in an expansion of the MHP's molecular secondary structure that enhances its conformational flexibility, and the extreme pH changes can cause the folding and rearrangement of protein structures, which results in looser, more disordered protein structures (Jiang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020).

Fluorescence spectra can reflect changes in the tertiary structure of proteins (Yufeng Li et al., 2024). In this study, the fluorescence spectra of MHPs from different treatment groups were shown in Fig. 1C, the maximum emission spectrum (λmax) of MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation was shifted from 342 nm to 348 nm. This phenomenon was attributed to micro-conformational changes caused by the grafting reaction between proteins and lactose. In addition, the ultrasound coupled pH-shift assisted glycosylation promotes protein unfolding and exposes previously buried functional groups that contribute to enhancing the glycosylation reaction (Jiang et al., 2022). Based on this information, we further analyzed the changes in surface hydrophobicity (H0) of MHPs (Fig. 1D). The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the H0 decreased significantly in glycosylated proteins, and the degree of reduction was negatively correlated with the degree of unfolding in the protein's tertiary structure. The unfolding of proteins exposed more hydrophilic groups that were concealed in the original protein structure, which resulted in a further decrease in H0 (Li et al., 2021). Therefore, the results of fluorescence spectroscopy and H0 suggest that ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation can effectively alter the spatial structure of MHPs, and promote the binding of lactose to proteins, which is also consistent with the FT-IR results.

Calorimetry is commonly employed to analyse changes in the thermal behavior of proteins (Li, Zhang, et al., 2022). The differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) spectra of MHPs from different treatment groups are shown in Fig. 1E. All of the samples exhibited a stable exothermic peak. The thermal transition temperature of the control sample was 92.3 °C, and the thermal stability improved after binding with lactose, although the degree of improvement varied. It is worth noting that the glycosylated protein samples prepared by administering the ultrasound combined with pH-shift treatment for 20 min displayed the highest thermal transition temperature, 98.5 °C. Previous studies have indicated that the formation of covalent bonds in proteins or changes in the spatial structure of protein molecules are the primary factors that affect thermal stability (Ma et al., 2020). The results of this study indicate that ultrasound combined with pH-shift treatment effectively enhances covalent cross-linking between lactose and MHPs, alters protein structure and increases spatial hindrance effects, which together prevent thermal aggregation. Ma et al. (2020) reported that a higher degree of protein unfolding was associated with higher thermal stability. In our study, the fluorescence spectroscopy results (Fig. 1C) indicate that tertiary conformation is promoted when protein molecules bind with lactose, which could be an additional reason for the improved thermal stability of the proteins.

To clarify whether the protein size alters post-modification, we measured the hydrodynamic diameter of the protein before and after MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation. In Fig. 3A-B, the results showed a significant change in protein size after modification: compared with the native protein (141.8 nm), the protein treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation exhibited an increased average particle size (187.3 nm for U + pH -10 and 190.2 nm for U + pH -20). This phenomenon is attributed to the ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation treatment promoting the unfolding of the protein structure: the high-energy ultrasound disrupts the non-covalent bonds that maintain the protein's compact conformation, while the glycosylation reaction further induces the exposure of internal structural domains. As a result, the protein transitions from a compact state to a more extended conformation, leading to an increase in hydrodynamic diameter. The change in Zeta potential can reflect the charged state on the surface of protein particles and the stability of the system. In Fig. 3 C, the trend of Zeta potential changes for all samples was the most unstable at pH 5. In contrast, the absolute Zeta potential of proteins in the U + pH -10 and U + pH -20 treatment groups was the highest, regardless of acidity or alkalinity. This proves that the combination of ultrasound coupled pH shift treatment can lead to protein unfolding, causing more charges to transfer from the inside of the protein to the surface, thereby making the protein system more stable (Yu, Ji, Chen, et al., 2020).

The particle size (A-B) and ζ-potential (C) analysis of control protein and treated protein samples.

Scanning electron microscopy was used to examine the microstructure of MHPs treated with different glycosylation methods. As shown in Fig. 2, the freeze-dried natural MHPs exhibited a large block structure, and the structure expanded after lactose glycosylation treatment. In addition, the MHPs treated with U + pH -10 assisted glycosylation displayed a loose, disordered structure. When the ultrasound duration was extended to 20 min, the disordered structure of the protein was more pronounced, likely because ultrasound can reduce protein particle size, which facilitates the binding of lactose to the protein (Lv et al., 2020). Notably, MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation exhibited a more relaxed, disordered structure, regardless of treatment time (10 min or 20 min). Together, these facts suggest that ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment can alter the folding structure of proteins, which effectively enhances the degree of protein glycosylation. This finding is consistent with the results of FT-IR and fluorescence spectroscopy.

Protein functional analysis

Protein solubility is one of the most crucial factors that influences functional characteristics such as foaming, emulsification and gelling (Li, Xu, et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4A, the natural MHPs had the lowest solubility (17.6 %) of all the samples. The solubility of glycosylated proteins increased significantly to 57.4 %, which suggests that the covalent binding of hydrophilic sugars to proteins can enhance interactions between proteins and water molecules, thereby increasing spatial repulsion and preventing aggregation. The solubility of MHPs treated with ultrasound-assisted glycosylation reached 74.8 %. Notably, the proteins treated with ultrasound combined with pH-shift assisted glycosylation exhibited a solubility of 82.3 %, which was significantly higher than the other treatment groups. This difference is likely due to its relatively high grafting rate. Another possible reason is that ultrasound treatment can convert insoluble protein particles into soluble ones (Jiang et al., 2022). Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated that ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation treatment can alter the surface chemical properties of protein molecules to effectively enhance their water solubility (Jiang et al., 2022). Li et al. (2020) also found that the combination of pH-shift and ultrasound treatment increased the solubility of rapeseed protein.

The change of solubility(A), FC and FS (B), WAC and OAC (C) of MHPs under different treatments.

The foaming ability (FA) and foam stability (FS) of proteins are important parameters for evaluating their functional properties. Fig. 4B showed the effect of different treatments on the FA and FS of MHPs. The FA of untreated MHPs was 125 %, while the FA of MHPs treated with ultrasound-assisted glycosylation for 10 and 20 min increased to 143 % and 145 %, respectively. The FA of MHPs treated by ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation increased to 160 %. A similar trend was seen in the FS of the MHPs. This is likely due to the changes in protein structure caused by the binding of lactose and protein. The additional unfolding of the protein molecular spatial structure after ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment facilitates their diffusion through the air-water interface and results in the formation of more stable foam (Wang et al., 2020). In addition, ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment reduces molecular interactions between protein molecules, which means that the protein molecules are more evenly dispersed throughout the solution; as a result, the cohesion and flexibility of the foam increase (Jiang et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2021).

The interactions between proteins and water or oil significantly influence the flavor and texture of food. High WAC and OAC values can enhance flavor retention and improve palatability while also indicating good emulsion ability (Lafarga, Alvarez, Villaro, Bobo, & Aguilo-Aguayo, 2019). As shown in Fig. 4C, the water absorption capacity (WAC) and oil absorption capacity (OAC) of the MHPs exhibited varying degrees of improvement after treatment with different glycosylation methods. The WAC of MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation increased from 3 g/g protein to 4 g/g protein and the OAC increased from 4 g/g protein to 6 g/g protein. These were the highest WAC and OAC among all the samples. The enhancement of WAC and OAC in MHPs can be attributed to the interactions between the side chains of non-polar residues and the hydrocarbon chains in fats (oils) and water molecules. Furthermore, the exposure of hydrophobic groups after protein expansion also contributes to increased oil retention (Resendiz-Vazquez et al., 2017).

Interfacial behavior and emulsifying performance

The water/oil interfacial tension of proteins can reflect their emulsifying properties. In general, a lower water/oil interfacial tension indicates better protein emulsifying properties (Li, Peng, et al., 2022). The interfacial tension values of MHPs treated with different glycosylation methods were shown in Fig. 5C. Compared to the native MHPs (12.3 ± 0.23 mN/m), interfacial tension at the water/oil interface decreased across all treatment groups to 10.9 ± 0.19 mN/m in MHPs treated with glycosylation, 9.7 ± 0.21 mN/m after U + pH -10 assisted glycosylation and 8.4 ± 0.18 mN/m after U + pH -20 assisted glycosylation. This reduction can be attributed to the introduction of sugar, which effectively enhances the amphiphilicity of proteins. In addition, the more relaxed structure of the MHPs in the ultrasound coupled with pH-shift treatment group promotes protein rearrangement at the oil/water interface, which is a significant factor in reducing protein interfacial tension (Li, Xu, et al., 2022).

The Δf (A), ΔD (B) and interfacial tension (C) and emulsification performance(D) analysis of MHPs under different treatments.

The thickness and stiffness of the oil/water interface layer are also important factors that influence protein emulsification, and a thick film at the oil/water interface can often promote emulsion stability. Δf reflects changes in mass adsorbed on the sensor surface, while ΔD reflects film viscoelasticity properties of the film. An increase in Δf usually indicates an increase in the amount of adsorbed material, which is related to the growth of the interfacial film thickness (Foudazi, Qavi, Masalova, & Malkin, 2015; Wei et al., 2020). Fig. 5A-B shows the Δf and ΔD curves of MHP adsorption performance on oil-coated sensors for each treatment. Negative Δf values and positive ΔD values were recorded in all samples, which indicates that these proteins were adsorbed onto the sensor. As the MHPs solution was continuously introduced into the QCM-D instrument, the value of Δf gradually decreased as ΔD values increased, which suggests that the oil film adsorbed by proteins on the sensor became thicker. After 60 min, washing the sensors with deionized water resulted in a slight increase in Δf and a decrease in ΔD, which represented the removal of MHPs that were either unbound or loosely bound to the oil film on the sensor. Previous studies have demonstrated that a thicker oil/water interface can generate greater repulsion between oil particles, which contributes to increasing protein emulsifying (Gong et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2024). Compared to the other treatment groups, the MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation had lower Δf values but higher ΔD values, which indicates the formation of a denser film on the oil's surface. As a result, the binding affinity and adsorption ability of the MHPs at the water/oil interface increased significantly, a finding that supports the interfacial tension results (Fig. 5C).

The emulsifying properties of the MHPs were further analyzed by EAI and ESI. EAI reflects the emulsion formation abilities of proteins, while ESI indicates the stability of the protein emulsion (Tontul et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 5D, the EAI (15 m2/g) and ESI (25 min) of the control MHPs were relatively poor. Both EAI and ESI increased significantly with glycosylation treatments. The MHPs treated with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation, had the highest EAI (45 m2/g) and ESI (55 min). The improved emulsification properties of MHPs after glycosylation modification may be due to their increased water solubility, which is essential for effective emulsification, facilitates their diffusion through the oil/water interface and aids in the formation of a stable layer that prevents oil droplets from aggregating (Gong et al., 2022). Additionally, the presence of hydrophilic chains attached to the protein surfaces can increase the steric repulsion between oil droplets, which in turn stabilizes emulsions (Jiang et al., 2022; Nooshkam, Varidi, Zareie, & Alkobeisi, 2023). The MHPs treated by ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation exhibited superior emulsifying properties and enhanced adsorption at oil/water interfaces that can be attributed to their enhanced water solubility, the presence of hydrophilic lactose chains, a high degree of grafting and looser spatial structures.

Collectively, the results suggest that treatment with compared with other methods, ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation can significantly improve the grafting rate, solubility, foaming ability, water/oil absorption, interfacial adsorption performance and emulsification abilities of MHPs compared to the other treatment methods. However, there were no significant differences observed between the MHPs that underwent treatment with U + pH -10 and those treated by U + pH -20. Therefore, we chose the glycosylated proteins treated with U + pH -10 for additional studies on the polyphenol (EGCG) loading capacity.

Analysis of load capacity for EGCG

The EGCG load capacity of MHPs was shown in Fig. 6A, and the load capacity of MHPs on EGCG was initially increased and then decreased with the increase of EGCG concentration, and the maximum number of loads was obtained when the EGCG concentration was 0.5 mM. After glycosylation treatment, the load capacity of the MHPs efficiency improved significantly, likely because the molecular unfolding induced by the preparation process exposes more EGCG binding sites, and changes in the protein's secondary structure also help to enhance the protein's EGCG binding ability (Wang, Huang, Jing, Ma, & Wang, 2021). Therefore, treating proteins with ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation modification can significantly enhance their EGCG loading capacity.

The average particle size (A), encapsulation efficiency (B), in vitro bioavailability (C), degradation rate (D) and antioxidant capacity (E-F) of complex in different processing groups.

The average particle size of the protein-EGCG complex was shown in Fig. 6B. The average particle size gradually increased with increasing EGCG concentration. This phenomenon occurs because EGCG acts like a multidentate ligand at high concentrations and connects proteins to form a protein-polyphenol complex. As the concentration of EGCG continues to rise, these complexes aggregate to form insoluble particles (Chen et al., 2019). However, the rate of increase of the average particle size was significantly lower in the glycosylated MHP-EGCG complex than the native MHP-EGCG complex. Lactose competes with proteins to bind to EGCG, which protects the protein surface from EGCG bridging through the steric hindrance provided by its sugar chains and results in the formation of fewer insoluble complexes (Zeng et al., 2024).

The bioavailability of EGCG is determined by measuring its residual content in micelles at the end of digestion in an in vitro gastrointestinal simulation system. As shown in Fig. 6 C, free EGCG was degraded substantially during digestion because of its instability; about 21.0 % of the EGCG remained after digestion. The formation of the glycosylated MHP–EGCG complex increased the encapsulation of EGCG, which effectively prevented its own degradation; 48.9 % of the EGCG remained after digestion, which was also significantly higher than previously reported glycosylated ovalbumin (36.4 %) (Qi et al., 2024). The observed results can be attributed to the binding of sugar chains in the MHPs. The steric hindrance restricts protein hydrolysis and modifies the enzyme's binding sites on the protein, which results in a greater amount of retained EGCG post-simulated digestion (Liu et al., 2020). Similar results have been reported for whey protein isolate, which effectively enhances the bioavailability of quercetin (Wang et al., 2023).

MHP-EGCG complex antioxidant activity and storage stability

EGCG is a polyphenolic compound that is prone to degradation or hydrolysis in aqueous solutions, resulting in a loss of its antioxidant capacity (Mehmood et al., 2022). The EGCG content decreased significantly in all treatment groups (Fig. 6D), with about 44.5 % of the free EGCG group remaining after 7 d of storage at 4 °C, more than 65 % of the EGCG molecules were degraded. Compared to the free EGCG, the degradation of EGCG loaded with natural MHPs was significantly reduced. The greatest retention rate was seen in EGCG molecules loaded with glycosylated MHPs, where more than 68.6 % were retained.

Fig. 6 E-F shows MHP's ability to scavenge free radicals and reduce iron ions, which reflects antioxidant capacity. All treatment groups exhibit varying degrees of reduction in their ability to scavenge free radicals as storage time increased. Free EGCG showed the greatest decrease in its free radical scavenging ability, while EGCG loaded with glycosylated MHPs had the highest scavenging ability. During the 7 d storage, the reduction ability of iron ions in different samples was as follows: EGCG loaded with glycosylated MHPs > EGCG loaded with native MHPs > Free EGCG, which is consistent with the results of the free radical scavenging assay. These results are consistent with the results of the free radical scavenging assay and indicate that the MHP-EGCG complex greatly enhances EGCG stability. Furthermore, the sugar chains grafted with lactose can contribute to protecting EGCG stability in solutions through a steric hindrance effect that reduces degradation and oxidation (Fan, Liu, Gao, Zhang, & Yi, 2018).

To further analyse the antioxidant capacity of the MHP-EGCG complex, we investigated the protective effects of the samples against H2O2-induced oxidative damage to HepG-2 cells. Before the experiment, we established an in vitro cell model of H2O2-induced cell damage. As shown in Fig. 7A, HepG-2 cell viability decreased with increasing H2O2 concentration. After treating cells with 0.5 mM H2O2 for 4 h, the cell viability decreased to approximately 50 %, which became the standard for subsequent experiments. The survival rate of HepG-2 cells in the H2O2 (0.5 mM) treatment group was about 50 % (Fig. 7B). The survival rate of cells treated with the native MHPs-EGCG complex after H2O2 treatment increased compared to the control. Similarly, after treatment with the glycosylated (U + pH -10) MHP-EGCG complex, the cell survival rate improved significantly. Additionally, the cell survival rate increased continuously as increasing amounts of the complex were added. Further observations of HepG-2 cell morphology (Fig. 7C) found that cell morphology changed significantly after the H2O2 treatment, and cells displayed significant decreases in quantity and nuclear atrophy. The survival rate of cells cultured with the glycosylated MHP-EGCG complex was greater than the control group, and their cell morphology showed very few changes after the application of H2O2 (Fig. 7 C2).

Changes in cell antioxidant enzyme content (CAT, SOD, and GSH-Px) reflect the resistance of cells to oxidative damage. In the control group, the enzyme content decreased after treatment with H2O2, from 32.89 to 10.12 mmol/mg protein for CAT, 98.56 to 45.67 mmol/mg protein for SOD and 36.73 to 11.23 mmol/mg protein for GSH-Px (Table 2). However, the cells pre-cultured with glycosylated MHP-EGCG complex retained significantly higher levels of CAT, SOD and GSH after H2O2 treatment compared to other groups. Additionally, they possess the ability to inhibit the generation of reactive oxygen species and eliminate free radicals in the body. Previous studies reported that the combination of proteins and polyphenols can preserve the antioxidant activity of polyphenolic compounds and enhance the antioxidant capacity of proteins (Cozmuta et al., 2024). In this study, the complexes prepared by combining the antioxidant polyphenol EGCG with MHPs exhibited the ability to scavenge free radicals and iron ions. More importantly, the complexes effectively enhanced the activities of CAT, SOD and GSH in cells, thereby protecting them from oxidative damage caused by H2O2.

Conclusions

In this study, ultrasound coupled with pH-shift was employed to assist the glycosylation modification of MHPs and effectively enhanced their degree of glycosylation. MHPs treated by ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation had looser secondary and tertiary structures, which improves their solubility, foaming ability, foam stability, OAC and WAC. These changes contributed to their emulsifying properties and their adsorption at the oil/water interface. The schematic diagram of the effect of ultrasound combined with pH-Shift assisted glycosylation treatment on MHPs functional characteristics is shown in Fig. 8. On this basis, we evaluated the EGCG loading capacity of the glycosylated MHPs and found that glycosylated MHPs can effectively reduce the oxidative degradation of EGCG. Additionally, the MHP-EGCG complexes exhibited strong free radical scavenging and antioxidant abilities. In summary, ultrasound coupled with pH-shift assisted glycosylation modification greatly enhances the physicochemical functional properties of MHPs and provides new possibilities for the development of strategies for the use of shrimp by-products.
