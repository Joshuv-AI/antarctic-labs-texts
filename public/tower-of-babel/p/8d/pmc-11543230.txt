Sublingual hematoma as a rare complication of warfarin therapy in a patient with rheumatic heart disease: a case report
Authors: Muhammad Idrees, Hritvik Jain, Abdullah Masood, Rana Muhammad Usama, Ramez M Odat, Hamza Bin Abid, Umair Khalid, Armghan Mahmood, Jyoti Jain, Hamdah Hanifa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist that prevents blood from clotting and is widely used in cardiovascular diseases to prevent thromboembolic events. The therapeutic window of this drug is very narrow, requires close monitoring of blood international normalized ratio (INR), and depends upon various factors like genetics and drug interactions. Warfarin’s most common side effect is bleeding, commonly from the GI tract, skin, and the genitourinary tract1.

A rare complication of warfarin use is sublingual hematoma, which can lead to serious conditions like severe airway blockage. There have been only a few reported cases of warfarin-related sublingual hematoma, highlighting a lack of literature on this uncommon complication. We present a rare case of sublingual hematoma after warfarin therapy in a patient with rheumatic heart disease (RHD). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of sublingual hematoma as a complication of warfarin therapy in Pakistan. Surgical CAse Report (SCARE) 2023 criteria have been followed in reporting this work2.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 44-year-old female, a recently diagnosed case of RHD, who developed sublingual hematoma while on warfarin. She presented to the outpatient clinic with complaints of pain and swelling under her tongue, causing difficulty swallowing and speaking for the past 2 days. She had been taking oral warfarin (5 mg/day) for the past 3 months with an INR target of 2–3, which was being monitored weekly. The dosage of warfarin was recently increased (7.5 mg/day) 4 days ago due to a below-target INR of 0.9 after consulting with the cardiology team. There was no history of trauma or active bleeding from any site. The patient had a history of acute upper respiratory infection 1 week ago, for which she was taking cephalosporins and acetaminophen.

On general examination, the patient was well-oriented and conscious, with no apparent signs of respiratory distress. Her vital signs were as follows: heart rate of 88 beats/min, blood pressure of 140/85 mmHg, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and temperature of 98.8oF. Oral examination revealed a tense, tender, red sublingual swelling involving the floor of the mouth and some parts of the ventral surface of the tongue [Fig. 1]. The tongue was slightly pushed upwards, and the patient had difficulty moving the tongue. Minor swelling of the chin was also observed. On further examination, a few petechiae and purpura could be seen on the thenar eminence of the right hand, but the rest of the general and systemic examination was unremarkable. Initial laboratory studies revealed a markedly deranged coagulation profile with elevated prothrombin time (PT), activated prothrombin time (aPTT), and an INR of 6.1. Total leukocyte count (TLC) was slightly increased (14 200/mm3). The rest of the investigations were within the normal range.

Showing sublingual hematoma at the time of presentation.

The diagnosis of sublingual hematoma due to warfarin was made based on the high INR and general examination showing petechiae and purpuras. She was admitted on short-term admission to rule out any impending airway compromise. Warfarin was immediately discontinued, and she was treated with fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and intravenous vitamin K. In 24 h, her signs and symptoms resolved, with no signs of respiratory distress at any point of treatment [Fig. 2]. We have referred the patient to a cardiologist for long-term follow-up and treatment.

Discussion

Warfarin is a frequently prescribed oral anticoagulant for clinical conditions like atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, and valvular conditions RHD3. Although there are newer anticoagulants with reduced risk of bleeding complications, warfarin remains the primary choice for anticoagulation therapy in developing nations such as Pakistan. Potential complications may include bleeding in various areas, such as the gastrointestinal tract, skin, nose, retroperitoneum, genitourinary tract, and central nervous system. Severe complications such as intracranial hemorrhage may result in hospitalization and increase the risk of mortality. The risk of bleeding is associated with a supratherapeutic INR of >4.54,5. The concomitant use of analgesics like aspirin, NSAIDs, and acetaminophen also increases the risk of bleeding by raising the INR4. Studies have shown that infection also plays a role in increasing the INR, possibly due to antibiotics or acetaminophen interaction or fever causing increased response to warfarin6. This underscores the importance of vaccines, which prepare the immune system to combat dangerous pathogens, reducing the severity of diseases in vaccinated individuals7,8.

Other studies in patients treated with both warfarin and antibiotics have suggested that antibiotics may act as an inhibitor or inducer of cytochrome p450_2C9, which is reflected in the effect of warfarin. An increase in INR levels has been observed when warfarin is used with macrolides and penicillin. In contrast, a decrease in INR levels occurred when some antibiotics, such as rifampin, were used with warfarin9. We still need many studies to determine the role of antibiotics and warfarin in potentially changing INR levels.

One of the rare complications of warfarin therapy is sublingual hematoma, typically presenting as discomfort in the throat, dysphagia, dysphonia, drooling, and voice changes. It can be potentially life threatening, causing severe respiratory obstruction if not addressed promptly10. Lepore mentioned this condition as a ‘pseudo-Ludwig’s phenomenon’ in 197611. Other areas of hematoma may include the retropharyngeal, submaxillary, epiglottis, and vocal cords5,12–14.

In a clinical case study by Ghosh et al., a 47-year-old man on warfarin for atrial fibrillation presented with a painless red swelling of the tongue, causing speech and swallowing difficulties, and his INR was significantly elevated at 4.8. Conservative management included discontinuing warfarin, administering vitamin K, and fresh frozen plasma, which normalized his INR and reduced the hematoma. The patient was transitioned to dabigatran with no further bleeding or embolic events during follow-up15. Similarly, in another study by Pathak et al., a 50-year-old woman on chronic warfarin therapy for pulmonary embolism presented to the emergency department with painful red swelling under the tongue, which was diagnosed as a case of sublingual hematoma. She was conservatively managed with a reversal of INR with oral vitamin K16. A similar case of sublingual hematoma secondary to warfarin therapy in a 43-year-old woman with prior mitral valve replacement was reported by Bektas et al. She was also managed with fresh frozen plasma and vitamin K, and the swelling resolved within 24 h17. A 79-year-old female case was managed similarly to our case by receiving both: intravenous vitamin K and fresh frozen plasma after she developed a hematoma at the base of her tongue as a result of the use of warfarin, which underscores the importance of using this therapeutic approach to manage such cases18. While Wang et al. revealed a rare association between warfarin use and hematoma occurrence in small bowel intramural, where the patient presented with severe abdominal pain, computed tomography showed significant swelling in the jejunal wall. The treatment plan was limited to vitamin K and fresh frozen plasma19. Mishra et al. reported a case of spontaneous sublingual hematoma in a 45-year-old patient, which was caused by aspirin therapy due to rheumatic heart disease. The patient experienced oral bleeding, difficulty swallowing, and speech disturbances, and was treated by discontinuing aspirin and administering intravenous vitamin K and dexamethasone to prevent tongue swelling and airway obstruction20.

When there is no respiratory obstruction, the primary approach to treatment is conservative. This involves stopping warfarin, correcting any blood clotting issues, and ensuring the airway is clear. In developing nations, Vitamin K and fresh frozen plasma may be given, and the patient is monitored for a few days until the issue resolves on its own21. However, in cases of respiratory obstruction, endotracheal intubation should be attempted, and if this fails, emergent tracheostomy or cricothyroidotomy should be considered22. Surgical incision and drainage is also advised in severe cases23. Caution is to be exercised since these interventions must be performed after anticoagulation is reversed, due to a risk of bleeding associated with such invasive procedures.

Close monitoring of INR is essential, particularly after increasing the warfarin dosage. The treating clinician should also pay special attention to the drug-drug interactions and other conditions that might alter INR while prescribing any medication to a patient on warfarin therapy. Timely diagnosis and appropriate management play a significant role in preventing grave complications and minimizing mortality.

Conclusion

Warfarin remains a critical anticoagulant in resource-limited settings, despite its associated risks.

Vigilant monitoring and management of INR levels are paramount to mitigate bleeding risks, including rare but serious complications such as sublingual hematoma. Prompt and conservative treatment, coupled with awareness of drug interactions, can effectively prevent life-threatening outcomes and ensure patient safety.
