Investigating the visceral adiposity–major depressive disorder link: a cross-sectional NHANES 2011–2014 analysis and Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Mengying Hu, Fei Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, the intricate interplay between physical health and mental well-being has garnered significant attention, specifically highlighting the potential influence of adipose tissue distribution, notably visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in the abdomen, on psychological states. VAT is implicated in systemic inflammation and metabolic disruptions, which are hypothesized to contribute to the onset and progression of mental health disorders, particularly major depressive disorder (MDD).MethodsTo investigate the relationship between VAT and MDD, we analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) covering 2011 to 2014. We employed multivariate logistic regression analysis to ensure the robustness of our findings. Interaction and stratified analyses were conducted to elucidate the complex relationships between these variables, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots were utilized to visualize these interactions. Furthermore, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to ascertain the directionality of the causal relationship.ResultsThe weighted, multivariable-adjusted logistic regression analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between VAT and the risk of MDD, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.635 (95% CI 1.004–2.664). Further analysis, after accounting for potential confounding factors, revealed a more pronounced association in women, with an OR of 1.903 (95% CI 1.051–3.445). Interaction analysis indicated a potential gender-specific association between VAT and MDD, suggesting that the relationship may differ by sex (P for interaction = 0.003). Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis supported a bidirectional causal relationship between VAT and MDD. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness and reliability of the results.ConclusionThis study suggests that monitoring and managing VAT could enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of MDD and provide new insights for developing more effective treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40519-025-01777-6.

Background

Over the past few decades, the prevalence of obesity among adult males has seen a significant increase, rising from 3.2 to 10.8%, and the rate among adult females has escalated from 6.4 to 14.9% [1]. The intricate relationship between physical health and mental well-being has garnered increasing attention in recent years, particularly concerning the potential influence of adipose tissue distribution on psychological states. Among various adipose depots, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) has emerged as a pivotal factor in modulating systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [2, 3]. Although VAT accounts for only 5–20% of total body fat, it functions as a major endocrine and immune organ due to its extensive blood supply and active metabolic characteristics [4, 5]. Thus, considering VAT as a key clinical indicator for evaluating fat content is essential for a more comprehensive understanding and effective management of obesity and associated diseases.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a commonly occurring and complex mental health disorder that has attracted considerable research interest. It is characterized by a persistent depressive mood, anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure in previously enjoyed activities), and a range of somatic symptoms, which may include changes in appetite, sleep disturbances, and alterations in energy levels [6]. MDD not only leads to significant disability but also contributes substantially to the global burden of illness [7]. Its impact goes beyond the individual, affecting family dynamics, community structures, and the functioning of society as a whole.

In recent years, MDD and obesity, two major health concerns, are increasingly prevalent and often co-occur [8]. MDD is associated with changes in hormones, neurotransmitters, and inflammation, which can affect energy balance and fat distribution [9, 10]. Conversely, VAT, a major source of inflammatory molecules, may play partitioning a role in mood and cognitive functioning [11, 12]. It is still unclear how MDD and VAT are connected and how factors like gender, environment, and lifestyle might be involved. Understanding the relationship between MDD and VAT could lead to new insights and more effective treatments for both mental and physical health issues related to these conditions.

This study aims to investigate the relationship between MDD and VAT, focusing on their interactions through cross-sectional research and Mendelian randomization (MR) studies. This examination is essential for understanding the co-occurrence of depression and obesity, as well as for developing targeted treatment strategies.

Method

Study design

This study comprises two parts. First, a cross-sectional analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database was conducted to identify potential associations between VAT and MDD. Next, a two-sample bidirectional MR study was employed to further validate and elucidate the causal relationship between VAT and MDD.

Cross-sectional study

The selection of study samples

This study utilized data from the NHANES cycles of 2011–2012 and 2013–2014. NHANES, a comprehensive survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, evaluates health and nutrition through demographics, physical exams, biochemical markers, dietary intake, and health behaviors [13]. Out of the original 19,931 participants, several exclusion criteria were applied, and 5,832 participants who had both physical measurements and depression assessments were included in this study (Fig. 1). First, 9,825 without body composition data were excluded. Then, 3,731 who did not complete depression questionnaires were removed. To mitigate bias, an additional 543 participants who were either pregnant or under 20 years of age were excluded. The final sample comprised 5832 participants. For this study, VAT content was used as the independent variable, measured precisely by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) technology from the NHANES database. DXA provides reliable data on fat content in various body regions, including VAT [14]. Depressive symptomatology, the dependent variable, was assessed using validated depression questionnaires PHQ-9, which effectively measure depressive symptoms among participants [15]. The PHQ-9, based on DSM-IV (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) criteria, evaluates symptoms over the past 2 weeks through self-report, which covers nine symptoms, with participants rating frequency on a 4-point scale (0–3) [16]. In this study, a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10 is regarded as indicative of MDD [17].

The selection of covariates and criteria

To minimize potential interference from confounding variables, we carefully selected covariates for our analysis. We took into account their established associations with MDD and VAT, their significance in epidemiological studies, and their availability in the NHANES database. These covariates include sociodemographic factors, physical measurements, laboratory test results, and comorbid conditions. The sociodemographic factors include age, gender, race, smoking, and alcohol use. Physical measurements include BMI, A/G(android/gynoid) ratio, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), VAT, and body fat percentage. Laboratory tests encompass triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), fasting blood glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), lymphocyte and neutrophil counts, and the triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) index, calculated as ln[triglycerides*fasting blood glucose/2] [18]. Comorbidities considered include hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. Diabetes diagnosis is confirmed through lab tests, medication use, and self-reporting. Diagnosis of hypertension and hyperlipidemia was relied on self-reports from the NHANES questionnaire.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R software, following National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) guidelines for NHANES data. The data were first organized and described in terms of mean and variance for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. For normally distributed data, we used mean and standard deviations for descriptive statistics and employed independent samples t-tests to analyze differences between two groups and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons among multiple groups. For non-normally distributed data, we used the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for comparisons among three or more groups. Additionally, percentages were calculated for categorical variables, and Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were utilized for categorical variables. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation techniques. We employed logistic regression to investigate the association between MDD and VAT, adjusting for relevant covariates. Significant covariates (p < 0.05) were incorporated into the final model. To visualize this relationship, we utilized restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots with three knots, selected to minimize the Akaike information criterion (AIC).

To enhance the robustness of our findings, we performed stratified and multiplicative interaction analyses. The latter will assess the outcomes among individuals exposed to multiple factors compared to those exposed to fewer or no factors, allowing us to determine whether these factors have multiplicative effects. A p < 0.05 will be deemed statistically significant.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Data source

We utilized a genome-wide association study (GWAS) based dataset from the UK Biobank, encompassing 20,038 female and 19,038 male individuals [19]. The cohort comprised individuals from diverse ethnic backgrounds, including European, East Asian, South Asian, and African descent, with 96.9% of participants being of European ancestry. MRI scans were reviewed by expert radiologists to ensure data quality, and advanced deep learning techniques were employed to accurately calculate adipose tissue volume. For our second data source, we utilized the UK Biobank resource [20]. This prospective cohort recruited over 500,000 individuals aged 37–73 years across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. All participants provided written informed consent for data collection, analysis, and record linkage. A recent study established two distinct sub-cohorts within the UK Biobank to predict VAT: a VAT training dataset, where VAT mass was directly quantified using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; GE Healthcare Lunar iDXA scanner) for prediction model development, and a VAT application dataset, wherein VAT levels were derived algorithmically using the developed prediction models [coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.76 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.78)]. Following stringent quality control procedures, the final cohorts comprised 4,198 participants in the training dataset for model construction and 325,153 participants in the application dataset for subsequent analyses.

For the genetic instruments related to MDD, we relied on the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium’s GWAS meta-analysis, which integrated data from seven MDD studies (https://pgc.unc.edu/). This analysis encompassed 135,458 MDD cases and 344,901 controls, enabling a precise evaluation of the relationship between MDD and VAT [20]. For our second data source, we utilized the cohort from the UK Biobank (113,769 MDD cases and 208,811 controls). Crucially, our replication focused on key genetic loci and pathways implicated in MDD pathogenesis, as established by Howard et al. [21].

The selection of instrumental variables

To ensure the accuracy and validity of the chosen instrumental variables, we adhered to stringent criteria for screening single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) closely associated with both traits as instrumental variables [22]. The chosen SNPs had to meet three core assumptions. First, the genetic variation must be independently associated with the exposure trait; second, the genetic variation must not be influenced by other potential confounding factors; and third, the genetic variation should affect the outcome trait only through its impact on the exposure trait [22].

We set a genome-wide significance level of p < 5 × 10–8 and chose SNPs that clustered within a 1-Mb window with low linkage disequilibrium (LD R2 < 0.01). The effectiveness of these SNPs was evaluated using the F-statistic and R2 value. SNPs with an F-statistic of 10 or higher were deemed robust, confirming their association with the target traits [19]. We calculated the F-statistic using a formula that considers sample size (N), number of instrumental variables (K), and the proportion of variance explained by these variables (R2). We identified 40 SNPs associated with VAT and 43 SNPs associated with MDD, which serve as important genetic instruments for our analysis [23].

Statistical analysis

In this study, we investigated potential causal links between exposures and outcomes using MR analyses. We employed five MR methods, including inverse-variance weighted (IVW) [24], weighted median [25], MR-Egger regression, simple mode, and weighted mode [25]. Our primary focus was on the IVW method, which provides unbiased estimates under the assumption that instrumental variables do not exhibit horizontal pleiotropy [26]. The IVW method is considered reliable if at least half of the variables are valid, and it is supported by other methods for sensitivity analysis [27]. We deemed a causal relationship valid if all robust methods indicated the same direction and the IVW method produced a significant p-value (< 0.05).

The MR-Egger regression method accounts for pleiotropy by including an intercept term, which may reduce statistical power but increases confidence intervals (CIs) [28]. We converted beta values to odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity among causal estimates suggests possible violations of MR assumptions. We used Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.05 indicates significant heterogeneity) and MR-Egger regression to assess pleiotropy. Additionally, we assessed the potential pleiotropy of instrumental variables using MR-Egger regression. To identify potential outlier instrumental variables, we performed a leave-one-out analysis, sequentially excluding each SNP and conducting MR analysis on the remaining SNPs. We also applied MR-PRESSO to check for horizontal pleiotropy [26]. Forest plots were used to visualize the impact of SNPs on outcomes, ensuring the stability of MR results [29]. As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted reverse MR to examine reverse causality by swapping the roles of exposure and outcome.

The study results are presented as ORs per standard deviation (SD) increase, with 95% CIs. All tests were two-sided, with significance set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3) with the ‘Two Sample MR’ and ‘MR-PRESSO’ packages.

Result

Baseline characteristics

The study encompassed a total of 5832 participants, including 3026 males (51.96%) and 2806 females (48.04%). With valid VAT indicators and PHQ-9 survey data, these participants were considered highly representative of the broader non-institutionalized U.S. population, estimated at approximately 70,982,033 residents. Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of the participants ’ attributes, categorizing them based on depression status. The analysis revealed a higher prevalence of women among the MDD group, with 64.5% of the MDD group being female, significantly more than the normal group. Laboratory tests revealed that patients with MDD exhibited significantly higher levels of fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin, and triglycerides, indicating a pronounced dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism in this population. Body composition assessments demonstrated that the MDD group had a significantly higher body mass index (BMI) and greater accumulation of both VAT and SAT compared to the normal group. Furthermore, the MDD group showed a significantly higher prevalence of smoking and comorbid diabetes than the normal group (Table 1). Multivariate regression analysis

In this study, VAT levels were categorized into four quartiles (quartiles 1 to 4) for analysis, as shown in Table 2. The preliminary analysis revealed a significant positive correlation (quartile Q4 vs. Q1) between VAT and MDD across all quartiles (p < 0.001), indicating a relationship between elevated VAT levels and an increased risk of MDD. In Model 2, after adjusting for variables such as gender, age, and ethnicity, the association between VAT and MDD remained significant (quartile Q4 vs. Q1). Upon conducting stratified multivariate regression analysis by gender, a significant correlation between VAT and MDD was found among female participants (p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model 3, which controlled for confounding factors, diabetes comorbidity, smoking status, and BMI, a positive correlation between VAT (quartile Q4 vs. Q1) and MDD persisted. Notably, among females, individuals in the fourth quartile exhibited a significantly heightened risk of MDD compared to those in the first quartile. Conversely, for male participants, none of the models revealed a significant correlation. These findings underscore the gender-specific associations between VAT and MDD, suggesting the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Subgroup analyses

Further subgroup analyses examined how factors such as age, gender, race, marital status, smoking, and drinking habits influence the relationship between VAT and MDD (Table 3). Interaction effects were evaluated by incorporating multiplicative interaction terms between VAT and each stratified variable into the model. The results of the subgroup and interaction analysis clearly demonstrated that the association between VAT and MDD was particularly significant in females, with an OR of 2.67 (95% CI 1.94, 3.70) and a high level of statistical significance (p < 0.001). However, in the male group, this association was not found to be significant (P = 0.221). The p-value for the gender interaction analysis was 0.003, further confirming the significant role of gender in the association between VAT and MDD. Mendelian randomization analysis

In this study, a total of 43 instrumental variables (IVs) associated with MDD and 40 IVs related to VAT were identified from GWAS databases (Supplementary Table 1). To increase the number of IVs, the P-value threshold for VAT-related IVs was lowered to 1 × 10⁻5. To ensure independence, the Phenoscanner online platform (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) was used to exclude confounding variables. Afterward, 2 SNPs (rs148107115 and rs75940532) associated with sleep disorders and 1 SNP (rs77407885) related to emotional dysregulation were removed. All IVs had F-statistics greater than 10, indicating their strong statistical power. The primary analysis employed was the IVW method to investigate the causal relationship between the exposure and outcome. All IVs were subsequently validated using GWAS Atlas (https://atlas.ctglab.nl/), confirming concordance with initial Phenoscanner-based screening performed during our analytical period.

When MDD was considered the exposure and VAT the outcome, the IVW analysis significantly revealed a causal association between MDD and VAT, with an OR of 1.080 (95% CI: 1.005–1.161, p = 0.035) (Fig. 3). Furthermore, four additional methods supported the robustness of the IVW results. The MR-Egger regression test for horizontal pleiotropy indicated no significant pleiotropic effects (MR-Egger intercept = 0.005, SE = 0.006, p = 0.320) (Table 4). The global test P-value of MR-PRESSO was 0.366, and no individual SNP significantly influenced the robustness of the results in the leave-one-out analysis (Table 4), further confirming the stability of the study. The Cochran Q statistic for heterogeneity analysis was 48.55, with a p-value of 0.370, suggesting no significant heterogeneity among SNPs (Table 5). Heterogeneity testing in MR

Concurrently, we conducted a reverse MR analysis. When VAT was considered the exposure and MDD the outcome, the IVW analysis revealed a modest positive association between VAT and MDD, suggesting a 6% increase in MDD risk for every standard deviation increase in genetically predicted VAT, with an OR (95% CI) of 1.06 (1.010–1.110, p = 0.009) (Fig. 3). The MR-Egger regression test for horizontal pleiotropy also showed no significant pleiotropic effects (Egger intercept = 0.003, SE = 0.003, p = 0.271) (Table 4). The global test P-value of MR-PRESSO was 0.343, and the leave-one-out analysis did not reveal any significant impact of individual SNPs on the robustness of the results (Table 4), further validating the stability of the study. The Cochran’s Q statistic for heterogeneity analysis was 43.91, with a p-value of 0.271, indicating no significant heterogeneity among SNPs.

Building upon evidence from Karlsson et al. [20], a total of 192 IVs associated with VAT were identified from GWAS databases. Our Mendelian randomization analysis robustly demonstrates that genetically predicted VAT exerts a causal effect on MDD risk. The IVW method revealed a significant positive association (OR = 1.100; 95% CI 1.030–1.164; p = 0.003), which was consistently supported by four complementary sensitivity approaches (MR-Egger, weighted median, penalized weighted median, and contamination mixture methods). Further sensitivity analyses confirmed the absence of directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept, p = 0.414) and heterogeneity (Q-pval > 0.05), with complete methodological details and results archived in Supplementary Table S (3–5) and Supplementary Figure S5.

Discussion

In this study, we revealed a significant positive correlation between VAT levels and the risk of MDD, particularly among women. Furthermore, the MR analysis provided additional support for a bidirectional causal relationship between MDD and VAT. These findings offer valuable insights into the mechanisms through which visceral fat influences mental health, highlighting their practical implications within the realm of public health.

Previous numerous studies have indicated that indicators associated with adipose tissue accumulation, such as VAT and weight-adjusted waist circumference index, are correlated with an increased risk of MDD. A cross-sectional study conducted in Korea with 7,238 participants found that each 1 cm2 increase in VAT was associated with a 1.006-fold increase in the risk of depression among women [30]. Similarly, Lee et al. found that in overweight premenopausal women, depressive symptoms were linked to VAT accumulation rather than SAT. Moreover, VAT accumulation increased the risk of cardiovascular disease in women [31]. Recent research utilizing the NHANES database indicated that each one-unit increase in the weight-adjusted waist circumference index was associated with an 18% increase in the likelihood of depressive symptoms (OR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.34) [32].

Previous longitudinal study has demonstrated that changes in body composition positively affect symptom improvement in patients with depression [33]. Therefore, interventions designed to reduce VAT may offer benefits for alleviating depressive symptoms [34]. Our research provides evidence that suggests a potential correlation between elevated VAT and the development of depressive symptoms. Additionally, our study indicates that patients with MDD exhibit a significant increase in VAT, highlighting the importance of examining VAT in patients with MDD, as VAT accumulation is associated with increased cardiovascular risk [35, 36], and may also be linked to a higher risk of other adverse events [37, 38].

Our study supports numerous previous findings highlighting a significant gender dimorphism in the relationship between depression and obesity. A comprehensive cross-sectional study conducted in Germany reported a comorbidity rate of 1.3% (95% CI 0.8–2.0%) for depression and obesity in males, whereas the rate in females was significantly higher at 2.0% (95% CI 1.3–3.0%). Subsequent analysis indicated that low socioeconomic status and limited social support were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of comorbid depression and obesity among women [8]. Another cross-sectional study involving healthy individuals supported these findings by demonstrating a strong correlation between depressive symptoms and metabolic risk factors, including waist circumference, fasting blood glucose, and blood pressure, in women [39]. These gender disparities in depression are likely due to distinct molecular mechanisms, particularly the differences in the primary dysfunctional cell types and genes involved in depression between genders [40]. A plausible explanation for these differences is that women experience unique life events (such as pregnancy, menopause, and caregiving responsibilities), distinct risk patterns (including hormonal fluctuations and higher rates of autoimmune disorders), and differential disease exposures compared to men [41]. Specifically, women show more pronounced activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, especially during menopause when estrogen levels decline. This activation may lead to significant HPA axis dysregulation and potentially elevated cortisol levels [42, 43]. Future research should prioritize exploring the specific interplay between depression and VAT in female patients to develop more targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for this complex condition.

Our findings are consistent with previous meta-analyses, which demonstrate a bidirectional association between depression and obesity, especially among young and middle-aged women [44, 45]. However, a study by Speed et al. suggests that elevated BMI may potentially lead to depression, rather than vice versa [46]. This finding does not contradict our research, as increased BMI can elevate the risk of depression due to associated social stigma and physiological factors [47]. Subsequent research indicates that BMI may act as an intermediary that is influenced by inflammatory factors in the development of depression [48]. However, the impact of depression on body composition is more nuanced, while BMI alone may not fully capture the distinction between fat and lean mass. Therefore, VAT is considered a more appropriate indicator. A longitudinal study of a Chinese aging cohort suggests that abdominal obesity, assessed based on waist circumference, reduces the risk of depression in elderly Asian men [49], challenging the hypothesis that increased VAT is a direct cause of depression [33]. However, this conclusion is significant only in a specific population (elderly Asian men), highlighting the gender heterogeneity in the interaction between VAT and depression [50]. Future research should delve deeper into potential differences across genders, age groups, and ethnicities, aiming to provide a more comprehensive and accurate scientific basis.

Although the exact mechanisms underlying the bidirectional relationship between depression and VAT remain unclear, research has identified several potential biological pathways. First, depression and obesity may have overlapping genetic predispositions [51], of which the overactivation of the HPA axis is identified as a key mechanism linking these conditions. Current research indicates that dysregulation of the stress system associated with MDD [52] contributes to excessive activation of the HPA axis and disruption of its negative feedback mechanisms [37, 53, 54]. This condition is analogous to but less severe than Cushing’s syndrome and is termed pseudo-Cushing’s syndrome [55]. The cortisol levels in patients with pseudo-Cushing’s syndrome are elevated, which promotes appetite and VAT accumulation, but inhibits thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue [9, 56]. A study evaluating body composition in 10 depressed women and 12 healthy women found a significant positive correlation between VAT and adrenal gland volume in the depressed group, indirectly supporting the hypothesis of HPA axis overactivation and VAT functioning as an endocrine organ [57]. Concurrently, chronic inflammation related to VAT accumulation further influences MDD progression through cytokine-mediated immune and neural pathways. For example, elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP disrupt the HPA axis and hippocampal neurogenesis, while microglial activation alters serotonin (5-HT) and dopamine (DA) pathways, critical for mood regulation [58]. Research has shown that leptin resistance and impaired signaling pathways associated with VAT accumulation can enhance central inflammation, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms [59, 60]. Additionally, in animal models, both peripheral and central administration of leptin have demonstrated antidepressant effects, reversing depressive-like behaviors induced by chronic unpredictable stress [61]. Furthermore, MDD frequently manifests as a dysregulation of the stress system in response to stressful environments, which may promote social avoidance, sleep disorders, and emotional eating [62]. These lifestyle factors further contribute to the risk of VAT accumulation [63].

This study has several limitations. First, as the NHANES data come from large-scale epidemiological research, the methods for assessing depression are somewhat crude. Future studies should use more precise measures to evaluate depression severity. Second, while gender-specific differences were noted in the cross-sectional analysis, the lack of corresponding GWAS data limited the exploration of these associations in the MR study. Future research should investigate gender-specific links between genetic predisposition to MDD and elevated VAT. Moreover, the MR analysis was limited to individuals of European ancestry, which may restrict the applicability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Finally, differences between the European cohort and the diverse American population in the cross-sectional survey may introduce confounding variables. Future research should aim for a more homogeneous ethnic sample to better examine the VAT–MDD relationship. Our findings provide a new perspective on the treatment and management of MDD, emphasizing the vital role of comprehensive assessment and management of body composition in depression treatment. The strengths of this study include its integration of a large-scale cross-sectional analysis from NHANES with MR. This methodology extensively investigates the epidemiological relationship between VAT and MDD risk while addressing potential limitations in traditional epidemiological research such as residual confounding, reverse causality, and measurement errors through the use of MR. This approach further elucidates the potential causal link between VAT and MDD.
