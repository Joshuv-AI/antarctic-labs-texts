Causal Effects of Plasma Proteins in Polycystic Ovary Syndrome: A Proteome‐Wide Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Tong Yu, Pengfei Zeng, Hang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine and metabolic disorder with complex pathogenesis and limited targeted therapeutic options. This study presents a proteomics‐informed approach to identify potential plasma protein targets for PCOS intervention using causal inference methods.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive proteome‐wide Mendelian randomization analysis by integrating 1 Mb plasma cis‐acting protein quantitative trait loci datasets with PCOS genome‐wide association study summary statistics. To enhance robustness, we applied complementary approaches including summary‐data‐based MR, the Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments test, and colocalization analysis. Identified targets were further assessed for druggability using curated drug databases.ResultsThe MR analysis revealed 33 plasma proteins significantly associated with PCOS risk. Among these, FGF23 and SH2B3 showed the strongest evidence of a causal role, supported by SMR, HEIDI, and colocalization analyses. FGF23 was positively associated with PCOS risk and implicated in inflammatory and metabolic pathways, while SH2B3 was inversely associated and linked to anti‐inflammatory signalling.ConclusionsThis study establishes a causal link between specific plasma proteins and PCOS and identifies FGF23 and SH2B3 as promising candidates for targeted drug development. These findings demonstrate the value of proteomics‐integrated genetic analyses in uncovering novel therapeutic avenues for complex diseases like PCOS.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, plasma proteome, polycystic ovary syndrome, therapeutic target

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common reproductive, endocrine, and metabolic disorder, affecting approximately 10% of women of reproductive age worldwide [1]. It is characterized by irregular menstruation and often accompanied by hyperandrogenism [1]. Women with PCOS face an increased risk of infertility, cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, and endometrial cancer compared to those without the condition [2]. The exact pathogenesis of PCOS remains unclear but is thought to involve hyperinsulinemia, hyperandrogenism, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [3]. Current first‐line treatments focus on lifestyle modifications, including improvements in diet, exercise, weight management, mood, and sleep [4]. However, the clinical presentation of PCOS is highly heterogeneous, with symptoms and associated comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose tolerance varying widely between individuals [3]. The significant clinical heterogeneity of PCOS remains the central challenge to effective treatment. This variability, driven by a complex pathogenesis with interwoven metabolic and endocrine dysfunctions, has fundamentally limited the development of targeted therapies. Therefore, moving beyond a one‐size‐fits‐all approach requires a deeper understanding of the molecular drivers behind the disorder′s diverse manifestations to identify novel and more personalized therapeutic targets.

Plasma proteins play key roles in various biological processes, including signalling, transport, growth, repair, and immune defence. These proteins often possess specific binding sites that can be targeted by small molecules or biologics, making them valuable druggable targets [5]. Advances in high‐throughput proteomics have enabled large‐scale analyses of plasma cis‐acting protein quantitative trait loci (cis‐pQTL), providing insights into genetic variations in the human blood proteome [6]. By integrating genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) with cis‐pQTL data and employing proteome‐wide Mendelian randomization (MR), it is possible to assess the impact of plasma proteins on PCOS risk and protection, independent of confounding factors, and to identify potential drug targets for PCOS [7]. In this study, we aimed to identify which plasma proteins could be used as potential therapeutic targets for intervening in PCOS by combining proteome‐wide MR, summary‐data‐based MR (SMR) analysis, and colocalization analysis.

Methods

Exposure and Outcome Data Set

In this study, we used the meta‐analysis of PCOS GWAS data as the outcome and plasma cis‐pQTL data as the exposure. The PCOS summary statistics were derived from a large‐scale GWAS meta‐analysis, including 10,074 PCOS cases and 103,164 healthy controls from European populations [8, 9]. For the cis‐pQTL data, we incorporated data from five major proteomics studies (Table 1). Ferkingstad provided data on 35,559 proteins from 4907 Icelandic participants [10], Sun′s study contributed data on 3301 plasma proteins from 3994 European individuals [5], and the UK Biobank Pharmaceutical Proteomics Programme (UKBPP) provided data on 2941 proteins from 54,219 UK participants [11]. Zhao′s study included 91 plasma proteins from 14,824 individuals [12, 13]. There was no overlap between the datasets. All cis‐pQTL data included in this study met the genome‐wide significance threshold (P < 5×10⁻⁸).

Proteome‐Wide Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To evaluate the causal effect of plasma cis‐pQTL on PCOS, we conducted proteome‐wide MR analyses. MR analysis is a reliable method for determining causal associations between exposure and outcome phenotypes, under the assumption that exposure and outcome phenotypes are representative of the same underlying population, to calculate an effect estimate [14]. Cis‐pQTL data served as the exposure, and PCOS GWAS data were used as the outcome, aiming to determine whether alterations in plasma protein levels causally influence PCOS risk. 1 Mb cis‐Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with plasma proteins were included if they reached genome‐wide significance (P < 5×10⁻⁸). Independence of instrumental variables (IVs) was ensured by applying a linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold of r² < 0.001 and a clumping distance of > 10,000 kb [15]. IV strength was assessed using the F‐statistic, with IVs having an F‐statistic > 10 considered strong; weak IVs were excluded [16].

For proteins associated with a single SNP, causal estimates were calculated using the Wald Ratio method [17]. For proteins linked to multiple SNPs, five complementary methods were applied to assess causality: inverse variance weighted (IVW) [15], weighted median [18], MR‐Egger [19, 20], simple mode [21], and weighted mode [22], with IVW as the primary analytical approach. Additionally, MR‐PRESSO was employed to identify and correct for horizontal pleiotropy, ensuring robust results [23]. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the MR‐PRESSO global test and Cochran′s Q test [24, 25, 26]. All two‐sample MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) and MR‐PRESSO (version 1.0) in R software (version 4.4.2). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

SMR Analysis and HEIDI Test

The summary‐data‐based Mendelian randomization (SMR) and the Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) test occupied the ability to distinguish a pleiotropic model from a linkage model, as a supplementary method to examine for proteome‐wide MR analyses. SMR is used to identify associations between genetic variants by integrating GWAS data with cis‐pQTL data [27]. In this study, the SMR analysis was performed using the SMR software tool (version 1.3.1) to analyse cis‐pQTL and PCOS GWAS data, applying a significance threshold of P SMR < 0.05. To ensure the reliability of the results, LD between genetic variants was assessed and excluded using the HEIDI test [28]. Genetic variants with P HEIDI < 0.05 were considered indicative of LD and were excluded from the analysis [28].

Colocalization Analysis

To determine whether the causal association between plasma proteins and PCOS could be attributed to LD, we conducted colocalization analysis using the coloc R package. This analysis employed a Bayesian framework to evaluate five hypotheses at each locus: H0: No association with either trait. H1: Association with trait 1 only (p1). H2: Association with trait 2 only (p2). H3: Association with both traits, but due to distinct causal variants. H4: Association with both traits due to a shared causal variant. Bayesian posterior probabilities (PPs) were assigned to each hypothesis [29]. Default prior probabilities were applied in this study: p1 = 1×10−4, p2 = 1×10−4, p12 = 1×10−5. PP.H3 + PP.H4 > 0.8 was used to indicate the protein with high‐support evidence of colocalization [14].

Potential Drug Target Identification

Information about the identified targets was retrieved from multiple databases, including DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), ChEMBL (https://depmap.org/portal/), TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), and DrugCentral (https://drugcentral.org/). This analysis helped determine whether drugs targeting these proteins are currently used or being developed for PCOS treatment. Druggability was assessed based on three criteria: (1) targeting drugs have been approved; (2) targeting drugs are undergoing clinical trials; (3) targeting drugs are in preclinical pipelines [30].

Results

Proteome‐Wide MR Analysis, SMR and HEIDI Tests between Plasma Proteins and PCOS

Proteome‐wide MR analysis identified 33 proteins significantly correlated with the risk of PCOS (p < 0.05, using either the Wald Ratio or IVW method). The results of this analysis are presented in Table 2 and Figure 1. A comprehensive summary of all MR estimates derived from the proteome‐wide analysis is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Among these, proteins such as UROD, SPOCK2, SERPINA10, RALB, PRDX6, PKP2, JUND, GNPNAT1, GNMT, GDI2, FGF23, UROS, SERPINA10, CHMP6, CXCL10, GNPDA2, IDI2, ITGB6, OSTN, PARK7, PIK3IP1, PRDX6, RALB, and SIGLEC5 were positively associated with an increased risk of PCOS. Conversely, proteins like PRSS22, ERLEC1, DARS2, CRABP2, COLEC12, APOA4, FCRL6, LYZL2, MFAP4, RPA2, SH2B3, and SORBS1 were negatively associated with PCOS risk. The Cochran′s Q test and MR‐PRESSO analysis indicated no significant pleiotropy (p > 0.05) in the associations between these proteins and PCOS, except for NUDT5 (Supplementary Table S2).

Colocalization Analysis Between Plasma Proteins and PCOS

Colocalization analysis was conducted to mitigate potential confounding effect stemming from LD. We performed colocalization analysis to mitigate potential confounding effect stemming from LD (Figure 3). The high support of colocalization evidence (PP.H4/PP.H3 > 2) was observed for 6 targets: PRDX6, FGF23, PKP2, SERPINA10, SH2B3, and SIGLEC5. These findings suggest that the associations between these proteins and PCOS are unlikely to be confounded by LD, supporting a potential shared causal variant. In contrast, the remaining targets did not show strong colocalization evidence, indicating that their associations with PCOS may be influenced by LD.

Druggability Evaluation for Candidate Protein Targets

To evaluate the druggability of candidate targets, we searched 6 candidate targets as possible drug targets in drug databases. However, these targets have not been recognized as drug targets specifically for PCOS in existing studies, they remain promising candidates for future drug development (Table 3). Burosumab, targeting FGF23, is approved for treating hypophosphatemia. SH2B3 is primarily targeted by drugs used in cancer. Pazopanib, which targets SH2B3, is approved for advanced renal cell carcinoma, advanced soft tissue sarcoma, and advanced thyroid cancer. There is no relevant information available in the Drug Information databases for COLEC12, PKP2, SERPINA10, and SIGLEC5. The findings highlight the untapped therapeutic potential of FGF23 and SH2B3 for PCOS treatment.

Discussion

We conducted a proteome–wide MR, SMR test, HEIDI test and colocalization analyses to explore the causal roles of plasma proteins in PCOS to provide preclinical clues for the drug development. MR analysis, SMR test, and HEIDI test identified 33 protein–PCOS associations. After teasing out the possible effects of linkage disequilibrium by colocalization analysis, we found strong evidence that genetically predicted higher level of FGF23was associated with an increased risk PCOS, whereas genetically predicted higher level of SH2B3 was associated with a reduced risk of PCOS. Therefore, we identified FGF23 and SH2B3 as the potential drug targets for PCOS with robust evidence.

FGF23 is a phosphorylated hormone, and elevated levels of FGF23 are an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease [31]. Our results found that FGF23 was positively associated with the risk of PCOS, and its role may involve the MAPK signalling pathway. Increased expression of FGF23 has been observed in women with PCOS, particularly those with cardiovascular risk and obesity [32]. Leptin, a biochemical marker for diagnosing and treating PCOS, is elevated in women with PCOS and correlates with insulin resistance and testosterone levels [33]. Leptin also directly stimulates FGF23 secretion in osteoblasts [34], potentially explaining the elevated FGF23 levels in women with PCOS. Higher FGF23 levels may activate the inflammatory cascade by inhibiting the synthesis of 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D and are positively associated with resistin, lipocalin, and HOMA‐IR [35] [36], all of which are risk factors for PCOS [3].

SH2B3, a member of the SH2B adaptor protein family, functions as an intracellular adaptor and a negative regulator of signalling pathways downstream of various cytokine receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases [37]. Evidence suggests that SH2B3 is in negative regulation of inflammatory cytokine [38]. Although the exact pathogenesis of PCOS remains unclear, chronic low‐grade inflammation is increasingly recognized as a key contributing factor [3]. Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines have been observed in the ovarian cells of patients with PCOS [39], and these inflammatory markers are closely associated with obesity, hyperandrogenism, and insulin resistance—three major risk factors for the condition [40] [41]. Persistent inflammation also disrupts ovarian function by impairing follicular development and oocyte quality, further exacerbating PCOS symptoms [42]. As a result, anti‐inflammatory strategies have gained attention as potential therapeutic approaches for managing PCOS [43]. In our study, SH2B3 expression was found to be negatively associated with PCOS risk. Higher levels of SH2B3 may help mitigate the abnormal inflammatory response characteristic of PCOS, suggesting that SH2B3 could serve as a promising therapeutic target for the condition.

Our findings offer several important implications for clinical practice and future research. For clinical practice: although FGF23 and SH2B3 are not yet ready for routine clinical application, they represent highly promising candidate biomarkers for development. Future validation could establish their utility in patient risk stratification and monitoring disease progression in PCOS. For future research: we have outlined two primary directions. First, we emphasize the urgent need for experimental validation (using appropriate cell and animal models) to confirm the causal roles of FGF23 and SH2B3 and to dissect their underlying molecular mechanisms in PCOS pathophysiology. Second, we recommend initiating preclinical studies to explore the therapeutic potential of modulating these targets, such as developing FGF23 inhibitors or SH2B3 activators.

Strength and Limitation

The primary strength of our study lies in its comprehensive Mendelian randomization framework. By systematically integrating multiple analytical methods, we rigorously tested for causality while minimizing potential biases from confounding factors and genetic linkage. This multi‐faceted approach provides robust genetic evidence for the causal roles of specific plasma proteins in PCOS.

However, these findings must be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, our analysis relied on genetic data derived predominantly from European‐ancestry populations; therefore, further studies are essential to confirm the applicability of our results to more diverse ethnic groups. Second, our investigation was constrained by the genetic variants available in the source GWAS datasets and could not account for undiscovered genetic loci. Finally, while our analysis identifies promising therapeutic targets and evaluates their druggability, these insights are based on genetic evidence and computational predictions. Substantial experimental research and clinical validation are required to translate these findings into viable therapies for PCOS.

Conclusions

In this study, we have successfully established a causal relationship between plasma proteins and PCOS through proteome‐wide MR, SMR test, HEIDI test and colocalization analyses. Notably, we identified FGF23 and SH2B3 as key proteins that may contribute to the therapeutic effects observed. These findings underscore the promise of proteomics‐driven research in advancing targeted therapies for PCOS.
