A Case of Genetically Confirmed Pseudohypoparathyroidism Type 1a Presented with Multifocal Plate-Like Osteoma Cutis and Review of Literature
Authors: Jee-Woo Kim, Minjae Kim, Boram Kim, Jee-Soo Lee, Moon-Woo Seong, Jung-Im Na
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

A 19-month-old Korean boy with a history of recurrent apnea and desaturation presented with multiple erythematous hard plaques on his trunk and upper extremities (Fig. 1A~C). Skin lesions spontaneously developed after birth without trauma and gradually increased in size and number. On physical examination, he was obese with a body weight of 17.5 kg, which was above the 97th percentile. He had a round moon face and mild brachydactyly in the fifth fingers (Fig. 1D). Laboratory work-up showed elevated parathyroid hormone (113 pg/ml, reference 8~76 pg/ml), hyperphosphatemia (7.9 mg/dl, reference 2.5~4.5 mg/dl), and a calcium level close to the upper normal limit (10.2 mg/dl, reference 8.8~10.5 mg/dl). Thyroid function tests revealed subclinical hypothyroidism, with isolated thyroid stimulating hormone elevation (4.72 mU/L, reference 0.3~4.0 mU/L). The histopathology of the skin lesion showed well-differentiated ossified plaques with adipocytes and vessels between bone trabeculae (Fig. 2). Genetic test for GNAS was recommended after interdisciplinary consultations. Sequencing of the GNAS in DNA isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes revealed a pathogenic heterozygous variant, NM_000516.7:c.348del p.(Val117Trpfs*16), which was reported as a loss-of-function variant. The patient was diagnosed with Albright hereditary osteodystrophy (AHO) with pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1a.

Osteoma cutis (OC) is characterized by mature lamellar bone formation within the dermis or subcutaneous layer of the skin. The majority of OC (up to 85%) cases occurs secondary to trauma or inflammatory conditions1. However, 14% of OC cases are primary, which includes three well-described ossifying syndromes: fibrodysplasia ossificans progressive and two GNAS-associated disorders: AHO and progressive osseous heteroplasia (POH). Plate-like OC is a rare variant of primary OC, which was first defined by the following criteria: congenital, idiopathic (not associated with metabolic changes or other osteomas), and primary (no evidence of trauma, infection, or other predisposing events)2. Our case satisfied most of the criteria for plate-like OC except for calcium-phosphorus metabolic aberrations. Our case was unusual for several reasons. First, current knowledge classifies plate-like OC as a limited non-progressive variant of POH, which is rarely found in AHO. However, our case revealed that plate-like OC may also be present in individuals with AHO or PHP type 1a. Second, our patient had a history of severe hypoxia due to upper airway obstruction, which was suspected to be a laryngospasm3. Similarly, one AHO case diagnosed as PHP1b was reported to present with hypocalcemia-induced laryngospasm, a rare symptom of electrolyte disorder. Our patient had a frameshift variant (c.348del) in the GNAS gene. To date, GNAS variants found in previous cases of histologically confirmed OC include a heterozygous nonsense variant within exon 11 (c.637C>T p.Gln213*) and a homozygous nonsense variant (c.838A>T p.Arg280*). Around 30 GNAS variants have been reported in PHP 1a cases4. Currently, there are no effective treatments for GNAS-associated ossifying disorders, although some reports have introduced therapies such as intravenous bisphosphonates5. Although surgical excision may be considered for isolated OC, it may not be applicable for OCs with genetic mutations due to its relentless course. When encountering OC in the skin, clinicians may consider the possibility of a genetic disease and endocrine aberration, warranting a multidisciplinary approach.
