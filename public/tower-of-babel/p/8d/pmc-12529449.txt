Global Perspectives on Barriers to Oral Health Care Access for Afghan Refugee Women and Girls: A Scoping Review Protocol
Authors: Jennifer Mardini, Dileep Sharma, Janet Wallace, Kay Franks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAfghanistan's recent shift in political landscape has intensified gender‐based challenges. The restrictive gender norms and practices have profound implications for the well‐being of Afghan women and girls. Among dental professionals, there is limited awareness about the trauma and barriers that impact a refugee's ability to access or commit to oral health care.ObjectivesThis scoping review protocol will explore and conceptualize the current knowledge surrounding barriers that refugee women from Afghanistan experience in accessing oral health care.MethodsDatabases including Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and CINAHL, supplemented by a manual search of reference lists and grey literature search to locate studies reporting on the barriers to accessing oral health care in refugee women and girls from Afghanistan. Two independent reviewers will screen titles, abstracts, and complete full‐text reviews, with data extraction confirmed by a third reviewer. Findings from our scoping review will be presented using a PRISMA‐SCR diagram and thematic narrative synthesis.Expected OutcomesThe findings from this scoping review will highlight the current knowledge gap and inform an educational package for oral health therapy students in Australia, focused on overcoming barriers and providing culturally sensitive care for Afghan refugee women and girls.Keywords: Afghan, Afghanistan, asylum seekers, dental care, oral health, refugees, women

Introduction

In August 2021, Afghanistan reverted to Taliban control, an ultraconservative repressive religious faction, like its previous regime between 1996 and 2001. This regression has dramatically changed the landscape of women and girls' ability to access healthcare services and education [1, 2]. The heightened restrictive gender norms and practices impacting girls and women have led to Afghanistan being rated as the worst place to be a woman or a girl [3]. The gender‐related human rights violations affecting women and girls within Afghanistan invariably result in irrevocable consequences. Many traditional Afghan families do not allow their female relatives to be treated by male doctors; therefore, access to healthcare services is nearly impossible due to the limited availability of female doctors [2]. The decree placed on women attending university and girls receiving an education violates human rights. The draconian measures also limit female independence, restrict freedom, and remove women from the public sphere, contributing to high emigration rates in Afghan females [3].

The refugee journey for women and girls differs from that of their male counterparts. Girls and women that are resettled as refugees or asylum seeker often struggle to engage in or access health services. Previously identified barriers include a lack of services that offer or consider specific cultural knowledge and a disregard or minimal awareness of the trauma that Afghan women and girls have experienced [4]. The term ‘interminable suffering’ is prominently used throughout the studies documenting the Afghan females’ experience of ongoing challenges [5]. Reports also describe the repercussions of intergenerational trauma encompassing compromised physical health and mental wellbeing within the Afghan population, once resettled [6, 7]. These findings underscore the need for research in the exploration of female Afghan refugee experiences and addressing their specific oral health needs.

The Australian Migration Act 1958 defines a refugee as, “a person that has a nationality and is outside the country of his or her nationality due to a well‐founded fear of persecution, is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of that country” [8]. Australia partners with the United Nations Higher Commission for Refugees (UNHCR) and supports the global resettlement priorities through initiatives such as ‘Women at Risk’ subclass visa 204. Since the establishment of the “Women at Risk” visa, over 30,000 visas have been granted to vulnerable women and their dependents from various countries [9]. The visa provides eligible single mothers, abandoned or single women residing outside their home country without the protection of a male relative and facing the risk of victimization and abuse, an opportunity to resettle. Notably, Afghan refugees accounted for 88.1% of the visa applications received by the Australian Government in 2021‐2022 [10, 11, 12, 13]. According to Australia's Offshore Humanitarian Program 2023‐24, of the 2159 visas granted to vulnerable women and children, 40% were for Afghan nationals [14].

Australia's National Oral Health plan 2015–2024 highlights the need for addressing oral health concerns and appropriateness of care for the refugee population to be valid and well supported. Importantly, it emphasizes that refugees are priority population based on social disadvantage and low income. The plan also outlines the need to provide integrated, accessible, and affordable oral health services, accessed by refugee populations. Accessibility to care is often multifaceted and can include barriers to care due to transportation, affordability of care, language, and the appropriateness of service delivery [15].

The World Health Organisation global oral health status report identifies the inequities of oral health in refugee populations from an international perspective. The burden of oral disease extending beyond the mouth, to an already systemically, financially, and socially vulnerable group [16]. Stark contrast between the oral health of refugees arriving to Australia to that of the resident population has been reported previously [17]. The limitations recognized in the literature surround barriers with the need to delve into underexplored areas to develop practical solutions to improve access to dental care within refugee populations [18]. Robertshaw et al. highlighted crucial observations regarding the challenges faced by health professionals offering primary healthcare to refugees in high‐income nations [19]. The review will therefore explore the oral health care access of refugees, specifically Afghan women and girls globally.

Rationale

Role of educating future dental practitioners in refugee oral health has been noted in literature [20]. While the existing research primarily concentrated on the oral health of refugee children, a notable lack of understanding among health professionals presented regarding the challenges and barriers faced by this population, as was their perceptions of oral health [20]. Enhancing understanding and awareness among dental professionals can play a pivotal role in fostering positive experiences for refugee girls and women, ultimately contributing to enhanced long‐term oral health outcomes for both the individuals and their families. Robertshaw et al., highlighted the need for health professional to receive comprehensive training in areas such as cultural competence, asylum policies and procedures, and health conditions to adequately address the needs of refugees [19].

Research into the available evidence on oral health concerns of female refugees from Afghanistan and the barriers to their access to care can assist in the development of an educational package that is pertinent, culturally responsive, and effective for students. This scoping review will identify the gaps to guide in development of strategies to mitigate oral health risks within minority populations through culturally responsive and inclusive approaches for Afghan refugee women and girls.

Methodology

This scoping review will follow the PRISMA‐ScR protocol and is registered on Open Science framework (https://osf.io/a496h/).

Research Question

This scoping review will focus on the following research question: What are the perceived barriers to accessing oral healthcare among Afghan refugee women and girls from a global perspective?

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

In this scoping review, studies meeting the specified selection criteria will be considered for inclusion. Specifically, studies including Afghan women or girls recognized as a refugee (or asylum seekers) and involves oral health discussion and/or are included in their medical screening. Additionally, studies will be included when barriers to oral healthcare access are reported. Both quantitative and mixed‐method research designs are eligible, and relevant grey literature will be considered in alignment with the research question. Articles will be excluded if they do not include or specify women or girls from Afghanistan within the data or relate to oral health. Participants

Studies that include women and girls’ who have fled Afghanistan, displaced and have sought refuge at an interim location in transit or settled within their host country as either an asylum seeker or refugee will be the focus of this scoping review. Studies conducted within Australia and internationally that include settings such as refugee camps, hospitals, schools and dental clinics will be included. All settings will be accepted provided participant group includes female refugees from Afghanistan.

Search Strategy

The databases to be utilized for the search includes Scopus, Web of Science, PUBMED, and CINAHL. With consultation of a librarian, the search terms Afghan, Afghanistan, asylum seekers, dental care, dental hygiene, dental professional, dentist, displaced persons, girls, hazara, oral health, pashtuns, refugees, woman, women were determined and discussed by authors. Table 2 provides a summary of the planned search terms to be used in the scoping review process. A pilot search was completed with less specific terms and piloted to ensure the breath of data captured. The search terms were then amended to appropriately reflect the chosen population group and the database‐specific requirements. The process of hand searching will then be conducted within the reference list(s) of the included studies to determine if further relevant papers can be identified. No language or date limitation will be applied within the literature search.

Source of Evidence Selection

Reference management system EndNote 21 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, United States) will be used to collate the identified sources. Duplicates will be filtered before the completion of the title and abstract screening. Subsequently, citations will be imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and screened using titles and abstracts. Those articles that are noted as relevant to the research question will be included for full text review. Following the completion of the full text review, articles will be categorized as included or excluded, with a justification associated with the excluded text. Two or more independent reviewers will be involved in all levels of screening, and full text analysis will be performed by two reviewers. Any disagreements that may arise within the title and abstract screening or the full text review will result in a discussion with additional reviewer involved in the final decision. The identification, screening, and eligibility processes, along with the search results, will be conducted following the PRISMA‐SCR framework [21]. The full study inclusion process will be thoroughly documented in the final scoping review and depicted in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram, which will be utilized for the scoping review.

Data Extraction

The data extraction process will be initiated on a data extraction chart which is customized to the research question (Table 3). This chart may require revisions as the studies are identified, and the review process progresses. The studies which meet the inclusion criteria will have data extracted by two or more reviewers. The tool will be used to ensure standardised extraction process and support the recording of relevant information in each article. The data extraction tool will be used to identify the number of female participants (total and specifically from Afghanistan), the oral health concerns highlighted in the paper, and the barriers to oral care and access. The reviewers will deliberate on the findings, and any differences of opinion regarding the inclusion of information will be settled through additional reviewers and discussion.

Data Presentation

The extracted data will be analyzed based on the methods of the studies and the discussion of the authors based on their capture of the population and the concept. The results will likely be a flow chart and focus on the study methodology and the outcomes reported. Additionally, the tables and chart will be accompanied by a narrative summary of findings from the data table and analysis. Quantitative data will be presented through numerical summaries, while qualitative data will be analyzed thematically to identify key patterns and insights. A quality assessment will not be conducted as part of this scoping review.

Dissemination Plan

The findings of this review will be disseminated through peer‐reviewed journal article publication, presented at national and international conferences, and incorporated into the relevant curricula of tertiary health programs.

Discussion

The scoping review aims to comprehensively examine the existing literature surrounding female Afghan refugees and oral health, whilst mapping the barriers to care present for this at‐risk population. By conducting this review, the authors seek to identify research gaps to form the conceptual basis for the development of educational package to upskill oral health students. This package will be specifically informed by the review findings on oral health barriers for female refugees. A potential and tangible outcome of the educational package, which can be disseminated to oral health programs across Australia through curricular enhancements ensures that future oral health professionals can approach the care of female refugees. Additionally, a culturally sensitive approach and responsive clinicians to acknowledge the unique challenges faced by female refugees from Afghanistan is critical. Overall, this may foster improved oral health outcomes and engagement of services for this priority and at‐risk population.

Conclusion

Concurrently, through the identification of existing oral health resources specifically tailored to refugee girls and women within Australia we will determine that the resources are fit for purpose and address the needs of the population. As prospective health care professionals, students in oral health programs should be guided to develop strategies that mitigate oral health risks within minority populations through culturally responsive and inclusive approaches to care.
