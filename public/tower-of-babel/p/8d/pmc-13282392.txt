Assessment of the ADLIFE intervention as a digital solution for patients with advanced chronic diseases: a quasi-experimental trial

ABSTRACT
This multicentre, non-randomized, quasi-experimental study evaluated the effectiveness of the ADLIFE digital toolbox—comprising clinical decision support, care-plan management, and patient empowerment—for patients aged >55 with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and chronic heart failure. Across four pilot sites (Spain, UK, Denmark, and Israel), 185 patients received the intervention and were compared with 185 retrospective standard-of-care control matched using propensity score. The primary outcome was the number of emergency room (ER) visits, analyzed through multivariate hurdle regression. Results in the pooled analysis (N = 370) indicated that while ADLIFE was associated with a significant reduction in the likelihood of an initial ER visit (OR 0.55; 95% CI: 0.31–0.96), no significant differences were found for subsequent visits among patients who had already utilized emergency services (IRR 1.74; 95% CI: 0.88–3.43). Significant effect heterogeneity was observed, with a reduction achieved in only one location (AMCA: OR 0.30; 95% CI: 0.15–0.63). These findings suggest that while digitally enabled care models hold potential for initial acute care prevention, their effectiveness is heavily contingent upon complex contextual factors. Given the small sample size and site-specific results, these data should be interpreted with caution. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT05575336; September 2022.

Introduction

Population ageing and medical advances have increased the life expectancy of individuals with chronic diseases, including advanced chronic diseases (ACDs). Patients living with ACD often experience temporary or permanent functional limitations, resulting in increased healthcare resource use1,2.

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and Chronic Heart Failure (CHF) are two of the most prevalent ACDs3,4. Both COPD and CHF have been identified as significant contributors to morbidity and mortality3,4, in addition to their substantial social and economic burden. Providing integrated supportive care through digitally enabled interventions may offer meaningful benefits for patients with such conditions5–8. This approach has the potential to enhance quality of life, promote independence, and optimize healthcare resource utilization from early stages1,2. However, there is limited evidence on the implementation of such approaches; the quality and homogeneity of the evidence base need to be improved9.

The EU-funded ADLIFE project (H2020, SC1-DTH-11-2019, 875209) aimed to address the current societal and health system challenges in Europe arising due to the increasing number of persons living with ACD. The ADLIFE intervention was designed to integrate personalized care via a digitally enabled toolbox to support personalized care planning and care delivery for ACD patients. It consisted of the deployment and use of the ADLIFE toolbox by healthcare providers (HCPs) and patients in the pilot sites. The ADLIFE toolbox is a digital solution that includes three Information and Communication Technology (ICT) components: a platform to manage and customize personalized care plans by the interdisciplinary healthcare team with the patient and for the patient, services supporting the clinical decisions, and a platform encouraging independence and self-management of patients10. Thus, the ADLIFE intervention aimed to deliver personalized and dynamic care that facilitates the early detection of patient needs, slowing down the patients’ functional deterioration, ensuring their quality of life, and promoting shared decision making, whilst in parallel improving the healthcare professionals’ adherence to clinical guidelines while preparing personalized care plans and optimizing healthcare resource use. The ADLIFE assessment protocol proposed a threefold evaluation approach of the effectiveness, implementation, and socio-economic impact of the ADLIFE intervention compared to the Standard of Care (SoC)11.

The aim of this paper was to evaluate the effectiveness of the ADLIFE intervention in terms of a reduction in the patient Emergency Room (ER) visits when applied in real-life settings, as a proxy of the appropriateness of care for ACD patients and, consequently, a gain in health-related outcomes12–14.

Results

Descriptive analysis

One hundred eighty-five patients were recruited for the ADLIFE intervention group, and an identical number of patients were selected for the control group. Figure S1 in Supplementary Material shows the flowchart of the recruitment. For the pooled sample (N = 370), the average age was 71.4 (SD 8.26), 47.3% were female; 14.6% patients had a CHF diagnosis at baseline, 68.9% a COPD diagnosis, and 16.5% both. The selection of the control group based on propensity score matching resulted in a control population comparable to the ADLIFE population in all patient characteristics, except for the follow-up variable. Hurdle models

Model 1 shows the overall effect of the ADLIFE intervention. The zero-hurdle part of the model determined whether a patient attended the ER or not. The estimated odds ratios (ORs) indicated the extent to which that variable is associated with an increase in the likelihood of visiting the ER. The variables showing an association with the presence of ER visits were intervention, site, and baseline diagnosis. The intervention turned up as a protective factor, i.e., the odds of an ADLIFE patient having visited the ER were 45% lower compared to the control group (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: [0.31–0.96], p value < 0.05). Furthermore, a greater tendency to visit the ER was observed in patients from AMCA compared to OUH and Osakidetza, with results adjusted for covariates. The follow-up did not show a significant association with visiting the ER. Results of the count part of the model explored the ADLIFE intervention effect on the ER visits for those patients having at least one ER visit. The ADLIFE intervention did not show a significant effect in the count part of the model (IRR = 1.74, 95% CI: [0.88–3.43]), i.e., although ADLIFE had a significant effect on avoiding the first ER visit over the follow-up, no effect was observed on reducing subsequent ER visits. Follow-up, needing oxygen at home, and the Charlson index were positively related to an increase in the number of ER visits. Model 2 shows the effect of the ADLIFE intervention across pilot sites by including the interaction of the intervention and the pilot site. ADLIFE intervention showed a significant effect on avoiding ER in AMCA, with a 70% reduction in the odds of ER visits (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: [0.15–0.63], p value < 0.05). No statistically significant differences associated with the ADLIFE intervention were found in Osakidetza, OUH, or UHCW. However, results in Osakidetza showed a 77% a trend towards reduction in the odds of ER visits (OR = 0.23, 95% CI: [0.05–1.03], p value < 0.1). Statistically significant differences across pilot sites were identified for the control group in OUH with respect to the reference site AMCA. The effect of baseline diagnosis and follow-up in Model 2 remained similar to that in Model 1. The right-hand and upper side of Table 4 and the solid lines in Fig. 2 show the hurdle part of Model 2. The parameters estimated for the count part of Model 2 were the same as those in Model 1, as the interaction term was not significant.

Model 2 presented a better goodness-of-fit than Model 1, as indicated at the bottom of Table 4. Notably, no ER visits were recorded in either study group at the UHCW site due to the small sample size (N = 24). To ensure that this absence of events did not bias the overall estimates in models 1 and 2, a sensitivity analysis that excluded the UHCW site was performed. The results were consistent with those of the primary analysis, and the detailed coefficients are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

The ADLIFE intervention was designed to integrate personalized care via a digitally enabled toolbox to support individualized care planning and delivery for ACD patients. The aim of this paper was to evaluate the effectiveness of the ADLIFE intervention in terms of the patients' ER visits when applied in real-life settings.

A statistically significant reduction in the likelihood of a first ER visit was observed in the pooled analysis. However, this effect was not consistent across pilot sites. When modeling the interaction between intervention and site, the reduction in first ER visits was evident only in AMCA, whereas no intervention‑related differences were detected in Osakidetza, OUH, or UHCW. These differences likely reflect variation in local care pathways, patient profiles, and the degree of integration of ADLIFE within existing service models15,16. No evidence was found that the intervention reduced subsequent ER visits among patients who had already attended the ER. Since the regression model accounting for the effect by sites presented a better goodness-of-fit than that estimating the pooled, the ADLIFE overall effect should be interpreted with caution, highlighting the importance of contextual and organizational factors when interpreting the intervention’s impact17–19.

When situating our findings within the landscape of digitally enabled care for chronic conditions, it becomes clear that few previous initiatives offer direct points of comparison. Existing programs have explored digital tools to support integrated or proactive care, but typically in more specific populations and organizational settings. For example, digitally supported domiciliary‑care models have shown improvements in coordination and functional status in frail older adults20. Regional integrated‑care reforms supported by digital enablers have reported modest reductions in ER use21. Digital components embedded within collaborative‑care models for multimorbidity have demonstrated benefits in monitoring, patient engagement, and continuity of care22. Condition‑specific interventions also reinforce the value of proactive digital follow‑up; for instance, combining telemonitoring with structured tele‑intervention reduced the risk of cardiovascular death or worsening heart‑failure events in high‑risk patients23. Although these initiatives differ substantially from ADLIFE in scope and target population, they collectively illustrate both the potential and the challenges of embedding digital tools within integrated care pathways.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the reduced sample size—31% of the original target—limits statistical power, increases uncertainty around the estimates, and restricts the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, the observed trends suggest that the intervention may have a potential effect that warrants evaluation in larger and more heterogeneous populations. Second, follow‑up duration in Osakidetza and UHCW did not reach the 9‑month period defined in the original version of the study protocol11, potentially reducing the likelihood of capturing ER events. To minimize this impact, ER utilization was standardized as rates per 100 person-months, and follow-up time was incorporated as a covariate term in the hurdle models. Third, because UHCW recorded zero ER visits in both study arms, a sensitivity analysis excluding this site was conducted, confirming the robustness of the pooled estimates. Finally, because only one pilot site was available in each country, the study cannot disentangle health‑system‑level differences from site‑specific variation in implementation.

In summary, ADLIFE showed evidence of reducing the likelihood of a first ER visit in real‑world conditions, although this effect was not consistent across all pilot sites and did not extend to subsequent ER use. These findings suggest a potential benefit of digitally enabled, personalized care models for patients diagnosed with ACD, while underscoring the influence of local context and implementation. Larger studies in more diverse settings are needed to confirm and refine these observations, including the use of additional patient-centered measures to complement utilization-based outcomes.

Methods

Study design

The ADLIFE evaluation was a quasi-experimental trial following a multicentre, non-randomized, unblinded, and controlled design. Patients belonging to the intervention group received the ADLIFE intervention, while patients in the control group received SoC. The ADLIFE intervention was originally planned to be implemented across seven pilot clusters: Assuta Ashdod Hospital together with Maccabi Healthcare Services Southern Region (AMCA) in Israel; the Basque Public Health Service (Servicio Vasco de Salud—Osakidetza) (Osakidetza) in the Basque Country, Spain; Odense University Hospital (OUH) in South Denmark; Region Jämtland Härjedalen (RJH) in Sweden; Gesunder Werra-Meißner Kreis (GWMK) in Germany; and University Hospitals Coventry & Warwickshire NHS Trust (UHCW) and National Health Service Lanarkshire (NHSL) in United Kingdom. Although the evaluation plan initially included seven pilot sites, the final implementation comprised four sites (Osakidetza, UHCW, OUH, and AMCA). This modification resulted from organizational, regulatory, and operational constraints that prevented three planned sites from deploying the intervention as designed.

The study population consisted of patients older than 55; diagnosed with severe heart failure (HF) (defined as NYHA III-IV and/or ACCF/AHA C-D) in stable phase, i.e., at least 2 months without decompensation that required hospital admission; and/or severe COPD (defined as GOLD scale >2 (FEV1 < 50) and/or mMRC ≥2 and/or CAT ≥ 10 and/or use of oxygen at home). Candidates for the intervention group were recruited by healthcare professionals or research assistants according to the eligibility criteria. They were contacted by email, post, phone, or face-to-face meetings, and provided with information describing the ADLIFE intervention, their expected role, and their required technical skills. In the event of a patient lacking the necessary technical skills, an informal caregiver might be appointed from their immediate circle to provide occasional or regular support to the patient’s needs. Candidates for the intervention group were recruited between 23/02 and [24/07]. The retrospective control group was selected from Electronic Health Records (EHRs) data covering the same period to ensure temporal comparability. Patients were followed up from 2 to 16 months. Patients participating in the intervention signed an informed consent and received ADLIFE intervention training. The control group, which received treatment according to local SoC, was retrospectively selected by the research team who performed the analyses from EHRs using the propensity score matching methodology24 to guarantee that they received usual care with no exposure to the ADLIFE while ensuring comparability between the control and the intervention group. All patients who were eligible but did not participate in the intervention were eligible for the control group. The sample size calculation determined that each of the four pilot sites finally implementing the intervention should include 148 subjects in the intervention group and 148 in the intervention group to recognize a difference greater than or equal to 0.6 units as statistically significant, resulting in a total target of 1184. This calculation was made under the assumption of an alpha risk of 0.05 and a beta risk of 0.2 in a two-tailed test, and with the further assumption that the common standard deviation is 1.2 and an estimated dropout rate of 20%. Recruitment across all sites was substantially constrained by organizational, regulatory, and technological delays, which reduced the effective recruitment window. Consequently, the final sample comprised 370 participants.

The trial was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT05575336; September 2022. All pilot sites received approval from their Ethics Committees. All procedures involving human subjects/patients were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Basque Country (PI2021016).

Data collection

Information on socio-demographic, clinical, and resource use variables was collected from two data sources: EHR and ADLIFE toolbox platforms. Variables were observed at baseline and endline. The ADLIFE study protocol11 shows the comprehensive variable list of collected information.

The ADLIFE intervention and the Standard of Care

The ADLIFE intervention involved deploying and using the ADLIFE toolbox by healthcare professionals and patients. The ADLIFE toolbox included three main components: the Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS), the Patient Care Plan Management Platform (PCPMP), and the Patient Empowerment Platform (PEP).

The CDSS was based on assessment scales and evidence-based clinical guidelines reconciled for the target diseases of ADLIFE’s chronic patients. Through direct communication with patients’ EHRs, the CDSS provided healthcare professionals with recommendations on goals, treatments, follow-up, monitoring, and early warning indicators of potentially preventable situations.

Using CDSS outputs, recommendations, and updated patient clinical information, healthcare professionals created and managed individualized, multidisciplinary care plans through the PCPMP. This platform allowed care teams to define goals, schedule interventions, assign responsibilities, and regularly update care plans according to patients’ evolving needs and clinical status.

Patients and informal caregivers accessed these care plans through the PEP. The PEP supported active participation in care by enabling patients to monitor goals and activities, complete questionnaires, report symptoms, access educational materials, and communicate with healthcare teams. Patients participated in the PEP through usability testing sessions during the technical development phase25. Secure data exchange between healthcare organizations and the ADLIFE platform was enabled through technical and semantic interoperability solutions, allowing integration with local EHR while maintaining deployment within each site’s own infrastructure. Analysis

The control group was selected by propensity score matching to ensure baseline comparability; matching criteria included age, sex, CHF diagnosis, COPD diagnosis, Charlson index, as well as number of ER visits and number of hospital admissions observed during the 12 months prior to the ADLIFE intervention.
