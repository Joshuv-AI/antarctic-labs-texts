A urine extracellular vesicle lncRNA classifier for high-grade prostate cancer and increased risk of progression: A multi-center study
Authors: Wen Tao, Bang-Yu Wang, Liang Luo, Qing Li, Zhan-Ao Meng, Tao-Lin Xia, Wei-Ming Deng, Ming Yang, Jing Zhou, Xin Zhang, Xin Gao, Liao-Yuan Li, Ya-Di He
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo construct a urine extracellular vesicle long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) classifier that can detect high-grade prostate cancer (PCa) of grade group 2 or greater and estimate the risk of progression during active surveillance, we identify high-grade PCa-specific lncRNAs by combined analyses of cohorts from TAHSY, TCGA, and the GEO database. We develop and validate a 3-lncRNA diagnostic model (Clnc, being made of AC015987.1, CTD-2589M5.4, RP11-363E6.3) that can detect high-grade PCa. Clnc shows higher accuracy than prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3), multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), and two risk calculators (Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial [PCPT]-RC 2.0 and European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer [ERSPC]-RC) in the training cohort (n = 350), two independent cohorts (n = 232; n = 251), and TCGA cohort (n = 499). In the prospective active surveillance cohort (n = 182), Clnc at diagnosis remains a powerful independent predictor for overall active surveillance progression. Thus, Clnc is a potential biomarker for high-grade PCa and can also serve as a biomarker for improved selection of candidates for active surveillance.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common malignancy affecting men, with an estimated 1.3 million diagnoses and 416,000 deaths worldwide in 2017. Since 2007, the increasing incidence rates, together with a growing and aging population, have led to a 42% increase in PCa cases.1,2 Currently, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is still a widely used biomarker for PCa. However, it has a low specificity (25%–40%) in an equivocal range of PSA (2.0–10.0 ng/mL), which results in a substantial increase in the detection of clinically indolent disease.3,4 It remains a major dilemma for managing clinically indolent diseases. Although active surveillance (AS) could provide the ability to tailor treatments according to dynamic progression criteria, there is still no consensus on the optimal follow-up regimen.5

There are several PSA-derived blood tests (e.g., prostate health index [PHI]) and urine-based assays (e.g., MiProstate [MiPS], SelectMDx). These two urine tests, MiPS and SelectMDx, need a digital rectal examination (DRE) prior to collection. In addition, these blood- and urine-based assays were developed in patients largely outside the PSA level 2.0–10.0 ng/mL gray zone population.6,7,8,9

Extracellular vesicles are small, double-lipid membrane vesicles that could be shed into various biofluids, including blood and urine, from cancer cells.10 Urinary extracellular vesicles contain high-quality cellular RNA and are extremely stable, which makes them promising for profiling RNA expression.11 Many long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play important roles in cancer progression, showing great potential to become diagnostic markers.12,13,14 For example, prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3), the lncRNA to receive US Food and Drug Administration approval as a biomarker test for PCa, can be detected in urine extracellular vesicles.8,15 In the present study, we aimed to explore lncRNA expression profiles from urine-derived extracellular vesicles to develop a multi-lncRNA-based classifier, termed Clnc, to detect high-grade PCa (≥grade group [GG] 2) at initial biopsy. Clnc is made of ENSG00000224746 (AC015987.1), ENSG00000255007(CTD-2589M5.4), and ENSG00000254027(RP11-363E6.3). Among these three lncRNAs, ENSG00000255007 was reported to be associated with the aggressiveness of ovarian cancer.16 However, the biological functions of ENSG00000224746 and ENSG00000254027 have not reported until now. Furthermore, we compared this assay performance with PCA3, multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), and two standard-of-care risk calculators (RCs), Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT)-RC 2.0 and European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC)-RC.17,18 We also evaluated the performance of this lncRNA classifier in estimating the risk of progression during AS.

Results

Discovery of specific urinary lncRNA markers to detect high-grade PCa

To investigate specific lncRNAs markers in the detection of high-grade PCa, we first performed RNA sequencing of urinary extracellular vesicles from 11 patients with high-grade PCa and 11 case-matched patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (GEO: GSE147761).19 Subsequently, we analyzed lncRNAs data of 52 pairs of PCa and normal adjacent tissue (NAT) from TCGA cohort. It is well known that leukocytes are common in urine. In order to reduce the influence of urine leukocyte RNA, we further evaluated lncRNA profiles of 499 PCa tissues from TCGA and 32 normal blood samples from a GEO dataset (GEO: GSE100206).20

Using differential analysis, we identified 1,681 urine extracellular vesicle lncRNAs that had significantly different levels between individuals with benign prostatic hyperplasia and those with high-grade PCa. In the combined TCGA and GEO cohorts, 3,390 lncRNAs were markedly changed between PCa and healthy leukocytes, and 1,890 lncRNAs were significantly differentially expressed between 52 pairs of PCa and NAT (Figures 1 and S1; Table S1). Using a series of statistical filters, we finally sought six lncRNAs (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000233255, ENSG00000254027, ENSG00000225489, ENSG00000255007, ENSG00000245025) that showed to be the most important and specific markers of high-grade PCa. Of these six lncRNAs, five lncRNAs (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000233255, ENSG00000254027, ENSG00000225489, ENSG00000255007) were upregulated in high-grade PCa and one (ENSG00000245025) downregulated (Figure 2).

To confirm whether these six markers could be used to distinguish high-grade PCa from healthy tissue, we performed qRT-PCR to detect the expression levels of 30 pairs of high-grade PCa and NATs and 30 matched urine samples (Figures 2 and S2). Five markers (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000225489, ENSG00000254027, ENSG00000233255, ENSG00000255007) had high expression levels in high-grade PCa tissue and matched urine extracellular vesicles but low expression levels in NATs. ENSG00000245025 had low expression levels in high-grade PCa tissue and matched urine extracellular vesicles but high expression levels in NATs. Further correlation analysis displayed that all these six lncRNAs in urine were significantly correlated with matched cancer tissue (Figures 2 and S3). These findings suggested that urine extracellular vesicle lncRNA levels could represent cancer tissue lncRNA levels using qRT-PCR and that these six lncRNAs could be used as diagnostic markers in high-grade PCa.

Construction and validation of a urine diagnostic model to detect high-grade PCa

To build the diagnostic model, we recruited 139 patients with high-grade PCa and 211 control subjects (including 155 patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia and 56 patients with GG1 PCa) from the training cohort. Eleven patients with high-grade PCa and 11 control subjects with benign prostatic hyperplasia from the discovery cohort were also included in the training cohort. The total positive biopsy rate was 55.71% (16.00% GG1 and 39.71% ≥GG2) for the training cohort. We analyzed the expression levels of these six markers by qRT-PCR. We used the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) to select three lncRNAs (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000255007, ENSG00000254027) and build an lncRNA classifier with the logistical regression model that could differentiate individuals with high-grade PCa from controls (Figure S4). This 3-lncRNA combination, termed Clnc, showed an Area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.756 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.706–0.806) and was considered an ideal lncRNA classifier (Figure 3).

We compared the performance of Clnc with alternative models. Clnc was found to be superior to PCPT-RC 2.0, ERSPC-RC, mpMRI, and PCA3 alone for predicting ≥GG2 PCa in both the training and validation cohorts (Figure 3). Subsequently, we combined Clnc with two standard-of-care RCs, PCPT-RC 2.0 and ERSPC-RC, for predicting ≥GG2 diseases. The combination of this three-lncRNA signature had superior performance over PCPT-RC 2.0 or ERSPC-RC alone (Figure 3; Table 2). We found that there were statistically significant differences in the median Clnc values between patients with biopsy negative/GG1 PCa and those with ≥GG2 PCa (p < 0.0001 for the training cohort, p < 0.0001 for the validation cohort 1, and p < 0.0001 for the validation cohort 2). Using a cut point of 0.3657 in the training cohort, Clnc showed a negative predictive value (NPV) of 70.69% and would have avoided 46.86% of all biopsies (n = 350) or 77.73% of unnecessary, negative/GG1 biopsies (n = 211). Similarly, the 0.3657 cut point in the validation cohort 1 yielded a similar NPV of 79.43% while avoiding 48.28% of biopsies. In validation cohort 2, the same cut point produced an NPV of 80.15% while avoiding 41.83% of biopsies. Waterfall plots showed the distributions of biopsy results from patients with different Clnc values (Figure S5).

Application of Clnc in an AS cohort

Interestingly, we found a high consistency of Clnc values between random urine and first-morning urine, suggesting that random urine was also suitable for the detection of high-grade PCa (Figure S11). Next, we prospectively enrolled an additional 182 patients undergoing AS from TAHSY (n = 103) and Foshan First Municipal People’s Hospital (n = 79) and collected urine samples before undergoing prostate biopsy. The median time from AS enrollment to data analysis was 2.6 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 2.4–3.0). No patients were lost to follow-up before data analysis. Discussion

The lack of PCa specificity and the unclear benefits for reducing PCa mortality associated with the use of PSA warrant more objective measures for identifying clinically significant PCa. Several blood- and urine-based commercial assays, including 4Kscore (Opko, Miami, FL, USA), PHI (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), SelectMDx (MDx Health, Herstal, Belgium), and PCA3 (Gen-Probe, San Diego, CA, USA) have been developed. These assays showed varying efficacy for predicting ≥GG2 PCa. However, they are potentially limited by cohort composition or require DRE prior to specimen collection and special sample processing. There may be also inherent specificity issues in the equivocal PSA range (e.g., 4Kscore, PHI).6,7 Additionally, despite the promising initial results, there are conflicting data about whether PCA3 could independently predict Gleason score. Its clinical effectiveness for monitoring in AS is still uncertain.21

In this study, the levels of urine extracellular vesicles ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000255007, and ENSG00000254027 were significantly increased in patients with high-grade PCa compared with biopsy negative/GG1. The classifier (Clnc) composed of these three lncRNAs showed superiority to PCA3, mpMRI, and two well-established RCs (PCPT-RC 2.0 and ERSPC-RC). The addition of Clnc achieved superior performance than PCPT-RC 2.0 and ERSPC-RC did alone. Furthermore, Clnc at diagnosis was a powerful independent predictor of overall AS progression after adjustment for clinicopathological factors. Our classifier mainly targets the population with an equivocal PSA range (2.0–10.0 ng/mL) at initial biopsy. For this population, some standard-of-care variables (i.e., age, family history, race, and PSA) may be less informative. Moreover, some commercially available urinary biomarkers (i.e., Progensa PCA3, SelectMDx, MiPS, and ExoDx Prostate) have AUCs of 0.70–0.77, while Clnc alone had an AUC of 0.756–0.776, supporting the perfect performance of Clnc. Additionally, most of these commercial urine tests need a DRE prior to collection.8,9 The ExoDx Prostate (IntelliScore) is an available potential alternative urine extracellular vesicle gene expression assay for detecting high-grade PCa that does not require DRE either.10,15 However, it is still not validated in the non-Western populations. Moreover, the role of the ExoDx Prostate in patients enrolled in AS protocols is uncertain.

Recent studies have shown strong evidence for a pivotal role of lncRNAs in PCa. Plenty of lncRNAs were reported to have the ability to function as oncogenes and/or tumor suppressors. For example, SChLAP1, one of the most well-characterized oncogenic lncRNAs, is associated with disease recurrence and progression in PCa.22,23 HOTAIR, another well-studied oncogenic lncRNA, is often overexpressed in PCa and several other cancers and could promote tumor growth.24 MEG3 could act as a tumor-suppressive lncRNA by increasing p53 protein levels and preventing proliferation.25 As mechanisms of action, lncRNAs have been proposed to influence gene expression at epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional levels.12 They can bring microRNAs (miRNAs), mRNAs, and DNA into proximity with one another and with some proteins, including RNA-binding proteins, transcription factors, chromatin-modifying complexes, and E3 ligases, acting as a molecular scaffold.26 Recent studies also showed that some lncRNAs may be translated.27 Intriguingly, lncRNAs are enriched and stable in urine extracellular vesicles, making them prime candidates for cancer biomarkers.20,28

For the biological functions of these three lncRNAs in our diagnostic model, we performed gene set enrichment analysis to further explore the underlying functions of each lncRNA based on their significantly correlated mRNAs. Using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways analyses, all of these three lncRNA-related mRNAs are found to be significantly involved in biological pathways associated with PCa (Tables S4–S6), suggesting that the increased expression of these lncRNAs may contribute to PCa development. Herein, the roles of these three lncRNAs in PCa warrant further experimental investigations.

There are several strengths in our study. First, we began with a high-throughput screening for urine extracellular vesicles lncRNAs that showed differential levels between patients with high-grade PCa and those with biopsy negative. This methodology could improve the possibility of identifying biomarkers appropriate for surveillance. Second, we used a central pathological review of all diagnostic biopsies to reduce interpathologist variability. Third, the combination of MRI-targeted biopsy and systematic biopsy could increase the detection of clinically significant cancers, providing improved diagnostic accuracy over either targeted or systematic biopsy alone.29 This could be shown by the fact that the positive biopsy rate for ≥GG2 PCa in the present study was significantly higher than that in our previous work (37.94% vs. 26.72%).19 Thus, compared with the previous study, the present work enrolled a more contemporary patient cohort. In addition, our previous work evaluated the performance of a circular RNA (circRNA) classifier in detecting high-grade PCa.19 Because of relatively low expression levels of circRNA,12 we used digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) to determine the circRNAs. In the present study, we employed traditional qRT-PCR analyses for the lncRNAs. Although ddPCR shows a great precision, it costs much more than traditional qRT-PCR analysis, which may limit its widespread clinical utility.

In summary, our data indicate that Clnc could identify high-grade PCa in patients with an equivocal PSA 2.0–10.0 ng/mL. This classifier could improve PCPT-RC 2.0 and ERSPC-RC. In addition, Clnc could serve as a biomarker for improved selection of candidates for AS and to tailor more individualized follow-up regimens.

Limitations of the study

This study has a few limitations. Although our results were confirmed in TCGA cohort, most participants are Chinese. In addition, various techniques of MRI-targeted biopsy, with cognitive fusion or MRI-ultrasound fusion techniques, were used. However, recent studies showed a lack of superiority of any one fusion approach.30 We believed that our results might provide a more generalizable view of clinical practice if centers were permitted to use their local resources and expertise.

Method details

Study design, outcomes, and patient selection

In the discovery stage, we identified the high-grade PCa specific lncRNAs markers by combined analyses of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (TAHSY), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohorts. The urine samples used in the discovery stage were collected from 11 patients with high-grade PCa and 11 case-matched patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia at TAHSY (GSE147761).19 There were 52 paired PCa and normal adjacent tissue (NAT) samples from TCGA. The lncRNA profiles of 32 blood leukocyte samples of healthy controls were acquired from a GEO dataset (GSE100206).20

In the retrospective, single-centre cohort (training cohort), we collected 350 urine samples from 350 patients at TAHSY between June 2017, and June 30, 2021. The validation stage had three independent cohorts. The first (Cohort 1) enrolled 232 participants from June 2017, to March 2021, at Foshan First Municipal People’s Hospital. Cohort 2 was composed of 251 participants at The First Affiliated Hospital of University of South China (n = 149) and Foshan Municipal Chinese Medicine Hospital (n = 102) enrolled from June 2017 to May, 2021. Participants were regarded as eligible if they had not been diagnosed with PCa, were aged 45 years or older, had a PSA 2.0–10.0 ng/mL, and scheduled for an initial prostate needle biopsy. Demographic and clinical data (i.e., age, race, family history, DRE, mpMRI, and PSA) were obtained at the time of enrollment. Patients with a history of a prior biopsy, or clinical symptoms of urinary tract infection (including prostatitis) at the time of enrollment, or invasive treatments for benign prostatic hyperplasia or lower urinary tract symptoms within 6 months, or taking medications or hormones that have an effect on serum PSA levels within 3–6 months were excluded. Cohort 3 was comprised of 453 ≥GG2 PCa samples and 98 GG1 PCa/benign prostate tissue samples from TCGA cohort (Figure 1; Table S4).

In the prospective AS cohort, we enrolled additional 182 patients from TAHSY (n = 103) and Foshan First Municipal People’s Hospital (n = 79) between July 2016 and August, 2021. The enrollment criteria include: histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma, PSA≤10 ng/mL, clinical stage ≤ cT2a, biopsy Gleason score ≤6, a maximum of three cores involved with cancer, ≤50% involvement of any single core, and no previous surgery, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy, or chemotherapy for PCa. The clinicopathological characteristics of this cohort are summarized in Table 3. The AS protocol included quarterly PSA testing and DRE, and prostate systematic and targeted rebiopsies every 12 months.

The primary outcome was the detection of high-grade PCa of GG2 or greater. For the AS cohort, the primary endpoint was overall AS progression, which was defined as at least one of PSA progression, clinical progression, and histopathologic progression. PSA progression was defined according to the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center guidelines (an estimated PSA doubling time <3years, http://nomograms.mskcc.org/Prostate/PsaDoublingTime.aspx). Clinical progression was defined as increased cancer stage to ≥ cT2b. Histopathologic progression was defined as ≥ GG2 diseases, or >3 rebiopsy cores with tumor, or >50% cancer in any single core. Patients who had overall AS progression during followup were recommended to undergo curative treatments.

Biopsy protocol

All eligible patients underwent prostate systematic and targeted biopsy by a transrectal approach. All men had mpMRI before prostate biopsies, with an interval of less than 3 months between MRI and biopsies. MRI was performed at 3T with a pelvic phased-array coil, with or without an endorectal coil. All MRI scans were reviewed by two dedicated genitourinary radiologists with more than 15-year experience in reporting MRIs of the prostate, and the consensus was required for each case. We evaluated the likelihood of clinically significant PCa according to the Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System, version 2 (PI-RADSv2) (1, highly unlikely; 2 unlikely; 3, equivocal; 4, likely; 5, highly likely).37 For prostate systematic biopsy, a total of 12 cores were acquired from the peripheral zone of the prostate at the base, mid gland, and apex.38 Targeted biopsy was performed for each lesion with a PI-RADSv2 score of 3 or higher. The guiding methods used for targeted biopsy varied among centers: cognitive fusion or MRI-ultrasound fusion techniques were used. Men with negative mpMRI results (PI-RADSv2 score ≤2) had systematic biopsy only.

Histopathology

All biopsy specimens were evaluated by a central pathological review. In detail, H&E stained sections from individual sites representing all patients enrolled were sent to and centrally reviewed by a dedicated uropathologist (J. Z.) with 20 years of experience. The GG was recorded in accordance with the modified Gleason grading system (the International Society of Urological Pathology consensus: GS2-6 = GG1, GS 3 + 4 = GG2, GS4 + 3 = GG3, GS8 = GG4 and GS9-10 = GG5).39,40 We assessed the highest GG for each case. In cases in which the GG differed from that reported by the local pathologist, the samples were further reviewed by a third dedicated uropathologist (X. Z.) who had 24 years of experience. The final review diagnosis was then decided by consensus. The uropathologists did not have any information regarding the urine lncRNA assay results.

Extracellular vesicle lncRNAs sequencing

The urine preparation, extracellular vesicle isolation and characterization, RNA extraction, RNA sequencing library preparation, and lncRNAs sequencing were previously described.19 We have described the protocols using EV-TRACK (ID: EV230965; https://evtrack.org), which are publicly accessible at http://evtrack.org/review.php. In brief, the total RNA yield (ng/mL of urine) was quantified using 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). The total RNA yield extracted from urinary EVs is 0.32 ng/ml to 6.44 ng/ml. The quality and profile of the isolated RNA were measured with NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer and 2100 Bioanalyzer. The ratio of the absorbance measured at 260nm to that at 280nm ranged from 1.8 to 2.1. The EV RNA had pronounced peak between 25 and 200 nucleotides(nt), showing little or no bands of 28S (approximately 7000nt) and 18S (approximately 4000nt) in electropherograms. At least 18ng total RNA was used for constructing strand-specific libraries with NEBNext Ultra II (Directional) RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, #E7760). The minimum requirement for RNA input amount is 10ng according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, the rRNA-depleted RNAs were fragmented using fragmentation buffer. Then, first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the fragmented RNA as a template and random oligonucleotides as primers. The second-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Second Strand Synthesis Enzyme Mix and Reaction Buffer with dUTP Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Then, the purified double-stranded cDNA fragments were subjected to end-repair, modified to add A to the 3′ end and ligated to the sequencing adapters. The double-stranded cDNA was digested to remove the second-strand cDNA. The purified ligation products were performed for PCR amplification using P5 and P7 primers, then the amplification products of cDNA were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman, A63881). The libraries were sequenced on Illumina Hiseq X Ten system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). According to quality control and mapping results, the EVs-RNA sequencing obtained qualified raw and clean reads for each sample with Q20 values greater than 94. Moreover, the mapping rates of most samples are greater than 50%. The strand-specific library can preserve the orientation information of transcripts during RNA sequencing. Thus, it can identify RNA transcripts originating from sense or antisense strands.41 The expression of lncRNAs was normalized and calculated using fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads, StringTie and Ballgown were applied.31,32 Subsequently, the coding potential of transcripts was assessed using Coding Potential Calculator and Coding-Non-Coding Index software.33,34 The transcripts without coding potential but with lengths greater than 200 bp were defined as candidate lncRNAs. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.22.2).35 The significant lncRNAs were defined using |log2(Fold Change)| > 1 and p value <0.05.

Discovery of differentially expressed long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) markers

In the discovery stage, we performed RNA sequencing of paired urinary extracellular vesicles from 11 patients with high-grade prostate cancer and 11 case-matched patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (GSE147761).19 The RNA sequencing data and clinical information of 499 prostate cancer and 52 matched normal adjacent samples were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) in UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Thirty-two normal blood leukocyte samples of a Gene Expression Omnibus dataset (GSE100206) were used as healthy controls.20 The moderated t-test was applied to the differential analysis. The p value less than 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| greater than 1 were considered as the thresholds of the analysis.

In addition, we downloaded the gene expression values (fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads, FPKM) in 10327 samples across 33 cancer types [Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC, n = 79), Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA, n = 411), Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA, n = 1104), Cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC, n = 306), Cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL, n = 36), Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD, n = 471), Lymphoid Neoplasm Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBC, n = 48), Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA, n = 162), Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM, n = 168), Head and Neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC, n = 502), Kidney Chromophobe (KICH, n = 65), Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC, n = 536), Kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP, n = 289), Acute Myeloid Leukemia (LAML, n = 151), Brain Lower Grade Glioma (LGG, n = 529), Liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC, n = 374), Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD, n = 526), Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC, n = 501), Mesothelioma (MESO, n = 86), Ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV, n = 379), Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD, n = 178), Pheochromocytoma and Paraganglioma (PCPG, n = 183), Prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD, n = 499), Rectum adenocarcinoma (READ, n = 167), Sarcoma (SARC, n = 263), Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM, n = 471), Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD, n = 375), Testicular Germ Cell Tumors (TGCT, n = 156), Thyroid carcinoma (THCA, n = 510), Thymoma (THYM, n = 119), Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC, n = 548), Uterine Carcinosarcoma (UCEC, n = 548), Uveal Melanoma (UVM, n = 80)] to further explore the expression levels of candidate lncRNAs.

Functional annotation of the selected three lncRNAs

We employed the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify the biological significance of each of three lncRNAs (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000255007, ENSG00000254027). Firstly, we identified significantly correlated differentially expressed mRNAs for each lncRNA in 52 pairs tissues from TCGA using Pearson correlation analysis via R software with p value < 0.05. The R packages “clusterProfiler” and “org.Hs.e.g.,.db” were utilized to predict the functions of mRNA correlated with lncRNA significantly.36 In Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analyses, a p value < 0.05 and the inclusion of at least 10 interesting mRNAs were considered a significant enrichment.

qRT-PCR analysis

Urine was collected as an overnight 8-h non-DRE sample (80-100mL) from bedtime (approximately 10 p.m.) to the first-voided morning urine. The sample preparation, RNA extraction and reverse transcription were described previously.18 Briefly, quantitative RT-PCR was carried out using Premix Ex Taq (RR390, Takara, Kyoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions on a LightCycler 480 thermocycler (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). The sequences of the primers and probes were listed in Table S7. Each sample was performed in triplicate, and GAPDH was used for normalization. The expression levels of lncRNA were determined by 2−Δct, where ΔCt = CtlncRNA–CtGAPDH.

The laboratory investigators had no information regarding patients’ clinical and outcome data and researchers at all institutions were masked to lncRNAs testing results. After all samples had been tested by the same central laboratory (The Core Lab Plat for Medical Science of Sun Yat-sen University), these blinded data were released to and compared with patient outcomes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The sample size of validation cohorts was calculated using the program PASS (version 11, NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA). Power calculations were performed in relation to a previous discrimination of area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) training results (i.e., PCA3 vs. Clnc) to achieve a statistical power of 80% and 5% significance (two sided). The assumed positive biopsy rate of high-grade PCa is 40–50%. This generated a minimum target of 202 (for a positive biopsy rate of 50%) and maximum 225 men (for a positive biopsy rate of 40%).

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression is a shrinkage method that can select the most predictive variables from high-dimensional data in the regression, minimizing the potential over-fitting.42,43 In our analyses, a histologic biopsy diagnosis for all patients was entered as binary variable in the logistic regression model (≥GG2 for 1, and benign/GG1 for 0). The penalty parameter λ which is determined by 10-fold cross-validations in the LASSO logistic regression controls the amounts of variables in the model.44 Herein, we chose optimal λ via 1-SE (standard error) criteria of the minimum misclassification error. Finally, three lncRNAs (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000255007, ENSG00000254027) were identified in LASSO logistic regression analysis and were used to construct the Clnc. The analysis was performed using the R package “glmnet”.

Given the expression values of each selected lncRNA (ENSG00000224746, ENSG00000255007, ENSG00000254027) in TCGA cohort originated from the tissue samples, we recalculated Clnc scores of each patient using the following logistic regression model: Logit[p = high-grade PCa] = 0.232 + 0.641×ENSG00000254027 + 0.023×ENSG00000255007 + 2.453×ENSG00000224746. The expression of each lncRNA was incorporated into the logistic regression model as a continuous variable. The primary outcome was the detection of high-grade prostate cancer of Grade Group 2 or greater. We also compared the performance of Clnc with prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3), the first lncRNA to receive Food and Drug Administration-approval as a biomarker test for prostate cancer. The receiver operator characteristic analysis, decision curve analysis, and calibration curve analysis were used to evaluate the performance of Clnc. and PCA3.

Besides lncRNA classifier, we used both PCPT RC 2.0 and ERSPC RC to predict the biopsy result for high-grade PCa (i.e., biopsy negative and GG1 vs. ≥ GG2). For all models, we used receiver operating characteristics (ROCs) to assess clinical performance. DeLong’s test was applied to evaluate the significance of AUC differences. The optimal cutoff values for Clnc were obtained at the point on the ROC curve at which Youden’s index (sensitivity+specificity-1) was maximal.45,46 The cut-point sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were assessed. To determine the clinical value of Clnc for predicting high-grade PCa, the decision curve analysis was used as in our previous study to evaluate the net health benefit.47 The calibration plot was applied to illustrate the level of agreement between Clnc predictions and the true risk of high-grade PCa.

Using a Clnc cut-point mentioned above, patients in the AS cohort were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. The log rank test was used to evaluate differences between the cumulative incidences in these two groups.48 We performed multiple Cox proportional hazard regression models for overall AS progression. The variables included age, PSA density, cT stage, percentage of positive biopsies, maximum tumor involvement, and Clnc at diagnosis. Calibration plots for selected prediction horizons were used to assess changes in the predicted risk of overall AS progression. When Clnc was added in the model, changes in prediction error (Brier score) and discrimination ability (c-index) were calculated for varying prediction horizons.49

All statistical analyses were done with SPSS (version 20.0; SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL,USA) or R software version 3.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a p value (two sided) of lower than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. The TRIPOD statement was followed throughout this study.50
