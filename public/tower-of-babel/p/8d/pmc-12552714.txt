Psychometric properties of the SMART tool for infant oral feeding skill assessment
Authors: Ravi Mishra, Anne Albi, Cynthia Lira-Crame
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremature and medically complex infants are at risk of feeding problems, specifically the delay in the development of independent oral feedings, which leads to prolonged hospital stays, higher costs, malnutrition, developmental delays, and family stress. Available oral feeding skill assessment tools lack strong psychometrics, feasibility, and cost-effectiveness. We developed a novel tool, the SMART tool, to address these barriers. The objective of this study was to establish the psychometric properties of the SMART tool for assessing infant oral feeding skills. The study design was a prospective, observational, multicenter trial. To establish criterion validity, we hypothesized that the performance of the SMART tool would be similar to that of a comparator tool, the Neonatal Eating Outcome Assessment (NEOA) tool. A convenience sample of 76 infants across four level-3 Neonatal Intensive Care Units were enrolled, and 136 complete feeding observations were included. Compared to NEOA, the SMART tool had a sensitivity of 0.76 (95% CI 0.63–0.89) and a specificity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.72–0.93), with a strong correlation (r = 0.7, p < 0.001). Inter-rater reliability of post-feed scores was 100% agreement. The SMART tool is valid, reliable, and feasible for assessing infant oral feeding skills.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21296-9.Keywords: Infant nutrition, Feeding skills, Psychometric, BreastfeedingSubject terms: Neonatology, Nutrition, Paediatric research

Introduction

The American Academy of Pediatrics policy on infant hospital discharge, initiated in 1998, includes two nutritional assessment criteria: a sustained pattern of weight gain of sufficient duration and competent oral feeding skills without cardiorespiratory compromise1,2. While weight gain assessment is quantitative, easily measurable, and depends on caloric and volume intake, feeding skill assessment (FSA) usually remains a qualitative discussion between clinicians. The inherent qualitative nature of FSA makes it challenging to measure, and no gold-standard assessment tool is currently available for clinical use. This introduces immense variation in communication among clinicians (physicians, nurses, speech-language pathologists, lactation consultants, nurse practitioners, and other staff) and families, which may lead to changing care plans and inconsistent feeding practices. For preterm and medically complex infants in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), this is particularly problematic. Acute and chronic feeding issues are well-documented in the literature and include prolonged length of stay, hospital readmission, family stress, and risk of ongoing feeding difficulties post-discharge3–7. In 2019, our health system (Advocate Health) identified the need for a standard FSA tool for our 16 NICUs in the Midwest region.

Literature review

The literature review was guided by five criteria in the search for an optimal FSA tool, which included: (1) application: use for infants (term and premature) with different manners of oral intake (breast and bottle), (2) holistic: comprehensive in assessing the many facets of feeding, (3) interpretability: provides a score for interpretation regarding safety and quality of feeds that can be communicated between clinicians and families, (4) robust psychometric properties for validity and reliability and (5) feasible: practical, readily accessible and efficient for bedside clinicians to complete training on the tool, perform scoring, interpret results, document in the electronic health record and at a low cost to the health system. None of the available tools in our literature review met all of these criteria (Supplementary Figure S1)8–12. Currently, no gold-standard FSA tool is available for bedside clinicians. According to research, available FSA tools should be used with caution8,13. All NICU clinicians need a valid and reliable assessment tool that is highly feasible to objectively evaluate infant feeding skills beyond the volume of oral intake.

Need for FSA tool

Psychometric properties refer to a clinical tool’s validity, reliability, and feasibility. Clinical tools with poor reliability, validity, or feasibility may be unfavorable for patients14. Measurement of the psychometric properties of any tool is essential before implementation into clinical care and evaluation of clinical outcomes. In the NICU context, the goal was to evaluate different FSA tools with numeric scores that elucidate the quality and safety of infant feedings. Three existing tools with scores were identified as possible options. The three FSA tools were the Infant Driven Feeding Scale (IDFS)15, the Early Feeding Skills Assessment Tool (EFS)9,16, and the Neonatal Eating Outcome Assessment (NEOA)10,17. IDFS was not considered further due to limited content validity using the consensus of experts (the Delphi method)18. Newer research has shown IDFS and EFS have cross-cultural validation with translations into non-English languages. The IDFS tool has been translated and validated in the Turkish language19. The EFS tool has been translated and validated into many languages, including Spanish, Turkish, Portuguese, Persian, and Indonesian20–24. EFS and NEOA have strong psychometric properties with established validity and reliability, and are organized to identify the deficit areas in FSA more easily to facilitate domain-specific intervention10,16,17. However, both tools have poor feasibility because they are complex for the bedside clinician, time-consuming to administer, and not integrated into electronic health records. The EFS tool is copyrighted. The EFS Tool research version has been psychometrically tested; however, it lacks a pre-feed assessment section and a score-based clinical decision tree. The NEOA has been used for detailed feeding assessments by therapists. However, the complexity of the tool makes it challenging for a bedside clinician to use it in daily practice. The lack of integration of both tools into electronic medical records to calculate and document scores and develop actionable tables is another significant hindrance to easy adoption. Without an adequate FSA tool that could satisfy the selection criteria, a novel FSA tool called the SMART tool was designed to meet the needs of NICU clinicians, providing an objective measure of feeding readiness, quality, and safety. The objective of this study was to establish the psychometric properties (validity, reliability, and feasibility) of the SMART tool for infant oral feeding skill assessment. To establish criterion validity, we hypothesized that the performance of the SMART tool would be similar to that of a comparator tool, the Neonatal Eating Outcome Assessment (NEOA) tool.

Methods

SMART tool design

Ravi Mishra, MD, and Anne Albi, SLP/IBCLC, are the co-creators of the SMART tool, a novel infant FSA tool. The SMART tool evaluates feeding skills across five domains: state of arousal, motor tone, autonomic instability, response to stimulation, and total oral skills. These domains form the acronym of the SMART tool. All five domains are conducted before (pre-feed) and after each feeding (post-feed). The Total Oral Skills (TOS) is assessed with different measures in the pre- and post-feed sections. In the pre-feed setting, TOS refers to the duration of non-nutritive suck. In the post-feed setting, TOS refers to nutritive suck performance as measured by five sub-items, which include rooting, sucking bursts, bolus control, suck swallow breathing coordination, and stress cues. The three tools, EFS, NEOA, and SMART, assess similar components of the Total Oral Skills; however, each tool differs in scoring methods. The SMART Tool considers Total Oral Skills as one of the five factors. In contrast, the EFS and NEOA consider each component of Total Oral Skills as an independent factor.

The Synactive Theory of Development, developed by Heidelise Als, provides the theoretical foundation for the design of the SMART tool25. Synactive means “together in action” and refers to infant development using interactions. There are continuous interactions within subsystems (State, Motor, Autonomic, and Attention), continuous interaction of subsystems with the whole organism (infant), and continuous interactions between the infant and its environment, including the parents and care providers. These multilayered, continuous interactions lead to infant development. Understanding these interactions provides a window of opportunity to influence infant development. This view of development has been defined as Synactive, since at each stage in development and at each moment of functioning, the various subsystems discussed are existing side by side, often truly interactive or in a relative supporting, holding pattern, as if providing a steady substratum for one of the system’s current differentiation (synaction)26. It also aligns with trauma-informed care principles, the neonatal integrative developmental care model, life course health development intervention, and infant-family-centered developmental care27–30.

The SMART tool (Table 1) is practical for bedside clinicians due to its simplified scoring, fast and intuitive features, and clinical support decision tree for safe feeding. It includes pre-feed and post-feed sections, with a total score of 100 possible points for each, where higher is better for standardizing interpretations. For feeding safety, clear instructions were included as “Hard Stops”, which help identify signs of severe autonomic instability and prevent aspiration or choking (Table 1, Section C). This is a unique feature of the SMART tool, and impacts infant safety and end-users’ decision-making process. A decision tree (Table 1, Section E) is included in the SMART tool, which helps guide the initiation of oral feeding and qualify feeding skill levels. Oral feeding should only be offered if a pre-feed score is 70 or more. The post-feed score is to classify the feeding skill level into three categories: Caution (under 60), Developing (60 to 90), and Capable (above 90). The SMART tool comprises a glossary of terms (Table 2) and defined procedures to guide care.

SMART tool Glossary.

The SMART tool was shared with a system-wide workgroup of physicians, nurses, speech-language pathologists, and lactation consultants to solicit feedback. The authors established the score cut-offs for the clinical support decision tree. The subject matter experts (SMEs) (Lactation Consultants and Speech Language Pathologists) were selected from all four sites, having more than five years of experience in infant feeding at the bedside, who assisted in testing the tool. The SMEs participated in the content validity testing.

Study design

A multicenter, prospective, cross-sectional, observational study was conducted at four level 3 NICUs in the Chicagoland area. A convenience sample of infants who met the study criteria was prospectively recruited over time at each site during the study period. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations. Institutional Review Board (Advocate Aurora Health IRB #21.001E) protocol approval was obtained, and the study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05729828). The study was conducted according to the protocol. The study protocol was explained to the legal guardians, and informed consent was obtained. The goal of the validity studies was to conduct one feeding observation per infant using a cross-sectional design, with a trained scorer completing both the NEOA and SMART tool assessments based on their observation of the oral feeding. The inter-rater reliability studies involved observation by two observers for the same feeding event. The intra-rater reliability studies had more than one observation per infant by the same observer, six hours apart. Validity tests were conducted at four locations, while reliability tests were conducted at one location. Inclusion criteria included (1) medically cleared for oral feedings and at least at 33 weeks, 0 days post-menstrual age, (2) ability to tolerate enteral feedings, and (3) parental consent available. Exclusion criteria included respiratory support of more than two liters per minute of oxygen via nasal cannula.

There were four sites, each with a site lead. One site had two site leads, making a total of five site leads. The site leads completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI Program) certification before the start of the study to maintain standards in research, ethics, compliance, and safety. The site leads were involved in study participant identification, recruitment, obtaining consent, data submission to the electronic portal, and ensuring data quality at each site. Additionally, a total of 26 scorers were present from all sites. The scorers included NICU nurses, lactation consultants, and speech-language pathologists. Four hours of standard education and training on the study protocol, NEOA, and SMART tool were provided virtually. All scorers and site leads were required to score three additional feedings using both tools to assess accuracy. Feedback and peer coaching were provided to reach the desired fidelity of feeding skill assessment across scorers. Trained scorers strictly observed feedings and provided no feedback to feeders during the study. The scorer initially documented each feeding observation on paper, and then the site lead transferred the data to an online software form for future analysis using the REDCap software31. The scores were automatically calculated online.

Statistical analysis

The SMART tool’s psychometric properties were evaluated using “Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Status Measurement Instruments Criteria” (COSMIN Criteria)14. The plan for psychometric evaluation was to collect evidence for general and measurement properties (Supplementary Table S2). Data capture was done using REDCap32 Software hosted at Advocate Health. Data management and analysis were conducted with SAS statistical software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and GraphPad Prism software (Version 10; Boston, MA). All analyses were performed by research personnel employed by the health system. For all statistical estimates, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. According to COSMIN standard criteria, to establish content validity, it is estimated that at least seven observations should be tested per item to see a proper difference, not due to chance. Since this tool has ten questions (including five items and five subitems), the plan was to observe at least seventy feeding events for validity testing33.

The psychometric properties were evaluated using four types of tests: content validity, criterion validity, convergent validity, and reliability34. Psychometric properties are statistical properties of the qualities of a tool. Psychometric properties are not inherent to the tool; rather, they depend on how the tool’s scores are interpreted to estimate the construct14. The interpretation of scores depends on the tool, the patient, the context, the clinician’s observations, and their biases. If the clinician administers the tool in a non-standard manner, the validity and reliability of the results will be compromised. For example, jaundice estimation by visual inspection was established in 1969 for white, full-term infants, known as Kramer’s rule of jaundice cephalocaudal progression35. However, this score is inaccurate in cases of dark-skinned infants, premature infants, variable progression of jaundice, various etiologies, or severe jaundice36. The original scores, determined by Kramer’s rule, were not intended to apply to all populations. Hence, the validity and reliability depend on the interpretation of the scores in each context. Clarity of procedures, glossary, and decision-making algorithms will minimize errors. Construct is the concept that a test is designed to measure. Conceptualizing a construct changes what, why, and how it is measured. Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests. Validation is a joint responsibility of the test developer and test user14.

Content validity: Content validity evaluates the relation between test content and the construct it intends to measure. There was no patient data collection in content validity tests. The content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were calculated for content validity37,38. SMEs independently evaluated each item of the SMART tool. An online survey was conducted using Qualtrics software®, and data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel software®. The item has an acceptable level of statistical significance for the CVR and CVI if the calculated number is greater than the minimum required threshold based on the number of experts37.

Criterion validity: Criterion validity compares the performance of a new tool against a similar available tool with established psychometric properties. For criterion validity14, the SMART tool was compared to a benchmark tool, the Neonatal Eating Outcome Assessment (NEOA) tool. Due to the detailed design, robust psychometric properties, and the ability to get an FSA score at term equivalency, NEOA was used as a benchmark tool for validity testing. We obtained permission from the authors of NEOA to consider it a comparator tool for research purposes. The post-feed SMART tool total scores were compared to NEOA tool total scores at term equivalence. For criterion validity, the results were grouped into a binary option for each tool to generate a 2 × 2 contingency table. The groups were “normal (77 to 90)” and “not normal (18 to 76)” for NEOA, and the groups were “capable (91 to 100)” and “not capable (25 to 90)” for the SMART tool. For dichotomous scores, diagnostic tests included the calculations of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value39. Spearman’s Rho was used to calculate the correlation strength between the two variables. Continuous scores were summarized using the area under the receiver operator characteristic curves40.

Convergent validity: Construct validity describes the relationship between the tool and other tools measuring the same construct. For convergent validity, we proposed that the scores on both tools (SMART and NEOA) would improve with advancing gestational age. To establish convergent validity, Spearman’s Rho correlation coefficient was used to establish the correlation between the Post-Feed Total scores from the SMART tool and the NEOA tool total score in different groups based on birth gestational age. The term equivalency for NEOA tool scoring was used.

Reliability: Reliability or precision refers to the consistency of scores across instances of testing procedures34. Pre-feed and post-feed scores from the SMART tool were compared in reliability studies. Inter-rater reliability was measured by two scorers observing one infant feeding session and scoring using the SMART tool. Intra-rater reliability was calculated with one scorer using the SMART tool to observe two distinct infant feeding sessions on the same infant six hours apart. Reliability studies for the SMART tool provided paired scores. All scores were converted to respective categories based on the decision tree. The categories were then compared to check the agreement between the two scores. McNemar’s test was used to test the symmetry of a paired table for two categories, and Bowker’s test was used for three categories. Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to test the agreement of SMART categories.

Results

Observations

Seventy-six infants were enrolled in the study (Table 3) between May 5, 2021, and November 23, 2021. The median birth gestational age was 33 weeks (range 25 to 39 weeks), with a median birth weight of 2030 g (range 600 to 5250 g). Out of 143 feeding observations, 7 had partial scores (excluded), and 136 had complete scores. The distribution of the 136 complete scores was validity (94 scores), inter-rater (20 scores on 10 feedings), and intra-rater (22 scores on 12 feedings, with two pre-feed scores without a matching post-feed score). Baseline vitals (n = 136) described in median (range) were heart rate 161 (128 to 200), Respiratory rate 46 (24 to 64), and oxygen saturation 100% (88 to 100%). Interventions implemented by the feeder include the use of various nipple flow rates and the application of other infant-directed feeding techniques, such as swaddling and co-regulated pacing.

COSMIN criteria: general properties

The purpose of the SMART tool is to measure oral feeding skills in infants in the NICU. This study assessed its psychometric properties compared to the NEOA tool. The inclusion criteria for the study were infants above 33 weeks and with low oxygen need (≤ 2 L per minute). The structure of the SMART tool is based on the Synactive Theory of Development25. The SMART tool’s design includes a decision tree to signal readiness, safety, and feeding quality, making scores interpretable and clinically relevant. After training, the scorers reported a completion time of less than 5 min for the SMART tool following the oral feeding observation, which makes it highly feasible to integrate into routine clinical workflow. The SMART tool is a multidimensional, formative model (Supplementary Figure S3)41. All items had moderate to strong correlations with total scores.

COSMIN criteria: measurement properties

Content Validity (Table 4): 14 SMEs responded to an online survey. All SMEs worked at a level 3 NICU. For CVR, all items were considered essential except for the motor tone. The item, motor tone, was not removed from the tool, as it is a subsystem in the Synactive Theory of Development25. In the dimensionality test (Supplementary Figure S3), Motor Tone moderately correlated with Total Score, State, and Response to Stimulation. Total Oral Skills moderately correlated with Autonomic Instability and Response to Stimulation. For CVI, all items were related to the tool’s purpose.

Criterion Validity (Table 5): The SMART tool’s sensitivity (True positive rate) to detect NEOA normal category cases is 32/42, or 0.76 (95% CI 0.63–0.89). The SMART tool’s specificity to detect NEOA non-normal category cases is 43/52, or 0.82 (95% CI 0.72–0.93). There was no “missing feeding observation” data in the validity study. Comparing the SMART tool score ratings and the NEOA tool score ratings, Spearman’s Rho of r = 0.706 (n = 94) indicates a moderate to strong positive correlation of score ratings between the tools, which is statistically significant at p < 0.0001, signifying high confidence. Discussion

Safety and quality of infant feedings using the SMART tool

Clinicians need a gold-standard FSA tool with well-established psychometric properties to measure and trend the development of infant feeding skills in the NICU. None of the available tools in our literature review met all of the selection criteria8,10–13,16. This study established the validity, reliability, and feasibility of the SMART tool for FSA. These results are highly relevant to bedside clinicians in NICUs, as they allow implementation of the SMART tool to objectively measure infant feeding skill development, including readiness, safety, and quality of every oral feeding. Traditional measurements of feeding competence have relied on volume of intake rather than feeding skill. The SMART tool provides a numeric value to represent feeding skill (quality) in addition to feeding volume (quantity). Human-centered design principles were rigorously considered to increase the feasibility of the SMART tool, such as a focus on the end-user clinicians, interprofessional collaboration, iterative design with feedback, and using a system perspective for easy integration into the workflow43.

A detailed comparison of the SMART tool with other available infant feeding skill assessment tools is provided in Table 8. It demonstrates the robustness of the SMART tool in providing a comprehensive assessment of infant oral feeding skills, being intuitive to understand, feasible, and achieving high diagnostic testing accuracy.

The latent variable evaluated in the SMART tool is feeding skills. Five constructs assess feeding skills, including state, motor tone, autonomic instability, response to stimulation, and total oral skills. These constructs aim to objectively describe the safety and quality of oral feedings in infants and classify the infant’s feeding skill level. This aligns with the formative model of measurement research41. This implies that the individual constructs of the SMART tool cause the latent variable of feeding skills and not the other way around; feeding skills cause the indicators of the five constructs. We observed the multidimensional aspect of the SMART tool. The constructs (state, motor tone, and autonomic instability) correlate moderately with the total score. In contrast, the following constructs (response to stimulation and total oral skills) strongly correlate with the total score. See Supplementary Figure S3 for the Dimensionality Test.

There are several advantages to using an objective, systematic, standard assessment for infant feeding skills. This includes (1) definite criteria that help to identify safety “hard stops” before and during feeding, (2) provide a numeric value to represent feeding skills (quality), in addition to feeding volume (quantity), (3) using the tool to identify the etiology of oral feeding difficulty, (4) creating an individualized feeding plan, (5) monitoring progress over time to determine the effectiveness of feeding interventions, (6) communicating skill development with all involved in the infant’s care and family, and (7) potentially using it as a predictive tool regarding long-term developmental outcomes8. The proactive approach, focused on the safety and quality of feedings, using a standardized feeding assessment tool, can enhance pleasurable feeding experiences and minimize adverse events that lead to stress and trauma. While we do not recommend for or against using these scores as a basis for NICU discharge, users are encouraged to document the safety and quality of feedings in daily progress notes and discharge summaries to provide continuity of care and assess feeding skill development post-discharge.

Limitations

The SMART tool is intended for breast- and bottle-fed infants. However, only 4% of the study’s observations were of directly breastfed infants. This limits the generalization of the results to this patient population, and additional research is needed. Other patient populations not widely represented in the sample size include infants with significant co-morbidities and extremely preterm infants. The tool was designed for infants beginning at 32 weeks post-menstrual age; however, during study protocol development, inclusion criteria were agreed at 33 weeks due to inter-site variations in feeding practice. Due to COVID-19, there were limitations, such as low census, slow enrollment, learning attrition among scorers, and variable learning with online training. This may have led to inter-site variations in score interpretations, called construct-irrelevant variance. Reliability tests were conducted at only one site to overcome the limitations during COVID-19. Few measurement properties under COSMIN criteria were not measured as they do not apply to the SMART tool because the SMART tool is multidimensional; hence, inter-item correlations are low, and structural validity and internal consistency reliability tests are not applicable.

Conclusion

The results of this study are highly relevant to all NICU clinicians. The study established the validity, reliability, and feasibility of the SMART tool for FSA, allowing this novel tool to become the “standard FSA tool” NICU clinicians use to objectively evaluate feeding skills beyond the volume of oral intake across this health system. It meets all of the criteria that our health system was aiming for in the search for a standard FSA tool: (1) application: use for infants (term and premature) with different manners of intake (breast and bottle), (2) holistic: comprehensive in assessing the many facets of feeding, (3) interpretability: provides a score for interpretation regarding safety and quality of feeds that can be communicated between clinicians and families, (4) robust psychometric properties and (5) feasible: readily accessible and efficient for bedside clinicians to complete training on the tool, perform scoring, interpret results document in the electronic health record and at a low cost to the health system. The results of this study were presented at national conferences44,45. We have implemented this tool in our health system based on its strong psychometric properties. Future studies will evaluate the SMART tool’s effect on clinical outcomes.
