Site-specific ribozyme-mediated alkylation of DNA substrates
Authors: Yuanlin He, Timothy J Wilson, Jie Deng, Yuhang Luo, Kaizhou Yan, Yuting Xie, David M J Lilley, Lin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMTR1 is an in vitro-selected ribozyme that catalyses the transfer of an alkyl group from exogenous O6-alkylguanine to N1 of a specific adenine in RNA. We show here that the ribozyme can also efficiently alkylate a DNA substrate strand, with almost complete alkylation in 20 min. We have determined crystal structures of the products of methyl and benzyl transfer. The structures are closely similar to that of the all-RNA ribozyme, binding the guanine product in an identical manner, and alkylation occurs at the equivalent location as in the RNA, i.e. N1 of dA63. 2′-O-methylation of C10 and U45, which are hydrogen-bonded to the exogenous guanine, leads to an order-of-magnitude faster rate of alkyl transfer. The results indicate that MTR1 could be a useful tool for the site-specific modification of DNA, including the creation of fluorescent labels or targets for chemical crosslinking.

Introduction

Fluorescently-labeled oligonucleotides have broad applications in diverse fields including disease diagnosis, nanotechnology, bioimaging, and fundamental research [1–3]. Conventionally, fluorophores are introduced into oligonucleotides by solid-phase synthesis, but this synthetic method suffers from several drawbacks. For instance, the limited length (∼100 nt) of oligonucleotides, incompatibility with labile functional groups, and the high cost of phosphoramidites [4–8]. To circumvent these issues, enzymatic methods have been developed as an alternative approach. Two key ideas of the enzymatic approach are to introduce nucleotide analogues carrying fluorophores into oligonucleotide fragments using engineered polymerases [9–11], and to assemble short fragments into longer oligonucleotide chains by ligation [12–14], thereby obtaining fluorescent oligonucleotides. Beyond co-synthetic labeling, enzymatic strategies are also widely adopted for post-synthetic RNA labeling [15]. Key applications involve engineered tRNA-modifying enzymes such as tRNA guanine transglycosylase (TGT) [16, 17] or agmatidine synthase [18], along with methyltransferases that utilize synthetic SAM analogs to enable precise fluorescent labeling [19], photocrosslinking [20], and click-chemistry modifications [21].

Recent advances in ribozyme and deoxyribozyme technology have enabled novel strategies for site-specific RNA modification and labeling [22–27]. Initially, this modification occurs on ribose rings of nucleotides that attach fluorophores to the 2′-hydroxyl group of ribose in certain adenosine nucleotides [28–32], and this method has been applied to rRNAs [28] and mRNAs [33]. Selective N7-alkylation of internal guanosine was achieved using fluorescein- or biotin-iodoacetamide [34, 35] or epoxides [36]. Through the development of engineered ribozymes, specifically the methyltransferase ribozymes MTR1 [37] and SAMURI [38] that uses SAM analogs, the field achieved a major breakthrough, enabling sequence-targeted adenine modification via programmable binding arms.

MTR1 was generated by Höbartner and colleagues [37] using in vitro selection, acting as an alkyl transferase on an RNA substrate, transferring an alkyl group from exogenous O6-alkylguanine to the N1 of a specific adenine. The ribozyme is flexible in terms of acceptable alkyl donors. The alkyl group can be as small as methyl, or significantly larger such as 4-aminomethyl-benzyl, and this can also carry a fluorophore or potentially a crosslinker. This ribozyme is well characterized in terms of both structure [39, 40] and catalytic mechanism [41, 42]. Although MTR1 was initially selected to alkylate an RNA substrate, we aim to extend its application to DNA due to its superior aqueous stability compared to RNA, and potentially serve as a useful tool in the chemical biology of DNA. This could be simply achieved by exchanging the S31 strand (colored red in Fig. 1A) for DNA, leaving the catalytic core of the ribozyme still comprising RNA.

Previous work on the all-RNA MTR1 ribozyme shows that the exogenous O6-alkylguanine is bound in the core of the RNA, coplanar with and hydrogen bonded to cytosine (C10) and uracil (U45) nucleobases and the target adenine (A63) [39, 40]. C10 acts as a general acid to protonate N1 of the O6-alkylguanine [41], and consequently, the reaction is greatly accelerated when the pH is lowered to 6.0. In the all-RNA ribozyme, the A63 N1 nucleophile is directed towards the electrophile in an almost perfectly in-line SN2 trajectory. Höbartner and colleagues [37] have shown that MTR1 will accept a single deoxyribonucleotide at the target A63, as expected since only the nucleobase enters the active center. However, the entire P1 and P3 helices will become DNA-RNA hybrid helices when the ribozyme binds a fully-DNA substrate; this could affect the geometry of these helices and the trajectory, and thus might affect the ability of the ribozyme to alkylate a DNA substrate.

We therefore tested the ability of MTR1 to alkylate DNA, and found that a DNA substrate became almost fully alkylated. We solved crystal structures of MTR1 bound to DNA, with two different alkyl donors. This showed that the target remains A63 N1, and that the ribozyme core remains unchanged, with retention of in-line attack trajectory. Thus, MTR1 has the potential to act upon DNA in a site-specific manner and thus could serve as a valuable reagent for the modification of DNA.

Material and methods

Synthesis of RNA oligonucleotides

RNA oligonucleotides were synthesized using t-BDMS phosphoramidite chemistry [43]. Unmodified oligonucleotides and those having 2′-O-methyl modification were synthesized on an Applied Biosystems 394DNA/RNA synthesizer as described in Wilson et al. [44], using UltraMILD ribonucleotide phosphoramidites [45, 46] (Link Technologies), and deprotected as described in Deng et al. [39]. The DNA substrate strand was synthesized using β-cyanoethyl phosphoramidite chemistry [43] using an Applied Biosystems 394 DNA/RNA synthesizer. All oligonucleotides were purified by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions in 7 M urea. RNA- and DNA-containing bands were excised and recovered by elution from the crushed fragments.

RNA transcription and purification

RNAs were generated by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase from DNA templates containing a T7 promoter, that were prepared by PCR. Transcription reactions were performed at 37°C for 6 h. The resulting RNA products were purified by electrophoresis in 10% denaturing polyacrylamide gels in 7 M urea. The target RNA band was excised by UV shadowing and electroeluted in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 150 V for 12 h at 4°C. RNAs were precipitated from the eluate with isopropanol, washed two to three times with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in water.

Kinetic analysis of MTR1 ribozyme-catalyzed alkyl transfer

The MTR1 ribozyme was assembled from two strands (all written 5′ to 3′). All sequences are tabulated in Supplementary Table S3.

Standard single-turnover assays were performed at 37°C in 25 mM MES (pH 6.0), 100 mM KCl, 40 mM MgCl2 and 50 µM O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine, with 1 µM MTR1 ribozyme annealed to ∼20 pM radioactively-[5′-32P]-labelled target DNA. The RNA was annealed by rapid cooling from 80°C in 50 mM KCl, to which buffer and salts were added, followed by equilibration at 37°C for 20 min. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 200 µM O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine, which was also equilibrated to 37°C. Reactions longer than 5 min were incubated under mineral oil to prevent evaporation, and 2 µL aliquots were taken at time intervals and added to 13 µL 90% formamide, 50 mM EDTA to stop the reaction.

Unmodified and modified target DNA were separated by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 14.4% gels (13.3% acrylamide, 1.1% bis-acrylamide) with 90 mM Tris.borate (pH 8.5), 10 mM EDTA (TBE), 7M urea. Crystallization, structure determination, and refinement

The MTR1 ribozyme for crystallization was assembled from three strands [40] as shown in Fig. 1A (Supplementary Table S3).

Crystallization complexes were formed at a final concentration of 200 µM by mixing RNA and DNA strands in a 1:1:1 molar ratio in 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM KCl, and 5 mM MgCl2 (HKM buffer). First, the mixture was heated to 95°C for 1 min, followed by gradual cooling at room temperature. Then, the substrate (O6-methylguanine or O6-benzylguanine) was added to a final concentration of 500 µM.

Crystallization was achieved by combining 0.8 µL of the sample complex with 0.8 µL of a solution containing 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM LiCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 50 mM MES (pH 7.0–7.6), and 34–36% MPD, using the hanging drop vapor-diffusion method at 18°C. Well-diffracting crystals appeared and grew to full size throughout 4 to 7 days. Subsequently, the crystals were rapidly frozen through mounting onto nylon loops and immediate immersion in liquid nitrogen.

X-ray diffraction data were collected on beamline BL10U2 or BL02U1 at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) and processed using XDS [47]. The structures were determined by molecular replacement using PHASER [48] with PDB 7Q7X and 7Q7Y [40]. The model was manually adjusted using Coot [49] and subjected to several rounds of adjustment and optimization using Phenix.refine [50], and PDB REDO [51]. Model geometry and the fit to electron-density maps were monitored with MOLPROBITY and the validation tools in Coot. Simulated annealing omit maps were calculated based on the composite omit map in the PHENIX suite using the method anneal. Atomic coordinates and structure factor amplitudes have been submitted to the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the corresponding accession codes listed in Supplementary Table S1, together with the statistics of crystal diffraction data and structure refinement.

MTR1-catalyzed fluorescent labeling of long ssDNA

Preparation of fluorescently labeled small molecules

Preparation of fluorescently labeled small molecules

We sought to develop a low-cost fluorescence-based detection method and selected the fluorophore Cyanine5.5 NHS ester (Duofluor D10013; ex/em = 680/698 nm; ε = 1.98 × 105 M−1cm−1) attached to guanine O6 via an O-(4-(aminomethyl)-benzyl) linkage (BG-NH-Cy5.5). BG-NH2 (Biosynth) was reacted with Cy5.5 at a molar ratio of 1:1.2 in a labeling buffer containing 50 mM Na3BO3 (pH 8.5) at room temperature in the dark for 5 h. After completion, the reaction product was purified by thin-layer chromatography using a mobile phase consisting of methanol and dichloromethane in a volume ratio of 1:9.

ssDNA preparation and fluorescent labeling

ssDNA preparation and fluorescent labeling

The MTR1 ribozyme was assembled from two strands (Supplementary Table S3). To generate the substrate, DNAs were amplified by PCR from the plasmid pUC57-TETP6B using Phanta® Max Super-Fidelity DNA polymerase (Vazyme P505) with the primer sequences P1 to P4 (Supplementary Table S3).

After PCR, the dsDNA was separated by electrophoresis in 8% polyacrylamide gel under denaturing conditions in TBE, 7M urea. Specific DNA bands were excised from the gel by UV shadowing. DNA was extracted from gel fragments by overnight incubation in elution buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 0.4 M NaCl, 2% SDS) at 42°C using a temperature-controlled shaker. The eluate was centrifuged at 16 000 × g for 20 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Equal volumes of isopropanol and 1/10 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) were added, together with 1 μL GlycoBlue, and the mixture was placed at −80 °C for at least 30 min. The DNA was recovered by centrifugation at 16 000 × g for 30 min. at 4°C, washed twice with cold 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in water. The DNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer.

MTR1-catalyzed labeling of ssDNA substrates was carried out in a 20 μL reaction volume under the following conditions: 2 μM MTR1 ribozyme, 1 μM ssDNA substrate, 100 μM BG-NH-Cy5.5, 5 mM MES (pH 5.8), 40 mM MgCl2, 5 mM NaCl, and 120 mM KCl. The mixture was gently vortexed, briefly centrifuged, and preincubated at 37°C for 1 h, followed by heating at 95°C for 1 min. After natural cooling to room temperature, the reaction was continued at 37°C for a total of 12 h. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, and 300 mM NaCl to a final volume of 100 μL. DNA was precipitated with ethanol, washed twice with 100 μL of cold 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 10 μL water. All samples were analyzed by electrophoresis in a 10% polyacrylamide gel under denaturing conditions and visualized using excitation at 685 nm with a Sapphire RGBNIR imaging system (Azure Biosystems IS1026).

Off-target effect analysis

The off-target assay was performed by incubating the MTR1 ribozyme with DNA substrate strands of varying degrees of complementarity. The sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

The reactions were performed essentially as described above for ssDNA labeling (see above), with the following modifications: The reaction mixture contained 20 μM MTR1 ribozyme, 10 μM ssDNA substrate, and 50 μM SNAP-Surface® Alexa Fluor® 647 (New England Biolabs S9136S). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 12 h. All subsequent steps—including reaction termination, ethanol precipitation, and denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (10% gel)—were identical to those described in the standard protocol. Visualization was performed using a Sapphire RGBNIR imaging system (Azure Biosystems IS1026).

N 1-methylation of adenine and Sanger sequencing

Using the same 200 nt ssDNA as the substrate strand, the methylation reaction was performed following the protocol described for ssDNA labeling, with the sole modification that BG-NH2 was replaced by an equimolar concentration (100 μM) of O6-methylguanine. The products were submitted to iGene Biotechnology (Guangzhou) for Sanger sequencing. The chromatogram was aligned to the wild-type sequence to assess base substitutions, premature stops, or signal attenuation indicative of problems arising from the incorporation of N1-methyladenine.

Results

Design of the MTR1-DNA ribozyme

In this work, we have studied two forms of the MTR1 ribozyme with a DNA substrate strand, based on the sequence shown in Fig. 1A. For the crystallographic analysis, we used exactly that sequence, comprising three strands. For the analysis of alkyl transfer activity and kinetics, we used a two-strand construct, comprising a DNA substrate strand and an RNA ribozyme strand in which P2 is a stem-loop (Fig. 2A). The alkyl transfer reaction is shown schematically in Fig. 1B, where the alkyl donor is either O6-methylguanine or O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine (Fig. 1C).

DNA alkylation activity of MTR1

We have explored the alkylation of a DNA substrate strand by MTR1 using O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine as the alkyl donor. We find that the ribozyme can efficiently transfer the 4-aminomethyl-benzyl to a DNA substrate strand. As previously observed using an RNA substrate strand [37, 39], the alkylation leads to a significant retardation of electrophoretic mobility in a polyacrylamide gel of the DNA strand due to a combination of the mass of the alkyl group and the positive charge located at A63 N1 (Fig. 2). The 4-aminomethyl-benzyl group is transferred to A63 N1 in the DNA substrate (see section on crystal structures below). These data show that the MTR1 ribozyme can catalyze the transfer of an alkyl group to DNA as well as to RNA.

We have used the electrophoretic separation of alkylated DNA from the unalkylated substrate to measure the extent of reaction under our standard conditions of 25 mM MES (pH 6.0), 100 mM KCl, 40 mM MgCl2, with 1 µM ribozyme strand and 20 nM DNA substrate, and 50 µM O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine at 37°C (Fig. 2). The reaction progress plotted as a function of time is well fitted by a single exponential function, from which we calculate a rate of alkyl transfer of 0.17 ± 0.02 min−1 (mean of 4 determinations). Thus, MTR1 alkylates a DNA substrate approximately three times more slowly than the RNA substrate it was originally selected to modify.

Effect of mutation of the general acid on the DNA alkylation activity of MTR1

Previous structural and mechanistic analysis of alkylation of RNA by MTR1 has established a critical role for general acid catalysis by the nucleobase of C10 [39–42]. We therefore explored the effect of introducing a C10U mutation on the alkylation of the DNA substrate. We performed an alkyl transfer assay using O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine as alkyl donor under standard conditions at pH 6.0 and 37°C (Fig. 3A). While the unmodified ribozyme gave ∼50% alkyl transfer after 5 min, incubation with the C10U ribozyme led to undetectable alkyl transfer after 120 min (Fig. 3A, compare tracks 2 and 3). Clearly, alkyl transfer to a DNA substrate by MTR1 is highly sensitive to the C to U transition mutation at C10, consistent with the same catalytic mechanism being used on both RNA and DNA substrates.

Introduction of 2′-O-methyl groups at C10 and U45 accelerates alkyl transfer by MTR1 on the DNA substrate

Höbartner and colleagues [40] reported that O2’-methylation of C10 and U45 leads to a significantly faster rate of methylation on RNA at N1 of A63. We therefore explored the effect of the equivalent double-modification on alkyl transfer to a DNA substrate. A 5-min incubation with O6-(4-aminomethyl-benzyl)guanine as alkyl donor under standard conditions at pH 6.0 and 37°C led to a significantly greater fraction of the DNA substrate becoming alkylated compared to the unmodified MTR1 (Fig. 3A, compare tracks 2 and 4). We carried out a time course of 4-aminomethyl-benzyl transfer using the double O2’methyl-modified ribozyme using the DNA substrate (Fig. 3B), from which we plotted the progress curve for the reaction (Fig. 3C). Fitting to a single exponential function gave an observed rate of reaction kobs = 1.12 min−1. Repeated measurements gave kobs = 1.5 ± 0.3 min−1 (mean of 4 determinations). This is almost ninefold faster than the reaction using the unmodified ribozyme, showing that this modification affects the reaction on the RNA and DNA substrates in a similar manner.

Crystal structure of MTR1 with a DNA substrate strand

As MTR1 clearly catalyses alkyl transfer to DNA, we decided to determine the molecular structure of the RNA-DNA hybrid form by X-ray crystallography. We therefore placed MTR1 with a completely DNA substrate strand (the sequence shown in Fig. 1A) into crystal trials in the presence of either O6-benzylguanine or O6-methylguanine. Diffracting crystals were obtained for both species. In contrast to the all-RNA MTR1, both DNA-substrate forms crystallized in the tetragonal space group P41, at resolutions of 2.82 Å (benzyl; PDB ID 9KFV) and 2.65 Å (methyl; PDB ID 9KBR). Crystallographic statistics are presented in Supplementary Table S1. For ease of comparison in this paper, we use the same numbering of nucleotides that was used for the all-RNA MTR1 structure [39]. The new structures required changes to the numbering in the PDB files, so that C10 is now at position 12 and U45 is now at position 42, for example. A comparison table of sequence numbering is presented as Supplementary Table S2.

The structures obtained are closely related to the all-RNA MTR1 [39], superimposing with root mean square deviation (RMSD) values of 0.993 Å (benzyl) and 0.914 Å (methyl) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In addition, the product complex structures with N1-methyl A63 and N1-benzyl A63 are highly similar, superimposing with an RMSD = 0.437 Å (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The ribozymes adopt an approximately T-shaped three-way junction conformation (Fig. 4), where the hybrid DNA-RNA P1 and P3 helices form the top of the “T.” These hybrid DNA-RNA helices adopt an A-form conformation, with axes located outside their major grooves, and a helical periodicity of 12 bp/turn (Supplementary Fig. S2). The helix is modelled with the ribose of the DNA and RNA strands having C3’-endo conformation, although this cannot be deduced unambiguously from the electron density.

Like the all-RNA form of MTR1, the core of the ribozyme comprises four planes of base interaction (Fig. 5A and B). Rising up the P2 helix (3′ to 5′ on the S12 strand), there are two standard cis Watson–Crick base pairs (the planes containing G12 and C11), followed by the plane that includes the bound exogenous guanine product (the plane containing C10). The fourth plane comprises three coplanar adenine nucleobases connected by two hydrogen bonds (the plane including A9). The third plane is the active center of the ribozyme, comprising the exogenous guanine and the nucleobases of C10, U45, and A63. This structure is essentially identical to that in the all-RNA MTR1 ribozyme, including the approximately helical S12, and the corkscrew-like S23.

The catalytic core of the DNA-bound MTR1 ribozyme

The exogenous guanine product is bound in the third plane (Fig. 5C and D), where it is approximately coplanar with C10, U45, and dA63, sharing three hydrogen bonds on its sugar edge with the Watson-Crick edge of U45, and two hydrogen bonds on its Watson-Crick edge with the Watson-Crick edge of C10. In addition, the N7 of the exogenous guanine accepts a hydrogen bond from N6 of the target dA63. The exogenous guanine is also stacked on both faces. The structure of the active center is almost identical to that of the all-RNA ribozyme, suggesting that it should transfer an alkyl group to dA63 N1. While the methyl group is not visible in the electron density map (Fig. 5C), the connection with the benzyl group is clearly visible (Fig. 5D), consistent with transfer to dA63 N1. The active centers of these two product complexes superimpose closely, with an RMSD of 0.225 Å (Supplementary Fig. S1C). The truncated electron density is consistent with local mobility of the benzyl group, but its location is well defined. Thus, the manner of binding of guanine in the product, and the position of alkyl transfer are the same as for the all-RNA ribozyme. Furthermore, the reaction has again gone to completion with the DNA substrate strand.

The conformation around the target dA63, and comparison with the all-RNA form

The structure of the catalytic core of MTR1 seems to be little-affected by being bound to a DNA substrate strand, yet the rate of alkyl transfer is significantly lowered. We have therefore examined the immediate structural environment around dA63, and compared this to the equivalent position in the all-RNA ribozyme (Fig. 5E). In both forms of the ribozyme, the target adenine (dA63 and A63) lies at a 90° turn in the trajectory of the S31 strand connecting the P1 and P3 helices. For each form, dA63/A63 is stacked upon A40 (that is part of the A:A:A triple interaction) that is in turn stacked upon dG64/G64 that forms the first junction-proximal base pair of P1. The lower side (as depicted in Fig. 5E) of dA63/A63 is not stacked, and dG62/G62 is rotated around so that the planes of the nucleobases are 90° with respect to dA63/A63. Although the sugar puckers of dA63 and A63 are quite different, the positioning of the nucleobase is very similar, and thus they are juxtaposed with the exogenous guanine in essentially the same manner. dG62 + dA63 + dG64 + guanine align with their all-RNA equivalents with an RMSD = 0.44 Å. We considered the possibility that minor differences in conformation might have led to a small change in the guanine O6 – A63N1 distance, but both were 4.2 Å. Thus, nothing in the structures of the ribozyme product complexes provides an explanation for the difference in the rate of alkylation for the DNA substrate and all-RNA forms of the ribozyme.

Application of MTR1 as a potential laboratory tool for the site-specific alkylation of DNA

We have sought to demonstrate that MTR1 can be used as a practical tool for the addition of fluorescent probes at specific targets in relatively long single-stranded DNA molecules. Overlapping single-stranded DNA species of 100, 150, and 200 nt containing a common target for MTR1 were generated by PCR amplification from plasmid DNA in such a way that the individual single-strands could be separated by length in gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions (Fig. 6A). The purified ssDNA species were mixed with MTR1 and BG-NH-Cy5.5 and heated to 95°C, followed by slow cooling, and then incubated at 37°C for 12 h. The DNA species were then analyzed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis under denaturing conditions, and visualized by either UV shadowing (total DNA) or fluorimaging (fluorescence of Cy5.5 resulting from alkyl transfer) (Fig. 6B). Each ssDNA species exhibited a clear fluorescence signal, demonstrating successful site-specific labeling by MTR1.

To assess further the specificity of MTR1-mediated DNA alkylation, DNA substrates with varying degrees of complementarity to the ribozyme-binding arms were examined. Fluorescent alkyl transfer was observed exclusively for the fully complementary substrate (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B), indicating that efficient alkyl transfer strictly depends on correct base pairing between the ribozyme and its target DNA. In addition, Sanger sequencing of 200 nt ssDNA substrates following MTR1-catalyzed methylation revealed that modification-induced signatures occurred predominantly at the intended target position (A92) (Supplementary Fig. S4C and D). These results demonstrate that MTR1 enables highly site-specific modification of long ssDNA substrates with no off-target effects.

Discussion

These studies clearly demonstrate that the MTR1 ribozyme alkylates a DNA substrate very efficiently. A DNA molecule with an adenine at position 63 is alkylated or methylated at the N1 position almost to completion in 20 min., or in 3 min. using ribozyme with O-2′-methyl modification at C10 plus U45. The alkylation occurs at the equivalent site to that of the all-RNA ribozyme. In addition, the characteristics of the reaction are essentially identical, being totally dependent on C10, which has been established as the general acid protonating N1 of the exogenous O6-alkylguanosine in the all-RNA reaction [41]. The alkylation reaction proceeds well despite now taking place on a DNA-RNA hybrid in place of the dsRNA substrate. The reaction rate is also accelerated by an order-of-magnitude by O-2′-methyl modification at C10 plus U45, similar to the observations of Höbartner and colleagues [40] using the all-RNA ribozyme.

The structure of the ribozyme is closely similar to the all-RNA ribozyme. That was largely expected, since the ribozyme and its active core comprise RNA aside from dA63, and even there, the deoxyribose is located outside the core. The central region comprising the four planes of base interactions (including the exogenous guanine:C10:U45:dA63 active site and the A3 triple bracketed by the S12 helical backbone and the S23 corkscrew backbone) are essentially identical for the all RNA ribozyme and that with the DNA substrate (Fig. 5) apart from U39 that makes no interactions with other nucleotides, superimposing with an RMSD = 0.345 Å (Supplementary Fig. S3). The P1 and P3 helices are fully hybrid DNA-RNA helices when the substrate strand comprises DNA. They adopt a standard A-form conformation with a deep major groove (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Although in most respects the alkylation of RNA and DNA substrates is closely similar, the rate of alkylation of RNA is three times that of DNA. Given the near-identical structures of the ribozyme in both cases, this is perhaps surprising. And despite the presence of the DNA strand, the local environment is also very similar for both substrates. In the crystal structure of the all-RNA ribozyme, the distance between the A63N1 and the exogenous guanine O6 is 4.2 Å, and we have noted that this distance would need to shorten in the initiation of nucleophilic attack. This might be a source of variation between the all-RNA and DNA-substrate forms, yet the equivalent distance in the newform with the DNA substrate is also 4.2 Å. However, both crystal structures are product complexes, in which the reaction has already occurred. The difference in reaction rates between the two forms likely lies in the approach to the transition state, and this may not be reflected in the structure of the final product.

Alkylation of an RNA substrate is an order of magnitude faster when the C10 and U45 are modified by O2’-methylation [40], and we have observed a similar acceleration of alkylation of a DNA substrate (Fig. 3). Both nucleotides are involved in binding and positioning the exogenous O6-alkylguanine reactant, and this effect also likely arises from the approach to the transition state.

The observation that a ribozyme selected to act upon an RNA substrate can also act upon DNA indicates a relaxed specificity that might have been important in the early stages of switching from a putative all-RNA-based metabolism to one that includes DNA. It might have eased the transition into the use of DNA to encode genetic information if some RNA enzymes could be exploited to modify DNA before a full range of protein enzymes had evolved.

Our results indicate that the ribozyme MTR1 has the potential to be a valuable tool for the efficient site-specific modification of DNA. Almost complete modification is achieved with a 20-min incubation, and this can be reduced to 3 min. if the C10, U45 2′-O-methylated ribozyme is used. In principle, this could be used to introduce N1-methyladenine into a chosen location to investigate its biological effect, or for the site-specific introduction of fluorophores or crosslinkers into DNA. However, our tests indicate that efficient labeling of long ssDNA substrates requires denaturation to enable ribozyme binding, and labeling was not observed in the absence of this step. Compared with radioactive probes, fluorescent probes exhibit notable advantages, including enhanced safety, superior stability, rapid detection, high resolution, and compatibility with multicolor simultaneous analysis. These properties make them particularly suitable for dynamic studies in live cells and clinical diagnostic applications. Furthermore, efficient fluorescent labeling of DNA serves as a fundamental technical prerequisite for constructing functional fluorescent probes. The use of MTR1 offers several key advantages: (i) single-site modification, (ii) compatibility with long DNA substrates, (iii) multicolor fluorescence labeling (validated for Cy3, Cy5, and Cy5.5), (iv) a simple one-tube, one-step reaction protocol. Therefore, MTR1 could serve as a simple, efficient, and cost-effective tool to generate fluorescent probes for the reliable detection of nucleic acid species of interest. The methodology could also be exploited for the site-specific introduction of fluorescent probes for the measurement of FRET or fluorescence anisotropy within a chosen region in large RNA assemblies.
