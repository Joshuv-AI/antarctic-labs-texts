Understanding the Metabolic Fingerprint of Muscle Aging
Authors: Liang-Kung Chen
Published: 2025

Sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass and function, has emerged as a public health challenge in aging populations worldwide [1]. Despite growing recognition of its clinical importance, the biological mechanisms underlying sarcopenia development remain incompletely understood. In this issue of Journal of Nutrition Health and Aging, Kim and colleagues presented findings from a 14-year prospective study examining the association between plasma metabolites and incident sarcopenia in 33,797 participants from the UK Biobank [2]. The study identified 38 plasma metabolites significantly associated with sarcopenia risk and evaluated their incremental predictive value beyond conventional risk factors.

Nevertheless, several methodological issues warrant further consideration. First, the discordance between baseline and incident sarcopenia ascertainment may introduce diagnostic bias. At baseline, sarcopenia was defined using EWGSOP2 criteria [1], while incident cases were identified primarily through ICD-10 code M62.84 from hospital records. This heterogeneous approach created underdiagnosis risk. Hospital-based ICD coding usually underestimated sarcopenia prevalence, as the condition frequently went unrecognized unless explicitly sought [3]. The relatively low incidence rate (829 cases among 33,797 participants over 14 years) may reflect the healthier UK Biobank population and hospital-based ascertainment, suggesting that identified associations likely reflected more severe, clinically apparent cases rather than the broader spectrum of age-related muscle decline. Second, the application of diagnostic cutoffs derived predominantly from populations aged 65 years and older to a cohort spanning ages 40–69 years at baseline may also be problematic. This age-cutoff mismatch may introduce misclassification that could bias observed metabolite associations and predictive performance. Third, reliance on single-timepoint metabolite measurements further limited biological and predictive insights. Over a 14-year follow-up period, metabolite profiles would change over time. Cross-sectional baseline measurements cannot capture these dynamic trajectories. The observed modest predictive performance may partly reflect this temporal mismatch. Fourth, the absence of independent external validation substantially limited confidence in reported findings and generalization. The UK Biobank represented a relatively healthy, predominantly British population with better socioeconomic status than the general population [4]. Whether the identified 38-metabolite signature applies to more diverse populations, or individuals with higher comorbidity burdens remains unknown.

Despite these limitations, the metabolite associations revealed biologically plausible patterns worthy of research attention. The positive association between omega-6 fatty acids and sarcopenia risk aligned with their pro-inflammatory properties, though the disconnect between observational metabolite associations and intervention trial outcomes suggested that plasma metabolite profiles reflect complex dietary patterns and metabolic states rather than simple causal relationships [5]. The lipoprotein subclass findings extended emerging paradigms from cardiovascular disease research, where HDL particle characteristics provided more nuanced risk stratification than total HDL cholesterol. Kim's findings suggested that similar particle biology may influence sarcopenia through shared mechanisms involving inflammation, oxidative stress, or metabolic regulation. However, the literature examining lipids and sarcopenia remained inconsistent, with some studies showing protective effects of higher cholesterol in older adults—so-called "reverse epidemiology." The current study's null findings for conventional lipids but significant associations with particle subclasses may help reconcile these contradictions, though replication is essential. Notably, the aromatic amino acid findings in this study were different from other metabolomics studies. Yeung and colleagues, in a 4-year prospective study of Chinese older adults, found branched-chain rather than aromatic amino acids predicted sarcopenia risk [6]. Similarly, recent work using targeted metabolomics identified altered sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism in persons with sarcopenia [7]—metabolite classes poorly captured by NMR spectroscopy in the UK Biobank. These discrepancies highlighted heterogeneity across studies varying in ethnicity, dietary patterns, metabolite platforms, and sarcopenia definitions. This heterogeneity fundamentally constrained identification of robust, replicable biomarkers and underscores the urgent need for standardization and coordinated multi-cohort studies.

While metabolomics provides a window into systemic biochemical changes, understanding sarcopenia requires appreciating the complex, multi-level biological mechanisms driving age-related muscle decline. Recent advances in muscle aging research revealed interconnected pathways involving oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disrupted proteostasis that collectively compromise muscle structure and function. Oxidative stress emerges as an important pathological feature of muscle aging. Excessive reactive oxygen species production overwhelmed antioxidant defenses, causing cumulative cellular damage that impaired the regenerative capacity of muscle satellite cells [8]. This oxidative injury triggered mitochondrial dysfunction, creating a vicious cycle where damaged mitochondria generated additional reactive oxygen species while producing insufficient ATP to maintain muscle contractile function and protein synthesis. Critically, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction converged with chronic low-grade inflammation to accelerate muscle decline. Age-related inflammatory pathway activation, mediated through NF-κB and cytokines including TNF-α and IL-6, directly inhibited muscle protein synthesis and activated proteolytic pathways [9]. These inflammatory signals disrupted myogenic differentiation, impaired muscle regeneration, and promoted the infiltration of adipose tissue into muscle which further exacerbated metabolic dysfunction and inflammation [10]. The metabolite patterns identified by Kim and colleagues likely reflect these underlying inflammatory and oxidative processes, though the directionality and causality remained uncertain.

This mechanistic understanding gained particular importance when considering population-level heterogeneity. The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia demonstrated that Asian populations typically have lower muscle mass but may experience sarcopenia at different thresholds compared to European populations [11], challenging the notion of universal diagnostic criteria applied in studies like the UK Biobank. Furthermore, muscle-to-fat ratio, rather than muscle mass alone, has been found to be the more effective predictor for functional decline and mortality risk [12]. This finding suggested that metabolic crosstalk between muscle and adipose tissue may fundamentally influence aging trajectories. Besides, recent investigations have identified follistatin, activin A, and other circulating factors as potential biomarkers linking muscle health to systemic outcomes including respiratory function and all-cause mortality [13]. These mechanistic studies suggested that the metabolite associations observed in the UK Biobank may represent downstream consequences of deeper regulatory disturbances in muscle-fat-immune axis signaling. The prior report demonstrated that even in adults beyond age 75, targeted interventions including resistance exercise and nutritional optimization can effectively enhance muscle function [14]. This therapeutic optimism differs from the prognostic fatalism sometimes accompanying biomarker research and underscores that identifying at-risk individuals should catalyze intervention rather than merely documenting inevitable decline.

These mechanistic complexities help explain why Kim et al.’s findings, while scientifically valuable, yielded modest predictive performance. The metabolite signature achieved an AUC of 0.696 alone and when combined with conventional risk factors, improved prediction only marginally. While statistically significant, the clinical meaningfulness of such small improvements remained questionable. This modest performance aligned with broader patterns in biomarker research. Pencina and colleagues demonstrated that even biomarkers with robust disease associations often contribute minimally to risk prediction when added to well-constructed clinical models [15]. Ioannidis and Tzoulaki showed that, for cardiovascular disease, even "blockbuster" blood biomarkers provided negligible predictive improvement beyond clinical factors [16]. The fundamental explanation lay in correlation structures: metabolites associated with sarcopenia also correlate with age, body size, physical activity, and other conventional risk factors already captured in prediction models. This multicollinearity limited incremental value. For clinicians, the clinical utility of Kim et al’s findings may be limited due to modest predictive gains. However, their work illustrated promises of metabolomics in age-related conditions. The 38-metabolite signature provided hypotheses about biological mechanisms worthy of investigation, even if clinical predictive value remained modest and uncertain pending external validation. Kim and colleagues have delivered a rigorous investigation advancing understanding of metabolic factors associated with sarcopenia, while appropriately acknowledging limitations. Nevertheless, the identified metabolite patterns provided valuable clues warranting mechanistic investigation. Moving forward requires realistic expectations, methodological improvements addressing diagnostic and temporal measurement limitations, technological advances enabling comprehensive profiling, and multi-cohort validation. Only through such coordinated efforts will we translate metabolomic insights into interventions meaningfully improving outcomes for older adults.
