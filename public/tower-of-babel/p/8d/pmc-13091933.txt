Atypical retinitis pigmentosa associated with EYS variants
Authors: Normila Barthelemy, Spencer Barrett, Winston Lee, Jesse D Sengillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report an atypical phenotype of autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa (RP) associated with variants in the Eyes Shut Homolog (EYS) gene.MethodsCase reportResultsA 53-year-old woman with a prior history of HIV controlled on HAART therapy presented with complaints of long-standing decreased vision and nyctalopia in both eyes. Visual acuity was light perception (LP) and 20/40 in the right and left eye, respectively. Examination disclosed an unremarkable anterior segment of both eyes. Dilated fundus exam revealed clear vitreous media, optic nerve pallor, vascular attenuation, peripheral retinal atrophy, old laser scars, and rare pigment migration bilaterally. In the right eye there was an evident tractional membrane along both arcades and significant subretinal exudation. OCT imaging showed chorioretinal atrophy in both eyes, and significant vitreoretinal traction in the right eye. A hyperautofluorescent ring was noted on fundus autofluorescence (FAF) of the left eye. An inherited retinal dystrophy was suspected, prompting genetic testing to be performed. Panel based testing revealed multiple EYS variants, namely c.2470T > C, c.3250A > C, c.4402G > C, and c.3443+1G > T.ConclusionPhenotypic heterogeneity of EYS-associated RP can lead to a delay in diagnosis for atypical cases. A prior coats-like response may be considered in asymmetric cases exhibiting significant exudation or tractional bands.Keywords: Eyes shut homolog, Retinitis pigmentosa, Coats-like exudative vitreoretinopathy

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a progressive inherited retinal dystrophy, characterized by irreversible photoreceptor cell death. The worldwide prevalence of the non-syndromic form of RP is estimated to be around 1 in 4000 people.1 The inheritance pattern of RP includes autosomal dominant (ADRP), autosomal recessive (ARRP), X-linked (XLRP) and digenic forms.2 Amongst these different inheritance patterns, ADRP is generally least severe, with more variability between affected family members. X-linked RP is most severe, with earlier onset of symptoms during adolescence, faster progression and worse visual outcomes.3

Multiple genes are associated with RP, contributing to its clinical heterogeneity. The Eyes Shut Homolog (EYS) gene is strongly associated with ARRP, and is the most common cause of RP in Japanese populations.4 The EYS gene codes for proteins that help maintain the integrity of the photoreceptor cells by facilitating protein trafficking between the photoreceptor inner and outer segments. Truncating variants in the EYS gene can lead to near total loss of rod photoreceptors by middle age and midsize genomic rearrangement variants have been found to yield faster central cone loss.5,6

There are a few studies on the clinical course of EYS associated RP, however there can be significant variability in presentation which makes establishing a consistent genotype-phenotype association difficult. In this report, we present a case of ARRP associated with multiple EYS variants in which an unusual presentation led to significant delay in diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old African-Caribbean woman with a past medical history of controlled HIV on HAART therapy (undetectable viral load), presented to clinic with long-standing low vision in both eyes. At the time of this visit, the patient's HIV viral load was undetectable, with normal CD4 count. The patient's ocular history included cataract extraction with intraocular lens implantation in the right eye, and remote history of retinal photocoagulation in both eyes for an unclear indication.

On presentation, her best corrected visual acuity was light perception (LP) in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. Intraocular pressure was 17 in both eyes. Slit lamp examination revealed an unremarkable anterior segment, aside from a posterior chamber intraocular lens in the right eye. Fundus examination of the right eye revealed significant proliferative vitreoretinopathy with fibrotic bands along both arcades and temporal tractional retinal detachment with underlying exudation (Fig. 1A). The left eye revealed severe and diffuse chorioretinal atrophy with relative foveal sparing, sclerotic vessels, and old laser scars in the superior hemi-retina (Fig. 1B). Both retinas exhibited sparce pigment migration. On further evaluation, OCT revealed significant chorioretinal degeneration of the right macula with confirmed vitreoretinal traction along the arcades associated with a fibrous band (Fig. 1C). OCT of the left retina showed cystoid macular edema, a lamellar hole, and significant outer retinal atrophy of the temporal macula with relative foveal sparing (Fig. 1D). Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging of the left eye demonstrated a hyperautofluorescent ring within the macula and faint peripapillary sparing (Fig. 1F). Fluorescein angiography revealed no frank leakage from the vessels or disc.

At the time of presentation, prior fundus photos from 10 years prior were available for viewing but clinical documentation was not. When these images were captured, the patient did not have an HIV diagnosis, and a uveitis work up was negative. The right eye at that time revealed subretinal yellow exudation throughout the macula, mostly along the inferior arcade (Fig. 1G). Fundus examination of the left eye revealed vitreopapillary traction (Fig. 1H).

An inherited retinal dystrophy was suspected, and the patient underwent genetic testing through Invitae (CLIA ID: 05D2040778), using the Invitae Inherited Retinal Disorders Panel, which analyzes 330 genes associated with inherited retinal dystrophies through next-generation sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis. The genetic testing reported four variants in the EYS gene: c.2470T > C, c.3250A > C, c.4402G > C, and c.3443+1G > T. Based on ACMG criteria, EYS c.2470T > C and EYS c.3443+1G > T were classified as likely pathogenic, whereas EYS c.3250A > C and EYS c.4402G > C were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) with benign features, not causative of the patient's phenotype. In the pathogenic variant EYS c.3443+1G > T, the nucleotide change occurs at a highly conserved donor splice site in intron 22 of the EYS gene (Table 1). Family members were not available for variant testing and segregation analysis. However, gnomAD v2.1.1 co-occurrence analysis indicated that the c.2470T > C, c.3250A > C, and c.4402G > C variants are very likely to reside in cis (<1% estimated probability of occurring on different haplotypes), while c.3443+1G > T is statistically predicted to occur on the opposite allele. The EYS c.3250A > C and EYS c.4402G > C variants are considered benign variants based on population frequency and in silico evidence. Each showed an overall MAF of 0.008% with higher frequencies in individuals of African American ancestry (0.128% and 0.132%, respectively), which is above the threshold for a rare autosomal recessive disorder. In contrast, the c.2470T > C variant is exceedingly rare with an overall MAF of 0.004% and population-specific MAF of 0.06%. This variant was reported as a variant of unknown significance (VUS) by the laboratory but is predicted to be deleterious across multiple in silico prediction algorithms including CADD (PHRED-scaled score 24.4), REVEL (score = 0.75), AlphaMissense (score = 0.95), among others (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1). Thus, a pathogenic and likely pathogenic EYS variant with an exceedingly high probability of occurring in trans supports a suspected molecular diagnosis for the patient's phenotype.

In addition to the EYS variants, the three heterozygous variants in BEST1, DNAJC17, and IFT88 were reported by the testing laboratory. However, these were not considered causal nor contributory to the patient's phenotype. Specifically, each variant was classified as a VUS with insufficient evidence to support pathogenicity. The known disease mechanisms and phenotypic spectrum associated with these genes are also inconsistent with the patient's clinical presentation (Table 1).

Given the patient's medical history, a uveitis work-up was also pursued. Laboratory work-up was negative for rapid plasma reagin/fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption (RPR/FTA) and interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) for tuberculosis. Serum angiotensin converting Enzyme (ACE), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) panel, A1C, complete blood count (CBC) with differential, comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were all within normal limits.

Summary

In this report, we present the case of a 53-year-old woman with delayed diagnosis of RP due to an atypical presentation. Multi-modal imaging was consistent with RP with a prior coats-like response, and four variants were identified in EYS, a common cause of ARRP.

Discussion

Retinitis pigmentosa is a genetically heterogenous disorder caused by variants in over 100 genes. Autosomal recessive RP represents approximately 50–60% of cases of RP globally.1 ARRP, also represents 40% of RP cases without family history of RP.7 Variants in the EYS gene represent one of the most common genetic causes of ARRP worldwide. Its prevalence varies across populations, with a particularly high frequency in East Asian populations.8 A case series study of 245 patients of western European descent affected by autosomal recessive RP was performed by Littink et al. In this cohort, EYS accounted for approximately 5% of the cases and a founder variant was also identified in Western Netherlands.9 In addition, a molecular genetic screening study of EYS variants in Spain identified EYS pathogenic variants as the major genetic cause of 15.9 % RP cases in the Spanish population.10 In a retrospective genetic study of Swedish individuals affected with RP, EYS variants accounted for at least 16% of autosomal recessive RP.11

A retrospective study evaluating the phenotypic spectrum of EYS variants c.6416G > A and c.7228+1G > A in a Taiwanese cohort revealed that the location of the EYS variant may contribute to the severity of RP. Specifically, gene variants near the C-terminus led to more deleterious phenotypic effects than those near the N-terminus.12 In addition, a focused assessment of EYS c.6416G > A-associated RP in a Singaporean patient cohort revealed that the variant occurs in at least 11.3% of families with RP.13 In cases where the EYS c.6416G > A variant occurred in trans with another EYS c.8107G > T variant, sectoral retinal degeneration was observed. These findings have been corroborated in other Asian populations. A prospective study of a nationwide Chinese cohort revealed that patients who carried the EYS c.4957dupA and c.8805C > A variants had an earlier age of disease onset compared to the other EYS variants.8

Few US-based studies of EYS-RP have been published. One cross-sectional study found a unique FAF phenotype (crescent shaped hyperautofluorescent rings) to be associated with variants in the distal portion of the EYS protein.6 This cross-sectional study also identified a case of delayed diagnosis and treatment with intravitreal steroids due to a misdiagnosis of posterior uveitis in an EYS-RP patient with significant cystoid macular edema and a lack of pigment migration. Inherited retinal dystrophies often masquerade as uveitis due to significant overlap in exam and imaging features which include early cataract, pigmented cells in the vitreous, and cystoid macular edema. In the present case, a uveitis work-up was pursued while genetic testing results were pending as the patient had a history of HIV and a relatively atypical presentation. Her laboratory investigations were found to be negative. Imaging available from prior to her diagnosis of HIV also indicated an abnormal fundus at that time with subretinal exudation present in the right eye. The abnormal fundus findings, specifically subretinal exudation, were documented prior to patient's formal HIV diagnosis reducing the likelihood that HIV related retinopathy was the underlying etiology. The patient's HIV remained well controlled throughout the disease course. These factors helped to exclude any secondary exudative etiologies and support the diagnosis of RP with a coats-like phenotype. Multi-modal imaging was also helpful in arriving at the correct diagnosis in this case, particularly with the identification of a hyperfluorescent ring on fundus FAF imaging which is not expected in posterior uveitis. The hyperfluorescent ring has been reported in approximately 59-94% of patients diagnosed with RP, and is a relatively specific finding.14

Coats-like response in RP is characterized by unilateral or bilateral aneurysmal dilations, retinal telangiectasias, and exudative retinal detachment in the setting of a coexisting diagnosis of RP.15 It differs from coats disease, which is more often male predominant, sporadic, and without an underlying retinal dystrophy.16 The progression of Coats-like response in RP can have varied clinical findings depending on the disease stage. End-stage Coats-like response is marked by irreversible structural damage such as chronic retinal detachment, fibrosis, and retinal atrophy, which is distinct from early-stage localized exudation.17 These end-stage findings can obscure the typical features of RP, as in this case. Although the crescent-shaped hyperfluorescent ring phenotype has been associated with EYS-related RP, the Coats-like phenotype of the EYS variant is novel.

Although four EYS variants were identified in the patient, we were unable to perform segregation analysis due to loss to follow-up. This represents a limitation in interpretation regarding their pathogenicity within this case. However, based on population co-occurrence, we can infer with nearly 100% statistical confidence that c.3443+1G > T is predicted to occur on the allele opposite of the other three variants, c.2470T > C, c.3250A > C, and c.4402G > C. We further performed a more detailed, criterion-based re-evaluation of the three variants on the second allele using ACMG/AMP guidelines.18 Although the c.3250A > C and c.4402G > C variants are benign or of uncertain significance, we determined that c.2470T > C is likely pathogenic based on the combined evidence of PM1, PM2, PM3, PP3, and PP4 (Supplemental Table 1). The evidence for pathogenicity is based on its localization within the conserved EGF-like calcium-binding domain if EYS, exceedingly low minor allele frequency (<0.05%) in the general population, near consensus of deleterious computational predictions and specificity of the underlying RP phenotype in this patient. While additional evidence, such as identification of unrelated affected individuals harboring this variant or functional validation studies would further strengthen the interpretation, the current evidence supports a suspected molecular diagnosis in this patient.

Expedient diagnosis of atypical RP phenotypes is critical to prevent delayed diagnosis and non-indicated interventions. This is becoming increasingly important as more targeted gene therapies emerge. The current emerging gene therapies for RP are mostly tailored to specific genes.19 However, there is currently no treatment in clinical trials for EYS-RP.
