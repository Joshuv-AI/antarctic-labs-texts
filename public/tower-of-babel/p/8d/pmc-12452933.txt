ApoD mediates age-associated increase in vulnerability to influenza virus infection
Authors: Zhimin Jiang, Wenliang Pan, Yu Chen, Dongliang Zhou, Shuning Ren, Qi Tong, Litao Liu, Honglei Sun, Yipeng Sun, Yuhai Bi, Dayan Wang, Lu Lu, Juan Pu, Kin-Chow Chang, Jinhua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAging is a leading risk factor in influenza-related deaths. Additionally, the global population is aging at an unprecedented rate in human history, posing major issues for healthcare and the economy. Thus, there is an urgent need to understand the pathogenesis of severe influenza affecting older patients. Here, we found that highly elevated Apolipoprotein D (ApoD) expression with age drives extensive pulmonary mitophagy to attenuate type I interferon (IFN) response, leading to promotion of influenza virus replication and severe disease outcome. ApoD can therefore be a target for therapeutic intervention to protect against severe influenza virus infection in elderly populations.

The recent COVID-19 pandemic and yearly influenza season highlight the vulnerability of the elderly to respiratory viral infections (1). Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) patients over 65 y old were twice more likely to die than younger patients; patients over 80 were 20-fold higher than those in their 50 s to succumb to lethal infection (2). Seasonal influenza alone causes an estimated 300,000 to 650,000 global deaths each year, with 70 to 90% of these fatalities occurring in individuals who are 65 or older (3, 4). Notably, the global population is aging at an unprecedented rate. By 2050, the number of adults over 65 is projected to reach 1.6 billion, accounting for nearly 20% of total world population (5). This demographic shift will have significant health implications, one of which will be the need to provide better protection and treatment for the elderly against respiratory viruses. Consequently, there is a need to better understand the pathogenesis of severe respiratory viral infections in the elderly to be able to develop more effective prophylactic and therapeutic treatments.

Increased disease severity in elderly patients to influenza virus infection is largely attributed to dysregulation of pulmonary function and altered immune responses in the aging lung (6). In particular, impairment of innate immunity in the aging lung is a critical problem as the mucosal lining is the first line of defense against airborne viruses (1). A compromised innate immune response will negatively impact subsequent adaptive immune response (7, 8). For instance, aged dendritic cells (DCs) are found to exhibit defects in cytokine production, limiting the signaling activation of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and leading to poor antibody (Ab) response to influenza virus immunization (9). Additionally, aging disrupts RIG-I engagement in primary peripheral blood monocytes, leading to impaired type I interferon (IFN) response (10). Notably, most studies of immune aging are focused on circulating immunocytes (11–13), thus leaving a significant knowledge gap of age-related innate changes in the lung that could contribute to increased disease severity in the elderly to influenza virus infection.

Mitochondria are responsible for a wide range of cellular processes, including serving as signaling platforms in the regulation of the antiviral innate immune response (14). To maintain a healthy pool of cellular mitochondria, their biogenesis and degradation must be tightly regulated (15); a hallmark of cellular aging is that both processes are increasingly compromised with age. While macroautophagy decreases with age, increased levels of mitophagy have been observed in multiple organs in mice during physiological aging (16). In age-related lung fibrosis, mitophagy, however, is reduced in fibroblasts (17), whereas in other lung conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), mitophagy is enhanced with upregulation of PINK1 receptor expression (18). Collectively, age-related lung diseases could have distinct molecular drivers that regulate mitophagy. Influenza virus infection can impair host innate immune response by controlling mitophagy through different mechanisms (19–21). However, it remains unclear how influenza virus infection impacts on mitophagy in aged lungs or whether mitophagy worsens disease outcome in the elderly.

In the present study, we investigated the mechanisms behind increased severity of influenza virus infection with age using an aging-mouse model. We identified apolipoprotein D (ApoD), a glycosylated protein involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation, as an age-related molecular determinant that impairs the activation of innate immune antiviral response by triggering extensive mitophagy in the aged lung during influenza virus infection. A meta-analysis of age-related gene expression profiles using 27 datasets from mice, rats, and humans identified 56 genes consistently upregulated with age, with ApoD emerging as the most significant (22).

Levels of ApoD in the blood and lung of aged mice were significantly higher than those of younger mice. During influenza virus infection, elevated ApoD in senescent cells triggered extensive mitophagy through a PINK1-independent pathway, disrupting the activation of innate immune response. In contrast, an otherwise lethal dose of influenza virus in ApoD deficient mice resulted in improved survival, reduced viral load in the lung, and reduced pulmonary pathology; such clinical improvements were associated with reduced mitophagy and elevated type I IFN production. Removal of senescent cells in aged lung using the senolytic drug ABT-263 reduced ApoD levels, and alleviated pulmonary pathology in response to influenza virus infection. Together, we demonstrated that age-induced production of ApoD is a driver of severe influenza.

Results

Severe IAV Infection in Aged Mice Correlates with Elevated Mitophagy in Pulmonary Epithelial Cells.

We first examined senescence status in the lung of mice of contrasting age groups. Aged mice (21 mo old), relative to younger mice (3 mo old), exhibited significantly upregulated cellular senescence marker genes, including the cdkn1a (p21), γ-H2A.X and cdkn2a (p16) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), and increased in senescence-associated (SA) β-galactosidase activity (SA-β-gal) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Next, the morbidity and mortality from influenza virus infection (A/PR/8/34, H1N1) of aged and younger mice were ascertained. Consistent with previous findings (23), aged mice showed elevated viral load in the lung (Fig. 1A) and higher mortality (Fig. 1B) compared with young mice. With regard to body weight, weight loss peaked at 7 d post infection (dpi) in young mice and at 10 dpi in aged mice. Subsequent body weight increases in aged mice was much slower than in young mice. Furthermore, aged mice failed to recover their initial body weight at 22 dpi (Fig. 1C). Severe lung pathology was observed in aged mice at 4 dpi and 6 dpi (Fig. 1D). Additionally, infected aged mice exhibited attenuated messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of type I IFN and ISG15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), and elevated proinflammatory IL6 mRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F).

We then determined the effect of influenza virus infection on the mitophagy in aged mouse lungs by analyzing the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) level and mitochondrial protein expression. By reverse transcription-qPCR (RT-qPCR), mtDNA levels were significantly less in aged mice at 4 dpi and 6 dpi compared with younger mice (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence staining for ATP5B, a mitochondrial matrix protein, demonstrated that influenza virus infection reduced the ATP5B expression in young and aged lung tissues. However, reduction was greater in the aged lungs compared with corresponding young mice lung at 6 dpi, suggesting elevated mitophagy in older animals (Fig. 1F). Reduced detection by western blotting of TOMM20, an outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) protein, and HSP60, a mitochondrial matrix protein, and ATP5B at 4 dpi and 6 dpi (Fig. 1G), further indicated elevated mitophagy in infected lungs of aged mice relative to the younger group. No significant difference in the expression of these mitochondrial proteins was observed at 0 dpi (Fig. 1G), which suggests that basal mitophagy levels were similar between young and aged lungs.

An in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) was developed as a microscopic visualization technique for protein–protein interactions (24). By in situ PLA, we detected increased colocalization of mitochondria with endogenous LC3B at 4 dpi, as indicated by PLA puncta of LC3B with HSP60 (Fig. 1H), and of LC3B with TOMM20 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). To determine lung cell types that were involved in mitophagy and senescence during influenza infection, pulmonary immune cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and interstitial cells were sorted from single cell suspensions of aged lung tissue at 0 dpi and 4 dpi. Western blotting showed reduced ATP5B expression and elevated p16 (senescence marker) and viral PB2 expression in EpCam+ cells after infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). And p16 and EpCam were largely colocalized in the aged lung section at 4 dpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I). These results indicated that influenza virus replicated in aged epithelial cells to trigger strong mitophagy. When we used Mdivi-1, an inhibitor of mitochondrial fission (25), to treat aged mice, we found that IAV-induced mitophagy in aged lung tissues could be blocked by Mdivi-1, as assessed by mtDNA levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S1J). However, there were no significant differences observed for the expression of IFN-β and ISG15 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 K and L) and viral loads (SI Appendix, Fig. S1M) in Mdivi-1 treated aged and young lung tissues at 5 dpi. Together, these findings indicate that influenza virus infection in the aged lung induces elevated levels of mitophagy in pulmonary epithelial cells, attenuated innate antiviral immune response and elevated viral replication.

Next, cellular senescence in alveolar type II-like epithelial (A549) cells was induced with bleomycin (26). A549 cells exposed to 50 μg/mL of bleomycin for 72 h resulted in typical phenotypic senescence, characterized by increase in SA-β-gal activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), distinct enlarged morphology (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), and significantly elevated p16 mRNA expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). The bleomycin-induced A549 cells were used to mimic age-mediated senescence. Bleomycin-treated or control A549 cells were infected with influenza virus, and the results showed that bleomycin-induced cellular senescence further increased mitophagy during infection, as determined by elevated LC3B expression and degradation of the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) protein TIM23 and HSP60 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D), and by reduced immunofluorescence of ATP5B puncta (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Additionally, in situ PLA showed increased colocalization of LC3B and HSP60 in the bleomycin-induced A549 cells post infection, as determined by quantitative image analysis of PLA spots at 18 hpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). Western blotting indicated elevated detection of viral PB2 protein in bleomycin-induced A549 cells post infection. Mdivi-1 treatment reduced the difference of PB2 expression between control and bleomycin-induced senescent cells at 12 and 24 hpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). There were also no significant differences observed for the expression of IFN-β and ISG15 in Mdivi-1 treated bleomycin-induced and control A549 cells at 12 hpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 H and I). Notably, bleomycin-induced cellular senescence in IFNAR1-KO A549 cells did not increase viral replication (SI Appendix, Fig. S2J). Thus, these results reveal that increased viral replication in senescent cells is attributed to enhanced mitophagy-mediated attenuation of the innate antiviral immune response.

Age-Induced ApoD Coincides with Mitophagy during Influenza Virus Infection.

We hypothesized that an age-related host factor could be responsible for enhanced mitophagy in senescent cells during influenza virus infection. Recruitment of autophagosomal membrane-associated protein LC3 to mitochondria is a key step in driving mitophagy. To screen age-related regulators of mitophagy, we examined LC3B-interacting proteins by proximity-dependent biotin identification. LC3B was cloned into a mammalian expression vector tagged with a mitochondrial OMM-targeted APEX2 (mito-APEX) label for proximity-based identification of LC3B-interacting proteins localized or trafficked to mitochondria during influenza virus infection (Fig. 2A). Bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells, transfected with the mito-APEX-tagged LC3B plasmid for 18 h, revealed mitochondrial localization of LC3B (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Following 18 hpi and biotin affinity capture, biotinylated proteins were purified and analyzed by mass spectrometry to identify LC3B-interacting proteins (Fig. 2A). A total of 20 candidate proteins were identified as LC3B-binding partners (Fig. 2B). Further investigation into the expression of corresponding genes in bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells during infection identified ApoD as one of the most highly expressed candidates (Fig. 2C).

IAV infection increased the interaction between ApoD and LC3B in bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells, as indicated by biotinylation of ApoD in mito-APEX-LC3B system (Fig. 2D), and coimmunoprecipitation of ApoD with the mito-APEX-LC3B in the absence and presence of IAV infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). In addition, the AirID system, another proximity-based biotin labeling method, was used to examine protein interactions with ApoD which showed that IAV infection promoted the association of ApoD with mitochondria in bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). IAV infection also enhanced the colocalization of exogenous ApoD with TOMM20 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E) and of endogenous ApoD with LC3B (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F) in A549 cells, indicating that viral infection induces the mitochondrial localization of ApoD and its interaction with LC3B. To further confirm whether cellular senescence promoted the binding of ApoD to LC3B in mitochondria during IAV infection, in situ PLAs were conducted which indicated elevated number of ApoD and HSP60 puncta in bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells at 18 hpi relative to corresponding control cells (Fig. 2 E and F). Additionally, increased colocalization of endogenous ApoD and LC3B was observed in senescent cells after infection (Fig. 2 G and H). These findings demonstrate that the increased mitochondrial localization of ApoD facilitates its binding to LC3B in senescent cells, and promotion of mitophagy during IAV infection.

We next assessed ApoD expression in normal lung tissues of five elderly donors (>65 y) and five younger adults (<40 y) by immunohistochemical analysis which showed strong detection of ApoD in a large number of cells from older lungs relative to corresponding younger adult lungs (Fig. 2 I and J). Additionally, elderly patients with influenza exhibited significantly higher levels of ApoD in serum compared with younger patients (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, confocal fluorescence analysis of the human lung section revealed highly expression of ApoD in EpCam+ cells from an elderly donor when compared with the young donor (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Distinct colocalization of ApoD with surfactant protein-C (SFTPC) and less coexpression between ApoD and homeodomain-only protein homeobox (HOPX) was also observed in lung section from an elderly donor (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), which suggests that the pulmonary epithelial cells coexpressing ApoD were mainly alveolar type 2 epithelial cells. Similarly elevated expression of ApoD in the lungs and sera of aged mice (mock and infected) was detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–G). However, highly expressed ApoD induced by IAV in mouse lungs was not limited to bronchial epithelial cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). ApoD was also significantly induced in bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells at mock or infection condition when compared with that in control cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). Together, these results indicate that ApoD expression is elevated in the lungs and sera of aged human and mouse, and that ApoD localizes to mitochondria and interacts with autophagosome-associated protein LC3 during IAV infection.

ApoD Is Required for Influenza Virus-Induced Mitophagy.

Next, we found that use of ApoD−/− HEK293 cells significantly prevented IAV-induced degradation of mitochondrial proteins, as demonstrated by fewer ATP5B puncta detected through immunofluorescence (Fig. 3A), and by reduced IMM protein TIM23 degradation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Moreover, absence of ApoD abrogated virus-induced reduction of mtDNA levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Mitochondrial transmembrane potential was assessed using tetramethylrhodamine, methyl ester (TMRE) staining, with use of CCCP as a positive control for membrane potential reduction. At 24 hpi, significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential was observed in wild-type HEK293 cells, similar to CCCP treatment (Fig. 3B). However, ApoD deficiency restored mitochondrial membrane potential. Electron microscopy was used to visualize lysosomal mitochondrial degradation. In wild-type cells, mitochondria were enclosed by autophagosomes or autolysosomes postinfluenza virus infection or CCCP treatment. Conversely, in ApoD−/− HEK293 cells, numerous morphologically normal mitochondria were observed, indicating absence of significant mitochondrial degradation (Fig. 3C). Additionally, in Bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells depleted of ApoD expression by siRNA transfection, mitochondrial localization of LC3B and ApoD was significantly reduced, as measured by colocalization of endogenous LC3B or ApoD with HSP60 via in situ PLAs (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). These findings establish a critical role for ApoD in influenza virus-induced mitophagy.

The LC3B-Interacting Region (LIR) Domain of ApoD Is Essential for Interaction with LC3B and for Mitophagy.

We next assessed the functional importance of the LIR domain in ApoD in mitophagy. Amino acid sequence in ApoD harbors a highly conserved LIR motif, as predicted by the iLIR web server (27) (Fig. 4A). Comparative coimmunoprecipitation assays using WT ApoD and a panel of ApoD mutants at the LIR domain (as single mutation, double mutations, and complete deletion) demonstrated that the LIR domain is essential for binding to LC3B during IAV infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Single and double-amino acid substitutions in the LIR domain significantly reduced the degradation of mitochondrial HSP60 protein triggered by influenza virus infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B).

Next, we transfected separately WT ApoD and LIR-depleted ApoD mutant in ApoD−/− HEK293 cells followed by influenza virus (PR8) infection. Overexpression of WT ApoD effectively promoted the degradation of mitochondrial TOMM20 protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C) and significantly reduced detection of mtDNA (Fig. 4B), while the LIR-depleted ApoD mutant in ApoD−/− HEK293 cells did not promote mitochondrial degradation. Consistently, WT ApoD strongly colocalized with LC3B, indicating that the LIR motif is essential for its interaction during influenza virus infection (Fig. 4C); similar results were obtained by in situ PLAs (Fig. 4 D and E). Therefore, the LIR domain of ApoD is essential for binding to LC3B and plays a regulatory role in influenza virus-induced mitophagy.

ApoD Regulates Influenza Virus-Induced Mitophagy in a PINK1-Independent Manner.

PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy is a main pathway for eliminating damaged mitochondria, induced by various stimuli such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), CCCP, and infection. To determine whether ApoD-regulated mitophagy is dependent on the PINK1-Parkin pathway, expression of PINK1 and Parkin genes in PR8 virus-infected ApoD−/− HEK293 cells was assessed by RT-PCR. Parkin mRNA, but not PINK1 mRNA, was induced by influenza virus infection but this induction was unaffected by ApoD deficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). While CCCP treatment effectively induced PINK1-dependent mitophagy, it did not alter the expression of ApoD protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). ApoD overexpression in HEK293 cells did not promote CCCP-induced mitophagy (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). Although CCCP was previously shown to induce ubiquitination of mitochondria in Parkin overexpressing cells (28), we did not observe significant change in ubiquitination of mitochondria in the ApoD−/− HEK293 cells during influenza virus infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H), hence excluding the involvement of ubiquitination in the regulation underlying the functions. Additionally, PINK1 knockout did not affect the elevated mitophagy induced by ApoD overexpression in HEK293 cells (Fig. 4 F and G). ApoD overexpression had no impact on basal and starvation-induced levels of LC3B-II (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 I and J). However, ApoD overexpression in cells depleted of ATG5, an important factor in the formation of autophagy vesicles, failed to promote mitophagy during influenza virus infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6K). Collectively, our data suggest that ApoD is a binding partner of LC3 that regulates mitophagy induced by influenza virus independent of the PINK1 pathway.

ApoD Negatively Regulates the Innate Immune Response.

To determine the impact of elevated ApoD levels on influenza virus dynamics, we transfected ApoD-HA in HEK293 cells followed by PR8 virus infection. Overexpression of ApoD significantly increased virus titers at 12 and 24 hpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A) and enhanced the protein expression of viral NP and NS1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). In contrast, knocking down ApoD using siRNA resulted in significant decrease in virus titers in A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C) and viral proteins in HEK293 cells and A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D and E). We also used other influenza subtypes, avian H9N2, H5N1, and H7N9 viruses, to show that ApoD overexpression in HEK293 cells similarly increased protein expression of viral NP and NS1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F–H). To investigate the role of endogenous ApoD during IAV infection, we infected ApoD+/+ and ApoD−/− HEK293 cells with PR8 virus. Infected ApoD−/− HEK293 cells produced significantly lower virus titers at 12 and 24 hpi (Fig. 5A) and exhibited reduced protein levels of viral NP and PB2 at 6, 12, and 24 hpi (Fig. 5B). Additionally, influenza virus entry, as assessed by GFP detection at 18 hpi using recombinant PR8 expressing GFP (PR8-GFP), was reduced in ApoD−/− HEK293 cells as determined by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence (SI Appendix, Figs. S7I and S8A). Moreover, mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 blocked ApoD-mediated promotion of viral replication (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). A similar finding was made with ATG5 KO HEK293 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D).

Given that ApoD facilitated virus replication in the promotion of mitophagy, we investigated its impact on innate immune response. Overexpression of ApoD significantly reduced PR8-virus-induced mRNA expression of IFN-β, IFN-α, and ISG15 at 12 hpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 E–G). Conversely, ApoD deficiency resulted in substantial increase in PR8-virus-induced expression of IFN-β, IFN-α, and ISG15 at 12 hpi in A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 H–J). Furthermore, ApoD deficiency increased PR8-virus-induced IFN-β protein production in a time-dependent manner at 12, 18, 24, and 36 hpi (Fig. 5C). Consistent with such findings, ApoD deficiency in infected cells was associated with increased detection of phosphorylated IRF3 compared with the ApoD+/+ group (Fig. 5 D and E). Overexpression of ApoD failed to increase the viral protein expression in IFNAR1−/− A549 cells, indicating that ApoD facilitates virus replication by attenuating innate immune response (SI Appendix, Fig. S8K). Thus, ApoD serves as a potent negative regulator of type I IFN response to favor influenza virus replication.

Next, the impact of elevated ApoD expression in bleomycin-induced senescent cells on type I IFN response was assessed during influenza virus infection. Knockdown of highly expressed ApoD in senescent A549 cells by siRNA transfection (SI Appendix, Fig. S8L) significantly reduced progeny virus titers at 24 hpi (Fig. 5F). Notably, the knockdown of ApoD restored type I IFN response, as determined by RT-qPCR, with increased expression of IFN-β, IFN-α, and ISG15 mRNA (Fig. 5 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S8M). Additionally, reduced ApoD expression was associated with decreased expression of IL-6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8N). Taken together, ApoD functions as a negative regulator of type I IFN response during influenza virus infection.

ApoD Deficiency Protects Older Mice from Severe Influenza.

Our findings so far indicate that elevated ApoD expression is associated with severe influenza virus infection in elderly humans and aged mice. An ApoD-deficient (ApoD−/−) mouse model was used to investigate the in vivo role of ApoD in influenza virus infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–C). ApoD deficiency did not change the basal senescence state of the aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). ApoD−/− and WT young and aged mice were each intranasally (i.n.) infected with a severe dose of PR8 virus. Virus infection significantly induced ApoD expression in WT aged mice compared with young mice (Fig. 6A). All infected young ApoD−/− mice survived; 60% of infected young WT mice survived (Fig. 6B). Infected young ApoD−/− mice showed no significant body weight loss compared with WT counterparts (Fig. 6C).

While ~70% of ApoD−/− aged mice survived, all WT aged mice succumbed to infection by 8 dpi (Fig. 6B). WT aged mice showed significantly greater weight loss compared with ApoD−/− aged mice at 7 and 8 dpi (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C). Virus load in the lungs of ApoD−/− aged mice at 5 dpi was significantly less than that of corresponding WT mice (Fig. 6D). Immuno-detection of viral NP further confirmed higher progeny virus load in WT aged animals compared with ApoD−/− aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). There was no notable difference in virus distribution at the bronchial and alveolar epithelia between ApoD−/− and WT mice regardless of age. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining for viral NP and ApoD protein in lung sections of aged and young WT mice showed spatial coincidence of influenza virus with ApoD protein (Fig. 6E).

Gross pathology and histopathology of PR8-infected WT aged mice (relative to younger mice) showed more severe lung damage at 5 dpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S9F), characterized by acute necrotizing bronchitis, disruption of the respiratory epithelium, multifocal lymphocytic infiltration of the interstitium, vasculitis, and areas of high-protein edema suggestive of viral pneumonia (Fig. 6 F and G). In ApoD−/− aged mice, however, pulmonary pathology was less severe (Fig. 6 F and G). Young ApoD−/− and WT mice showed comparably mild pathological changes 5 dpi. The lung of PR8-infected ApoD−/− aged mice showed significant reduction in mitophagy relative to corresponding WT mice at 5 dpi as determined by the colocalization of LC3B and HSP60 on the lung sections via PLA (Fig. 6 H and I) and western blotting of mitochondrial HSP60 and ATP5B proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 G and H). Impaired type I IFN response in WT aged mice was restored in ApoD−/− aged mice during virus infection (Fig. 6J and SI Appendix, Fig. S9I). Moreover, ApoD deficiency in aged mice also significantly elevated IFN-β and ISG15 expression at 4 d post poly(I:C) stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 J–L), indicating that ApoD is acting on innate immune repression. Overall, our data indicate that ApoD is a driver of severe influenza disease in aged mice.

ABT-263 Lowers ApoD Expression and Reduces Disease Severity in Aged Mice.

Senolytics represent an emerging class of drugs that can selectively eliminate senescent cells. ABT-263, a potent senolytic that targets antiapoptotic pathways to deplete senescent cells, has recently shown to have therapeutic potential against SARS-CoV-2 infection in aged hamsters (29). ABT-263 administration for seven consecutive days reduced p16-positive cells in lung tissues from aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). Notably, ABT-263 treatment significantly reduced ApoD expression in uninfected aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 C and D). Influenza virus infection can induce cellular senescence in young animals (30). Intriguingly, influenza infection-induced lung cell senescence was exacerbated in aged mice compared with their young counterparts, as assessed by p16 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F).

ABT-263 was administered to young and aged mice via oral gavage for seven consecutive days before influenza infection and then daily until they were killed. Treatment with ABT-263 significantly reduced the p16-positive cells in lung tissues of aged mice at 5 dpi (Fig. 7A). In contrast, ABT-263 treatment failed to decrease the frequency of p16-positive cells in infected young mice at 5 dpi (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). Furthermore, ABT-263 treatment reduced expression of ApoD in infected, aged lungs at 5 dpi (Fig. 7B). Five out of six ABT-263-treated infected mice survived, while all but one aged mouse in the DMSO control group died (Fig. 7C). Aged control mice significantly lost more body weight compared with ABT-263-treated mice (P < 0.05 on days 4, 5, and 7) (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). Virus load in the lungs of ABT-263-treated aged mice was significantly reduced at 5 dpi (Fig. 7D). Immunohistochemical detection of viral NP was noticeably stronger in lung sections of control aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). Infected lungs from ABT-263-treated aged mice showed reduced pulmonary pathology relative to control aged mice (Fig. 7E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). However, ABT-263 treatment was less effective in ApoD−/− aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E) or young mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 F–H) at reducing viral loads in lungs and pulmonary pathology. The less severe infection outcome in ABT-263-treated aged mice was associated with elevated type I IFN response (Fig. 7F), attributed to the inhibition of mitophagy induced by influenza virus infection (Fig. 7G and SI Appendix, Fig. S11I). In summary, depletion of senescent cells by ABT-263 in aged mice lowers ApoD levels and restores innate immune antiviral response, thereby limiting influenza virus propagation and associated pulmonary damage.

Discussion

Until now, the contribution of pulmonary innate immunity to rising severity of IAV infection with age was poorly understood. In the present study, we found that IAV infection in the aged lung strongly induced mitophagy that results in severe or even fatal disease outcome. We identified ApoD, an LC3-interacting protein, as a critical factor responsible for driving mitophagy during infection. ApoD-induced mitophagy in aged lung impaired type I IFN innate immune response and facilitated virus replication. ApoD–/– aged mice showed reduced mitophagy and were protected from otherwise lethal IAV challenge; these mice exhibited reduced mortality, less body weight loss, reduced virus load in the lung, and reduced pulmonary damage, all of which were linked to restored type I IFN response. The successful oral use of a senolytic compound ABT-263 in aged mice in lowering ApoD level, and limiting virus propagation and associated pulmonary damage demonstrated the preventive and therapeutic potential of targeting ApoD in reducing severity of IAV infection in elderly patients.

Elevated expression of ApoD has been described in age-related neurodegenerative diseases and certain cancers. Microglial ApoD-induced NLRC4 inflammasome activation has been found to promote Alzheimer’s disease progression (31). Cancer patients with enhanced ApoD expression are associated with unfavorable prognosis and diminished response to immunotherapy (32, 33). There are few studies reported on the effects of ApoD on viral infection. In Bombyx mori (a silk moth), ApoD was identified as a proviral factor that facilitates BmNPV proliferation (34). ApoD has been found to facilitate rabies virus replication in C8-D1A cells (35). Human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV-OC43) infection upregulates ApoD which may protect against HCoV-OC43-induced encephalitis (36). A cross-linking mass spectrometry screen uncovered interactions between ApoD and SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein; however, ApoD overexpression in HEK293-ACE2 cells did not appear to affect virus replication (37). Our data showed higher influenza virus load in lungs of aged mice compared with younger controls, but this difference was not observed between ApoD−/− aged and ApoD−/− younger mice, suggesting that ApoD plays a specific role in driving severe IAV infection in aged animals.

Several viral and host factors have been identified as potential receptors that interact with LC3B to drive mitophagy, e.g., viral PB1-F2 and host RIPK2. PB1-F2 is able to translocate into mitochondria via Tom40 channels and impair innate immunity (38). PB1-F2 is also able to interact with TUFM and MAP1LC3B/LC3B to trigger mitophagy (20). NS1 of influenza virus in association with host ULK1 and BNIP3 can enhance mitophagy (39). Recently, viral NP protein was determined as being able to enhance mitophagy by interacting with MAVS and cargo receptor TOLLIP (19). The above cited viral factors appear to mediate mitophagy in a generic manner during influenza virus infection. Host RIPK2 can enhance the mitophagy during influenza virus infection (40) which involves the removal of damaged mitochondria and reduction of inflammation to protect the infected host. As far as we are aware, there is no notable discovery of host factor-driven mitophagy in senescent/aged cells triggered by influenza virus infection that disadvantaged the host. In this study, we established ApoD as a crucial host factor that, during influenza virus infection, triggers heightened mitophagy and subsequently dampens the innate antiviral immune response within aged lung epithelial cells. This finding offers fresh perspectives on the pathogenesis of influenza severity in elderly individuals.

Reducing senescence burden through treatment with senolytics or the use of genetically modified animal models that eliminates senescent cell populations has offered encouraging results in the mitigation of age-associated disease (41). Several recent reports suggested that senescent cell removal in aged animals by senolytic treatment or genetic manipulation significantly decreased disease severity during COVID-19 (29), enhanced influenza virus clearance (42) and ameliorated cellular immune responses upon influenza infection (43, 44). Senolytic ABT-263 was found to reduce virus load and ameliorate acute and long-term pulmonary disease in aged hamsters from SARS-CoV-2 infection (29). ABT-263 treatment not only depleted resident senescent cells in the aged lung but lowered the expression of SARS-CoV-2 receptor ACE2, thus reducing the viral attachment and entry (29). However, when we used ABT-263 to treat influenza virus infection, we demonstrated a distinct mechanism: it downregulated ApoD expression to inhibit IAV-induced mitophagy, thereby enhancing the antiviral innate immune response. Here, we need to indicate a recent report showing that ABT-263 treatments in aged female C57BL/6 mice (18 to 19 mo old) post IAV infection has less effect (45). In that report, the authors did not observe a reduction in cellular senescence markers following senolytic treatment. It might be due to the relatively young status of their aged mice and short-term use of senolytics for 6 d. The short-term application post infection and use of a heterogeneous age range of aged animals in their protocol may have masked potential benefits of ABT-263 therapy. In our study, ABT-263 was administered to aged mice (21 mo old) for seven consecutive days before influenza infection and then daily until euthanization. Treatment with ABT-263 reduced the frequency of p16-positive cells and lowered the ApoD expression in aged mice, thereby reducing viral load in the aged lung. Moreover, improved outcomes by ABT-263 treatment were not observed in aged ApoD−/− mice, indicating that ApoD is a major harmful factor driving the severe influenza virus infection in the elderly. Thus, the impact of senolytics on ApoD expression is crucial for assessing their potential to improve outcomes of influenza virus infections.

Accumulation of senescence cells has been found in chronic diseases, such as obesity, diabetes, and pulmonary disorders (46, 47). Some studies reported elevated ApoD expression in obese women (48) and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (49). Such individuals are also highly vulnerable to IAV infection (50) and SARS-CoV-2 infection (51). One mechanism behind such vulnerability could be associated with high ApoD expression. Our results indicate that pretreatment with ABT263 before infection can effectively eliminate senescent cells and thereby reduce ApoD expression. Therefore, preventive deletion of senescent cells by taking natural senolytics (for example flavonoid) might ameliorate influenza disease outcomes. Therapeutic strategies, including treatment with drugs against ApoD and reducing IAV-induced mitophagy by its inhibitors, may protect older individuals from the greater risk of influenza virus infection. Therefore, efforts should be made to translate these findings into clinical trials to assess the safety and effectiveness of senolytic compounds, mitophagy inhibitors, or ApoD-modulating drugs in elderly patients with viral pneumonia.

The current research has certain limitations. Aged mouse data should be interpreted with some caution as the mouse does not fully replicate all features of severe influenza infection of elderly humans. Aged animal models incorporating comorbidities like obesity and chronic cigarette smoke exposure, common in aged populations, may yield distinct results, better recapitulating clinically relevant senescent phenotypes. More extensive studies are also needed to assess the potential impact of host factor ApoD on the pathogenicity of influenza virus infection across the age range. Another limitation related to the use of bleomycin-induced senescent A549 cells is that this model of senescence cannot fully represent naturally occurring (age-mediated) senescence. Additionally, a key issue to be addressed in future research is on the mechanism responsible for mitochondrial translocation of ApoD during influenza virus infection.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that aging-associated induction of ApoD found in senescent pulmonary epithelial cells is responsible for the impairment of innate immune response to IAV infection through ApoD-mediated induction of mitophagy. Therefore, ApoD is a causal risk factor for heightened vulnerability of the elderly to IAV infections making ApoD a promising target for therapeutic inhibition to reduce infection severity.
