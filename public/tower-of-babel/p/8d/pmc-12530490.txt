Effectiveness and impact of 7 and 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines in Chinese children: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ran Peng, Tao Zhang, Eileen M. Dunne, Zhengyun Xu, Anli Sun, Jingyuan Cheng, Junfeng Yang, Genming Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) are highly effective against pneumococcal diseases, yet coverage in China remains low. We conducted a systematic review of observational studies published between 2006 and 2024 in mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan to evaluate the effectiveness and impact of PCV7 and PCV13. Twenty-seven studies were identified, of which 17 were included in meta-analyses. PCVs showed strong protection against invasive pneumococcal disease (pooled effectiveness: 88.8%, 95%CI: 80.7–96.8%) and significantly reduced the incidence of community-acquired pneumonia (14.3%, 95%CI: 10.1–18.5%). Vaccine-serotype CAP was reduced by 69.9% (95%CI: 60.0–79.7%), and moderate benefits were observed against severe pneumonia, mortality, and recurrent episodes. Evidence for otitis media remained limited. These findings highlight the substantial health benefits of PCVs in Chinese children and underscore the importance of expanding vaccine utilization to reduce the burden of pneumococcal disease.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a common cause of pneumonia, bacteremia, meningitis, and other infections such as otitis media in children worldwide. In China, an estimated 218,200 severe pneumococcal cases and 8,000 pneumococcal deaths occurred in children aged 1–59 months in 2017.1

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) have been proven to be highly safe and effective against pneumococcal diseases and are recommended by the World Health Organization for routine immunization of children.2 PCV7 was first introduced in Taiwan in 2005, replaced with PCV13 in 2011 and incorporated into Taiwan’s national immunization program in 2015. By 2017, the PCV13 vaccination coverage rate among children aged 1–5 years in Taiwan had reached 95.8%.3 Meanwhile, Hong Kong initiated a free-of-charge PCV7 vaccination program for children under 2 years of age in September 2009. PCV10 was introduced in October 2010 and subsequently replaced by PCV13 in December 2011.4 In mainland China, PCV7 (Prevnar, Wyeth Pharmaceuticals) was introduced in 2008, and PCV13 (Prevnar 13, Pfizer Inc.) was introduced in 2016.5 Two locally produced PCV13 became available in mainland China in 2020 and 2021, respectively. However, to date, PCV13 has not been included in the national immunization program in mainland China. Since PCVs were out-of-pocket vaccines in mainland China, the coverage rate of PCV13 remains significantly lower than that in other countries, with an uneven distribution.6,7 The results of a cross-sectional study across 10 provinces in China in 2019 showed that the one-dose coverage rate of PCV13 was 7.7% (95%CI: 6.9–8.4) and the three-dose coverage rate was 5.1% (95%CI: 4.5–5.8).6 As of 2022, the three-dose coverage rate of PCV13 in Shanghai was 18.9% (95%CI: 15.2–22.6), while the three-dose coverage rate of PCV13 in Jilin Province was only 0.8% (95%CI: 0–1.5).7 Systematically evaluating the effectiveness and impact of PCVs among Chinese children could provide evidence to support the expansion of vaccination coverage and for the inclusion of PCV13 in the national immunization program.

Given that PCV7 and PCV13 have been available on the private market for many years, we now have the opportunity to assess the effectiveness of these vaccines among Chinese children, in order to guide health policy discussions on pneumococcal immunization programs for children. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of literature published between January 1, 2006, and October 1, 2024, focusing on the vaccine effectiveness (VE) and impact of PCVs in children aged 0–5 years in China (including mainland, Taiwan, and Hong Kong).

Methods

Search strategy

We preregistered this project in PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD420251081325). We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and the Chinese databases China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, and China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP) for relevant studies published between January 2006 and October 2024. Referring to the search strategy for assessing the effectiveness of pneumococcal vaccines in adults,8 we developed a search strategy specifically for evaluating the effectiveness of PCVs in Chinese children (Supplementary Table S1). In brief, search strategies included the following terms or their synonyms: Streptococcus pneumoniae, PCV7 or PCV13, effectiveness, China or Chinese. For literature without an available electronic version, physical copies were obtained through Fudan University and the Biomedical Joint Library.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In accordance with the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes and Study Design) framework, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Studies that were conducted among Chinese populations (including mainland, Taiwan, and Hong Kong). (2) Studies involving children aged <18 years. (3) Studies that evaluated the health effects of PCV7 or PCV13 vaccination alone. (4) Studies must report at least one of the following clinical outcomes: invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD), community-acquired-pneumonia (CAP, including all-cause CAP, pneumococcal CAP, or vaccine-type CAP), carriage, relevant pneumococcal diseases such as otitis media.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) Studies targeting immunocompromised populations. (2) Studies assessing the combined health effects of PCVs with other vaccines (e.g., influenza vaccines). (3) Case reports, case series, animal experiments, modeling studies, public health economic evaluations, and narrative reviews were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The titles and abstracts of the retrieved records were screened by two reviewers. Duplicates, irrelevant articles or those with incomplete information were removed. Next, full texts were reviewed according to the eligibility criteria by two reviewers independently. Discrepancies in study selection were resolved by discussion.

Data of the included studies were extracted by one reviewer and checked by the other. The following information were extracted from each selected paper: title, authors, publication year, source, study region, age group, evaluated vaccines, control group, vaccination schedule (2 + 1 or 3 + 1), study period, sample size, study design, primary outcomes of interest, and VE or impact. VE is typically defined as the measurement of protection based on a comparison of disease outcome in vaccinated vs unvaccinated children. For some studies, VE was calculated from available data (for example VE= [1 – relative risk] × 100) if not reported in the original study.

For terminological clarity, our study exclusively reserves “impact” to denote population-level effects of vaccination programs (i.e., overall program effectiveness). Studies measuring such impacts will undergo qualitative synthesis but were excluded from meta-analyses, unless otherwise noted.

The quality of included studies was assessed by Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)9 or Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Cross-Sectional Study Appraisal Tool.10 NOS were used for Cohort and case–control studies and AHRQ for cross-sectional and surveillance studies.

Data analysis

Studies that reported vaccine effectiveness were included in the meta-analysis. Vaccine effectiveness was reported by stratifying by study outcomes, vaccine type, study design, NOS Scores and study sample size. We categorized the studies with NOS scores ≥7 as high-quality evidence and those with NOS scores <4 as low-quality evidence. Since NOS does not account for sample size, to enhance meta-analysis reliability, we incorporated sample size evaluation into quality assessment, addressing statistical power and result stability within the NOS framework. The minimum required sample size was determined using an iterative approach to ensure statistical validity. This involved using meta-analysis to derive effect size estimates and extract incidence data from eligible studies.

The I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among studies. When I2 ≤ 50%, heterogeneity was considered acceptable, and a fixed-effect model was applied. When I2 > 50%, heterogeneity was considered significant, prompting subgroup analysis. If heterogeneity persisted, a random-effects model was used.

Funnel plots, Egger’s test,11 and Begg’s test12 were used to evaluate publication bias. The recommended threshold for assessing publication bias is P ≤ .1, which is considered statistically significant.13 All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,836 records were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 1,256 records were screened by titles and abstracts. Of these, 55 abstracts were selected, leading to the review of 52 full-text articles. Following thorough evaluation, 27 studies were included in the qualitative analysis, with 17 further included in the meta-analysis (Figure 1). Across all studies, the most common evaluated outcomes were CAP, with 14 studies reporting all-cause CAP, and 12 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Five studies reported on all-cause CAP hospitalizations, and two reported vaccine-type CAP (VT-CAP). Five studies evaluated the VE or impact against vaccine-type IPD, and three were included for meta-analysis. Other included studies evaluated outcomes such as pneumococcal CAP, severe pneumonia, secondary CAP incidence, CAP case fatality rate, nasopharyngeal carriage, otitis media and tympanostomy tube insertion rates for otitis media as well. The quality assessment results – NOS scores and AHRQ – are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

IPD

Five studies reported the protective effect and impact of PCVs on IPD. Among them, one study was carried out in mainland China, and the other four studies were conducted in Taiwan China. Three studies were included in the meta-analysis (Figure 2). Two were case–control studies that compared the differences of IPD cases between vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals. Su et al.16 reported a VE of 80% against IPD for children who were age-appropriately vaccinated with PCV13 and Liu et al.18 reported VE of 96% against vaccine-type IPD for children who received ≥2 doses of PCV13. Chang et al.14 reported IPD incidence rates in hospitalized children with CAP during three distinct periods: the non-Immunization Program (non-IP) period, the catch-up vaccination period, and the complete vaccination period. Assuming that all children in the complete vaccination period would have received PCV13, VE of 96% was calculated by comparing differences in IPD incidence in the non-IP cohort with the complete vaccination cohort. The pooled VE against IPD was 88.8% (95% CI: 80.7%–96.8%). Figure 2.Meta-analysis of PCV13 vaccine effectiveness against IPD.

Meta-analysis of PCV13 vaccine effectiveness against IPD.

A surveillance study showed that during the period of private-market use of PCV7 and PCV13, the IPD incidence caused by 19A in Taiwan increased in children aged 0–5 years.15 After implementing the PCV13 catch-up vaccination program, 19A IPD incidence decreased significantly, particularly in children aged 2–5 years, where the decline was up to 53%. A case–control study showed that when children were age-appropriately vaccinated with PCV13, the VE for 19A-specific IPD disease was 89.0% (95% CI: 72.0%–96.0%).16

Two pre-post studies estimated PCV impact on IPD and were not included in the meta-analysis. Based on Taiwan CDC surveillance data between 2008–2010 and 2013,15 the IPD incidence decreased from 16.8 to 11.9 cases per 100,000 person-years in children aged 2–5 years, the overall IPD incidence among children under 2 years of age decreased from 12.9 to 10.2 cases per 100,000 person-years. To investigate the impact of Taiwan’s PCV13 catch-up vaccination program (March 2013) and its subsequent inclusion in the Taiwan’s immunization program (January 2015), Chen et al.17 analyzed clinical isolates of S pneumoniae from pediatric patients (≤18 years) between 2012 and 2016 and found that the IPD incidence per 10,000 hospital admissions decreased from 9.9 cases in 2012 to 2.3 cases in 2015 (P = .0014), but increased to 5.0 cases in 2016 (P = .2104), indicating a fluctuating trend.

All-cause CAP

A total of 14 studies reported the VE against all-cause CAP. Two studies (Lin et al.26 and Yu et al.4) employed a pre-post incidence design and were not included the meta-analysis. The pooled VE of PCV7 was marginally higher than that of PCV13 (P = .18) (Figure 3). Due to the high degree of heterogeneity observed across studies, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on NOS score to further investigate the sources of variability (Figure 4). Figure 4.Subgroup analysis of vaccine effectiveness of PCV13 against all-cause community acquired pneumonia.Notes: Studies included in meta-analysis met a minimum sample size criteria and have a Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) score ≥6, indicating low risk of bias.

Meta-analysis of vaccine effectiveness against all-cause community-acquired pneumonia, stratified by vaccine (PCV13 and PCV7).

Subgroup analysis of vaccine effectiveness of PCV13 against all-cause community acquired pneumonia.

The subgroup analysis was based on studies with an NOS score ≥4 and minimum required sample sizes. The minimum required sample size was calculated based on data from a nationwide population-based study conducted in 2020. In this study, the all-cause CAP incidence in children of <5y was 65.80 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 62.52–69.08).40 With a relative risk (RR) of 0.7, the minimum sample size was determined to be 5,600. Four cohort studies whose sample size met the criteria were included in the subgroup analysis. Based on a fixed-effects model, the pooled VE against all cause CAP was estimated at 14.3% (95% CI: 10.1%–18.5%) (Figure 4). Egger’s test (P = .1793) and Begg’s test (P = .3082) both yielded P-values greater than 0.1, indicating no significant publication bias (Supplemental Figure 1).

Among the 14 included studies, 12 studies defined PCV vaccination as a full schedule, and 2 studies included VE of incomplete immunization. A study from Suzhou showed that VE against CAP for at least one dose of PCV13 was 13.1% (95% CI: 6.0%–20.0%), and that for at least 3 doses was 22.2% (95% CI: 14.0%–30.0%).20 Similarly, a study from Shanghai showed that VE against CAP in children under 5 years old who received at least 3 doses of PCV13 was 18.0% (95% CI: 2.0–31.0), and 16.0% (95% CI: −41.0%–49.0%) in those with partial vaccination.25 As the population with incomplete vaccination was small, confidence intervals were wide, suggesting that the reliability of these results requires further validation.

The two pre-post studies assessed the impact of PCVs against CAP. One hospital-based surveillance study26 assessed the change in non-bacteremic pneumonia incidence from May 2004 to August 2006. The overall incidence decreased by 29.9% (95%CI: 0.3%–59.3%, P = .048), but no significant change was observed in patients under 5 years (reduction of 3.5%; 95% CI: −42.3%–49.4%, P = .97). The other study4 was a population-based interrupted time-series analysis from Hong Kong where PCV was linked to a marginal decline in all-cause pneumonia hospitalizations in the overall population (incidence rate ratio [IRR]: 0.9965, 95% CI: 0.9932–0.9998) and in the elderly (≥65 years, IRR: 0.9928, 95% CI: 0.9904–0.9953), with no significant impact on younger age groups.

CAP hospitalization

A total of 5 studies reported the protective effect of PCVs on all-cause CAP hospitalizations, four were cohort studies in children aged <5 years, while one21 was a cross-sectional study in children under 2 years. Among these, the study from Beijing32 reported the VE of receiving at least one dose of PCV13 at 12, 18, 24, and 30 months, and all four outcomes were included in the meta-analysis, along with the other 4 studies (Figure 5). The pooled VE against hospitalized all-cause CAP was 41.8% (95%CI: 22.2%–61.4%) (Figure 5). However, there was substantial heterogeneity among the studies, and thus subgroup analysis was conducted based on NOS scores. The study by Peng et al.21 was assessed using the AHRQ scale and was categorized as low quality due to insufficient sample size, less rigorous study design, and inadequate control of confounding factors. High-quality studies (NOS ≥ 7) yielded a pooled VE of 25.5% (95% CI: 17.3%–33.7%), which was significantly lower than that of low-quality studies (83.4%; 95% CI: 75.5%–91.2%) (P < .001) (Figure 6). Repeated CAP incidence and case-fatality rates

A cohort study from Suzhou showed the VE of PCV13 against repeated CAP incidence in children was 37.5% (95% CI: 15.3%–54.0%).20 Another cohort study from Taiwan indicated the CAP related case-fatality rate decreased from 3.2% in the non-immunization program cohort to 0.3% in the catch-up cohort and 0.8% in the full immunization program cohort (both with P < .001). In comparison with the non-immunization program cohort, the reduction in CAP related case-fatality rate was up to 75% among children aged 3 months to 17 years in the full immunization program cohort.14

VT-CAP

Two studies from Suzhou reported the VE of PCV13 against VT-CAP, as shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Liu et al. 202433 conducted VE analyses using two different control groups, in one, controls were children who were positive for non-PCV13-type S. pneumoniae (VE = 50.0%, 95% CI: 15.0%–70.7%), and in the other controls were children who tested negative for any S. pneumoniae (VE = 74.4%, 95%CI: 60.7%–83.3%). VT-CAP data were derived from a cohort and a case–control study with high methodological quality and reliability, yielding an estimate VE of 69.9% (95% CI: 60.0%–79.7%), with an acceptable level of heterogeneity (I2 = 30.47%).

Pneumococcal CAP (P-CAP)

A hospital-based surveillance study by Chen et al.17 in Taiwan showed the incidence of culture-confirmed pneumococcal disease per 10,000 hospital admissions declined from 71.7 cases in 2012 to 27.0 cases in 2016 (P < .0001). The estimated VE of PCV13 was approximately 62.5%. An interrupted time-series analysis34 using health insurance database in Taiwan demonstrated a significant decline in P-CAP hospitalization rates following the introduction of PCV13. Among children under 1 year of age and those aged 2–5 years, the incidence of P-CAP decreased by 59% and 47%, respectively. Additionally, among children aged 2–5 years, reductions were observed in intensive care unit (ICU) admission rates (from 6.8% to 3.9%), severe pneumonia cases (from 21.7 to 14.5 episodes per 100,000 child-years), and the need for invasive procedures (from 4.3% to 2.0%) (all P < .0001).

Nasopharyngeal carriage

Three studies evaluated the effect of the PCV7 on nasopharyngeal carriage among children aged 0–6 years. A cohort study in Beijing found that the PCV7 had no significant protective effect against overall pneumococcal nasopharyngeal carriage (VE: 6.2%; 95% CI: −29.3% to 31.9%).35 A cross-sectional study from Taiwan reported that during the period from July 2005 to December 2010, the carriage rate of PCV7 VT pneumococci among all recruited children significantly declined year by year (P < .001), as the PCV7 vaccination rate rose from 0.2% in 2005 to 45.5% in 2010.36 However, the carriage rates of non-PCV7 serotypes 15B and 19A significantly increased over the same period. The overall pneumococcal carriage rate was lower in the PCV7-vaccinated group compared to the unvaccinated group (8.9% vs. 13.6%, P < .001),36 with an unadjusted point estimate of VE of 34.6%. A cross-sectional study in Hong Kong covering the years 1999 to 2010 showed that the carriage rate of PCV7 serotypes among children (0–6 years) attending daycare centers or kindergardens decreased from 12.8% in 1999–2000 to 8.6% in 2009–2010 (P < .01),37 a decline of 32%.

Additionally, a birth cohort study in Taiwan reported that from 2014 to 2015, when routine PCV13 vaccination was introduced in Taiwan, the carriage rate of PCV13 serotypes significantly decreased. In contrast, the carriage rate of non-PCV13 serotypes increased year by year.38

Other findings

In 2015, a retrospective ecological study in Taiwan examined the impact of PCV7 vaccination on tympanostomy tube insertion rates due to otitis media. The results showed that among children born in 2004–2005, one year after PCV7 became available, the incidence rate of tympanostomy tube insertion fell by 10% (95% CI: 3.0%–17.0%). For children born in 2006–2007, the incidence rate declined by 79% (95% CI: 77.0%–81.0%).39

Another cohort study, using data from the Ningbo Immunization Information Management System for children born between 2017 and 2019 as the study population, employed a 1:1 matching method to select vaccinated and control groups. The adjusted VE of PCV13 vaccination against all-cause otitis media was −1.48% (95% CI: −28.52% to 19.87%), indicating no significant protective effect of PCV13 against all-cause otitis media.27

Discussion

Globally, the safety and protective effectiveness of PCVs in children have been well recognized.2,3,41 However, in China, due to limited research data and differences in geographic, cultural, and ethnic factors, the protective effect and impact of PCVs in Chinese needed further evaluation. This study summarized data from studies on the protective effectiveness of PCVs published domestically over the past 18 years and conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis. We identified 27 relevant publications, and the available data provide strong evidence that PCVs have reduced pneumococcal disease in Chinese children. The included studies reported on the effects of PCVs for a range of clinical outcomes. PCVs effectiveness was highest for the most specific outcome – vaccine-type IPD (VT-IPD), and lower for all-cause outcomes, which were expected findings as the vaccine should provide best protection against the serotypes included in the vaccine formulation.

PCV13 vaccination was shown to significantly reduce the occurrence of IPD, with a pooled VE of 88.8%. These results were consistent with data on PCV13 effectiveness from outside China. A systematic review indicated that for children ≤5 years old, PCV13 demonstrated significant effectiveness against VT-IPD, with rates ranging from 86% to 96% in a 3 + 1 dosing schedule and 67.2% to 86% in a 2 + 1 schedule.42 A multicenter observational study in Europe (SpIDnet) reported that the VE of at least one dose of PCV13 against VT-IPD (N = 2,353) was 84.2%, with decreasing from 93.1% within 12 months after booster vaccination to 85.1% after 24 months.43 Similarly, an indirect cohort study in the United States reported a VE of 86% for at least one dose of PCV13 against VT-IPD,44 consistent with our findings. One study from our review showed that PCV13 had significant VE against serotype 19A related IPD (VE = 89%,95% CI: 72.0%–96.0%).16 These results were consistent with the European SpIDnet study, which reported that PCV13 VE against IPD due to serotype 19A in fully vaccinated children was 89.1%.43

The all-cause CAP results showed that PCV13 provided a pooled VE of 14.3% against all-cause CAP, with low heterogeneity, indicating a certain level of reliability. A study from Turkey reported that the incidence of CAP in children under 5 years old decreased by 22% seven years after PCV13 was included in the national immunization program.45 Another population-based analysis in Canada found that the infant immunization program with PCV13 was associated with a reduction in CAP incidence in children aged 0–2 years, with a VE of 9.0%,46 consistent with our findings. Based on these results, to better clarify the public health significance of PCV13 vaccination in preventing CAP in children under 5 years of age, we estimated its impact using the 2020 CAP incidence rate for children under 5 years old. Referring to the national statistics for 2020, there were 94.43 million children under 5 years old in China.47 Assuming a vaccination coverage rate of 70%, PCV13 vaccination could prevent an estimated 646,150 CAP cases among Chinese children.

Our meta-analysis results indicated that PCV13 reduced CAP hospitalization rates by 25.5%. A time-series analysis in the United States showed a significant association between PCV13 vaccination and reduced pneumonia hospitalization rates in children, with reductions of 21% (95% CI: 14%–28%) in children under 2 years old and 17% (95% CI: 7%–27%) in children aged 2–4 years.48 Differences across studies may be attributed to methodological distinctions between cohort studies and pre-post study designs. Notably, in pre-post studies, not all children within the post cohort received PCV13 vaccination. These variations in outcomes are thus both anticipated and methodologically plausible.

Additionally, our study found that PCV13 had a significant effect in reducing VT-CAP incidence, with a vaccine effectiveness of 69.9% (95% CI: 60.0%–79.7%). Similarly, other pediatric observational studies also reported the protective effects exerted by PCVs following their introductions.49,50 Specifically, a systematic review showed a decrease in all-cause, radiologically confirmed and severe pneumonia hospitalization rates and pneumonia mortality in children 0–9 years old.49 A review by Lee et al. summarized evidence of protection of PCVs against invasive and mucosal infections caused by pneumococcal serotypes included in the vaccines.50 More recently, a decline in vaccine serotype nasopharyngeal colonization and clinical and radiological severity in children hospitalized with clinical pneumonia and bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia was observed in Cambodia following PCV13 introduction.51

Studies on pneumococcal carriage conducted in China have shown that while PCVs vaccination did not significantly reduce overall pneumococcal carriage, it demonstrated marked effectiveness in reducing carriage of vaccine-targeted serotypes.35 We also found evidence of serotype replacement in carriage studies from Taiwan, where PCVs have been included in national immunization programs. After PCV7 introduction, while the prevalence of vaccine-type carriage decreased significantly, the carriage rates of serotypes 15B and 19A increased annually.36,37 Similarly, after PCV13 introduction PCV13 serotype carriage declined significantly but an annual increase in non-PCV13 serotype carriage was observed.38 These findings are consistent with the phenomenon of serotype replacement, in which a decline in vaccine-type carriage leads PCV-vaccinated children to have increases in colonization by non-vaccine serotypes, which has been observed in many countries following PCVs introduction.52 These findings underscore the importance of continuous serotype monitoring during PCVs implementation and highlight the potential need for broader-spectrum vaccines (e.g., PCV15, PCV20) to address serotype replacement.

The single study on otitis media we identified reported that PCV13 use was not associated with significant changes in the incidence of otitis media due to any cause in children under 2 years old.27 In contrast, a longitudinal study in the United States reported that PCV13 VE against otitis media due to the six additional serotypes included in PCV13 but not PCV7 was 86%.53 In Israel, the incidence of otitis media cause by PCV7 declined by 96% following widespread PCV use.54 The lack of effect in the Chinese study might be due to the broad outcome definition (otitis media due to any cause) or the limited use of PCV13 in the Chinese population in contrast to the United States and Israel, where PCVs are part of the national immunization program with high uptake.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively synthesize the real-world evidence on the effectiveness of both PCV7 and PCV13 exclusively in the unique context of Chinese children, integrating data from mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong. Although international data showed PCVs have great impact in reducing pneumococcal diseases, data from mainland China are limited. Mainland China is one of the few countries that does not include PCV in their national immunization program (NIP). Our study provides evidence from a low-coverage, non-NIP setting from mainland China and from NIP setting like Hong Kong and Taiwan. No studies from mainland China have reported herd effects of PCVs, as the low and uneven vaccination coverage prevents the establishment of detectable herd immunity. However, several studies from Taiwan, where high-coverage NIPs have been implemented, provide strong indirect evidence of herd protection. For instance, the significant reductions in IPD incidence among unvaccinated age groups observed in surveillance studies,15,17 along with the marked decline in vaccine-type carriage in the broader community.36,38 These findings from Taiwan present a compelling picture of the additional population-level benefits that can be achieved in mainland China upon achieving high PCV coverage through a national immunization program. As noted, there is a pronounced lack of studies assessing herd effects and impact on reducing antibiotic resistance in mainland China. Future research should try to fill this gap and provide evidence for policy makers.

This study has several limitations. Several included studies reported population-level effects comparing disease incidence across two time periods, rather than direct VE obtained by comparing disease incidence in PCV-vaccinated vs unvaccinated children. Furthermore, while some studies explored the differences in effectiveness between complete and incomplete vaccination schedules, limited research on insufficient or delayed vaccination hindered a comprehensive evaluation of different vaccination patterns. Most included studies were conducted in economically developed regions (e.g., Suzhou, Shanghai, Beijing, Taiwan, and Hong Kong), with limited data from rural and less-developed areas, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to the entire country. Lastly, the current data on VE are specific to Prevenar 13. Future studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of new vaccines provided by other manufacturers in China.

Conclusion

This study, through systematic review and meta-analysis, confirms the significant protective effects of PCVs, particularly PCV13, in preventing pneumococcal-related diseases, especially IPD and CAP among children under five years old in China. Analyses from this study provide strong scientific evidence for promoting PCVs in China and incorporating them into the national immunization program, which will help comprehensively reduce the burden of pneumococcal related diseases and promote the realization of children’s health and health equity.
