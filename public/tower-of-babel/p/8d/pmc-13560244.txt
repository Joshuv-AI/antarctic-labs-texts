Exogenous gibberellin promotes lateral branch development in stumping Pinus yunnanensis by regulating endogenous hormones and TCP genes
Authors: Meiling Zhu, Sili Cheng, Zhaoliu Hu, Shuangcheng Bai, Yulan Xu, Nianhui Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While plant stumping can break apical dominance, promote lateral branching, and thereby increase yield, a significant technical challenge remains in further enhancing the coppicing regrowth capacity after stumping. This study used one-year-old Pinus yunnanensis Franch seedlings as subjects. Through exogenous application of gibberellin 3 (GA3) and Paclobutrazol (PAC), it investigated the regulatory role of gibberellin (GA) in sprouting and identified TCP genes related to branching within the GA pathway. Results indicate that exogenous hormone treatments altered endogenous hormone levels in P. yunnanensis seedlings. Exogenous GA3 treatment significantly increased endogenous IPA, ABA, GA3, GA1, SA, and SAG content. Correlation analysis with sprout number revealed a positive relationship between endogenous hormones and sprout number, indicating that exogenous GA3 significantly influenced sprout number. After hormone application, the cumulative number of sprouts increased with seedling age across all treatments. Therefore, to further investigate whether changes in sprout number were caused by gene alterations in the GA signaling pathway, this study analyzed the GA signaling pathway and its associated transcription factors. Fifteen TCP family members were screened from the P. yunnanensis transcriptome, phylogenetically classified into Class I and Class II subfamilies. Conserved motif sites within the Class II family co-occurred, exhibiting high conservation. Genes from the Class II subfamily were cloned, and their differential expression across various tissues was analyzed via real-time quantitative PCR. Expression patterns under different hormone treatments were also examined. Our results reveal that PyTCP4, PyTCP6, and PyTCP20 are significantly upregulated by PAC (a GA synthesis inhibitor) and show strong negative correlations with sprouts number, while GA3 treatment partially alleviates this effect. These findings provide direct evidence that TCP transcription factors act as repressors of shoot branching, linking their hormone-responsive expression to the regulation of sprouts formation in P. yunnanensis. These results indicate that TCP transcription factors play a crucial role in shoot and root emergence, with their expression influenced by exogenous hormones, thereby affecting the growth and development of emerging shoots and roots. This provides a reference and basis for analyzing the expression patterns and functions of TCP genes in P. yunnanensis, facilitating further research into their functions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09353-5.

Introduction

Gibberellin (GA), a vital phytohormone, regulates numerous processes during plant growth and development [1, 2]. To date, more than 130 GA compounds have been identified, yet only a few such as GA1, GA3, GA₄, and GA7 are biologically active [3]. GAs strongly promotes stem elongation and internode growth, whereas PAC inhibits GA biosynthesis by blocking the oxidation of kaurene to kaurenic acid, thereby reducing plant height and promoting branching [4–6]. In Pisum sativum L, GA inhibits lateral shoot growth by modulating sensitivity to strigolactones (SL) [7]. Transcriptome-revealed plant hormones and nutrients are key factors influencing branching in ornamental kale. Stumping treatment and exogenous hormone application revealed that auxin and SLs inhibited lateral bud outgrowth, respectively [8]. In Arabidopsis thaliana L., GA-insensitive mutants show weakened apical dominance and increased axillary bud formation, while overexpression of GA20ox elevates GA levels in leaf axils and suppresses axillary meristem initiation and lateral branching [9, 10]. Conversely, overexpression of the GA catabolic gene GA2ox increases tiller or branch number in Oryza sativa L [11], Solanum lycopersicum [12], and Populus [13]. However, the effects of GA are highly species‑dependent: exogenous GA3 promotes axillary bud outgrowth in Citrus reticulata [14], Prunus avium [15], and Jatropha curcas L [16], but not in Malus domestica [17]. In J. curcas, GA3 acts synergistically with cytokinins (CTKs) to promote bud growth, and CTKs can further regulate GA metabolism [18]. Collectively, existing research reveals that GA regulates plant branching in a complex, species-specific manner with unresolved molecular mechanisms, while TCP transcription factors integrate GA and other hormone signals to act as pivotal nodes in the GA-dependent regulatory pathway controlling lateral branch formation.

The TCP family is a class of transcription factors unique to plants, and its members contain a TCP domain, a specialized basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) superfamily domain consisting of approximately 59 amino acids [19, 20]. This domain originates from TB1 in Zea mays, CYC in Antirrhinum majus, and PCF1/PCF2 in O. sativa, hence the designation of the TCP gene family as an acronym of these three gene groups. [21, 22]. During plant growth and development, TCP transcription factor genes exert critical functions and modulate lateral branching development via tight crosstalk with hormone signaling pathways [23]. The TCP transcription factors BRC1 have been confirmed to locally repress shoot branching in A. thaliana [24]. As a core TCP family member [25], BRC1 modulates lateral branch growth by interacting with multiple hormones including GA, auxin, cytokinin and SL [9]. Furthermore, exogenous SL upregulates PsBRC1 expression whereas cytokinin suppresses it, demonstrating that PsBRC1 cooperates with SL and cytokinin to jointly modulate axillary meristem activity [26]. This study demonstrates that the pea orthologous gene PsBRC1 is highly expressed in axillary buds and is indispensable for strigolactone (SL)-mediated repression of lateral branching [27]. BRC1 interacts with HB21, HB40 and HB53 to form a protein complex that activates the ABA biosynthetic gene NCED3, thereby directly regulating lateral branch formation [28]. The rice TCP gene TIG1 modulates tiller development by regulating the auxin-responsive gene SAUR39 within the auxin signaling pathway [29]. These findings indicate that TCP transcription factors serve as central regulators of plant lateral branching by integrating GA and multiple hormone signals, whereas such regulatory mechanisms remain uncharacterized in the ecologically important species P. yunnanensis.

Pinus yunnanensis possesses outstanding tolerance to drought and infertile soils. Its deep, robust root system and needles with thick cuticles enable it to survive drought stress effectively. After afforestation, it not only stabilizes slopes and retains soil moisture but also improves site conditions through litter decomposition, rendering it a native pioneer tree species for ecological restoration of barren mountains in Southwest China [30]. This tree species boasts remarkable economic, social as well as ecological benefits [31]. Economically, its timber is widely used for construction, furniture, and paper pulp; its resin provides raw materials for rosin and turpentine production; and its edible pine nuts generate income for local communities. Socially, afforestation with this species creates rural employment in nursery management, planting, and harvesting, while also stabilizing slopes and reducing natural disaster risks (e.g., landslides) that threaten villages and farmland. Ecologically, as a pioneer species it rapidly colonizes degraded barren mountains, improves soil structure and fertility through litter accumulation, prevents soil erosion, sequesters carbon, and facilitates the subsequent regeneration of other native understory plants, thereby enhancing overall biodiversity recovery.

Long-term human disturbance and environmental stress have degraded P. yunnanensis stand quality, productivity and germplasm resources. Stumping promotes sprouting and high-quality cutting propagation by breaking apical dominance, while maintaining superior maternal traits and shortening the seedling cycle. Although GA facilitates axillary bud outgrowth, the effects of exogenous GA3 and the associated molecular mechanisms on sprouting in stumped P. yunnanensis remain unclear, particularly regarding the regulatory role of TCP transcription factors. Therefore, we treated stumped seedlings with GA3 to explore GA-mediated sprouting physiology and molecular regulation for germplasm improvement and seedling cultivation. As an ecologically dominant native tree in southwest China, P. yunnanensis sprouting regulation remains poorly understood under GA3 and PAC treatments, restricting targeted seedling regulation techniques. This study analyzed dynamic phytohormone responses during sprouting to clarify GA-related regulatory patterns and provide a theoretical basis for P. yunnanensis physiological research and cultivation.

Materials and methods

Research material

Seeds were collected from the “Midu P. yunnanensis Franch Asexual Seed Plantation,” a national P. yunnanensis seed base in Midu County, Dali Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China [32, 33]. They were sterilized by soaking in a 0.5% potassium permanganate solution for 30 min, rinsed with running water, and soaked in warm water at 50 °C for 24 h [34]. The soil and seedling trays were sterilized with potassium permanganate solution. The seeds were sown [35]. After germination, a layer of pine needles was spread on the surface of the seedling trays, a sunshade was installed, and the trays were covered with film. Regular management measures, such as watering and weeding, were carried out. When the P. yunnanensis seedlings were uniform, after hardening off, seedlings with good growth status were selected for single transplantation into the nursery pots. During the nursery period, the soil was a mixture of humus and red soil at a 3:1 ratio [36].

Seedling stumping and exogenous hormone treatment

In March 2024, after one year of seedling cultivation, P. yunnanensis seedlings with uniform growth status were selected. A single-factor experimental design was adopted with three treatments. Each treatment had three replicates, totaling nine plots; each plot contained 68 seedlings, with a total of 612 plants. Based on previous studies, the seedlings were stubbed at a uniform height of 5 cm, and wound healing cream was applied to the cuts immediately after stubbing to prevent water evaporation and microbial contamination. Before stumping, the seedlings were comprehensively surveyed to record the number of existing sprouts, which were marked with ties to distinguish them from the new sprouts that would emerge later. Cumulative sprout number was determined via serial observations of the same individual plants from March to December. Each seedling was labeled individually, and sprout numbers were recorded non-destructively at each time point without pooling or destructive sampling. The same individuals were monitored continuously to ensure accurate cumulative counting.

The prepared solutions were applied via foliar spraying, immediately after stumping. The experiment was conducted on sunny mornings after the dew had evaporated to avoid affecting the spraying effect. The spraying volume was controlled to ensure that the needles of the P. yunnanensis seedlings were thoroughly wetted, with droplets forming at the needle tips. Treatment 1 (T1) was sprayed with water as a control, Treatment 2 (T2) with a 300 mg/L GA3 aqueous solution, and Treatment 3 (T3) with a 500 mg/L aqueous solution of the gibberellin biosynthesis inhibitor, PAC.

Sampling and assay of endogenous hormones

For all assays, including endogenous hormone quantification, RNA extraction, and qPCR analysis, P. yunnanensis seedlings were sampled at 0 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 1 d, 2 d, 3 d, 5 d, 7 d, and 14 d after hormone spraying. During sampling, stem segments 2 cm below the stumping site were excised immediately, placed into pre-labeled cryopreservation tubes, and frozen in liquid nitrogen on-site. Samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C until use [37, 38]. For each treatment group, nine seedlings were randomly selected at each time point, and their tissues were pooled to form one biological replicate; three independent biological replicates were collected per time point. Latex gloves and masks were worn throughout sampling to avoid contamination and ensure the dryness of sample surfaces.

Based on preliminary experiments and transcriptomic data, samples collected at 4 h, 1 d, and 5 d after stumping were selected for endogenous hormone quantification. Endogenous hormones were quantified using UPLC-MS/MS (AB Sciex ExionLC coupled with 6500 + Qtrap mass spectrometer) in MRM mode. Stable isotope-labeled internal standards IAA‑D5 (indole-3-acetic acid‑d5) and ABA‑D6 (abscisic acid‑d6) were spiked during sample extraction to correct matrix effects and procedural losses. The limits of detection (LOD) for target hormones ranged from 0.05 ng/mL to 25 ng/mL based on the standard curves. Method recoveries were calibrated using the internal standards to ensure accuracy. The abbreviations of endogenous hormones used in this study are defined in Table S1 (List of abbreviations for endogenous hormones).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Research materials

Whole one-year-old seedlings of P. yunnanensis were collected from the nursery of Southwest Forestry University and used as experimental materials. For tissue-specific qPCR analysis, only untreated seedlings (0 h, i.e., no hormone or stumping treatment) were sampled. For other assays, including total RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing, and hormone quantification, samples were collected at 0 h, 4 h, 1 d, and 5 d after hormone spraying and stumping.

At each time point, seedlings were rinsed with running water to remove surface impurities, disinfected with sterile water, and gently blotted dry with filter paper. Tissues were immediately placed into 50 mL centrifuge tubes, frozen in liquid nitrogen on-site, and stored at −80 °C until use. For all assays, three independent biological replicates were collected per time point. Each biological replicate consisted of tissues pooled from nine randomly selected seedlings to minimize individual variation. Total RNA was extracted from these samples for RNA-seq and qPCR analysis. Clean reads were mapped to the Pinus tabuliformis reference transcriptome from the Chinese Pine Information Resource (CPIR, conifers.cn). The transcriptome assembly contained a total of 690 transcripts, with an average length of 1455.5 bp and an N50 length of 2070.93 bp, demonstrating high quality and completeness for subsequent gene expression analysis. All raw RNA-seq data were deposited in the SRA under Bioproject PRJNA1406671 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1406671/). All quality assessment images are included as Supplementary Figures (S1, S2, S3).

Extraction of RNA and cDNA reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from all tissues of one-year-old P. yunnanensis seedlings using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit. After confirming that the extracted total RNA had good concentration, purity, and integrity, it was reverse transcribed into single-stranded cDNA using the HiScript III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) [39].

Tissue specificity of candidate TCP genes and expression analysis in response to different hormone treatments

This study employed one-year-old P. yunnanensis seedlings as experimental plant materials. Eight distinct tissue types, namely young leaves, old leaves, young stems, old stems, dormant buds, apical buds, germinating buds and roots, were all harvested from the same set of 30 seedlings (Fig. 1). For each tissue type, tissue aliquots taken from 10 individual seedlings were combined to constitute a single biological replicate; three independent biological replicates were prepared per tissue, which required pooling tissue samples from 10 seedlings for each replicate, with all sampled tissues originating collectively from the 30 fixed seedlings. After collection, total RNA from different tissues was extracted sequentially, as described in Sect. "Extraction of RNA and cDNA reverse transcription", and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Results

Effects of hormone treatment on the sprouting ability of P. yunnanensis Franch seedlings

The cumulative number of sprouts in P. yunnanensis seedlings under different hormone treatments evolved over time, as shown in Fig. 2a. During the measurement period, all hormone treatments exhibited a steady increase in the cumulative number of sprouts over time. The number of sprouting shoots in the GA3 treatment was greater than that in the CK and PAC groups from 30 days after treatment (i.e., April). From September onward, it was significantly greater than that in the CK and PAC groups, with a significant difference (p < 0.05) among treatments.

As shown in Fig. 2b, the monthly increment in the number of sprouting shoots of P. yunnanensis under different hormone treatments showed multiple peaks over time. Across all treatments, the initial, dominant peak of sprouting occurred 30 days after stumping, corresponding to the March-to-April interval. This synchronous, early peak across all treatments is a conserved physiological response to the stumping stimulus, rather than a hormone-specific effect. Critically, this marked increase in both the cumulative number of sprouts and the monthly increment during March–April is driven by the interaction of two key factors: the immediate physiological response to stumping, and favorable seasonal environmental conditions. Stumping eliminates the apical dominance exerted by the main shoot apex, which constitutively suppresses axillary bud outgrowth via auxin signaling. The removal of this suppression immediately triggers a coordinated, rapid burst of axillary bud activation in the first month post-treatment, driven by the plant’s inherent wound response and resource reallocation to regenerative growth. Concurrently, the March–April period coincides with rising temperatures and increasing day length in the study area, which further promote cell division, elongation, and the mobilization of stored carbohydrates to support new growth. These favorable seasonal cues act synergistically with the stumping-induced signal to drive the observed early sprouting surge. In summary, GA3 effectively promotes the sprouting and growth of P. yunnanensis seedlings, which is closely linked to the activation of its core GA signaling pathway.

Response of endogenous hormones to exogenous hormone levels in P. yunnanensis seedlings after stumping

Temporal dynamics of endogenous hormone content under different treatments

To characterize the dynamic changes in endogenous phytohormones during sprouting and their responses to GA3 and PAC treatments, the temporal profiles of 24 hormones/metabolites were quantified in P. yunnanensis seedlings over a 5-day period. Cytokinin-related compounds exhibited distinct responses: IPA levels were significantly elevated in the PAC treatment compared with the CK and GA3 groups at all three time points, peaking at 5 days (p < 0.05). By contrast, the GA3 treatment rapidly increased IP and TZR concentrations, which reached their maximum at 5 days. Among auxin-related signals, GA3 significantly upregulated IAA at 4 h, while PAC induced sustained high levels of DZ throughout the treatment period. For gibberellins, exogenous GA3 triggered a sharp, transient increase in bioactive GA1, GA3, and GA8 within 4 h, which then gradually declined over time. This phenomenon is a typical hormone homeostatic response: plants perceive excessive active GAs and initiate negative feedback to accelerate GA degradation and suppress GA synthesis. In contrast, these GA levels remained consistently low in the PAC treatment. The GA precursor GA7 was significantly suppressed by GA3 but accumulated to high levels under PAC treatment, indicating feedback inhibition of GA biosynthesis. Ethylene precursor ACC and jasmonate-related signals (JA, JA-Ile, MEJA) showed divergent patterns: ACC concentrations were highest in the PAC group at 5 days, while JA and JA-Ile peaked at 4 h in the GA3 treatment before rapidly decreasing. SA and its conjugate SAG were strongly induced by GA3 at 1 day, whereas CK plants showed a gradual increase in SA over time. ABA levels were highest in the GA3 treatment at 4 h, but all three groups converged to similarly low ABA concentrations by 1 day. The auxin catabolite OPDA remained significantly higher in the GA3 group throughout the experiment, while PAC significantly promoted the accumulation of the cytokinin-related metabolite 6-BA at later stages. Collectively, these results demonstrate that GA3 and PAC induce highly specific, time-dependent alterations in multiple hormonal pathways, particularly cytokinin, gibberellin, and auxin signals, which may coordinate to regulate sprouting and branching development in P. yunnanensis.

The CK group (A) exhibits a balanced baseline regulatory pattern, with extensive but moderately positive/negative correlations between hormones and sprout numbers (SN) across months, reflecting the fundamental homeostatic regulation of endogenous hormones. In the GA3 treatment group (B), the network connection density increases significantly. Core hormones such as GA3, IPA, and IAA form a tightly positively correlated module and maintain strong positive correlations with sprout number nodes across all months, indicating that exogenous GA3 enhances the synergistic effects among hormones and continuously promotes sprout occurrence. In the PAC treatment group (C), the network connections become notably sparse; positive correlations between hormones and sprout numbers are substantially reduced while the proportion of negative correlations increases, suggesting that the promoting effects of core hormones on sprouting are weakened. Analysis of GA signaling and metabolic pathways

Exogenous hormone application after stumping disrupts endogenous hormone homeostasis, thereby altering sprout number. Therefore, to elucidate the molecular events underlying this process, this study sought to profile the expression of DEGs in hormonal signaling pathways during subsequent growth and development, and to map these DEGs to their corresponding pathways (Fig. 6). GA can break axillary bud dormancy and is a key factor promoting lateral branch germination in plants. A total of eight DEGs were expressed during GA biosynthesis and metabolism. The GA biosynthesis gene GA20ox3 was upregulated in the GA3 treatment group, whereas it was first downregulated and then upregulated in the PAC treatment group. The GA inactivation gene GA2ox1 was downregulated in both GA3 and PAC treatment groups. GID1, a GA receptor in the signaling pathway, plays an important role. GIDIC-2 exhibited an expression trend of upregulation, followed by downregulation, and then upregulation in all treatment groups, whereas GIDIC-1 showed a trend of downregulation, followed by upregulation, and finally downregulation. In the GA3 treatment group, GAI1-3 was downregulated, and both GAI1-1 and GAI1-2 displayed a trend of downregulation, followed by upregulation. All three GAI1 genes were downregulated in the PAC treatment group. From the changes in hormone content after treatment, it can be seen that after exogenous hormone application, the GA3 content in both the GA3 and PAC treatment groups showed consistent change trends. Still, the change in GA3 content in the GA3 treatment group was highly significant. The GA7 content in both the GA3 and PAC treatment groups also showed consistent change trends, with the change in the PAC treatment group being more substantial; however, compared with the GA3 content, the change in GA7 content was not significant. Cloning analysis of candidate TCP genes

Phylogenetic analysis of P. yunnanensis TCP family members

The hidden Markov model (HMM) for the TCP conserved domain (PF03634) was obtained from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org). Using HMMER 3.0 software, TCP proteins were searched against the P. yunnanensis transcriptome with a defined E value threshold of < 10⁻5. After removing sequences lacking the conserved domain and redundant sequences, a total of 15 TCP proteins were ultimately identified. Their gene names and IDs are listed in Table.S3, and their amino acid sequences and protein sequences were used for subsequent analysis.

The phylogenetic results (Fig. 8A) indicated that the TCP transcription factor family of P. yunnanensis was divided into two major classes: Class I and Class II. Class I is also known as the PCF subfamily, and Class II can be further divided into CYC/TB1 and CIN subfamilies. Among them, eight P. yunnanensis TCP members were distributed in the PCF subfamily and seven in the CIN subfamily. In addition, A. thaliana TCP members were present in all subfamilies, whereas P. yunnanensis TCP members were restricted to the PCF and CIN subfamilies only. To determine the conserved structural domains of the P. yunnanensis TCP transcription factor family, multiple sequence alignment of the conserved structural domains was performed using DNAMAN 9.0 software. Conserved protein motif analysis and PCR amplification with verification of TCP family members in P. yunnanensis

The conserved motifs of the P. yunnanensis TCP family were identified using the online tool MEME v5.5.7. A total of 10 conserved motifs were determined among the 15 TCP family members and designated as Motif 1 to Motif 10 (Fig. 9A). Discussion and conclusions

Effects of exogenous hormones on endogenous hormones in P. yunnanensis flat-crop seedlings

Plant hormone metabolism and balance are crucial for regulating plant growth and development. Exogenous hormone treatments significantly affect endogenous hormone homeostasis in flat‑cut seedlings, mainly by modulating the biosynthesis and catabolism of hormones [40]. Exogenously applied GA3 or PAC can directly trigger or suppress the synthesis of endogenous hormones, as well as regulate their metabolic pathways, thereby reshaping the balance of multiple phytohormones. Such dynamic changes in endogenous hormone levels, in turn, serve as critical signals to coordinate sprouting and branching development in seedlings (Fig. 13). Gibberellins (GAs) play fundamental roles in almost every aspect of plant growth and development, including cell elongation, leaf expansion, leaf senescence, seed germination, and leafy head formation [41]. Previous studies have shown that exogenous GA3 application can affect lateral branch development by regulating endogenous hormone production in J. curcas [16], which provides a reference for understanding the regulatory mechanism in P. yunnanensis.Fig. 13GA treatment model for P. yunnanensis after stumping

GA treatment model for P. yunnanensis after stumping

The study indicates that exogenous GA3 treatment significantly alters the endogenous hormone levels in P. yunnanensis stump seedlings, specifically increasing the contents of IPA, ABA, GA3, GA1, SA, and SAG. Furthermore, exogenous GA3 and PAC treatments were applied to P. yunnanensis flat-crop seedlings, and the changes in endogenous hormone levels were systematically analyzed. The results showed that exogenous GA3 treatment significantly increased the contents of endogenous IPA, ABA, GA3, GA1, SA, and SAG in P. yunnanensis seedlings, compared with the control (CK) and PAC treatments. In contrast, PAC treatment inhibits endogenous GA biosynthesis and triggers distinct changes in endogenous hormone levels. Specifically, it caused a downward trend in GA-related endogenous hormones (GA3, GA1) in the early stage of treatment. Meanwhile, GA20ox3, a key gene in GA biosynthesis, showed a expression pattern of decreasing first and then increasing, which is probably attributed to the intrinsic feedback regulatory mechanism in plants [42]. Studies on tomato have shown that PAC treatment upregulates the expression of the biosynthesis-related genes SlGA20ox-3 and SlGA3ox2 [43].

In addition, correlation analysis between exogenous hormone treatments and sprout number indicated that the increased levels of endogenous IPA, ABA, GA3, GA1, SA, and SAG induced by exogenous GA3 were positively correlated with the sprout number of P. yunnanensis seedlings. This confirms that exogenous GA3 effectively promotes the growth and development of P. yunnanensis seedlings by regulating endogenous hormone levels. These changes in endogenous hormone levels are closely related to the activation of the gibberellin core signaling pathway, as exogenous hormones can regulate plant growth and development by influencing endogenous hormone levels or their response timing [44]. Furthermore, ZT, IAA and GA interact to jointly regulate branching development after stumping. ZT directly promotes branching, while IAA indirectly modulates this process by inducing GA accumulation. GA also participates directly in branching growth. Such interactions can be observed from the dynamic changes of endogenous hormone contents under exogenous treatments.

Summary of key findings

The TCP family is a family of plant-specific transcription factors that dynamically maintain and regulate the optimal conditions required for plant growth as plants grow and develop [45, 46]. This study systematically explored the regulatory mechanism of exogenous hormones on sprout growth and development in P. yunnanensis flat-crop seedlings, combining endogenous hormone detection, transcriptome analysis, and TCP transcription factor cloning and characterization. Exogenous GA3 treatment significantly alters endogenous hormone levels in P. yunnanensis stump seedlings, specifically increasing the contents of IPA, ABA, GA3, GA1, SA, and SAG, which are positively correlated with sprout number, indicating that exogenous GA3 significantly promotes sprouting. Exogenous GA3 and PAC treatments induce differential expression of genes related to phytohormone signaling, which are involved in the signaling pathways of GA, IAA, CTK, ABA, SA, and JA, and these differential expressions regulate endogenous hormone contents and downstream signal transduction efficiency, thereby affecting sprout growth. A total of 15 TCP transcription factor family members were identified from P. yunnanensis, which were phylogenetically classified into Class I (8 members) and Class II (7 members), all containing a complete TCP domain, indicating high conservation during evolution. Tissue-specific expression analysis showed that PyTCP genes have distinct expression profiles in different tissues, and correlation analysis confirmed that PyTCP4, PyTCP6, and PyTCP20 are key of shoot branching, whose responsive expression to GA signaling directly modulates branching.

Crosstalk between GA signaling and TCP transcription factors

Gibberellin signaling and TCP transcription factors are closely involved in the regulation of plant lateral branching and sprout growth, and their crosstalk has been widely reported in various plant species. Gibberellin can break axillary bud dormancy and promote bud germination and branching [47]; for example, GA3 promotes axillary bud branching in J. curcas [48] and hybrid poplar [13], which is consistent with the finding that exogenous GA3 promotes sprout growth in P. yunnanensis in this study. The TCP transcription factor family, as plant-specific transcriptional regulators, plays key roles in plant growth, development, and hormone signaling, especially in lateral branching development [49]. In A. thaliana, AtTCP12 and AtTCP18 are expressed in axillary bud tissues and function in regulating meristems [50], and their homologous genes in P. yunnanensis (Class II TCP genes) may have similar functions, as indicated by their specific expression in dormant buds, terminal buds, and stems.

The crosstalk between GA signaling and TCP transcription factors is a key regulatory node in plant branching. In this study, exogenous GA3 treatment induced differential expression of TCP genes (especially PyTCP4, PyTCP6, and PyTCP20), and these TCP genes were closely correlated with sprout number, suggesting that GA signaling regulates TCP gene expression, which in turn modulates sprout growth. This is consistent with previous studies showing that GA signaling can regulate the expression of TCP transcription factors to affect plant architecture: for example, in P. tremula × P. tremuloides, decapitation-induced downregulation of GA2ox activates axillary buds, and this process is accompanied by the regulation of TCP-related genes [47]. Additionally, the upregulation of GA20ox3 by exogenous GA3 in this study may enhance GA biosynthesis, maintain high endogenous GA levels, and further regulate TCP gene expression, forming a GA-TCP regulatory loop that modulates sprout growth in P. yunnanensis.

Species-specific and conserved regulatory mechanisms

The regulatory mechanisms of GA signaling and TCP transcription factors in plant sprout growth show both conservation across species and species-specific characteristics. In terms of conserved mechanisms. The TCP transcription factor family in P. yunnanensis is classified into two subfamilies (Class I and Class II), which is consistent with the classification in A. thaliana, Z. mays, O. sativa, and S. lycopersicum [51], indicating that the TCP family is relatively conserved during plant evolution and may have similar biological functions. All 15 PyTCP members contain a complete TCP domain, which is consistent with TCP members in A. thaliana [50], reflecting the conservation of the core functional domain of TCP transcription factors.

The regulatory roles of GA in axillary bud dormancy and branching are conserved among different plant species. GA can break axillary bud dormancy and promote branching in J. curcas [48], hybrid poplar [13], and P. yunnanensis. In addition, downregulating GA2ox genes to sustain the level of active GAs acts as a conserved mechanism for axillary bud activation [47].

The multiple sequence alignment of PyTCP transcription factors showed that Class I has four fewer amino acid residues than Class II, which is consistent with Masson pine [52] but may differ from A. thaliana and other species, reflecting species-specific differences in TCP sequence characteristics. The tissue-specific expression profiles of PyTCP genes are species-specific: for example, PyTCP2 is highly expressed in young and old leaves, PyTCP3 in dormant axillary buds, and PyTCP20 in terminal buds and young stems, which is different from the expression patterns of TCP genes in A. thaliana [50] and other species, suggesting that PyTCP genes have evolved specific functions adapted to P. yunnanensis growth and development. The response of endogenous hormones to exogenous GA3 treatment in P. yunnanensis is species-specific: exogenous GA3 significantly increased the contents of IPA, ABA, SA, and SAG in P. yunnanensis, and these hormones were positively correlated with sprout number, which may be a unique regulatory characteristic of P. yunnanensis compared with other tree species such as Japanese larch and Elaeagnus bockii Diels [53].

Compared with previous studies on herbaceous and broad-leaved plants, this work innovatively revealed the overall response characteristics of the phytohormone network in P. yunnanensis seedlings during sprouting. We found that exogenous GA3 and PAC triggered distinct temporal alterations in GAs, CTKs, auxins, JAs and SA, and confirmed the existence of GA feedback regulation in this woody pine species. These findings supplement the theoretical system of hormonal regulation of branching in gymnosperms. Considering the important ecological and economic value of P. yunnanensis, the dynamic hormone profiles established in our study also lay a foundation for further exploring the molecular regulatory mechanism of sprouting and branching.

P. yunnanensis suffers severe germplasm degradation, and the GA‑TCP branching regulatory module established in model angiosperms cannot be directly applied to this gymnosperm, making it urgent to clarify the hormone regulation mechanism of its stumping sprouting. This study confirmed that exogenous GA3 and PAC reshaped endogenous hormone balance by altering hormone signaling gene expression to regulate sprout growth. We identified 15 PyTCP members and screened three key genes whose GA‑dependent expression controls branch development. These findings initially construct the GA‑TCP regulatory pathway governing sprouting in conifers, supplement gymnosperm TCP functional data, and supply theoretical support and core gene resources for artificial sprouting regulation and seedling cultivation of P. yunnanensis.

Study limitations

Despite the comprehensive exploration of the regulatory mechanism of exogenous GA3 on sprout growth in P. yunnanensis, this study still has some limitations that need to be addressed in future research. The study only analyzed the changes in endogenous hormone levels and gene expression at the transcriptional level; the post-transcriptional modification of key genes and the translation level of related proteins were not detected, which may affect the in-depth understanding of the GA-TCP regulatory network. The functional verification of key genes was not conducted; genetic transformation experiments are needed to further confirm their regulatory roles in P. yunnanensis sprout growth. The study only focused on GA3 and PAC treatments; the effects of other exogenous hormones and their combinations on P. yunnanensis sprout growth and the corresponding regulatory mechanisms were not explored, which limits the comprehensiveness of understanding hormone-mediated sprout regulation. The study did not explore the interaction between TCP transcription factors and other signaling molecules in the GA signaling pathway, which may be an important part of the complex regulatory network of P. yunnanensis sprout growth.

Conclusions

This study indicates that exogenous GA3 and PAC treatments induce differential expression of phytohormone signaling-related genes, which further modulate endogenous hormone contents and downstream signal transduction, thereby affecting sprout growth. A total of 15 TCP family members were identified in P. yunnanensis and classified into Class I and Class II subfamilies with high sequence conservation. Tissue‑specific expression and correlation analyses confirmed that PyTCP4, PyTCP6, and PyTCP20 act as key of shoot branching. Their expression responses to GA signaling directly modulate branch development, highlighting the critical roles of TCP transcription factors in regulating sprout emergence and outgrowth.

The results suggest that TCP transcription factors may play a key role in sprout formation in P. yunnanensis, with their expression influenced by exogenous hormones, thereby regulating sprout growth and development. This study provides a foundation for further exploration of the expression patterns and functions of TCP genes in P. yunnanensis, contributing to a deeper understanding of their role in the GA-mediated sprout regulatory network. Future research should address the limitations of this study, such as conducting functional verification of key genes and exploring the interaction between TCP transcription factors and other signaling molecules, to further clarify the molecular mechanism of GA-TCP crosstalk in P. yunnanensis sprout growth.
