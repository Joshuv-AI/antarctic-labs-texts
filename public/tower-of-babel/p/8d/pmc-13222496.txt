Challenges faced by prehospital emergency physicians providing emergency care to pediatric patients
Authors: Anne Kamphausen, Julia Steg, Tobias Bexten, Jens Kubitz, Christian Engelen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric emergencies constitute a small proportion of prehospital cases yet pose unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges for emergency physicians due to age-specific physiological differences, limited exposure, and infrequent opportunities for skill acquisition. Competency-based medical education frameworks and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) may offer suitable concepts to structure pediatric emergency training.MethodsThis mixed-methods study combined a retrospective analysis of 9,409 prehospital emergency physician protocols (2012–2021) with a survey assessing self-perceived competence and training needs among emergency physicians. Pediatric emergency characteristics, clinical management, and utilized interventions were analyzed. Survey data captured demographic characteristics, perceived competency across defined scenarios, sources of knowledge acquisition, and perceived gaps in pediatric emergency preparedness.ResultsAmong all prehospital missions, 606 pediatric emergencies (6.45%) were identified, predominantly involving adolescents with any condition, and any age with trauma and neurological conditions. Invasive interventions such as intubation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or intraosseous access were rarely needed. Survey responses (n = 437) revealed heterogeneous self-perceived competencies: seizures, anaphylaxis, and hypoglycemia were associated with high confidence, whereas neonatal care, pediatric polytrauma, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation demonstrated markedly lower confidence levels. Knowledge was primarily acquired during the initial emergency physician qualification training period, as well as through subsequent clinical exposure in routine practice, with limited engagement in ongoing pediatric-specific training. More than 70% of participants advocated for structured, mandatory, and simulation-based pediatric training.ConclusionPediatric emergencies in the prehospital setting are uncommon yet challenging, revealing gaps between training expectations, clinical exposure, and perceived competencies. Strengthened pediatric emergency training—particularly simulation-based practice and EPA-based curricular elements—may enhance preparedness, standardize entrustment decisions, and improve patient safety. Introduction

Pediatric emergencies represent a relatively small but particularly challenging proportion of cases encountered in prehospital emergency medicine. Children account for fewer than 10% of emergency physician deployments, yet these cases are often associated with heightened complexity and stress due to anatomical, physiological, and psychological differences compared to adults [1–4]. Infrequent exposure and limited practical training opportunities further contribute to uncertainty among emergency physicians when treating pediatric patients [5–8].

In prehospital emergency care, physicians are required to master a wide range of competencies to manage diverse medical conditions and traumatic injuries across all age groups. Consequently, training for physicians in prehospital emergency medicine must encompass a broad and multifaceted set of clinical competencies. Moreover, certain procedures are highly specialized and technically demanding, yet they are performed infrequently.

In Germany, authorization to practice as an EMS physician requires prior clinical training within a medical specialty, followed by formal certification in emergency medicine. This additional qualification is typically obtained after a minimum of 24 months of clinical training during specialty residency, completion of an 80-hour combined theoretical and practical course, and participation in 50 supervised prehospital missions. Physicians from a broad range of specialties—including anesthesiology, internal medicine, and surgery—may pursue this qualification and subsequently engage in prehospital emergency care.

The regulations include several pediatric emergency competencies, such as neonatal and pediatric resuscitation, airway management, mask ventilation, fluid resuscitation, and analgesia. Trainees are not required to demonstrate proficiency in neonatal care, nor is it specified whether pediatric competencies must be achieved within the 80-hour course or other training components. Minimum procedural requirements are defined for selected skills—e.g., fracture/dislocation reduction and endotracheal intubation—while no quantitative benchmarks exist for pediatric competencies, despite the expectation that these should be acquired during training.

In competency-based medical education, the gap between competencies and observable workplace tasks is addressed by entrustable professional activities. Entrustable professional activities (EPAs) are units of professional practice that can be entrusted to a learner once sufficient competence has been demonstrated [9, 10]. They represent practice-relevant tasks or responsibilities that can be delegated to trainees once they have demonstrated adequate competence and reliability. By translating abstract competency frameworks into observable clinical activities, EPAs provide a direct link between theoretical learning objectives and the practical demands of routine patient care. Incorporating EPAs into emergency medicine training enables a structured, competency-based approach that aligns assessment with authentic clinical performance, supports a graduated increase in trainee autonomy, and contributes to improved patient safety. This framework also facilitates transparent entrustment decisions by supervisors and guides trainees in acquiring the clinical skills and professional judgment necessary for independent practice in emergency settings. In addition to mostly broadly formulated EPAs, “nested EPAs” offer the opportunity to facilitate a more targeted evaluation of a specific application in an earlier training stage or for particular clinical and educational contexts within the broader formulated EPA [11]. Although various EPAs for emergency medicine have been proposed, their definitions and implementation remain heterogeneous and inconsistently applied across settings [12–14]. While EPAs have not yet been formally implemented in Germany, international developments highlight their potential relevance. In the United States, for example, the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has introduced core EPAs for entering residency, complemented by specialty-specific EPAs developed by professional organizations; however, implementation remains variable across institutions [15]. These differences underscore the need to adapt EPA frameworks to specific healthcare systems and training structures, particularly in physician-based EMS systems such as in Germany, where standardized competency-based approaches to prehospital care are still lacking.

Consequently, there is a need to better understand the scope of pediatric emergencies in the prehospital setting with regard to the current preparedness and perceived competence of emergency physicians. This study combined a retrospective analysis of operational protocols with a large-scale survey to investigate the characteristics of pediatric emergencies. It analyzes physician’s confidence in managing these emergencies, potential areas for improvement in training, and clinical practice in order to derive appropriate competencies that could usefully be taught within the EPA framework to better prepare emergency physicians in treating pediatric emergencies.

Method

Study design

This study employed a mixed-methods design, combining a retrospective analysis of the German Interdisciplinary Association for Intensive Care and Emergency Medicine (DIVI) operational protocol for emergency physicians in prehospital emergency medicine with data collected through a survey based on findings from these records. It adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and its conduct was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of PMU – Paracelsus Medical University, Nuremberg, Germany (IRB-2023-06).

Data collection

The retrospective analysis of the DIVI operational protocol for emergency physicians in prehospital emergency medicine covered a total of 9409 protocols in the period from 2012 to 2021. The DIVI operational protocol for emergency physicians is a standardized protocol template and was available as a handwritten paper form.

The protocols originate from one emergency service of the EMS physician ambulance car of Nuremberg City. The Nuremberg EMS system serves a predominantly urban population and operates four EMS physician response units, collectively managing approximately 24,000 physician-attended emergency calls per year.

In addition, the demographic data of the patients, such as age and sex, diagnosis, emergency scores (Glascow Coma Scale, National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics Score), and treatment, were recorded. The classification of age groups is: newborn (< 28 days), infancy (29 days to < 1 year), toddler (1–3 years), childhood (4–14 years), and adolescence (13–17 years).

Based on the findings of the retrospective analysis, an online survey using the free survey service ‘Google Forms’ (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) was conducted. The invitation to participate in the survey was sent to EMS physicians via e-mail distribution of EMS physician groups in the Nuremberg area, regardless of specialty or training courses attended.

In total, the survey consisted of 39 questions. To ensure a high degree of structure, the quantitative survey was mainly designed with closed single- or multiple-choice questions and supplemented by free response fields. In addition to demographic data such as age, gender, specialization and professional experience, the survey was intended to record how confident the EMS physician felt about treating pediatric emergencies. A 5-point Likert scale was used based on the study by Mulder et al. [16] and the Miller learning pyramid [9, 17]. In addition, we asked how EMS physicians acquired knowledge to treat these emergencies and how EMS physicians might be better prepared for pediatric emergency patients. The complete survey is shown in the appendix.

Data analysis

In the retrospective data analysis, data were checked for consistency. Incomplete data sets in the retrospective analysis were removed. All statistical analyses in this report were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA; 2016). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD), and census data are expressed as a composite ratio.

The results of the survey were presented as statistical tables in Microsoft Excel. Feedback on open questions and free text fields was processed based on the qualitative content analysis according to Mayring [18, 19]. The responses were first viewed and then paraphrased. Based on these results, various response categories were defined, and the summarized responses were classified into these categories.

Results

Retrospective analysis of pediatric emergencies

Demographic data

In the retrospective analysis of the DIVI operational protocol for emergency physicians in prehospital emergency medicine, a total of 9409 protocols were available in the period from 2012 to 2021. A total of 606 pediatric emergencies (6.45%; n = 9409) could be identified. A total of 268 pediatric emergencies (44%) were female. In three cases, the sex could not be determined. The average age of all pediatric emergencies was 8,7 years (SD 6,0). The distribution of emergencies varied depending on age. The incidence of emergency cases peaks in one-year-old children, with a secondary peak observed among 17-year-olds.

Medical treatment of pediatric emergencies

The basic medical procedure, the measurement of vital signs, was performed in almost all pediatric emergencies, with the exception of 5 cases (n = 601, 99%).

In 37% (n = 223) of all pediatric emergencies, an intravenous line was established. In 5 (0.83%) patients, an intraosseous line was placed. With increasing age, the frequency of “access placement” also increased: 9% (N = 112) of all infants treated, 25% (N = 141) of toddlers, 49% (N = 158) of children, and 54% (195) of adolescents received intravenous or intraosseous access. Intraosseous access was used in infants (2 cases), toddlers (2 cases), and children (1 case). In adolescents, only intravenous access was used. Only crystalloid solution was used (n = 206, 34%).

Medication was needed in 46% (n = 218) of patients. Benzodiazepine was the most frequently used class of medication in 87 pediatric emergencies (14%). Benzodiazepines were administered primarily via the nasal, rectal or sublingual routes.

Opiates were administered just as frequently (n = 83, 14%). The third most frequently used class of drugs was nonopioid analgesics. Metamizole and paracetamol were administered a total of 57 times (9%). Sedatives such as ketamine or propofol were used with approximately the same frequency (n = 53, 9%). Intubation and mechanical ventilation were performed in five cases (0.83%). No use of a laryngeal mask was documented. Likewise, no cricothyroidotomy was recorded.

Trauma treatment, such as applying a cervical collar or pelvic sling, spinal motion retraction, and using a tourniquet was necessary as an individual procedure in a total of 39 pediatric emergencies (6.4%). Infants were not subjected to trauma procedures. The most common treatments were cervical collar (n = 19, 3%) and spinal motion retraction (n = 14, 2%). The pelvic sling and tourniquet were each used only once (n = 1, < 1%).

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation had to be initiated in a total of 4 pediatric emergencies. This affected a total of 3 infants and one school-age child. In two infants, the suspected diagnosis was sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) or near SIDS, and one required cardiopulmonary resuscitation due to near drowning. One child had to be resuscitated due to multiple severe trauma.

Level of education and knowledge

According to the survey, 97% (N = 421) of all emergency physicians are certified in the specialty “emergency medicine”. Of the 15 individuals who had not yet completed the board certification, three had already attended the training course, and two others had attended both the training course and the accompanying emergency medical interventions.

For further expertise in emergency medicine, 85% of the survey participants had additional specialties or attended further training or continuing education courses. Only 14% stated that their further training was largely focused outside the field of pediatric emergency medicine. These included, for example, the intensive care transport course according to the DIVI, Advanced Cardiac Life Support courses and International Trauma Life Support courses, as well as various additional qualifications in adult medicine.

In contrast, 71% of participants stated that the training was related to pediatrics. The most common trainings were resuscitation courses for children of various age groups (n = 204; 47%) and the two-day course “(European) Pediatric Advanced Life Support (E)PALS” (n = 91; 21%). Newborn Life Support (NLS) was attended by 63 emergency physicians (15%), while 33 emergency physicians (8%) joined the “KITZ” Pediatric Emergency Training. The DIVI Pediatric Emergency Course was attended by 9 emergency physicians (2%). Furthermore, 9 emergency physicians (2%) specialize in pediatric intensive care medicine.

Expertise and experience

To assess the level of competence of the participating emergency physicians, pediatric emergency scenarios were defined based on the results of the retrospective evaluation. Survey participants were asked to assess their level of competence. Seven scenarios from the category of nontraumatic pediatric emergencies and five emergency scenarios from traumatology were selected.

The levels of competences “not competent at all” and “slightly competent” were most frequently assigned to the nontraumatic emergency scenarios “intoxication” and “neonatal care”. In the trauma emergency scenarios, the lowest level of competence was reported in the treatment of pediatric polytrauma as well as resuscitation.

The highest level of competency was reported in nontraumatic emergencies such as seizures, anaphylaxsis, and hyper or hypoglycemia, while survey participants felt confident in the treatment of fractures and injuries in the trauma scenarios.

Potential for enhancement

To identify potential areas for improvement in training and concomitantly achieve an enhancement in pediatric emergency care, 76% of survey participants (n = 330) advocated for the introduction of mandatory, regular practical training in pediatric emergency medicine. Furthermore, 73% of participants (n = 313) indicated that enhanced integration of practical exercises within the 80-hour course could lead to significant improvements in the management of pediatric emergencies. Furthermore, 63% (n = 266) of respondents identified regular, mandatory theoretical training in pediatric emergency medicine as another significant area for improvement (Table 5).

Discussion

This mixed-method study of preclinical pediatric emergency care demonstrates a heterogeneity of existing competencies, uncertainties and potential for improvement in the training of emergency physicians.

We found that approximately 6.5% of all prehospital physician cases in a representative cohort of emergency medical services were related to pediatric emergencies. This is consistent with the findings of other studies [20]. Most cases involved older children and adolescents, although a peak was observed in one-year-olds, underlining the vulnerability of both infants and teenagers. Trauma and neurological disorders represented the most frequent causes for deployment. While vital signs were almost universally recorded, invasive measures such as intravenous or intraosseous access were more commonly performed in older children. Other invasive procedures, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, intubation, and advanced trauma interventions, remain rare, reflecting both the infrequency and the high-stakes nature of such events. Our findings are consistent with the findings of Garrelfs et al., who also showed that invasive procedures are rarely necessary during prehospital pediatric emergency care [21].

The survey provided valuable insight into the level of knowledge and self-perceived competence of emergency physicians. Although many participants had attended additional pediatric-focused courses, self-reported competence varied considerably across scenarios. High confidence was expressed for common prehospital emergencies such as seizures, anaphylaxis, and hypoglycemia, whereas lower levels of confidence were expressed in the areas of neonatal care, pediatric polytrauma and resuscitation, which are regarded as critical emergencies. This aligns with the international finding that pediatric resuscitation is an area of insecurity for many providers [7, 8, 22]. In addition, one of the most common contributing factors to preventable deaths in children presenting to the hospital is delayed recognition of deterioration. The recognition of deterioration, which often occurs before cardiac arrest in children, obviously poses a broader diagnostic challenge [23]. Uncertainty in pediatric critical care may not relate only to technical resuscitation skills but also to the complex assessment of deterioration. It can be assumed that delayed recognition of deterioration could also apply to prehospital emergencies and could be perceived as challenging by physicians who are not specialized in pediatrics.

Our study further highlighted that knowledge acquisition largely occurs during initial training or through clinical practice, whereas ongoing pediatric-specific training, such as pediatric simulation opportunities, remains underutilized. A particularly noteworthy finding was the overwhelming demand among emergency physicians for regular, mandatory training in pediatric emergencies, especially in practical and simulation-based formats. Over 70% of respondents advocated for the integration of more pediatric content into existing emergency physician courses, and more than three-quarters supported recurring simulation training. This is consistent with current educational literature emphasizing the effectiveness of high-fidelity simulation, pediatric life support courses, and clinical rotations in improving competence and retention [24–26].

Taken together, these findings highlight a clear discrepancy between the low frequency but high acuity of pediatric emergencies and the self-perceived competencies of emergency physicians. Competencies are traditionally defined as “the ability to do something successfully or efficiently” [27]. Competencies are generally developed through the acquisition of knowledge and the accumulation of practical experience, with cognitive processes typically playing a dominant role in this process [10]. The competencies to take care of prehospital pediatric emergencies are the ability to do this successfully. The capacity to quantify and visualize these competencies is facilitated by the implementation of EPAs. In this context, EPAs may serve as a structured framework to define, assess, and operationalize competencies in prehospital pediatric care. However, their implementation in this setting requires careful adaptation to the specific challenges of prehospital medicine, particularly the rarity of critical pediatric cases.

Based on the EPAs for pediatric emergencies of the American Board of Pediatrics and the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, EPAs [28, 29] for training in prehospital emergency medicine should include the following:

On initial observation, it may appear that these EPAs, which are very broadly defined, are covered by the continuing education regulations for emergency medicine in Germany. While most pediatric cases may not require advanced invasive treatments, the minority of cases are often life-threatening and demand rapid, confident management. Nevertheless, the findings of the present survey suggest that a modest proportion of emergency physicians experience a degree of uncertainty with regard to the management of complex pediatric emergencies. This may suggest that the current training program is not adequate to address these challenges. Consequently, it is imperative to formulate “nested” EPAs within these broad EPAs. These nested EPAs should primarily address those areas where emergency physicians have identified knowledge gaps and a lack of competencies in the treatment of pediatric emergency patients. A key challenge in translating these EPAs into clinical practice is that competency acquisition cannot rely solely on real-world exposure, as critical pediatric emergencies occur infrequently. Consequently, EPAs in this context should not be viewed as exclusively experience-based but rather as competencies that can be developed and assessed through a combination of clinical exposure, structured training, and simulation-based education [8, 30]. In particular, high-fidelity simulation, case-based instruction, and mandatory recurring pediatric emergency training may represent feasible strategies to achieve and sustain EPA-related competencies despite the inherently low case volume. This need is further underscored by evidence that knowledge acquired during pediatric resuscitation training decays over time, highlighting the importance of regular refresher interventions [31]. Simulation-based assessment also enables standardized evaluation of both technical and non-technical competencies, including clinical decision-making, team communication, and situational awareness, all of which are essential in prehospital pediatric care. Our survey findings support this approach, as a substantial proportion of participants expressed a strong preference for regular, structured pediatric training, particularly in practical and simulation-based formats. However, several barriers may limit implementation in routine practice, including limited training resources, time constraints within existing EMS training programs, and the absence of standardized national curricula for pediatric prehospital care. To address these challenges, we propose a stepwise integration of EPAs into prehospital emergency medicine training in Germany. This could include (1) the incorporation of pediatric-specific EPAs into existing emergency medicine certification programs, (2) the development of mandatory, simulation-based refresher courses focusing on high-acuity, low-frequency scenarios, and (3) the use of EPA-based assessment tools to guide formative feedback and competency development.

Limitations

Limitations of our study include the retrospective analysis of pediatric emergencies and the selectivity of the survey cohort. First, the retrospective analysis relied on handwritten DIVI operational protocols, which may have been subject to incomplete or inconsistent documentation. Missing values, particularly for GCS and NACA scores, could have introduced bias in the interpretation of severity. Second, the study was conducted in a single urban region in Germany on one EMS physician ambulance car, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to rural settings or other healthcare systems with different structures of prehospital emergency care. The survey component also has inherent limitations. The response rate could not be determined, and it is possible that physicians with a particular interest or experience in pediatric care were more likely to participate, leading to selection bias. Furthermore, the self-reported levels of competence reflect subjective perceptions rather than objective performance measures and may therefore not fully represent actual clinical skills. The survey’s reliance on closed questions may have further restricted the scope of responses.

Conclusion

Pediatric emergencies represent a small but highly demanding share of prehospital cases, requiring both technical expertise and emotional resilience from emergency physicians. This study demonstrates that while most providers feel competent in managing common pediatric conditions, there are significant confidence gaps in rare but critical scenarios such as neonatal care, resuscitation and pediatric polytrauma. Despite high levels of general training, continuing education in pediatric emergency care is often insufficient and inconsistently practiced.

The results underscore the need for systematic improvements in pediatric emergency training, including mandatory and recurring simulation-based practice, expanded pediatric content in emergency medicine curricula, and structured opportunities for clinical exposure. The implementation, execution and review of entrustable professionals activities would strengthen physician preparedness and contribute to safer, more effective prehospital care for children in critical conditions.

Finally, although the study provides valuable insights into both epidemiological patterns and physician preparedness, the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions. Future studies should incorporate prospective multicenter designs, EPA assessments, and outcome measures to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how knowledge and competencies in the treatment of pediatric emergency medicine can be improved.
