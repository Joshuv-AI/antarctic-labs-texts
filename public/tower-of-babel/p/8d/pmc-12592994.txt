Dear Editor,

We thank the authors for their interest in our article, “Perme Score and Machine Learning for Detecting ICU-acquired Weakness (ICUAW): A Prospective Observational Cohort Study.”1

Our study investigated whether the Perme intensive care unit (ICU) Mobility Score, a functional mobility instrument, can be used to screen for ICUAW in adults undergoing prolonged invasive mechanical ventilation (MV), using the validated handgrip strength test (HGST) as a bedside reference. We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our aims and methods, and to address the four points that have been raised.

Feasibility of Perme Items in Invasively Ventilated Patients

The correspondents argue that several Perme items (bed mobility, sitting balance, sit-to-stand, standing balance, transfers, ambulation, and endurance) “cannot be performed” in intubated or ventilated patients, so the score would be infeasible. However, this characterization is inconsistent with the instrument's design and contemporary ICU practices. The Perme ICU Mobility Score was developed specifically for the ICU environment, where patients often have invasive devices and may be mechanically ventilated. The scale incorporates activity-based items and explicitly considers barriers to mobility, such as lines, drains, ventilation interfaces, and hemodynamic support. Items that are not yet achievable on a given ICU day are scored accordingly.2 This is a feature, not a flaw; it provides clinically meaningful information about the patient's functional capacity on a given day under real constraints, including invasive ventilation.

The feasibility and safety of early mobilization during MV are well supported. Pioneering studies have demonstrated that carefully screened ventilated patients can sit at the edge of the bed, stand, transfer to a chair, and ambulate short distances with the right staffing and equipment. These studies have also shown that the rate of adverse events is very low.3–6 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses corroborate that mobilization and rehabilitation are feasible and generally safe in the ICU and provide functional benefits.7 In this context, a mobility instrument that captures what can be achieved at the bedside, recognizing that gait and endurance may initially score zero, is appropriate and informative for clinical decision-making.

In our cohort of 97 patients, assessments were performed after the acute critical phase had resolved and only when the following safety criteria were met: Clinical stability, pressure support of 7 cm H2O, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cm H2O, and a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score of −1 to +1. Trained assessors applied a standardized protocol.1 As reported, gait and endurance were not performed at the initial assessment and were therefore scored as zero per protocol, reflecting real-world early ICU mobility rather than a failure of feasibility. Despite this conservative context, lower Perme scores were significantly associated with ICUAW (p < 0.001).1

Neurological Examination vs Functional Screening: Definition and Purpose

The correspondents suggest using a neurologist's clinical examination to diagnose weakness and localize the etiology central nervous system vs the peripheric nervous system. We agree that a neurological assessment is essential for determining the etiological classification. However, our study did not propose the Perme score as a replacement for a neurological examination or electrophysiology testing, nor as a tool for etiological classification. Our aim was pragmatic: To test whether a functional mobility score could screen for ICUAW risk and help physiotherapists and the interprofessional ICU team prioritize bedside strength assessment and early rehabilitation when a validated bedside reference is feasible under light sedation or awake conditions.

ICU-acquired weakness is defined as generalized limb weakness that arises during critical illness and is associated with adverse outcomes.8,9 While the MRC sum score is the common reference when feasible, alternatives such as the HGST have been used in ICU settings and provide prognostic information.10 In our protocol, we used the HGST as the bedside diagnostic reference for ICUAW with sex-specific cutoff scores (<11 kg for men and <7 kg for women) and restricted testing to patients who could follow commands under light sedation (RASS of approximately −1 to +1).1 This approach aligns with guideline-concordant sedation targets and acknowledges that deep sedation and delirium limit the validity of volitional strength testing.11,12

Importantly, we evaluated the Perme score alongside patient age and illness severity (SAPS 3) in multivariable logistic models. In these models, lower Perme scores remained independently associated with ICUAW (adjusted odds ratio per Perme point: 0.86; p = 0.0004). The corresponding model achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.846, with high sensitivity (96.4%) and modest specificity (38.1%), properties consistent with a screening instrument.

In complementary machine learning analyses (LASSO and Random Forest), Perme was consistently retained and prioritized. A five-variable model (Perme, SAPS 3, age, blood glucose, and lactate) achieved an AUC of 0.874. This finding reinforces the discriminative value of functional mobility in this setting.1 These results support our interpretation of the Perme score as a practical bedside functional screen rather than an etiological arbiter.

Premorbid Strength, CNS Causes, and Potential Bias

The correspondents argue that premorbid strength was not assessed and that CNS causes of weakness were not explicitly excluded (e.g., prior stroke), which could confound the findings. By definition, ICUAW concerns current weakness during the critical illness episode.8,9 Premorbid strength is rarely measurable in the ICU and is not required for ICUAW diagnosis. Indeed, most ICUAW studies proceed without objective premorbid dynamometry for this reason. To reduce potential confounding factors, we implemented explicit exclusion criteria (pre-existing neuromuscular disease or limb amputation), ensured adequate arousal and cooperation (GCS >8 with motor 6 or RASS −1 to +1), and adjusted for age and severity (SAPS 3) in multivariable models.1

We acknowledge the possibility that there may be unrecognized CNS contributors in critically ill populations. However, such residual, nondifferential misclassification (e.g., due to unmeasured premorbid deficits or unrecognized CNS lesions) would be expected to bias toward the null, attenuating the association between Perme and ICUAW. Despite these conservative pressures, the association persisted and remained independent in adjusted models. Similarly, Perme was consistently selected in machine-learning analyses. This pattern argues against an inflated effect driven by premorbid confounding.

We emphasize again that our a priori objective was functional risk stratification, not etiologic adjudication. In this context, a bedside mobility score that identifies patients with a higher probability of ICUAW and is supported by a concurrent strength reference is clinically useful, even without formal CNS/PNS localization. As reviews of ICUAW note, screening tools and functional assessments can guide resource allocation (e.g., physiotherapy intensity) and trigger more detailed neurological evaluation when warranted.8,9

Mood/Depression and HGST

The correspondents suggest that mood should have been assessed because it can influence HGST performance. We agree that volitional effort affects any volitional strength test. However, in the acute ICU, particularly among invasively ventilated patients, formal depression scales are neither routinely validated nor feasible and are often confounded by sedation and delirium.11–13 We maximized reproducibility by standardizing HGST (dominant hand, repeated trials, maximal verbal encouragement) and restricting testing to patients able to follow commands under light sedation (RASS −1 to +1).1 These steps align with ICU best practices and minimize the impact of fluctuating arousal, attention, and cooperation.11,12 This limitation also applies to other volitional tests (e.g., MRC sumscore), so it does not negate the observed Perme–ICUAW association nor the Perme score's value as a bedside screening tool to prioritize confirmatory strength or neurological assessment when indicated.8,9

What Our Study Did and Did Not Claim

For clarity, our manuscript did not claim that the Perme score classifies muscle weakness or substitutes for a neurological assessment. Rather, we concluded that the Perme score is feasible for invasively ventilated ICU patients when applied under standard safety criteria and that lower Perme scores are independently associated with ICUAW, as defined by a bedside strength reference HGST.1

We reported a cutoff of ≤9, which yielded a sensitivity of 76.3% and a specificity of 71.4% [odds ratio (OR) = 8.06] for ICUAW. We presented multivariable and machine learning results that suggest functional mobility encapsulates clinically relevant information about the risk of weakness.1

We also explicitly noted the limited negative predictive value of the multivariable model. This is another reason to position Perme as a complementary screening tool rather than a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Conclusion

In summary, the Perme ICU Mobility Score is a functional tool specific to ICUs that can be applied to invasively ventilated patients under standard safety criteria. Items that are not yet achievable are scored as such by design, providing day-of-care information about capacity and barriers. In our cohort of prolonged MV, lower Perme scores were independently associated with ICUAW as defined by a bedside strength reference. There was supportive discrimination in both logistic and machine-learning frameworks. These findings support the Perme score as a feasible bedside functional screen that complements, rather than replaces, direct strength testing and neurological evaluation. This helps prioritize confirmatory assessment and early rehabilitation.
