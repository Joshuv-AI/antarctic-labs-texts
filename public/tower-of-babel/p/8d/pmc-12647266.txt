The ketogenic diet in Parkinson’s disease: a potential therapeutic strategy
Authors: Barbara Pokora, Kacper Pokora, Agata Binienda, Jakub Fichna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterized by debilitating motor and non-motor symptoms. Its complex pathogenesis involves dopaminergic neuron degeneration, α-synuclein aggregation, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Current symptomatic treatments offer limited symptom improvement, highlighting the urgent need for new strategies, including lifestyle modifications. The ketogenic diet (KD), a dietary approach that shifts the body’s primary energy source from glucose to ketone bodies (KBs) like β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB), has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential. This review explores KD as a promising, multifaceted intervention for PD. The potential beneficial impact of KD on PD stems from several key mechanisms. β-HB exhibits potent anti-inflammatory properties, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and microglial activation by inhibiting pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome. The diet also improves mitochondrial function by enhancing electron transport chain activity and increasing ATP synthesis, which is crucial given the mitochondrial deficits observed in PD. Furthermore, KBs directly alleviate oxidative stress through enhanced antioxidant defenses. KD offers neuroprotection for dopaminergic neurons, provides an alternative fuel source to the brain, and optimizes cerebral glucose metabolism. It also boosts levels of essential neurotrophic factors, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). Beyond direct neurological effects, KD may enhance levodopa efficacy by improving its bioavailability and appears to play a crucial role in modulating gut microbiota dysbiosis, a frequently observed and potentially contributing factor in PD. While further research is essential, the comprehensive effects of KD on PD-related pathophysiology position it as a promising non-pharmacological strategy.

Introduction

Given the rising prevalence of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), alongside the absence of effective disease-modifying therapies and the limitations of current symptomatic treatments, the need for comprehensive and innovative approaches has become increasingly evident.

In recent years, dietary interventions have gained attention as potential therapeutic strategies, supported by a growing body of research. Among these, the ketogenic diet (KD) has emerged as a particularly noteworthy strategy for managing neurodegenerative diseases. Although KD has recently attracted public interest for its role in weight reduction, its neurological benefits were first described in 1921 by Dr. Russell Wilder.

The present work aims to synthesize and critically assess current evidence on the effects of KD in PD. Specifically, it examines the diet’s influence on key pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the disease and reviews findings from clinical trials evaluating its impact on various clinical and biochemical outcomes in individuals with PD.

Parkinson’s disease (PD)

PD is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Each year, 5 to 35 out of every 100,000 people are diagnosed with PD, and its global prevalence for 2050 has been projected to reach 230 to 320 cases per 100,000 [1, 2].

The disease is primarily characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, postural instability, rigidity, and tremor, as well as non-motor symptoms. The latter include cognitive impairment, depression, apathy, REM sleep behavior disorder, impaired sense of smell, and urinary system dysfunction. Additionally, non-motor symptoms include gastrointestinal (GI) disorders such as constipation, dysphagia, and delayed gastric emptying [3, 4]. Interestingly, these often precede the onset of motor symptoms and are increasingly considered prodromal markers of the disease [5].

PD is mostly a sporadic disorder, but about 25% of the overall risk of the disease is due to genetic variation [6]. The pathogenesis of PD is complex and still not fully understood. It is believed that various factors influence the development of PD. Among the most important are the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and the loss of the substantia nigra, combined with the intracellular aggregation of α-synuclein, which forms Lewy bodies and neurites [7]. Furthermore, issues such as gut dysbiosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction are also considered critical contributors to PD pathogenesis [8].

Due to the uncertain etiology of PD, there is currently no effective causal treatment for the disease, nor has a prevention strategy been developed. Currently, levodopa remains the gold standard in PD treatment. Due to its methylation by a catecholamine O-methyl transferase (COMT), levodopa is administered in conjunction with COMT inhibitors such as entacapone, tolcapone, and benzaseride [9]. While levodopa is the most effective drug, it has its limitations: over time, the response to treatment decreases, and increasing the dose becomes necessary. This, in turn, is associated with side effects such as the appearance of dyskinesias. Other drugs used for this condition also only address the symptoms, not the cause of the disease [10, 11]. Consequently, there is an increasing need for other methods to combat PD, such as lifestyle modifications to prevent the disease and slow its progression in those who have already developed it [12].

Ketogenic diet (KD)

KD is a nutritional intervention designed to shift the body’s principal energy substrate from glucose to ketone bodies (KBs). Under typical dietary conditions, carbohydrate metabolism supplies the majority of energy requirements. When carbohydrate availability is significantly reduced, as in KD, lipid catabolism increases, resulting in the hepatic production of KBs (acetone, acetoacetate, and β-hydroxybutyrate). Once circulating KB concentrations exceed a defined threshold, the metabolic state of ketosis is achieved. Ketosis may also be induced by prolonged fasting or severe caloric restriction. However, such interventions, unlike KD, are associated with an elevated risk of adverse outcomes, including malnutrition [8]. Although acetoacetate (ACA), β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB), and acetone are all KBs that provide alternative energy substrates for the brain, research investigating the impact of KD on neurodegenerative disorders predominantly emphasizes β-HB. Several factors account for this focus. First, under conditions of nutritional ketosis, β-HB constitutes the principal KB in systemic circulation, representing the majority of the dynamic range in circulating ketone concentrations. Moreover, plasma β-HB levels increase more rapidly and to a greater extent than ACA or acetone during ketogenesis [13]. Also, beyond its role as a metabolic substrate, β-HB functions as a signaling molecule [13, 14]. Furthermore, β-HB exhibits greater chemical stability than ACA, which is susceptible to spontaneous decarboxylation into acetone [15]. Collectively, these considerations explain why β-HB has become the principal focus of research, as it is thought to account for the majority of the therapeutic effects associated with KD.

KD was first introduced in 1921 by Dr. Wilder as a treatment for pediatric epilepsy [16]. He proposed a macronutrient ratio of 4:1 (fat to non-fat). This diet proved effective, with more than half of the children with epilepsy showing clinical improvement, leading to its adoption as a standard therapy.

However, strict adherence to Dr. Wilder’s protocol required substantial lifestyle changes, making long-term maintenance challenging [16]. Consequently, based on the results of several studies on the influence of “nutritional ketosis” on the course of neurological diseases, KD has been modified. During the classic KD, i.e., the diet developed by Dr. Wilder, carbohydrate consumption is limited to 10% of the total calorie intake [16–18]. The Modified Atkins Diet (MAD), introduced in 2003, is characterized by fewer restrictions on protein intake and total calories, making it more practical for long-term use. According to its assumptions, the ratio of fats to non-fat products is 1–2:1 [19, 20]. Another variant, the Medium-Chain Triglyceride Ketogenic Diet (MCT-KD), is a subtype of KD, in which the main fatty acids are octanoic acid and decanoic acid, which are metabolized faster than long-chain acids that form the basis of classic KD [21]. This results in a greater amount of KBs compared to the kilocalories consumed, allowing the patient to consume larger amounts of carbohydrates and proteins, making the diet easier to adhere to. Additionally, this subtype has been associated with enhanced mitochondrial function [20, 22].

Other KD subtypes include: the very low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD), with carbohydrate intake less than 10% at 800 kcal per day [23]; low glycemic index treatment (LGIT) – a high-fat diet that prefers products with a low glycemic index [24], which raise blood glucose levels more slowly and more gradually; the cyclic KD [25], which consists of periods in which the classic KD is used, alternating with periods of a high-carbohydrate diet; the targeted ketogenic diet (TKD), in which larger amount of carbohydrates can be consumed during periods of more intense physical activity [25] (Table 1). An increasing body of evidence suggests that the KD, owing to its metabolic and biochemical properties, may have therapeutic potential in a range of neurological disorders beyond its established applications [8]. Unfortunately, the use of KD also has certain limitations. In people using KD, side effects such as hypoglycemic episodes, the development of dyslipidemia, and GI problems are possible [26].

Ketogenic diet in PD

PD is characterized by a complex, multifactorial etiology. Current research implicates several interrelated pathogenic mechanisms, including persistent neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction [27], excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [28], reduced dopamine synthesis [8], impaired cerebral glucose metabolism [8], and the accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein in the form of Lewy bodies [29]. As with other neurodegenerative disorders, no disease-modifying therapy is currently available for PD. Consequently, strategies aimed at halting or at least slowing neuronal degeneration within the CNS remain a critical therapeutic priority.

In line with this objective, an increasing number of studies have explored interventions targeting both the onset and progression of PD. One such intervention is KD, which may influence disease pathogenesis and modify its clinical course (Fig. 2). Although the precise mechanisms underlying KD‘s effects in PD remain incompletely understood, evidence suggests that the diet can modulate glucose utilization in the brain, neurotransmitter metabolism, insulin signaling, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and ion channel activity, particularly potassium channels [20]. The impact of KD on neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation is a key factor that can promote the development of PD. It is characterized by microglial activation and enhanced release of inflammatory factors, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), IL-2, IL-10, IL-4, IL-6, and interferon γ, which contribute to neuronal injury and death [30, 31]. Growing evidence indicates that KD can mitigate this neuroinflammatory response, and the anti-inflammatory potential of KD likely stems from the synergy of multiple mechanisms [32, 33].

In animal models, KD has been shown to significantly reduce neuroinflammation. For example, in a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) induced mouse model of PD; KD was found to reduce levels of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and to decrease microglial activation in the substantia nigra. This intervention also enhanced dopaminergic transmission and motor function, with KD-fed mice performing better in the motor performance tests [34, 35].

β-HB, the principal KB, is well recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties. It upregulates the expression of anti-inflammatory genes, including NF-κBIA, MAP3K8, and TLR5, while concurrently downregulating pro-inflammatory genes such as TNFSF6, TNF-α, and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) [36–39]. In addition, β-HB exerts neuroprotective effects by attenuating microglial activation through stimulation of the hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCA2). As demonstrated by Fu et al. [32], activation of this pathway suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production via NF-κB inhibition and promotes the synthesis of prostaglandin D2, which in turn further attenuates NF-κB signaling and associated inflammatory cascades [40].

Furthermore, β-HB binding to HCA1 or fatty acids binding to the G protein–coupled receptor 40 (GPR40) enhances the expression of the scaffold protein β-arrestin-2 (ARRB2). This, in turn, inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation through an ARRB2-dependent mechanism [40–42].

Another significant anti-inflammatory mechanism influenced by KD is the inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activity. β-HB prevents the reduction of cytoplasmic potassium ion concentrations, thereby inhibiting NLRP3 activation, which would otherwise trigger caspase-1 activation and subsequent pro-inflammatory cytokine release in response to potassium depletion [36, 43].

Finally, KD‘s anti-inflammatory actions extend to the modulation of the Akt/GSK-3β/CREB signaling pathway, mediated by histone acetylation in the promoter region of the metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) in a rat model of PD [44].

The impact of KD on dopaminergic neurons

Apoptosis, the predominant mode of dopaminergic neuronal death in PD, plays a central role in disease progression, and its inhibition contributes significantly to neuroprotection [45]. The significance of this process is underscored by the fact that the primary pharmacological treatment for PD is levodopa, a precursor to dopamine [46]. KD engages several anti-apoptotic mechanisms that enhance neuronal survival. In animal models, KD feeding reduced the expression of pro-apoptotic mediators such as clusterin, caspase-3, and Bax, while upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 [47, 48]. KD also increases calbindin activity, acting as an intracellular calcium buffer, and prevents the dissociation of the pro-apoptotic factor Bad from its chaperone 14–3-3, thereby stabilizing neuronal survival pathways [49]. At the mitochondrial level, KBs attenuate the intrinsic apoptotic cascade by decreasing ROS production, inhibiting the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, and reducing cytochrome c release and subsequent caspase activation [50]. Additionally, KD has been shown to suppress apoptosis in hippocampal neurons by downregulating the AMPK pathway and reducing HSP70 expression [51]. Together, these effects highlight the capacity of KD to preserve dopaminergic neurons by directly interfering with apoptotic signaling cascades in PD.

β-HB has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects on dopaminergic neurons [52], with consistent evidence from MPTP-induced models of PD, where it prevents the loss of dopaminergic cells in the substantia nigra [53]. In vitro studies demonstrated that KD-fed rats showed decreased apoptotic activity in the dentate gyrus [54]. Additionally, in a mouse model of PD, octanoic acid (C8) significantly reduced MPTP-induced damage to dopaminergic neurotransmission in the striatum [55]. Studies have also shown that β-HB increases oxidative phosphorylation via a Complex II-dependent mechanism. Tieu et al. [56] demonstrated that mice injected with MPTP but also receiving β-HB exhibited a smaller reduction in substantia nigra neurons, indicating that β-HB can attenuate the neurotoxic effects of MPTP on dopaminergic cell bodies. Furthermore, after MPTP administration, dopamine and its metabolite levels were measured in mice, revealing lower concentrations of these compounds in animals deprived of the protective effect of β-HB [56]. A more recent study by Jiang et al. [57] demonstrated that mice administered MPTP and fed KD were characterized by less motor dysfunction, dopaminergic neuron loss, and neuroinflammation than mice that only received MPTP.

The impact of KD on mitochondria

Research increasingly suggests that mitochondria, key organelles responsible for cellular energy production, play a significant role in the pathophysiology of PD. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are considered central features of neurodegenerative diseases [26], and the long-term use of KD may positively affect mitochondrial function through several mechanisms.

Research indicates that defects in oxidative phosphorylation contribute to the pathogenesis of PD. This is clearly demonstrated by experiments in which the administration of the neurotoxin MPTP, which blocks Complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, induces characteristic features of PD in mice [56, 57]. These findings are not exclusive to animal studies, as a reduction in Complex I has also been observed in the tissues of human PD patients [58].

The mechanism behind the neuroprotective action of ketones is complex and often linked to improved mitochondrial function. A critical aspect of this involves their interaction with mitochondrial Complex I. Reduced activity of mitochondrial Complex I is believed to play a crucial role in the demise of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. In this context, ketones offer a vital advantage: they bypass Complex I, providing threatened neurons with an alternative fuel source while simultaneously enhancing overall mitochondrial function and increasing ATP production [59]. Evidence from various studies supports this mitochondrial pathway. For instance, in vitro studies demonstrated that mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction, induced by exogenous inhibitors of Complex I (rotenone) and Complex II (3-nitropropionic acid), was alleviated by KBs [59]. Further underscoring this, one specific in vitro experiment observed that β-HB protected cultured midbrain neurons from the toxic effects of MPTP on the mitochondrial respiratory chain [60]. Beyond direct mitochondrial support, KD also protects dopaminergic neurons in rat models of PD by upregulating glutathione, highlighting an additional protective mechanism [61].

KD also supports the repair and optimization of mitochondrial respiratory chain function. Studies have shown that four weeks of exposure to KBs combined with glucose restriction significantly restored the stability and activity of mitochondrial Complex I, enhanced ATP synthesis, and increased the NAD+/NADH ratio [62]. This suggests that ketones facilitate the repair of Complex I damage, which, as previously indicated, is compromised in PD.

The impact of KD on ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS)

ROS are a group of chemically reactive molecules derived from molecular oxygen that arise primarily as byproducts of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, although they can also be generated by enzymatic systems such as NADPH oxidases, xanthine oxidase, and cytochrome P450 enzymes [63]. At physiological concentrations, ROS function as important secondary messengers in cell signaling pathways, regulating processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, and host defense [64]. However, when produced in excess, ROS can overwhelm cellular antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative stress and the disruption of essential biomolecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, and membrane lipids. Representative ROS include the superoxide anion (O₂•−), hydroxyl radical (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [65].

Closely related are RNS, a class of reactive molecules generated primarily from nitric oxide (NO•) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂•). RNS can interact with ROS to produce highly damaging species such as peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which contributes to nitrosative stress. Similar to ROS, low to moderate levels of RNS play physiological roles in vasodilation, neurotransmission, and immune regulation, while their overproduction leads to pathological modifications of proteins, DNA, and lipids [65].

Research highlights the crucial role of oxidative stress in the pathophysiology of PD [50, 66]. ROS and RNS are closely associated with the inflammatory response in PD [63]. Moreover, oxidative stress contributes to PD progression by disrupting dopamine metabolism [66, 67].

KD shows significant potential in alleviating oxidative stress by acting on multiple levels. Evidence from animal models supports this, showing that mice fed KD exhibit lower ROS production compared to mice on a standard diet, thereby demonstrating the systemic effect of this dietary intervention [68].

KD has been shown in in vitro studies to regulate histone deacetylases (HDAC1, HDAC3, and HDAC4). Inhibition of these enzymes increases histone acetylation, thereby enhancing antioxidant system function and upregulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) activity [39, 69]. Neuroinflammation can also be attenuated via a cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) dependent pathway through activation of PPARγ [69].

Another mechanism by which KD affects mitochondria is by increasing the expression of uncoupling proteins (UCPs) [40], which are located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. UCPs exhibit neuroprotective effects by regulating proton transport and reducing the production of ROS [70].

Moreover, KBs, such as β-HB and ACA, are crucial in the process of attenuating oxidative stress. Together, they reduce ROS production by acting on mitochondrial respiratory Complex I. ACA alone further enhances the survival of hippocampal cells by decreasing ROS generation [71, 72]. In vitro studies confirm that treatment of cells experiencing oxidative stress with ACA and β-HB effectively lowers ROS production and improves cell viability [72, 73]. Furthermore, a reduction in free radical levels is also achieved through improved efficiency of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex, evidenced by increased NADH oxidation and inhibition of mitochondrial membrane permeability [74].

The impact of KD on abnormal brain glucose metabolism

The brain is the most energy-intensive organ in the body. While its primary energy substrate is glucose, in situations of glucose scarcity, KBs can cover up to 60% of its energy demand [75]. When KBs concentrations exceed 4 mmol/L, they become the CNSs primary energy source [26]. Research has shown that deriving energy from KBs instead of glucose can positively affect the nervous system [76]. β-HB supplies the brain with more energy per unit of oxygen than glucose, and its neuroprotective effect is linked to increased mitochondrial respiration and enhanced ATP production [26].

The influence of KBs on brain metabolism is well-documented. A study where healthy individuals received a β-HB infusion to achieve a concentration of approximately 5.5 mmol/L demonstrated a 14% reduction in brain glucose utilization and a significant 30% increase in cerebral blood flow, with unchanged oxygen consumption [77]. When ketones cover most of the brain’s energy demands, the remaining glucose can be more efficiently utilized for other purposes, such as the synthesis of alanine and glutamate, glycogenesis, and antioxidant protection [20].

KD, which supplies β-HB, also exhibits additional beneficial effects. Research suggests it can reduce demyelination, prevent oligodendrocyte death—cells responsible for producing myelin—and protect against axonal degeneration induced by glucose deprivation [78].

Furthermore, sustained ketosis has been shown to enhance glucose transport into the brain. In rat models, this effect is associated with elevated levels of the glucose transporter GLUT1 [79]. Additional contributing factors include increased expression of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) receptors, IGF1-binding protein (which protects IGF1 from proteolysis) in Purkinje cells [8], as well as upregulated mRNA expression of GLUT1 and GLUT3 [80]. Another mechanism involves the enhanced metabolic activity of astrocytes under the influence of KD [81]. These glial cells activate both glycolysis and glycogenolysis [82].

The impact of KD on brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)

BDNF is a protein essential for the function and development of the CNS. It promotes neuronal differentiation, development, and survival, and plays a key role in neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and synaptic plasticity [83, 84]. Notably, reduced BDNF levels within the nigrostriatal pathway have been documented in both patients with PD and animal models of the disorder. Simultaneously, BDNF administration has been shown to enhance dopaminergic neuron survival, improve dopaminergic neurotransmission, and augment motor performance in PD models [85].

β-HB elevates BDNF protein levels by increasing mitochondrial respiration and inhibiting HDACs, thereby activating promoter IV of the BDNF gene. This regulatory process involves the transcription factor NF-κB and histone acetyltransferase p300 [8].

BDNF is not the only neuroprotective factor upregulated by KD. Other important neurotrophins include NT-3, GDNF, and molecular chaperones, which are proteins that prevent the aggregation of polypeptides into potentially toxic structures [26].

The Impact of KD on PD treatment

Levodopa remains the first-line therapy for PD, however, its use comes with significant challenges. Beyond its known side effects, levodopa’s effectiveness often diminishes as the disease progresses [10]. GI dysfunction, a common comorbidity in PD, further contributes to fluctuations in levodopa absorption and plasma concentrations, thereby triggering or exacerbating the “on-off” phenomenon [86, 87]. Oral administration of levodopa depends on adequate GI function, yet the drug has been shown to delay gastric emptying in healthy individuals and to aggravate GI symptoms in patients with PD [88]. Furthermore, evidence indicates a direct association between levodopa sensitivity and the extent of α-synuclein accumulation within the enteric nervous system (ENS) [46, 88]. These factors underscore the importance of identifying strategies to enhance both the efficacy and tolerability of levodopa.

In this context, the use of KD alongside levodopa appears to be more beneficial than levodopa alone. This is primarily due to its potential for improved bioavailability through restricting dietary protein [89, 90]. Dietary protein can affect the bioavailability of levodopa by delaying gastric emptying. Moreover, levodopa itself is an amino acid. Other amino acids, especially large neutral amino acids, can dampen levodopa intestinal uptake and compete with its active transport across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [89, 91].

β-amyloid and TAU protein in PD

Although the accumulation of α-synuclein in Lewy bodies is the defining hallmark of PD, growing evidence suggests that other proteins, most notably β-amyloid (Aβ) and tau, also contribute, particularly in cases with cognitive decline. Traditionally associated with AD, these proteins are increasingly recognized as part of PD pathology, where KD may hold potential to modulate their effects.

Despite extensive research, the role of Aβ in PD-related cognitive decline remains controversial. Some studies report no significant difference in Aβ burden between PD patients with and without cognitive impairment [92, 93], while others demonstrate elevated levels of Aβ in cognitively impaired PD patients [94, 95]. Aβ plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are present in 30–40% of PD patients and may contribute to clinical symptoms of the disease [95, 96].

In addition, tau has been increasingly implicated in PD. Elevated tau levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have been linked to clinical manifestations in PD patients [97]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies further support the role for tau in PD pathology [98, 99]. Notably, some studies have found tau inclusions in up to 50% of PD brains, suggesting that tau is an underappreciated component of the disease and may represent a novel therapeutic target.

Most studies investigating the relationship between KD and Aβ or tau have been conducted in the context of AD. Rodents treated with KD, exogenous β-HB, or medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) showed reduced brain Aβ levels, protection against Aβ toxicity, and improved mitochondrial function [100, 101]. In transgenic mouse models of AD, KD reduced soluble Aβ deposits in the brain by 25% after only 40 days [102]. Furthermore, KD treatment has been suggested to influence the deposition of both Aβ and tau, potentially slowing the progression of the underlying pathology [103, 104]. Clinical studies in AD patients have reported favorable changes in disease-related biomarkers with KD, including increased CSF Aβ (reflecting reduced cortical deposition) and significantly decreased tau levels [105, 106].

The role of gut microbiota in PD and the impact of KD

In recent years, the influence of the gut microbiota on the development of various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, has been increasingly studied. Whether observed changes in the microbiota are a cause or a consequence of these diseases remains a controversial issue. Although gut microbiota dysbiosis in PD is extensively analyzed, its causal mechanisms and the possibilities for intervention still require further research [107, 108] (Fig. 3). The link between the gastrointestinal tract and PD

Communication between the gut and the brain occurs via the gut-brain axis, a bidirectional connection between the CNS and ENS. This axis facilitates the flow of information between these organs, encompassing diverse pathways: neurotransmitters, the endocrine system, immunological mechanisms, and bacterial metabolites. This enables the microbiota to influence brain processes like neurotransmission and vice versa [109, 110].

GI dysfunctions are a key non-motor symptom of PD, often appearing at a very early stage of the disease. Some studies have shown that PD patients suffer from constipation for over 20 years before the onset of motor symptoms [111]. GI symptoms, including excessive salivation, dysphagia, constipation, esophageal motility disorders, and gastric abnormalities, occur many years before the appearance of motor symptoms and may be associated with an increased risk of developing PD [112]. Furthermore, research indicates a higher incidence of PD in individuals with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and markers of immune system activity, such as calprotectin, are elevated in fecal samples of PD patients [113]. It is also suggested that Crohn’s disease and PD may share common pathophysiological factors. For instance, variants in the CARD15 and LRRK2 genes are implicated in the pathogenesis of both conditions [114, 115]. Further connecting the gut microbiome to PD, Li et al. [116] reported that animals lacking dendritic cell factor 1 displayed not only PD-associated behavioral changes but also significant alterations within their gut microbiome.

These observations support Braak’s hypothesis, which postulates that α-synuclein pathology, underlying PD, may originate in the GI tract. This theory is further supported by the presence of Lewy bodies in the ENS even before their appearance in other areas of the body, including the CNS [117]. Braak’s hypothesis suggests that the α-synuclein pathology begins in the ENS and then migrates to the brain, passing through the brainstem, midbrain, basal forebrain, and finally the cortical areas [118, 119]. Conversely, if α-synuclein pathology originates in the olfactory bulbs, it spreads via the olfactory tract into the brain. However, it still reaches the ENS, where it causes neuronal damage [120].

The vagus nerve appears to be a conduit for gut-brain communication, as α-synuclein aggregates are found in structures that parasympathetically innervate the intestines (as well as in the glossopharyngeal nerve) [118, 119, 121]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that complete truncal vagotomy may be associated with a reduced risk of PD development compared to highly selective vagotomy or control conditions [122, 123]. This theory has also been tested in animal models, where α-synuclein fibrils injected into the intestinal tissue of healthy rodents induced pathology in the vagus nerve and brainstem [124]. Further underscoring the gut-brain axis significance, a study revealed that patient-derived α-synuclein aggregates are capable of inducing both nigrostriatal alterations and ENS pathology following either intestinal or striatal injection in non-human primates [125]. However, it is important to note that Braak’s theory remains unconfirmed, with some studies even contradicting it [126]. Therefore, further research on this issue is essential.

Microbiota changes in PD

The fact that PD patients exhibit changes in the gut microbiota composition and intestinal inflammation has been presented multiple times [109, 127–130]. The typical composition of gut microbiota in healthy individuals primarily includes the phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, accounting for over 90% of the total bacteria, as well as Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia [131].

Patients with PD exhibit characteristic changes in their gut microbiota composition. Many of these patients were found to have small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), which accounts for symptoms such as malabsorption, abdominal distension, and flatulence [132]. Studies conducted by Scheperjans et al. [133] showed that, compared to a healthy population, fecal samples from PD patients had a significant reduction in Prevotellaceae (77.6%) alongside an increase in Enterobacteriaceae. These findings correlated with the severity of motor problems [134].

Another study also highlighted the significant role of the gut microbiota in PD pathogenesis, linking specific bacteria, which are more prevalent in the guts of individuals with PD, to the production of endotoxins that cause inflammation and methane production [135]. For instance, certain strains of Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae, which are present in larger quantities in patients with PD, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella, were found to secrete pro-inflammatory lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which are associated with more severe motor symptoms [134]. Additionally, PD patients exhibit higher levels of methane-producing bacteria, like Christensenella spp. and Methanobrevibacter, which increase intraluminal pressure and decrease peristaltic movements, leading to constipation [136]. Furthermore, a higher prevalence of H. pylori infection has been reported in individuals with PD [137]. Notably, the presence of H. pylori may intensify motor dysfunction and accelerate disease progression, while its eradication has been demonstrated to improve motor symptoms and facilitate levodopa absorption [138, 139].

Beyond these findings, PD is associated with an elevated count of Akkermansia bacteria and a reduced abundance of Prevotellaceae. Decreased levels of Prevotellaceae correlate with decreased mucin production, which, along with the changes in Akkermansia abundance, can contribute to increased intestinal permeability. This, in turn, leads to the formation of what is known as a “leaky gut” [110, 140]. This impaired gut barrier function and microbial imbalance can promote the translocation of bacteria, as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines and endotoxins (e.g., LPS), into systemic circulation [141, 142]. This process could contribute to systemic inflammation, BBB impairment, and ROS production. Ultimately, it may impact abnormal α-synuclein folding and exacerbate PD pathology [143].

However, these are not the only consequences of dysbiosis. In fecal samples obtained from PD patients, a significant decrease in short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) has been observed [144]. This reduction in SCFAs may play a crucial role in the ENS changes and gut dysmotility seen in PD. SCFAs are synthesized from indigestible carbohydrates by bacteria such as Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Bifidobacteria [145], as well as Akkermansia, Lactobacillus [142, 146], and Prevotella [147]. SCFAs inhibit the growth of Enterobacteriaceae [148], which produce LPS, thus indirectly suppressing inflammatory processes. Furthermore, butyrate, which is one of the SCFAs, can inhibit NF-κB activation, HDACs, and upregulate PPAR-γ [149], demonstrating anti-inflammatory and oxidative stress-modulating effects. Decreased butyrate production is also associated with elevated oxygen levels in the intestinal mucosa, increasing the risk of oxidative damage [150]. Patients suffering from PD showed a reduced population of SCFAs-producing microbes, such as Roseburia, Faecalibacterium [151], and Prevotellaceae. Through reduction in beneficial SCFAs, gut dysbiosis impares gut function and contributing to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. KD influences the brain indirectly via SCFAs, which cross the BBB by monocarboxylate transporters on astrocytes. SCFAs exhibit neuroprotective effects and enhance learning and memory [152, 153].

Notably, the reduction in Prevotellaceae in PD patients has several implications. Firstly, it decreases the biosynthesis of essential vitamins like thiamine and folic acid, which may contribute to observed vitamin deficiencies [154–156]. Secondly, a decline in Prevotellaceae coupled with an increase in Lactobacillaceae has been associated with lower concentrations of ghrelin and hydrogen sulfide [157]. Ghrelin, an intestinal hormone crucial for nigrostriatal dopamine homeostasis, shows impaired secretion in PD patients [158, 159]. Conversely, hydrogen sulfide exhibits protective potential for dopaminergic neurons. [160, 161].

Yet another mechanism by which microbiota affect the course of PD is the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). It is the key regulator at the crossroads of cellular pathways involved in inflammation, immunity, gut barrier integrity, and neurotransmitter synthesis [162, 163]. Its effects depend on the activating ligand, mediating either pro-inflammatory responses - via NF-κB - or anti-inflammatory actions [164]. AhR is strongly influenced by metabolites produced by commensal microbes, especially from dietary tryptophan (Trp) catabolism. Species such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Clostridium generate AhR agonists that can suppress the pro-inflammatory phenotype of glial cells [163, 165].

In PD, altered Trp metabolism and disrupted AhR signaling have been observed in patients, with reductions in neuroprotective Trp metabolites frequently reported [166]. Experimental evidence shows that dietary Trp supplementation improves motor deficits, enhances tyrosine hydroxylase expression, inhibits NF-κB activation in the substantia nigra, and lowers circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines in toxin-induced, animal PD models [167].

The impact of KD on restoring proper microbiota

Microbiota-targeted interventions, including dietary changes, probiotics, antibiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), are increasingly recognized as promising strategies for both preventing and treating PD [134, 168]. KD has a documented impact on gut microbial composition and function, suggesting it may exert beneficial effects in PD through the gut–brain axis [169].

KD has been shown to counteract dysbiosis by restoring healthier microbial proportions. For example, it increases the abundance of Bacteroides and Prevotella while reducing Firmicutes and Actinobacteria [169, 170], essentially reversing the microbial imbalance typically observed in PD [133]. Clinical studies in obese, insulin-resistant patients further support this effect, showing that VLCKD increased Bacteroides and decreased Firmicutes [169, 171]. Conversely, a high intake of saturated fats induces different microbial shifts, including increases in Bacteroides, Bilophila, and anaerobes [172, 173]. Mouse studies demonstrate similar diet-driven changes, such as elevations in Actinobacteria (Bifidobacterium, Adlercreutzia), lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, Streptococcus), and Verrucomicrobia (Akkermansia muciniphila) [174].

More recent work has clarified that KD not only modifies microbial communities but also promotes taxa with neuroprotective potential. Both KD and MCT-KD increase beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, Blautia, and Romboutsia, while reducing pro-inflammatory species including Desulfovibrio and Turicibacter [35, 146]. These microbial shifts are accompanied by altered purine metabolism in the substantia nigra, linking gut modulation to neuroprotection. Desulfovibrio is of particular interest, as it has been shown to promote α-synuclein aggregation in animal models [175].

Animal experiments provide direct evidence that KD-induced microbiota changes mediate neuroprotection. In a 2023 study [57], PD was induced in mice via MPTP administration, followed by eight weeks of KD feeding. KD protected dopaminergic neurons, improved motor performance, and reduced inflammation in the brain, plasma, and gut. Importantly, it also reversed MPTP-induced dysbiosis, reducing harmful taxa (Citrobacter, Desulfovibrio, Ruminococcus) and restoring beneficial bacteria such as Dubosiella. Crucially, FMT from KD-fed mice to MPTP-treated mice reproduced these benefits—improving motor function and reducing neuronal loss—even in the absence of KD. This demonstrates that KD‘s neuroprotective effects are at least partly mediated by its influence on the gut microbiota rather than by direct metabolic effects alone.

Emerging clinical and preclinical findings further suggest that microbiota alterations precede the onset of PD motor symptoms and may drive neurodegeneration via systemic inflammation and microglial activation [176]. KD appears well positioned to intervene in this process, overlapping with broader microbiota-targeted strategies—including probiotics, synbiotics, FMT, and antibiotics—that are increasingly regarded as potential disease-modifying interventions in PD [129, 177].

In addition to shaping microbial composition, KD fosters the expansion of commensal species that produce metabolites capable of acting on the AhR. Through this pathway, KD may help restore immune homeostasis, dampen neuroinflammation, and protect dopaminergic neurons. However, a potential drawback is that KD is typically low in dietary Trp [178], the precursor of several neuroprotective AhR ligands and serotonin pathway metabolites. Because patients with PD already show reduced levels of protective Trp derivatives, further restriction could exacerbate this deficit and undermine AhR-mediated signaling. Thus, while KD exerts multiple beneficial effects on the microbiota and gut–brain axis, its limitations must be carefully considered when evaluating its therapeutic potential in PD.

Clinical studies

In 2017 in New Zealand, Phillips et al. [179] compared the impact of a low-fat diet and a KD in PD. 47 patients suffering from PD were randomized, of whom 44 started adhering to the respective diet, and 38 completed the study (6 patients withdrew for reasons unrelated to the diets, and 3 patients withdrew due to diet-related difficulties). The dietary intervention lasted 8 weeks, and during this period, patients were assigned to either a low-fat or a KD plan. Both dietary plans provided the same amount of calories (1750 kcal) and the same amount of protein (75 g). The diets differed significantly in carbohydrate intake − 246 g in the low-fat diet and 16 g in the KD – and in the quantity of fats consumed − 42 g in the low-fat diet and 152 g in the KD. During the study, each patient had 4 clinical visits scheduled, and each of these included a neurologist’s assessment, as well as body weight measurements and blood tests to determine the levels of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C), triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol, urate, and C-reactive protein (CRP). Both groups experienced a significant reduction in their Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) scores. However, in Part 1 scores of MDS-UPDRS, which evaluates nonmotor experiences of daily living, the ketogenic group showed a greater decrease (−4.58 ± 2.17 points, representing a 41% improvement from baseline Part 1 scores) compared to the low-fat group (−0.99 ± 3.63 points, an 11% improvement) (p < 0.001). The most substantial inter-group reductions were observed in issues with urination, pain and other sensations, fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and cognitive impairment. There were no inter-group differences in the reduction observed in Parts 2 through 4 of the scale, which assess motor features of PD. Beyond the impact on MDS-UPDRS, some of the measured metabolic parameters also changed. Both groups experienced a decrease in body weight and BMI, though without significant inter-group differences. Similarly, there were no differences between the groups regarding changes in HbA1C, CRP, or triglycerides. However, significant inter-group changes were noted in HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, and uric acid levels. These parameters decreased in the low-fat group but increased in the ketogenic group. Both groups experienced side effects. In the low-fat group, excessive hunger was the most common complaint. Conversely, the KD group reported a transient worsening of tremor, rigidity and nausea, especially during the initial weeks. The authors attributed this exacerbation of tremor to the KD causing a sudden increase in fat intake, temporarily intensifying dopamine depletion and oxidative stress in the neuronal nucleus, followed by an adaptive response over time [180]. Besides these, both groups also reported excessive irritability, thirst, lightheadedness, palpitations, and headaches. Both groups experienced weight loss, averaging around 4–5 kg. While weight loss is often linked to a negative impact on PD severity [181], another study suggests that PD progression is faster in patients with metabolic syndrome [182].

In 2019, Krikorian et al. [183] investigated the effect of nutritional ketosis on the treatment of mild cognitive impairment in PD. The study enrolled 18 individuals diagnosed with PD and cognitive symptoms consistent with mild cognitive impairment. Four participants did not complete the study: three due to complications from chronic medical conditions and one due to fatigue. During the 8-week intervention, patients were assigned to either a high-carbohydrate diet group (7 patients) or a KD group (also 7 patients). Carbohydrates were the only macronutrient whose intake was modified. Individuals in the high-carbohydrate group had no restrictions on the form of carbohydrates consumed, while the low-carbohydrate group aimed for up to 20 g of carbohydrates daily, exclusively from vegetables, with restrictions on fruits, grain products, and sweets. Patients underwent neurocognitive assessments (lexical access, memory impairment, memory recall), motor evaluations, and anthropometric and metabolic measurements. The KD group demonstrated improvements in lexical access (F(1,11) = 6.55, p = 0.02, Cohen’s f = 0.76) and memory (F(1,11) = 8.42, p = 0.01, f = 0.87), as well as a reduction in memory interference (F(1,11) = 4.03, p = 0.06, f = 0.60). No impact was observed on motor functions, whether assessed by the MDS-UPDRS Part 3 scale or the finger-tapping test. Additionally, the ketogenic group showed a statistically significant decrease in body weight (95±12.1 kg vs. 83 ± 12.5 kg; F(1,11) = 39.4, p < 0.0001, f = 1.89) and a tendency towards waist circumference reduction (104±8.6 vs. 94 ± 15.3; F(1,11) = 3.54, p = 0.10, f = 0.55). However, no significant differences were noted between the groups in terms of fasting glucose or fasting insulin concentrations. A significant decrease in daily calories intake was observed in the low-carbohydrate group (from 2205 ± 624 kcal to 1667 ± 404 kcal, p = 0.004), whereas no significant change occurred in the high-carbohydrate group. This study suggests that executive functions are a key area vulnerable in early-stage PD with mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI) and can be improved through a KD. The positive impact on memory appears to support the hypothesis of enhanced hippocampal and medial temporal lobe function in patients adhering to a low-carbohydrate diet [184], as ketone metabolism may offer particular benefits for these brain structures [185]. Additionally, the reduction in patient BMI in this study seems linked to improved memory. This could be due to the evidence suggesting that BMI is inversely proportional to central ATP production [186] and that obesity is associated with brain atrophy and cognitive decline. Furthermore, weight reduction, especially in overweight individuals, can be associated with decreased insulin and inflammatory mediators, which also contributes to improved brain function [187].

In 2021, Tidman et al. [188] also investigated the impact of the KD on PD patients. However, this study broadened its focus to include symptoms of anxiety and depression in PD patients. Sixteen PD patients were enrolled, and all completed the 12-week study. Before and after the intervention, patients were assessed for biomarkers (HbA1c, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, CRP), weight, waist circumference, UPDRS scores, the Parkinson’s Anxiety Scale (PAS), and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-Revised (CESD-R-20). The dietary intervention involved consuming 1750 kcal daily, consisting of 152 g fat, 17 g protein, 16 g carbohydrates, and 11 g fiber. Patients maintained an average ketone level of 6.4 mmol/l during the 12-week period. All study participants showed statistically significant changes between baseline and 12 weeks in body weight (p ≤ 0.0001), waist circumference (p = 0.0002), BMI (p < 0.0001), fasting insulin levels (p = 0.00018), and HbA1c (p ≤ 0.0001). No significant differences were observed for other biomarkers, including HDL, fasting glucose, triglycerides, and CRP. Similarly, scores on UPDRS Part 1 (p = 0.00079) and the PAS (p = 0.00086) significantly changed over the course of the study. However, no significant differences were noted in the other parts of the UPDRS scale or on the CESD-R-20 depression scale. All patients reported very mild side effects. The most common were GI complaints, headaches, mood swings, irritability, and decreased energy. Regarding GI issues, the authors of the study linked them to a “ketogenic flu,” characterized by diarrhea and cramps [189]. This typically appears in the first week and is linked to elevated ketosis. However, the authors also noted that some patients experienced a different side effect: constipation, which caused severe pain and discomfort [190].

A more recent study, published in 2024 by H. Choi et al. [191], assessed the feasibility and acceptability of KD enriched with MCTs in PD patients. It also investigated the impact of this nutritional intervention. MCT oil shortens the time to induce ketosis and increases average β-HB levels compared to sunflower oil [192]. Supplementing with 30 mL of MCT three times daily provides 630 kcal, which is about 30% of a typical person’s daily energy requirement and up to 50% of daily lipids. This allows for less radical dietary changes compared to a traditional KD, as MCT is more rapidly converted to KBs, thereby shortening the keto-induction period. The study was conducted in two parts: a 1-week, double-blind, randomized inpatient phase with daily assessments, and a 2-week open-label outpatient phase. Sixteen patients were randomized: 9 were assigned to a standard diet for 6 days, while the other 7 followed an MCT-KD. During the 1-week hospitalization period, a baseline assessment was performed on the first day, and on subsequent days, patients received their assigned diet. Both diets provided the same caloric intake. The standard diet comprised 35% of energy from fat, 10–15% from protein, and 50–55% from carbohydrates. In contrast, the MCT-KD provided 80% from fat (including 25% from MCT oil), 10–15% from protein, and 5–10% from carbohydrates. After completing the 1-week inpatient phase, all participants were asked to follow a 2-week open-label MCT-KD at home, maintaining the same macronutrient ratios as the hospital diet. Fifteen of the 16 participants completed the study (one participant withdrew after the inpatient observation, citing difficulties with diet adherence). During the first week, patients showed no differences in symptoms during ketosis induction. Daily assessment of PD symptoms revealed similar outcomes in both the KD and standard diet groups, although the standard diet group displayed a tendency for less pain and more frequent urination. However, the only statistically significant difference was a longer daily percentage of rest time in the standard diet group (p = 0.0005). In the final survey completed during the 3-week visit, the average Likert scale score was 2.26/3 among the 15 participants who finished the study, indicating good diet acceptability. Only 3 out of 15 participants stated they would not use KD in the future. The most frequently reported benefits included increased energy, improved motor function, reduced appetite, constipation relief, and less fatigue. Reported adverse effects were fatigue, dizziness, headache, decreased energy, urinary incontinence symptoms, gait freezing, and diarrhea. Among the adverse effects, fatigue, headache/dizziness, and possible PD symptoms (new onset of gait freezing, increased urination frequency) were most common, along with difficulties in diet preparation and adherence compared to a spouse’s diet. One patient in the KD group experienced a serious adverse event during the home phase: deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism. However, this patient had a pre-existing β-thalassemia diagnosis, and it was determined that the incident was unlikely related to the dietary intervention. There was no significant inter-group effect on mobility as measured by timed up and go test (TUG) or UPDRS-3 at either day 7 or week 3. However, tendencies toward motor improvement were observed in 9-hole pegboard and keyboard motor tasks. It is possible that the intervention period was too short for neuroplastic changes to occur and for motor improvement to be seen. Another hypothesis, proposed by the authors, was that ketone metabolism might be more related to non-motor symptoms, which the results seem to reflect. Indeed, the severity of non-motor symptoms decreased during the week 3 visit, with a more pronounced reduction in the KD group. Areas where 3/7 or more participants showed an improvement of ≥ 2 points included the GI system, urinary system, sex and other functions, while 3/7 or more participants experienced a worsening of ≥ 2 points in sleep/fatigue. UPDRS measurements, 3-back test, and resting-state electroencephalography (rsEEG) did not differ significantly between groups. The speed of performing MN and PQ keyboard tasks improved in both groups. Blood ketosis was achieved by day 4 in the KD group, and by week 3, ketosis levels were significantly higher in the KD group compared to the standard diet group. Plasma levodopa metabolites, DOPAC and dopamine, showed a statistically non-significant upward trend over 3 days in the KD group compared to the standard diet group. BDNF did not show a significant intervention effect. The authors suggest that the increase in levodopa metabolites might be linked to increased levodopa decarboxylase activity, possibly due to changes in the microbiota (e.g., Enterococcus faecalis has the ability to convert levodopa into dopamine) [193]. Between screening and week 3, a significant reduction in body weight (2.6±1.3 kg, p < 0.0001), waist circumference, and skeletal muscle mass index was observed in both the standard diet and KD groups. A significant inter-group difference in glycemic levels was noted: participants in the KD group exhibited stable glucose levels (60–140 mg/dl), whereas the standard diet group showed post-meal glucose spikes for 2 hours, with baseline levels of 80–100 mg/dl rising to 150–180 mg/dl during meals. This allowed for a greater reduction in insulin dosage among KD patients. Total triglyceride concentrations decreased, and HDL concentrations increased in both groups throughout the study up to week 3, but to a greater extent in the KD group than in the standard diet group. No statistically significant differences were found in CRP or HbA1c levels in either intervention group.

Another study, conducted by Koyuncu et al. [194] in 2021, investigated how KD, compared to a regular diet, might affect voice quality in PD patients. Voice quality is often diminished in PD and can be assessed using the Voice Handicap Index (VHI). Seventy-four patients complaining of voice disorders related to PD were randomly assigned to either a regular diet or a KD group; 68 individuals completed the study. Patients were assessed at the beginning of the experiment and after three months. At the study’s conclusion, it was observed that VHI parameters significantly improved in the KD group (p˂ 0.001). This study also noted side effects of the KD: five individuals on the KD experienced GI disturbances, compared to two patients in the control group who reported similar symptoms.

In 2022, Tidman et al. [195] described a case study of a 68 year old woman diagnosed with PD. The patient followed a traditional KD (70% fat, 25% protein, and 5% carbohydrates) for 24 weeks. Her laboratory test results, depression scale scores, anxiety scale scores, and UPDRS scores were compared at baseline, at weeks 12 and 24. At the beginning of the study, the participant reported occasional sleep disturbances, mild motor symptoms, mild anxiety and depression symptoms, and difficulties maintaining normal blood glucose levels. She considered her pre-intervention diet to be a standard low-fat diet, but she frequently consumed processed foods rich in carbohydrates. The patient showed a slight decrease in CESD-R-20 scores and a mild reduction in depression symptoms, as well as a decrease in PAS scores with some reduction in anxiety symptoms, but these declines were not statistically significant. A noticeable and reportable improvement in UPDRS Part 1 was observable as early as 12 weeks, and this improvement was maintained at a similar level after 24 weeks. When the study began, the participant met the criteria for metabolic syndrome. After 24 weeks, there was a significant improvement in HbA1c, fasting insulin levels, and triglyceride concentrations, along with a substantial reduction in waist circumference and body weight. Additionally, a slight increase in HDL and a decrease in CRP were observed, though these were not statistically significant. All these improvements meant that after 24 weeks, the patient no longer met the diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome. Upon completing the study, the patient reported increased energy and less hunger compared to the beginning of the study. Furthermore, she noted that the constipation issues she experienced at the start of the intervention had resolved.

An older study by Vanitallie et al. [196] in 2005 enrolled 7 participants with PD. Two patients did not complete the study, one was unable to prepare the diet, and another withdrew for an unrelated reason. The remaining five prepared and adhered to a KD at home for 28 days, though the authors noted occasional deviations from KD adherence in two of the completing patients. After the study, all five participants showed improvement on the UPDRS scale. However, the authors cautioned that a placebo effect could not be ruled out. Additionally, four of these patients avoided an increase in cholesterol levels.

In 2020, Norwitz et al. [197] explored another approach for utilizing ketosis in PD, investigating how a ketone ester drink might affect exercise endurance in individuals diagnosed with PD. His interest stemmed from the fact that physical exercise is a disease-modifying treatment for PD. Unfortunately, its application becomes limited as functional impairment progresses. Fourteen patients diagnosed with PD were enrolled in this study. Participants took part in both trials, one week apart, receiving either a ketone ester drink or an isocaloric control drink in a randomized order. Blood glucose levels were measured from a finger prick before consuming the drink, pre-exercise (30 minutes post-drink), and post-exercise. The exercise protocol began with a warm-up, followed by an endurance test. Finally, after heart rate stabilization, an anaerobic threshold test was conducted, where participants cycled at their maximum possible speed for 1 minute. Each time, the study assessed how the given drink affected the duration participants could sustain cycling at 80 revolutions per minute on a stationary bike, compared to baseline. The ketone ester extended the time participants were able to maintain an 80 rpm cadence by 24 ± 9% (p = 0.027).

Despite promising initial results, studies on the KD in PD face several significant limitations. Primarily, the available research is often characterized by small study populations and short intervention durations, which limit statistical power and the ability to fully understand long-term effects. This is largely due to the relatively recent surge in interest in KD for PD. Patient withdrawal from studies further complicates statistical analysis (Table 2). Moreover, certain studies encounter challenges related to the accuracy of self-reported data, such as food diaries and questionnaires, which can impact the reliability of findings. The potential for a placebo effect and the confounding influence of weight loss on PD symptoms also require careful consideration in interpreting results.

Beyond study design, the practical application of KD in PD treatment presents its own set of limitations. Long-term adherence to rigorous traditional KD guidelines is challenging due to significant deviations from typical dietary habits. High intake of fats from sources like cheeses, eggs, butter, oils, and meat can lead to poor tolerance, potentially resulting in a lack of motivation and discontinuation of the diet [198]. However, emerging KD modifications, such as the MAD or MCT-KD, have shown improved tolerability in some studies [199, 200], suggesting their potential for better long-term adherence. Despite these limitations, the overall promise of the KD in PD encourages continued and more robust research efforts.

Side effects of KD

It appears that the side effects of KD are infrequent and generally not severe. The most challenging period for patients is the initial phase of diet implementation, during which hypoglycemia, dehydration, and GI disturbances are most common [201, 202]. A period known as the “keto flu” has been described as a transient and harmless episode often occurring at the start of dietary treatment [189].

Yet another challenge in implementing KD is its proper nutritional balancing. The forced elimination of entire food groups rich in carbohydrates and other components can lead to deficiencies in nutrients such as protein, thiamine, folic acid, vitamin A, vitamin E, vitamin B6, calcium, magnesium, iron, and vitamin K [203].

KD, being rich in fat and low in carbohydrates, also involves limiting the energy content of food portions. This leads to metabolic effects that are still not fully understood, which ultimately result in body weight reduction [204]. Therefore, the use of KD in individuals with PD can raise concerns, as their nutritional status is a significant factor affecting lifespan, and people with PD are already at a high risk of malnutrition. This heightened risk can stem from impaired taste and smell perception, which diminishes the appeal of food (e.g., hyposmia), as well as difficulties with preparing and consuming meals.

Another concern regarding KD is its potential impact on the lipid profile, as high dietary fat intake can elevate serum lipid fractions. In a study evaluating KD in children with epilepsy [205], cases of hyperlipidemia were observed. However, this adverse effect occurred less frequently than GI disturbances, and the mean cholesterol concentration in the study population was only marginally higher than baseline values.

Beyond the previously mentioned side effects, others are also possible, including, hepatitis, pancreatitis, hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperuricemia, hypertransaminasemia, hypomagnesemia, and hyponatremia. Late complications include decreased bone mineral density, cardiomyopathy, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, impaired liver function, optic neuropathy, anemia, constipation, and increased atherosclerosis [206].

Due to the limited number of studies on KD in PD patients, this section also considers side effects observed in research on other populations. An example is a 2020 meta-analysis examining the effect of KD on epilepsy treatment [207]. The most frequently reported side effects included GI symptoms such as diarrhea, constipation, and vomiting. Less common adverse events in this meta-analysis were weight loss, infections, nausea, dysphagia, lethargy, headache, abdominal pain, irregular menstruation, drowsiness, and kidney stones. Another large study investigating the impact of KD on AD patients noted increased irritability and fatigue, but no other serious side effects were reported [208]. In a study by Lin et al. [201], patients suffering from epilepsy, frequently experienced hypoglycemia during KD initiation, with blood glucose dropping below 40 mg/dL, and six patients developed excessive ketosis with urine ketone levels of 160 mg/dL, which manifested as facial flushing.

In elderly populations, the adverse effects of KD may carry heightened clinical relevance. Age-related vulnerabilities—such as reduced renal function, decreased bone mineral density, and a greater risk of sarcopenia and malnutrition—can exacerbate complications associated with a restrictive high-fat diet [209]. Even mild GI disturbances or appetite suppression may contribute to frailty by accelerating weight loss and muscle wasting. Moreover, the increased risk of dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, hyperlipidemia, and kidney stone formation is of particular concern in older adults with comorbidities, including cardiovascular and renal disease. Thus, while KD can be tolerated in the general population, its implementation in elderly patients requires careful monitoring and individualized adjustments to prevent potentially serious adverse outcomes.

Conclusion

PD is a complex disorder with multifactorial origins. Current therapies primarily address symptoms and have notable limitations, underscoring the need for alternative approaches, including lifestyle-based interventions. KD, by promoting the use of KBs as an energy source, offers considerable potential for neuroprotection in PD through mechanisms such as enhancing mitochondrial function, reducing oxidative stress, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects.

Beyond these direct neurological actions, KD may also help restore gut eubiosis, an effect of growing relevance given the expanding evidence implicating GI dysfunction in both the pathogenesis and progression of PD. Clinical studies discussed in this paper indicate that KD‘s influence on these mechanisms can improve cognitive performance and other non-motor symptoms, domains in which current pharmacological treatments have limited impact. Additionally, KD may benefit motor symptoms by increasing the bioavailability of levodopa, the gold-standard therapy for PD.

Overall, KD shows promise as a complementary therapeutic strategy capable of targeting multiple aspects of PD pathology. When properly implemented, it may support pharmacological treatment and improve overall quality of life in affected individuals. However, it is not without limitations: long-term adherence can be challenging, and potential side effects may hinder sustained use. Therefore, while encouraging, particularly in ameliorating certain PD symptoms, its efficacy and safety must be confirmed through larger, well-designed clinical trials.
