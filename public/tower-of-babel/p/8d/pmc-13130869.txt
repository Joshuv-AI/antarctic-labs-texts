Scalable Distributed Memory Implementation of the Quasi-Adiabatic Propagator Path Integral
Authors: Roman Ovcharenko, Xiangyu Xu, Benjamin P. Fingerhut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The accurate simulation of dissipative quantum dynamics subject to a non-Markovian environment poses persistent numerical challenges, in particular, for structured environments, where sharp mode resonances induce long-time system bath correlations. We present a scalable distributed memory implementation of the mask-assisted coarse graining of influence coefficients (MACGIC)-quasi-adiabatic propagator path integral (-QUAPI) method that exploits the memory resources of multiple compute nodes and mitigates the memory bottleneck of the method via a new premerging algorithm and a hash-based look-up (hMACGIC-QUAPI) while preserving numerical accuracy. The source code of the hMACGIC-QUAPI implementation is available in a publicly accessible Git repository under GPL license. The developed distributed memory implementation spreads the paths over the computing nodes by means of the MPI protocol, and efficient high level path management is achieved via the hash map-based implementation with constant access time. The efficiency of the implementation is demonstrated in large-scale dissipative quantum dynamics simulations that account for the coupling to a structured non-Markovian environment containing a sharp resonance, a setup for which convergence properties are investigated in depth. Broad applicability and the nonperturbative nature of the simulation method is illustrated via the tuning of the mode resonance frequency of the structured environment with respect to the characteristic system frequency. The simulations reveal a splitting of resonances due to a strong system–environment interaction and the emergence of sidebands due to multiexcitations of the bosonic mode that are not accounted for in perturbative approaches. The simulations demonstrate the versatility of the new hMACGIC-QUAPI method in the presence of strong non-Markovian system bath correlations.

Introduction

The time evolution of a quantum system is decisively affected by the interaction with the environment through which, regardless of the precise nature of the environment, phenomena like energy relaxation and dephasing arise. , For example in condensed matter, phonons are responsible for the dissipation of energy. In quantum computing, undesirable decoherence of superconducting qubits arises from the inevitable coupling to the electromagnetic environment. − Similarly, the precise nature of energy or charge transfer dynamics can only be rationalized via the interaction with the environment and theoretical descriptions have to account for the nuclear degrees of freedom of the solvent or a biological environment. ,

The finite memory time of the environment induces non-Markovian behavior by imposing correlations between the system and the environment that affect decoherence and transfer rates. Under conditions where intersite and system-environment interactions are of similar magnitude or the dynamics of the environment is slow, the Born-Markov approximation is no longer valid and the finite bath memory time imposes strong correlations that persist on a time scale that is comparable to the bare system dynamics. Particular challenges arise when the quantum system interacts with a structured environment, e.g, the discrete modes of the nuclear degrees of freedom or the dispersive readout modes of superconducting qubits. Such structured environments can potentially induce long-time memory for the interaction between the system and the environment and the underlying non-Markovian memory effects are difficult to capture by methods rooted in perturbation theory. The accurate simulation of the real-time nonequilibrium quantum dynamics subject to such non-Markovian dissipation is a persistent numerical challenge. −

The path integral formulation of quantum mechanics provides a nonperturbative description of the time evolution of the reduced density matrix that is particularly appealing for harmonic baths where the environmental degrees of freedom can be integrated analytically in the form of the Feynmann–Vernon influence functional. For example, the hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM) , method maps the effect of the non-Markovian system–bath interaction onto the hierarchical elements of the reduced density matrix. Alternatively, by assuming a finite time span of non-Markovian memory, an augmented reduced density matrix tensor is iteratively constructed in the quasi-adiabatic propagator path integral (QUAPI) method. , While in principle being applicable to arbitrary functional forms of the spectral density, the size of the augmented reduced density matrix tensor scales exponentially with the system-bath correlation time, and the applicability of the QUAPI method is thus generally limited to cases where system-bath correlations decay very rapidly and the spectral density function does not include discrete underdamped modes of the environment.

A number of approaches recently addressed longtime system-bath correlations that arise from a ‘sluggish’ environment with long non-Markovian memory. The time-evolving matrix product operators (TEMPO) method adopts matrix product states , for the representation of the augmented density matrix tensor. The small matrix decomposition of the path integral (SMatPI) approach of Makri exploits a recursive decomposition of the augmented density matrix tensor and avoids the exponentially increasing memory requirements of QUAPI via the recursive spread of the entangled influence functional terms over longer time intervals. Recently, we have introduced the mask assisted coarse graining of influence coefficients (MACGIC-QUAPI) that exploits physical properties of the bath correlation function, i.e., a fast decay at early times and a slower temporal variation at later times. This behavior facilitates an intermediate coarse grained representation of the influence functional and allows for a decoupling of the total system-bath correlation time from the number of considered Feynman paths. MACGIC-QUAPI simulations provide numerical access to long system-bath correlation times and allow for accurate simulations of relatively complex systems, like energy equilibration in the FMO trimer complex and the coupled excitation energy and charge transfer dynamics in a model of the bacterial reaction center.

Besides refs and , computational implementation aspects of the QUAPI method are largely unaddressed. Quantum mechanical real-time path integral methods were implemented in the PathSum package of the Makri group and the Julia program QuantumDynamics.jl for simulations of dynamics of open quantum systems became available recently. Here, we present a scalable distributed memory implementation of the MACGIC-QUAPI method in conjunction with a novel premerging algorithm and a hash-map based look-up algorithm, termed the hMACGIC-QUAPI approach. Distributed memory parallelization allows to exploit physical memory resources of different compute nodes while the inherent nonlocal structure of the MACGIC-QUAPI algorithm requires careful synchronization, ordering and merging of paths. The optimized premerging algorithm reduces the memory bottleneck of the method and further facilitates the distributed memory implementation. Numerical efficiency of the implementation is demonstrated in large scale dissipative quantum dynamics simulations of a quantum system subject to strong interaction with a structured non-Markovian environment. By varying the frequency of the sharp environment resonance with respect to the characteristic frequency of the quantum system, profound non-Markovian effects in the system dynamics are revealed that demonstrate the limits of perturbative approaches.

Section presents the new path premerging algorithm and describes the parallel distributed memory implementation. Numerical accuracy of the path premerging algorithm is demonstrated in Section and the performance of the parallel distributed memory implementation is analyzed in Section . The developed program is finally applied to investigate the impact of a non-Markovian environment with sharp resonance frequency on the dynamics of a two-level quantum system (Section ).

Nevertheless, numerical applications are limited by the exponentially growing number of paths with propagation time, imposing a O(M 2N ) numerical scaling, with M being the number of system states and N the number of time steps (eqs −). Starting from eq , the QUAPI method relies on a finite time span of the non-Markovian memory to iteratively construct the propagator tensor. ,, During the memory time, the magnitude of correlation between two time points i and j, reflected in η ij , drops significantly for the large separation d = i – j, and becomes negligible for some threshold value Δk max ≪ N. The assumption of a finite memory time τ = Δk maxΔt is physically well motivated by the broad environmental spectra in condensed phase and formally represents the only limitation of the method.

Mask-Assisted Coarse Graining of Influence Coefficients (MACGIC-QUAPI)

The exponential scaling of the QUAPI method for increasing memory time τ was addressed by introducing a coarse grained intermediate representation of the influence functional. The idea behind the method is based on generic properties of typical bath response functions, i.e, the observation that influence coefficients η ij show a faster decay for small time lags d = i – j than for large ones. It is thus possible to construct an efficient numerical representation that relies on a nonuniform temporal grid where a denser temporal spacing is employed for small time lags d and a sparser temporal spacing is employed for large time lags. Since the original bath memory grid is uniform, an auxiliary nonuniform coarse grained time grid with Δk eff ≪ Δk max elements is introduced that is selected via a mask from the uniform grid (mask assisted coarse graining of influence coefficients, MACGIC-QUAPI).

The auxiliary grid is employed for a reduction of number of paths used for propagation via merging , while the original uniform time grid is employed for propagation of the reduced density matrix. Since the scaling of the MACGIC-QUAPI method is determined by the mask size Δk eff, O(M 2Δk eff ), that is typically substantially smaller than Δk max, the algorithm allows to decouple the numerical effort from memory time τ. The algorithm thus provides access to longtime memory effects of a ‘sluggish’ environment beyond the capabilities of the standard QUAPI method and convergence to the latter result is retained for Δk eff → Δk max. Details of the mask optimization procedure are given in ref and applicability to nonmonotonous decaying bath response functions was demonstrated in ref .

Every quantum path, as a particular forward or backward propagation evolution of the system coordinate through memory time τ = Δk maxΔt, is associated with a particular complex amplitude of forward and backward propagation along the path, defining its weight. Amplitude and path are subsumed as configurations that constitute the propagator tensor Ω.

Propagation from time step t – 1 to t involves going through all paths and propagating them one time step further (see Supporting Information (SI) for implementation details of the propagation­() routine), which produce ×M 2 additional paths (corresponding to forward and backward propagation) at the current time t, i.e., transiently increasing the size of the propagator tensor to Ω­(t, unmerged) (Scheme a). The merging of paths that coincide on the coarse grained temporal grid, specified by mask function of size Δk eff, subsequently reduces the size of the tensor. Here, among the identical path selected by the mask, the paths with highest weight are retained and the complex weight of merged paths is accumulated. Because all possible paths are considered during merging and the weights of redundant paths are summed up, the trace of the reduced density matrix is preserved in the MACGIC-QUAPI method.

The merging procedure reduces the number of distinguishable paths formally to M 2k eff ≤ M 2Δk max . The required searching within an array data structure is proportional to the size of the enlarged Ωarr(t, unmerged)-array, O(N paths·M 2), and a simple algorithm would require O(N paths 2·M 4) operations to perform the merging on the mask. The consecutive use of sorting­() via the Radix sort algorithm (requiring O(Δk eff·N paths·M 2) operations) and merging­() routines allows to reduce the computational scaling to formally linear complexity O((Δk eff + 1)·N paths·M 2), although at the expense of an additional computationally expensive sorting­() step on top of the enlarged auxiliary array.

The memory bottleneck of the MACGIC-QUAPI algorithm is the intermediate tensor Ω­(t, unmerged) that formally contains M 2Δk eff+2 distinguishable paths. Further reduction of the number of paths is possible through the filtering of the propagator functional where paths with weights below a given threshold θ are not accounted for in the simulation but the efficiency of path filtering is highly problem dependent. ,,

Scalable Distributed Memory Implementation

In the following we present the implementation of a refined hMACGIC-QUAPI method that mitigates the memory bottleneck of the algorithm via the reordering of merging and propagation routines. The novel C++ implementation numerically represents paths and their associated complex amplitude as configuration objects conf. MPI parallelization allows to exploit the distributed memory resources of multiple compute nodes.

In the new path premerging strategy that precedes the propagation step, the memory bottleneck of the original MACGIC-QUAPI algorithm is evaded. This is realized by initially reducing the set of paths Ω­(t – 1, Δk eff–merged) via merging with a shifted mask of reduced size Δk eff – 1, generating the premerged Ω­(t – 1, (Δk eff – 1)–merged)-tensor at time step t – 1 that is propagated to t. The mask of reduced size Δk eff – 1 is obtained by shifting the original mask by one time step to latter times (c.f. Scheme ), i.e. mask­[i] → mask­[i] + 1, ∀i. Upon propagation, the size of Ω­(t, Δk eff–merged) is recovered. Thus, the largest data structures in the improved algorithm are Ω­(t – 1, (Δk eff – 1)-merged) and Ω­(t, Δk eff-merged), of size O(M 2(Δk eff–1)) and O(M 2Δk eff ), respectively, where the size of the latter can be further reduced by filtering. The number of paths kept in memory is thus M 2-times reduced compared to the original algorithm, reducing its memory bottleneck.

A reduced mask of size Δk eff – 1, effectively neglects explicit correlations between the most recent time step and the most distant time step, i.e., for paths that will be merged with high certainty on a given mask. At the same time, long-time correlations are accounted for by paths that persist after merging. Such premerging is justified for the majority of physically relevant computational problems, since the most recent time lag is always included into the mask, while the most distant time delay plays the least important role (cf. eq ). Especially for ‘sluggish’ environments with long-time bath memory covering ≈ tens of time steps, the approach is well suited because the effect of influence coefficients with large time lags d = i – j → Δk max should become negligible for converged long-time bath memory. Another scenario concerns oscillatory or fast decaying bath correlation functions. Provided that the initial decay is appropriately discretized and represented by the reduced mask of size Δk eff – 1 (which can be systematically achieved by reducing the time step Δt and increasing Δk eff), the revised merging procedure is expected to be robust in this scenario as well. Large variations of accuracy with respect to Δk eff are generally a sign of nonconverged settings (finite memory time or mask size) and for converged dynamics, the “derivative” of the reduced density matrix with respect to Δk eff approaches zero. Details how the MACGIC approach can be efficiently used to treat oscillatory bath correlation functions have been presented in ref . After the propagation step at time t, the tensor Ω­(t, Δk eff-merged) should contain the same configurations as in the original postmerging implementation. As the premerging approach is an approximation, its numerical accuracy and stability is demonstrated in Section .

Data Structure and Look-Up Algorithm

The premerging hMACGIC-QUAPI algorithm is implemented in C++ and uses the concurrent version of C++ Standard Template Library (STL) serial containers as implemented in the Threading Building Blocks (TBB) library. Paths and their associated complex amplitude are numerically represented as configuration conf objects. The abstract set of configurations is denoted as Ω-tensor. Propagator tensors Ω in subroutines premerging­() and propagation­() are represented by two different data structures, the C++ data structure tbb::concurrent_vector (Ωvec) and an intermediate data structure tbb::concurrent_hash_map (Ωmap), respectively (Scheme b, a detailed discussion of data structures is given in the SI).

The premerging algorithm exploits the built-in search, insertion, and deletion properties of the associative map container, capitalizing on the advantage that operations can be performed concurrently by different TBB threads. In particular hash maps Ωmap (consisting of (key; value) pairs) provide the crucial property of a constant access time to any value by its key, i.e., searching, inserting, and deletion operations scale as O(1), regardless of the size of the Ωmap-tensor. Thus, the O(Δk eff N paths M 2) scaling of the sorting step in the original algorithm can be circumvented (cf. Scheme ).

The records value of an associative map are distributed in heap memory in no specific order, so the hash map grows dynamically in an efficient way with an increase in the number of paths. A particular value = (conf; sum) is defined by a particular configuration conf = (paths; weight) and the running sum sum of the weight if paths are merged. In Section we numerically demonstrate that despite the significantly slower traversal times of associative maps, competitive performance can be achieved compared to the simpler array data structure.

Premerging

In the premerging­() routine (Scheme a), we iterate through configurations of the Ωvec(t – 1)-tensor at time step t – 1. For every configuration in Ωvec(t – 1) the mask of size (Δk eff – 1) is applied and we retrieve the key of the masked configuration. We further check if the unique key already exists in the new Ωmap(t – 1)-tensor. If such key is absent, we insert the key and the corresponding conf to the hash map. If the key is present, i.e., an identical masked but otherwise distinguishable path exists, we perform a comparison if the weight wght (measured as absolute value) of the new path is larger than that of the old path contained in the hash map. If this is the case, the old path is replaced with the new one by updating the configuration corresponding to the key in the hash map. Remember, that only the path with the largest weight is saved and employed for further propagation.

Propagation

The propagation­() routine serves to evolve the set of paths from the previous time step t – 1 by one time step Δt to time t. For this purpose, the output of the premerging­() routine in the form of a hash map data structure of size M 2(Δk eff–1) serves as input of the propagation­() routine. The implementation of the propagation­() routine uses (i) propagation of a single time step Δt at a time and (ii) computes necessary matrix elements of the propagator tensor on-the-fly at each iteration, thus avoiding the precomputation of the propagator tensor (see the SI for implementation details of the propagation­() routine). Compared to the original formulation of the iterative QUAPI method, where path amplitudes (denoted as augmented reduced density tensor A Δk max ) are propagated via matrix-vector multiplication with the tensor propagator matrix T 2Δk max by the Δk maxΔt time increment at once, the current approach is significantly less memory demanding. Although the tensor formulation highlights the propagation as a linear map, the scheme is memory wise suboptimal for a numerical realization, as the augmented reduced density tensor A Δk max of size M 2Δk max and the propagator tensor of size M 4Δk max need to be precomputed and kept in computer memory.

After the propagation at time t, the paths are saved in the memory efficient, array-like tbb::concurrent_vector data structure of size M 2Δk eff which serves as input to the premerging­() routine at the next time step. The tbb::concurrent_vector data structure thus helps to mitigate the memory overhead by storing the largest data structure in a memory efficient array-like structures, while using hash maps for efficient look-up on intermediate arrays of a smaller size.

Finally, shared-memory parallelization of the loop over curr. paths is performed using a task-based parallelization strategy, as implemented in the intel TBB library. Here, the build-in TBB scheduler redistributes on-the-fly the iteration space between computing threads. As similar paths typically have similar amplitudes and are assigned to the same thread, the presence of the filtering may lead the significant load imbalances of computing cores in standard thread-based parallelization. Such runtime imbalance is overcome in a task-based parallelization strategy (see SI for details on the parallelization of the propagation routine).

In summary, the hMACGIC-QUAPI method benefits from the following algorithmic and methodological advances: (i) the premerging algorithm allows to reduce the memory bottleneck, and thus reduces the memory requirements of the method; (ii) the run-time scaling is improved by circumventing the expensive radix-sort algorithm via hash-based indexing of paths, that allows time-constant access to paths, yielding overall linear scaling of the propagation algorithm with the number of paths. As the required C++ implementation induces compute overheads, we explore in Section the runtime behavior for typical parameter settings.

Distributed Memory Parallelization

In this section we present the distributed memory parallelization of the hMACGIC-QUAPI method. The implementation makes accessible the compute resources of multiple compute nodes which is particularly appealing for the substantial compute and memory requirements of the QUAPI method. An efficient algorithm has to perform the work loads of path merging and propagation as independent as possible on an individual compute node. This requirement is realized by leveraging a multinode parallel execution via the message passing interface (MPI) with consecutive intra- and internode premerging operations.

General Code Structure

Scheme shows the global code structure of the distributed memory parallel hMAGCIC-QAUPI method. A guiding principle of the implementation is to provide the maximum amount of memory of each compute node by equally distributing the paths of the Ω-tensor. Each node then performs the path premerging and propagation steps independently on a set of assigned paths.

Communication Infrastructure between MPI Processes during One Propagation Step

Initially at every propagation step, the premerging of the Ωvec(t – 1)-tensor is performed, using the mask of size (Δk eff – 1). For parallel execution, the premerging operation is split into two parts: it is first performed independently within each MPI process using the premerging routine described in Section (intraprocess premerging). The routine operates on all paths that reside within memory of a particular process. However, for different processes with separate memory, there may still exist identical paths that are consolidated by an interprocess premerging routine. Per se, identical paths may reside in all processes, requiring an all-to-all communication model for interprocess premerging. Especially this step is highly dependent upon the internode communication speed and poses the performance bottleneck of the program. Thus, the effective implementation of this routine is crucial for the overall performance (cf. Section ). Moreover, merging brings the processes out of balance. To avoid the accumulation of imbalances during propagation, we implemented load balancing between individual processes in a two-step routine with a pairwise communication model (cf. Section ).

Subsequently, propagation of the density matrix is performed via intraprocess propagation, filtering, and intraprocess density matrix with the propagation routine of Scheme , each operating independently on the premerged Ωmap(t – 1) tensor. The generated Ωvec(t)-tensor at time t is of size O(M 2Δk eff ). Finally, the density matrices at time t, residing in different processes, are added up (reduction operation) to obtain the final density matrix. Since the size of the density matrix is M × M, the interprocess communication of this generally rather small matrix is a high-speed operation.

MPI Implementation of Interprocess Path Merging

The parallel distributed memory algorithm outlined in Scheme requires to synchronize the configurations in every process which is an all-to-all communication pattern. Efficient interprocess path merging thus poses a particular challenge of the algorithm. We thus implemented a strategy (Scheme ) that balances concurrency and reduces the memory overhead. The algorithm iterates sequentially through the processes and the current process (called sender) broadcasts a local set of configurations Ωloc residing in the current process to all other processes (called receivers). The receivers obtain a set of configurations from the sender and perform the main workload of the interprocess path merging routine in a TBB-parallelized loop. Receivers are classified into two groups: the ones that have already taken the role of the sender (processes < sender) and others that have not yet acted as sender (processes > sender). A set of configurations received from the sender (denoted Ωrem on receivers) is then compared to the local set of configurations. The latter can either be the original, unchanged set of configurations Ωloc or an already changed set of local configurations Ωloc *, depending if the receiver process has already acted as sender (processes > sender or processes < sender, respectively). In this way, the locally stored set of configurations are synchronized with the received set of configurations for every process with minimal memory overhead. The overall memory requirement is ∼2/N proc per process, where N proc is the number of spawned MPI processes.

The main purpose of the group of receivers with processes > sender is to accumulate the weights of identical paths that are present in the local Ωloc-tensor. As the original Ωloc-tensor is still required for transmission via the MPI library when the process becomes sender (processes = sender), identical paths in Ωloc cannot just be replaced. We thus use an auxiliary data structure Ωaux that is merged with the Ωloc data structure after the broadcasting of the original local set of configurations. Similarly, the original weights are replaced with the accumulated sums, generating the locally merged Ωloc *-tensor. For optimal performance, the local tensors Ωaux and Ωloc * are represented as hash maps, while the transmitted tensors Ωrem are implemented as array. While Scheme depicts Ωloc as single data structure for simplicity, the actual implementation of the Ωloc consists of two objects: one hash map and one array (see SI for numerical implementation details of the interprocess path merging routine)

Memory requirements are minimized by using a compact representation that replaces each path with the masked path key, instead of storing the full tensors Ωaux- and Ωloc. This allows to continuously update the locally merged Ωloc *-tensor via the comparison with the received set of of configurations Ωrem for processes < sender receivers.

Load Balancing

The asynchronous operation of interprocess path merging brings the workload of individual processes out of balance. Load balancing is thus implemented in a two-step process: First, the total number of configurations is calculated and divided by the number of processes N proc to obtain the balanced average number of configurations per process. Two lists are then created containing processes with less or more configurations than the average, where the imbalances have arisen due to the interprocess premerging. Deficient and overcrowded processes are subsequently combined pairwise to obtain a route map, i.e., a map containing sending processes, receiving processes, and the number of configurations to transmit.

In the second part, the route map is then used to choose a given number of configurations from the sender, pack them into the MPI message, and send them to the receiver. Such a model corresponds to a pairwise communication pattern. Moreover, the number of transmitted configurations is given by the difference of the number of actual configurations residing in a particular process and the average. Such deviations are much smaller than the total number of configurations residing in any particular process, the load balancing routine is thus much faster and substantially less communication intense than interprocess merging.

Results and Discussion

Numerical Accuracy and Look-Up Algorithm

We start by demonstrating the numerical accuracy of the mask assisted premerging algorithm introduced in Section . Further, we point out advantages of the fast look-up of paths using the tbb::concurrent_hash_map data structure. Numerical experiments are initially performed on a single compute node and compared to reference simulations described in refs and that used the original postmerging algorithm. The latter implements a look-up of paths arranged consecutively in an array data structures and shared-memory parallelization with the openMP interface (see SI for technical details).

Two benchmark models, i.e., bridge mediated electron transfer (denoted “3-state system”) and bath assisted long-range charge transfer along a chain of seven sites (denoted “7-state system”) serve to illustrate the numerical accuracy to the implementation. Figure a,b demonstrates that for both models, the numerical exact reference dynamics is reproduced with excellent accuracy with the new premerging algorithm (max. population deviations <0.001 and 0.01 for 3- and 7-state systems, respectively). Similar high accuracy is obtained for all benchmark simulations presented in ref , spanning a wide range of physical parameter space and dynamical regimes, i.e., coherent excitation energy transfer in dimeric Frenkel exciton models (see SI, Figure S3) and a 24-site model of the trimeric Fenna-Matthews-Olson complex (see SI, Figure S4).

Filter threshold θ = 10–6 corresponds to ∼104 paths for both reference systems, θ = 10–12 corresponds to 2 × 107 and 4 × 107 paths for the 3- and 7-state system, respectively.

Memory requirements of the path premerging algorithm are analyzed in Figure c,d. For propagation, the new premerging implementation selects a slightly larger number of paths (ratio 0.9–1, cf. black lines in Figure c,d), with a weak dependence on the filter threshold θ. Such an increase in the number of paths is responsible for the accuracy: the more relevant paths contribute, the more accurate the results generally are. A substantial advantage of the premerging algorithm is that the memory bottleneck is caused by exactly the number of relevant paths used for propagation, scaling as O(M 2Δk eff ). In contrast, the bottleneck of the postmerging algorithm is given by the size of an intermediate array, containing an enlarged number of paths O(M 2Δk eff+2) (Scheme a). Red lines in Figure c,d demonstrate that in real world applications, path ratios range from 1.14 (θ = 10–6) to 1.42 (θ = 10–12), demonstrating the smaller bottleneck memory footprint of the premerging implementation. While the memory gain in the premerging algorithm is strongly filter and task specific, no crossover of the red line and black lines in Figure c,d is observed, i.e., the ratio of path numbers is above one even for large θ.

The used tbb::concurrent_hash_map data structure (see Schemes b and ) introduces memory overhead compared to the C array, as the hash map stores the path key in addition to forward and backward paths and the associated weights. In the case of a large mask (Δk eff → Δk max), the additional memory requirements may reduce the memory advantage of the premerging algorithm but the new implementation is expected to be especially suited for long-time bath memory of ‘sluggish’ environments that require a mask of reasonable size.

Figure compares relative timings of the post- and premerging implementation. For the large mask sizes (solid lines) we find that the premerging implementation is up to 1.75 times faster than the original postmerging implementation (filter threshold θ = 10–6). Such speedup is observed despite the slower dynamical memory allocation in heap memory, compared to the statically allocated stack memory of the original postmerging implementation. The reasons for the observed performance gain lies in a better core balance of the TBB task-based parallelization as compared to the openMP thread-based parallelization. This effect becomes apparent for relatively large filter thresholds (θ = 10–6), where a lot of paths are filtered out during the course of propagation, leading to a significant imbalance of the loads of threads. As filter threshold decreases (θ = 10–12), the core imbalance is reduced, resulting in similar timings for both methods.

Comparing the numerical performance of the pre- and postmerging algorithms, for both model systems (7-state and 3-state) we observe a strong dependence on the employed mask size. For a large mask (solid lines in Figure , Δk eff = 16), the performance advantage of the premerging algorithm is mitigated and the relative timing ratio approaches ∼1. For reduced mask sizes (dashed lines in Figure , Δk eff = 4 and 6, respectively), the premerging implementation is significantly faster for all investigated θ and reaches the maximum efficiency for small filter thresholds where many paths are employed for propagation. Note that with the hMACGIC-QUAPI method, total memory time can be efficiently extended to substantial fractions of the full propagation time, i.e., numerical effort is decoupled from total memory time for which long-time correlations are taken into account. As demonstrated in ref , in such a scenario the required memory grows approximately linear with propagation/memory time.

The varying relative numerical efficiency of the pre- and postmerging implementations are rooted in the different paths look-up algorithms. The Radix sort algorithm used in the postmerging implementation becomes particularly efficient for partially sorted arrays between consecutive time steps, i.e., large mask sizes (Figure , Δk eff = 16). Sorting is avoided in the hash-table based look-up of the premerging algorithm, therefore the performance does not depend on a particular order of paths in memory and the algorithm becomes particularly efficient for smaller, sparse and more irregular masks that pose challenges to the postmerging implementation. For small masks (Δk eff = 4 and 6, dashed red and green lines in Figure ) the relative timing ratio grows monotonically with θ as the Radix sort algorithm becomes dominant, scaling linearly with the number of paths. The hash-table based implementation avoids the sorting step and the interplay between efficient look-up and shared-memory parallelization is responsible for the qualitatively different dependence with respect to θ. In all regimes the TBB implementation is either as fast as, or substantially faster than the openMP-based implementation.

Numerical Efficiency

Comparative simulations between state-of-the-art real time path integration methods are largely absent from the literature. To underscore the efficiency of the developed hMACGIC-QUAPI implementation, we have conducted comparative simulations to the iQuapi and the SmatPI methods of the PathSum package developed by the Makri group. We find that hMACGIC-QUAPI provides a numerically exact full Quapi implementation, faster than the iQuapi method in PathSum and the MACGIC-approximation makes the method competitive to the similarly approximate SmatPi method (Figure a). Specifically, for a large mask close to the total memory time (Δk eff ≈ Δk max, black, red and green) the numerical performance of the hMACGIC-QUAPI method is comparable to the SMatPI with the same R max = Δk eff. For large mask sizes close to the total memory time SMatPI is slightly faster, while for small masks hMACGIC-QUAPI catches up in performance with SMatPI and becomes even faster for small mask size (Δk eff = Δk max – 6 and Δk eff = Δk max – 8, yellow and purple line). An advantage of the SMatPI method are the significantly lower memory requirements, largely independent of the entanglement length R max. This drawback is addressed in hMACGIC-QUAPI via the mask size (Figure b) and in the current work multinode parallelization. Comparing the numerical error of the methods shows that for large masks hMACGIC-QUAPI provides similar accuracy that can even exceed the accuracy of SMatPI simulations (cf. red and black, Figure c). However, for decreasing mask size, the SMatPI method yields a higher accuracy. Note that the chosen mask in the benchmark simulations does not correspond to the optimal mask, which allows to further increase the numerical accuracy of hMACGIC-QUAPI with a small mask size. While such findings can strongly depend on model settings, algorithm parameter settings and the model simulations are not representative for all physical situations, the findings underscore the competitiveness of the hMACGIC-QUAPI method.

Non-Markovian Dynamics Subject to a Structured Environment

For a demonstration of the capabilities of the developed premerging distributed memory implementation of the hMACGIC-QUAPI method, we simulated the dissipative dynamics of a two-level quantum system (TLS) subject to the interaction with a structured environment. The considered spectral density J(ω) is characterized by a sharp resonance with characteristic frequency Ω, augmented by an Ohmic background (J(ω) ∝ ω): J(ω)=2αωΩ4(Ω2−ω2)2+(2πκωΩ)2 10 where 2πκΩ describes the width of the resonance. Structured environments of the form of eq impose strong non-Markovianity in the dynamics and pose a persistent challenge to simulations of the real time dynamics of open-quantum systems. , The model is relevant for excitation energy transfer in biological light harvesting complexes where discrete nuclear modes can modulate the transfer dynamics or novel quantum technologies, like photonic cavities or superconducting quantum interference (dc-SQUID) devices.

The discrete underdamped mode at characteristic frequency Ω induces long-time system-bath correlations that can strongly affect the system dynamics. In the time-domain, the narrow peak in the spectral density given by eq leads to a slowly oscillatory decay of the corresponding influence coefficients and thus a long-time non-Markovian memory that is challenging to converge in QUAPI simulations. Specifically, for the investigated parameter regime 2πκΩ ≈ 35 cm–1 (see below), the memory time spans some ≈ 228 time steps (until influence coefficients drop to 1% of the zero-time lag value).

For numerical solution we resort to a primary reaction coordinate representation − where the TLS interacts with a primary mode that in turn is coupled to a harmonic environment: H=ℏΔ2σx+ℏgσz(B†+B)+ℏΩB†B+(B†+B)∑iℏνi(bi†+bi)+∑iℏωkbi†bi 11 The Hamiltonian of eq represents an effective quantum system with extended Hilbert space, composed of the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom of the primary mode (first three terms in eq , denoted H TLS+HO). We consider a bias-free TLS (ϵ = 0), and B and B † denote annihilation and creation operators of the primary mode, respectively. The operators b i and b i † are the corresponding annihilation and creation operators of bath modes and σ x/z are Pauli matrices.

It has been demonstrated that the form of eq , where part of the environment has been absorbed into the extended system H TLS+HO, is more amenable for simulations with the QUAPI method. The effective bath of H TLS+HO , represented by oscillators b i , has the form of an Ohmic spectral density J(ω) = κω exp­[−ω/ωc] with system-bath coupling constant κ and cutoff frequency ωc. The original system-bath coupling α, coupling constant κ and the coupling g, describing the interaction between the TLS and the primary mode, are interconnected: αΩ2 = 8κg 2. Generally, the effective system-bath interaction κ may be chosen small and the bath memory time is substantially reduced by the simplified form of J(ω). In practice, the harmonic oscillator basis of the extended system H TLS+HO has to be truncated.

We would like to note, that the presented, strongly non-Markovian simulations in the primary mode representation of eq are computationally demanding. Strong non-Markovian effects of the structured environment are exemplified by a hybridization of system and environmental degrees of freedom and thus a strong renormalization of the system frequency (cf. Typically reported dynamics are characterized by a decay of coherence, that approximately resembles exponential damping and regular oscillatory dynamics, in stark contrast to the current case. Below, we demonstrate that convergence on a < 2% scale can be achieved with the hMACGIC-QUAPI method for such strongly non-Markovian dynamics. For the converged dynamics shown in Figure runtime was ∼32 h on the 96 cores of a four socket Intel Xeon Platinum 8160 processor system (2 TB shared memory). The peak memory requirement was 24–26 GB per MPI process or at most 104 GB per node (taking into account duplicating data due to the interprocess mask merging).

At resonance Ω = Δ, both peaks have the same intensity and should be located at Δ – g = 0.82Δ and Δ + g = 1.18Δ which is in good agreement with the numerical values of 0.87Δ and 1.15Δ, respectively. We assign the differences to a nontrivial renormalization of system resonance frequency due to the nonperturbative system bath interaction already at κ = 0.056. While for strongly detuned off-resonance conditions, the frequency positions of both peaks reasonably follow the perturbative predictions, substantial deviations are recognized for close-to-the-resonance dynamics (green and purple lines in Figure b). The arising sideband shoulders due to vibrational excitations among excited states are not accounted for by the perturbative treatment.

A range of sophisticated, nonperturbative real time propagation methods of dissipative quantum dynamics have recently emerged, like matrix-product operator based TEMPO, where numerical cost of the powerful algorithm is determined by a singular value decomposition in each step, the small matrix path integral, and multisite tensor network path integral frameworks that address the exponential scaling with system size in multisite problems. In the current work, we have presented a versatile, parallel implementation of the QUAPI method that extends applicability of numerically exact QUAPI benchmark simulations and makes distributed compute resources of modern clusters accessible. The MACGIC approximation makes long-time non-Markovian memory of sluggish environments accessible. In this context, the results of Figure suggest that the mask discretization via Δk eff takes a role comparable to the entanglement parameter in the small matrix path integral. Makri and co-workers have recently demonstrated numerically that for correlations persisting for very long memory times on the order of 50–100 time steps, SMatPI enables efficient iteration as well as the inclusion of important residual memory that become challenging with stand-alone TEMPO implementations. In ref we have similarly shown, that the hMACGIC-QUAPI method allows to retain such residual memory correlations of >70 time steps.

Another notable aspect of the hMACGIC-QUAPI implementation is the treatment of multiple, noncommuting baths as detailed in ref , for which computational costs steeply increase with the QUAPI method and numerical approaches relying on tensor network methods became available only recently. Surprisingly, for the case of two independent noncommuting environments, where one of them is considered as a pure dephasing environment, we have found that the filtering of paths according to a threshold θ is not viable, as all paths carry a significant amplitude and filtering immediately starts to sacrifice the accuracy of the dynamics. Although the low entangled state representation of TEMPO was shown to be very suitable for a wide range of physical systems, the two-bath problem could lead to situations where large number of matrix elements retain substantial magnitude in singular value decomposition.

Conclusions

We have presented a new path premerging algorithm of the hMACGIC-QUAPI method that mitigates the memory bottleneck of the method while preserving numerical accuracy. The used hash map data structure facilitates dynamical memory allocation and the efficient look-up of paths, and is well suited for the irregular allocation requirements of the mask merging algorithm with generally sparse masks.

Performance increases are realized via a hybrid MPI-Intel TBB parallel implementation of the algorithm. The scalable distributed memory parallelization allows to spread the considered paths among the memory of multiple compute nodes, thereby overcoming the memory bottleneck inherent to QUAPI methods. We showed that the current implementation provides a memory scaling as 2.11/n with n being the number of MPI processes. Thus, the peak memory usage per node may be substantially reduced in computations. Scaling of the algorithm has been explored up to 20 nodes, making the implementation suitable for modern supercomputing architectures with infiniband interconnect.

The distributed memory implementation facilitates large scale numerical simulations and was applied to investigate the non-Markovian dynamics imposed by a structured environment. The numerical challenging problem of ultralong system bath correlation times is recast by including the primary mode explicitly into the system Hilbert space which makes the problem tractable for hMACGIG-QUAPI simulations and facilitates the investigation of convergence properties. We find that even for weak coupling of the extended system to an effective Ohmic environment, the system dynamics is highly non-Markovian. The detuning of the primary mode frequency demonstrates the limits of perturbative treatments, as reflected in pronounced sidebands due to vibrational excitations.
