Optimizing Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) Protocols for Upper Limb Motor Recovery in Post-Stroke Patients: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Huda Alhalabi, Yitong Qiu, Yixin Wei, Yi Chen, Qiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a noninvasive intervention aimed at enhancing neuroplasticity and supporting motor recovery in stroke survivors. This meta-analysis examines the efficacy of rTMS in enhancing upper limb motor function and independance in daily activities, utilizing key assessment tools such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE), Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), Modified Barthel Index (MBI), and Action Research Arm Test (ARAT).MethodsA systematic review of 10 studies involving 382 participants was conducted. Outcomes were analyzed for mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using forest plots. Subgroup analyses examined the influence of stroke stage and session length. Evidence quality was assessed using the GRADE framework.ResultsrTMS significantly improved motor performance, with the FMA-UE showing the most consistent and clinically meaningful gains, where the overall standardized mean difference (SMD) was 1.28 (95% CI: 1.82 to 0.74, p < 0.0001, I² = 0%), particularly during subacute and subacute-to-chronic stages. Furthermore, protocols with 15–20 sessions yielded significantly better outcomes (SMD = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.90 to 1.10, p < 0.001) than shorter protocols. ARAT results were less consistent, indicating challenges in fine motor recovery, particularly in chronic stroke populations.ConclusionThis meta-analysis confirms that rTMS effectively enhances upper limb motor function and independence in post-stroke patients. Tailored protocols during subacute stages optimize recovery, though further research is needed to refine protocols and explore long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of disability worldwide, with 55%–75% of survivors experiencing upper limb (UL) motor impairments, significantly impacting daily activities and quality of life (Wolf et al., 2006). Effective rehabilitation of upper limb function is crucial, as it facilitates independence, prevents muscle atrophy, and promotes neuroplasticity through motor recovery exercises (Bertani et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024). Despite advancements in post-stroke rehabilitation, achieving substantial and lasting motor recovery remains a challenge (Lefaucheur et al., 2020).

Among the various rehabilitation strategies for stroke, noninvasive brain stimulation, particularly repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), has gained considerable attention for promoting upper limb motor recovery. rTMS works by modulating cortical excitability and enhancing neuroplasticity, thereby supporting the restoration of motor function (Cotoi et al., 2023). It is commonly delivered in two forms: high-frequency stimulation to the ipsilesional hemisphere to facilitate cortical activity, and low-frequency stimulation to the contralesional hemisphere to suppress maladaptive interhemispheric inhibition (Hsu et al., 2012). Although rTMS has shown potential in improving motor outcomes and functional independence, the evidence remains mixed. Variability in study design, stimulation protocols, stroke stages, and patient characteristics contribute to inconsistent results (Adeyemo et al., 2018). Factors such as time since stroke, severity of motor impairment, and combination with conventional therapies play significant roles in influencing treatment efficacy (Page et al., 2012), emphasizing the need for protocol optimization.

To assess therapeutic effects, standardized tools such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE), Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), and Modified Barthel Index (MBI) are frequently used (Hoyer et al., 2020b; Mahoney & Barthel, 1965; Shah et al., 1989; Wolf et al., 2001; Yozbatiran et al., 2008). Their consistent application allows for more accurate comparison across clinical studies and supports synthesis of treatment outcomes (Chen et al., 2023a). However, prior meta-analyses have encountered several limitations. Tang et al. (2023) explored neural mechanisms using functional imaging but did not include quantitative motor outcome synthesis. Fong et al. (2024) assessed combined rTMS and rPMS but observed no added benefit over rTMS alone. Ahmed et al. (2023) found that noninvasive brain stimulation improved FMA and MBI scores, especially in acute and subacute patients, yet protocol heterogeneity and insufficient stratification limited clinical interpretation. Zhang et al. (2025) confirmed rTMS benefits in early and severe stroke stages but did not analyze variations in stimulation parameters or lesion characteristics. Similarly, Luo et al. (2024) suggested that combining rTMS with rPMS may enhance motor function and reduce spasticity, though their conclusions were constrained by small sample sizes, low methodological quality, and potential biases. In light of these findings, there remains a clear need for more comprehensive and stratified investigations. The current study seeks to address these gaps by systematically evaluating the effects of rTMS on upper limb function across different stroke stages, severity levels, stimulation protocols, and functional outcome domains, aiming to guide more effective and evidence-based clinical applications.

Although numerous meta-analyses have examined the effects of rTMS on upper limb recovery after stroke, many have treated it as a uniform intervention without accounting for critical clinical moderators such as stimulation laterality (ipsilesional vs. contralesional), stroke stage (subacute vs. chronic), and session number. These limitations reduce the clinical applicability of their findings. The present study addresses these gaps by stratifying outcomes according to stroke stage and treatment parameters, and by evaluating dose–response relationships based on the number of sessions and stimulation laterality. Using standardized outcome measures—including the FMA-UE, WMFT, ARAT, and MBI—this meta-analysis provides a more detailed and reliable synthesis. It further incorporates neurophysiological insights into cortical excitability modulation and interhemispheric rebalancing to support mechanistic interpretations. By framing results within the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), the study offers a broader understanding of rTMS effects across impairment, activity, and participation levels. Overall, it aims to consolidate fragmented findings, clarify treatment efficacy, and inform the development of optimized, evidence-based rehabilitation protocols.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy

This research is a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). To identify relevant studies, we used a comprehensive searching strategy based on high-quality literature. The search included the following terms: “After Stroke,” “Upper Limb,” “Motor Function,” “Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation” (rTMS), and “Motor Cortex” (M1). Databases searched included PubMed, MEDLINE, AND Cochrane Library for studies published in English between 2022 and 2024.

Eligibility Criteria

The studies were chosen following the PICOS framework and then carefully screened according to pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Stroke patients diagnosed via transcranial CT or MRI, presenting with varying degrees of upper limb motor dysfunction. Eligible patients were 18 years or older, with no restrictions on nationality, race, gender, or disease duration. (2) The control group received either conventional rehabilitation therapy or sham-NIBS, while the experimental group underwent NIBS interventions for comparison. NIBS mainly consisted of high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS), low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS), and intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS). (3) Studies utilizing the following outcome measures were included: FMA, ARAT, MBI, and WMFT. Additionally, assessments considered the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), motor evoked potential (MEP) latency, MEP amplitude, and central motor conduction time (CMCT). (4) Only RCTs were included in the analysis.

Exclusion criteria: Studies were excluded if they were non-RCTs, had inconsistent outcomes or lacked corresponding result indicators, provided incomplete or unreliable data with evident errors, or did not include baseline data or had an unbalanced baseline.

Study Selection

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote for systematic organization and management. Initially, duplicates were identified and removed to avoid redundant analysis. Next, the remaining articles underwent an initial screening process based on the titles and abstracts to assess their relevance to the study. Articles which did not meet the inclusion criteria at this stage were excluded. For articles where the title and abstract were inconclusive or when reviewers had differing opinions, the full text was reviewed in detail to make an informed decision regarding inclusion. This multi-step process ensured that only the most relevant studies were selected for further analysis, enhancing the quality and precision of the study.

Data Extraction

Database were collected from all eligible studies based on key characteristics, including study design, sample sizes, and participant demographics (age, gender, stroke type). Details of the rTMS intervention were recorded, covering frequency (high frequency HF or low frequency LF), intensity, session length, and duration. Outcomes focused on motor function recovery measures: FMA-UE, WMFT, ARAT, and MBI, with data collected on baseline, post-intervention, and follow-up scores, including mean differences, standard deviations SDs, and standard errors SEs. Risk of bias factors were noted, such as randomization methods, blinding, and handling of missing data.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated by the Cochrane Collaboration RoB2_IRPG Excel tool (Beta Version 9). It was assessed across five key domains. First, we evaluated the randomization process to ensure allocation was random, concealed, and resulted in comparable baseline characteristics between groups. Second, we assessed deviations from intended interventions by examining whether participants, caregivers, or outcome assessors were aware of the assigned interventions, and whether deviations from the trial protocol occurred. Third, we considered the impact of missing outcome data, investigating whether the missingness could bias results based on the outcome's true value. Fourth, we evaluated bias in the measurement of outcomes, ensuring that measurement methods were appropriate and consistent across groups, and that assessors were blinded to the group allocation. Finally, we examined the selection of the reported results, verifying those analyses followed pre-specified plans and that selective reporting was avoided. This comprehensive bias assessment helped ensure the robustness and validity of our findings. The included studies were evaluated for. Studies were classified as low risk if no domains indicated potential bias, some concerns if at least one domain raised issues but none were considered high risk, and high risk if bias was identified in at least one domain.

Statistical Analysis

For this meta-analysis, Mean Differences (MDs) were used as the effect size to compare outcomes between the rTMS and sham rTMS groups. MD was selected because all included studies measured outcomes on consistent and continuous scales, allowing for direct and clinically meaningful interpretation in the original units of measurement. Calculations incorporated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the precision of estimates. A random-effects model was employed to account for potential heterogeneity across studies, with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method utilized to estimate variance between studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on stroke stages and the number of rTMS sessions to explore potential moderators of the treatment effect. Forest plots were generated to visualize individual study results, subgroup effects, and the overall pooled estimate, including MDs with 95% CIs. Studies were weighted by sample size and variance to ensure precise studies contributed more to the pooled effect.

Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics and subgroup-specific random-effects analyses, with I² values categorized as low (0%–25%), moderate-to-low (25%–50%), moderate-to-high (50%–75%), or high (>75%), to quantify variability across studies and ensure appropriate modeling of pooled results. The results included Z-values and P-values for both subgroups and overall pooled results, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, where symmetry would suggest minimal bias, and asymmetry could indicate potential publication bias or heterogeneity. To ensure the robustness and reliability of this meta-analysis, several statistical methods were employed. Funnel plots were utilized to assess publication bias with symmetrical distributions indicating minimal risk of bias. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the effects of key variables, specifically the number of rTMS sessions and the stages of stroke recovery.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 1,351 studies were identified through database searches: 240 from MEDLINE, 236 from PubMed, and 875 from Cochrane. The selection flow process is shown in Figure 1.

The PRISMA flow diagram for selecting studies in the meta-analysis.

Study Characteristics of Included Studies

The 10 included RCTs were conducted across China (7 studies), Lithuania, Brazil, and Taiwan, with sample sizes ranging from 10 to 72 participants. The studies focused on different stroke recovery stages: four on chronic stroke, four on subacute stroke, and three on a mixed subacute-to-chronic stage. Ischemic stroke was more prevalent than hemorrhagic stroke. rTMS interventions included high-frequency (HF-rTMS), low-frequency (LF-rTMS), and iTBS, with intensities between 70% and 100% of the resting motor threshold (RMT) and pulse counts from 576 to 1,800 per session. Stimulation primarily targeted the primary motor cortex (M1), with some studies also focusing on the supplementary motor area (SMA) and premotor cortex (PMC), applied to either the ipsilesional or contralesional hemisphere. The heterogeneity of protocols highlights the need for systematic evaluation to determine optimal rTMS parameters for upper limb motor recovery post-stroke.

Control interventions included sham rTMS, and sham iTBS combined with conventional therapy, with session durations ranging from 20 to 120 min. The outcome measures included FMA-UE, ARAT, and WMFT. The number of therapy sessions varied significantly, from single sessions to as many as 30 sessions across different studies (Table 1).

Risk of Bias

The risk of bias results, as shown in Figure 2, indicate generally low risk across most studies, with some concerns in specific domains. In the randomization process (D1), most studies demonstrate low risk, though three studies exhibit some concerns, potentially indicating issues with allocation concealment or baseline imbalances. For deviations from intended interventions (D2), all studies show low risk, suggesting adherence to the planned protocols with minimal deviations that could impact outcomes. In the domain of missing outcome data (D3), all studies are rated as low risk, indicating minimal or no missing outcome that could bias the results. Measurement of the outcome (D4) also shows low risk across all studies, suggesting consistent and appropriate outcome assessment methodologies that are unlikely to have differed between groups. Most of studies are rated as low risk for the selection of the reported result (D5), while three studies exhibit some concerns, indicating that the results reported are unlikely to be selectively chosen based on favorable outcomes.

Risk-of-bias assessment of the included studies.

Overall, the studies included in this analysis generally present a low risk of bias, with minor concerns noted in the randomization process for a few studies. These findings suggest that the included studies have implemented robust methods, enhancing the reliability of the meta-analysis findings.

Meta-Analysis Results

The Effectiveness of rTMS on FMA-UE by Stroke Stage

The meta-analysis evaluates the effectiveness of rTMS on FMA-UE scores across different stroke stages, as shown by the forest plot in Figure 3. In the chronic stroke subgroup, the pooled MD was 0.85 (95% CI = [−3.30]–[4.99], p = 0.69), with low heterogeneity (I² = 6.7%). The results indicate no statistically or clinically significant effect for this group. In the subacute stroke subgroup, the MD was 5.93 (95% CI = [2.62]–[9.23], p < 0.001), demonstrating a statistically and clinically significant effect with low heterogeneity (I² = 27.1%). For the subacute-to-chronic stroke subgroup, the MD was 7.45 (95% CI = [4.77]–[10.13], p < 0.001), showing highly significant and clinically meaningful improvement with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). Overall, across all stroke stages, the pooled MD was 5.28 (95% CI = [2.69]–[7.88], p < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 39.3%). The test for subgroup differences (p = 0.0317) indicated statistically significant variation in rTMS effectiveness across stroke stages, with chronic stroke showing minimal benefit compared to the stronger effects observed in subacute and subacute-to-chronic stages.

Forest plot of mean difference and 95% CIs for the effects of rTMS with FMA-UE by stroke stage.

It is also noteworthy that Chang et al. (2024) assessed post-stroke quality of life and self-perceived disability using the Stroke Impact Scale (SIS), which encompasses eight domains: strength, hand function, activities of daily living (ADL), mobility, communication, emotion, memory and thinking, and social participation. The SIS consists of 59 items rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. Their findings demonstrated that the group receiving both repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) and iTBS showed statistically significant improvements in the Strength (p = 0.019) and ADL (p = 0.040) domains, with a marginal improvement observed in Hand Function (p = 0.050). In contrast, the sham rPMS + iTBS group exhibited significant gains only in the Mobility domain (p = 0.034). However, there were no significant between-group differences in total SIS scores or across the eight individual domains.

These findings emphasize the effectiveness of rTMS, particularly in subacute and subacute-to-chronic stroke stages, while highlighting the need for further exploration of its role in chronic stroke recovery. The funnel plot (Supplementary Materials) showed a basically symmetrical distribution of studies, suggesting minimal evidence of publication bias.

The Effectiveness of rTMS on FMA-UE by Sessions

The forest plot in Figure 4 highlights the effectiveness of rTMS on FMA-UE scores across different session lengths. The 10-session subgroup showed a significant effect (MD = 3.71, 95% CI = [1.37, 6.05], p = 0.0019), but the wide confidence interval indicates lower precision. The 20-session subgroup demonstrated the strongest and most reliable effect (MD = 7.23, 95% CI = [4.46, 10.01], p < 0.001) with a narrower confidence interval and no heterogeneity, suggesting it is the most effective. The 15-session subgroup showed a strong effect (MD = 8.10, 95% CI = [4.66, 11.54], p < 0.001), but with only one study, the evidence is limited. Overall, 20 sessions provide the most robust results which is consistent with previous research (Lv et al., 2023), while evidence for 15 sessions is insufficient, and results for 10 sessions are less precise. The funnel plot indicated overall symmetry, suggesting minimal publication bias. The 20-session subgroup shows the most consistent results, while 10 sessions demonstrate more variability.

Forest plot of mean difference and 95% CIs for the effects of rTMS with FMA-UE by sessions.

Yichen Lv et al. (2023) emphasized functional independence as a rehabilitation goal, suggesting that the improvements observed in FMA scores may contribute to enhanced overall patient satisfaction and quality of life, although standardized QoL instruments like SF-36 or EQ-5D were not used.

The Effectiveness of rTMS on WMFT by Stroke Stage

The forest plot in Figure 5 highlights the effectiveness of rTMS in improving WMFT scores, demonstrating significant gains across stroke stages, despite the primary focus of studies in this meta-analysis being on overall motor function recovery rather than specifically targeting WMFT. For subacute stroke, the pooled MD was 4.70 (95% CI = [3.67]–[5.73], Z = 8.96, p < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) indicating consistent and reliable improvements in time-based performance on functional tasks as measured by WMFT. In the subacute-to-chronic stroke subgroup, the MD was 8.27 (95% CI = [4.58]–[11.96], Z = 4.39, p < 0.001), though this finding is based on a single study and should be interpreted cautiously. The overall pooled MD was 4.96 (95% CI = [3.97]–[5.95], Z = 9.81, p < 0.001), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 31.3%), reinforcing rTMS's effectiveness in enhancing upper limb task performance. While the primary focus of these studies was on general motor recovery, the significant improvements in WMFT scores highlight rTMS's potential to enhance task-specific upper limb function. The funnel plot indicated a generally symmetrical distribution of studies, suggesting minimal publication bias.

Forest plot of mean difference and 95% CIs for the effects of rTMS with WMFT by stroke stage.

Lu Wang et al. (2022) reported a significant improvement in WMFT scores following rTMS, and their discussion links enhanced motor function with improved daily activities and self-efficacy, which are indirectly associated with better quality of life and psychosocial well-being.

The Effectiveness of rTMS on WMFT by Sessions

The forest plot in Figure 6 highlights the effectiveness of rTMS on FMA-UE scores across different session lengths. The 10-session subgroup showed a significant effect (MD = 3.80, 95% CI = [0.39, 7.21], p = 0.0288), but the wider confidence interval indicates lower precision and variability in the effect. The 20-session subgroup demonstrated the strongest and most reliable effect (MD = 4.70, 95% CI = [3.67, 5.73], p < 0.001) with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%), making it the most consistent and effective intervention. The 15-session subgroup also showed a strong effect (MD = 8.27, 95% CI = [4.58, 11.96], p < 0.001), but with only one study included, the evidence remains limited. Overall, the 20-session subgroup provides the most robust and reliable results, consistent with its high contribution to the overall model, while evidence for 15 sessions is limited and results for 10 sessions are less precise. These findings underscore the importance of session length in optimizing rTMS interventions. The funnel plot was reasonably symmetrical, with no obvious clustering or missing studies on either side. This indicates a low likelihood of publication bias.

The Effectiveness of rTMS on MBI by Stroke Stage and Sessions

In this section, the subgroups for stroke stage sessions were analyzed, where subacute stroke studies used 20 sessions, and subacute-to-chronic stroke studies used 10 sessions. The forest plot in Figure 7 evaluates the effectiveness of rTMS on MBI scores across these subgroups categorized by stroke stage and session length.

Forest plot of mean difference and 95% CIs for the effects of rTMS with MBI by stroke stage and sessions.

For the subacute stroke subgroup (20 sessions), the pooled mean difference (MD) was 10.00 (95% CI = [7.12, 12.88], p < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). This result underscores the robust and consistent impact of rTMS in significantly improving functional independence, as measured by MBI, for subacute stroke patients. The relatively larger effect size for this subgroup indicates that rTMS is particularly effective during the subacute phase of recovery, likely benefiting from heightened neuroplasticity and responsiveness to intervention. In the subacute-to-chronic stroke subgroup (10 sessions), the pooled MD was 6.98 (95% CI = [3.23, 10.73], p < 0.001), also with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). While this result highlights significant improvements in MBI scores, the smaller effect size compared to the subacute stroke group suggests that rTMS remains beneficial but may have reduced efficacy as recovery progresses into the chronic phase when neuroplasticity begins to decline. The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, suggesting a low probability of publication bias.

The overall random-effects model, combining both subgroups, reported an MD of 8.88 (95% CI = [6.60, 11.16], p < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). These findings emphasize the consistent and statistically significant benefits of rTMS across different stroke stages in improving MBI scores. In summary, rTMS demonstrates substantial effectiveness in enhancing functional independence for subacute stroke patients, particularly with 20-session protocols. Its benefits, while still significant, are slightly reduced in subacute-to-chronic stroke patients with fewer sessions. This highlights the importance of early and intensive rTMS interventions during the subacute phase to optimize recovery potential as reflected in MBI improvements.

Alongside with the statistical evidence for MBI score significant improvements, Lu Wang et al. (2022) mainly used MBI to evaluate the ability of daily life activities based on the patient's actual daily performance. This scale includes 11 items, such as eating, bathing, grooming and dressing, etc., and each item is scored at 5 levels with a full score of 100. A higher score indicated a better ability of daily life activities.

In the study by Wu et al. (2023), the evaluation of patients’ ability to perform ADL was conducted using a 10-item scale encompassing tasks such as eating, grooming, dressing, and transferring. The maximum attainable score was 100. According to the scoring criteria: scores below 20 indicated total dependence; scores between 21 and 40 signified severe impairment with high dependency; scores from 41 to 59 reflected moderate impairment requiring some assistance; scores above 60 indicated mild impairment with basic self-care capabilities; and a perfect score of 100 denoted complete independence in daily activities.

The Effectiveness of rTMS on ARAT

The effectiveness of rTMS on ARAT scores is depicted in Figure 8, with the included studies primarily examining chronic stroke and subacute-to-chronic stroke populations. In the chronic stroke subgroup, which included three studies with 10 sessions each, the pooled MD was 2.86 (95% CI = [−1.45]–[7.18]), suggesting a trend toward improvement, though the results were not statistically significant (Z = 1.3, p = 0.194). There was no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) in these studies. For the subacute to chronic stroke subgroup, a single study with 10 sessions reported an MD of 2.60 (95% CI = [−13.79]–[18.99]), with no significant effect (Z = 0.31, p = 0.756).

Forest plot of mean difference and 95% CIs for the effects of rTMS with ARAT by stroke stage.

The overall random-effects model, combining all studies, yielded an MD of 2.85 (95% CI = [−1.33]–[7.02]), with a nonsignificant effect (Z = 1.34, p = 0.181) and no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The insignificant results might be attributed to two key factors. First, the session length of 10, as seen in the current analysis, has previously shown limited efficacy in measures such as the FMA-UE, where longer session protocols demonstrated stronger and statistically significant results. Second, the predominant focus on chronic stroke populations in the included studies contributed to the minimal observed benefit. Chronic stroke is known to show less responsiveness to rTMS compared to the stronger effects typically observed in subacute and subacute-to-chronic stages, as evidenced in prior analyses of FMA-UE outcomes. These findings underline the need to optimize rTMS protocols, including session lengths and targeting earlier recovery stages, to maximize therapeutic benefits for motor function recovery.

Ipsilesional vs. Contralesional rTMS Effects on FMA-UE

In response to the growing interest in optimizing stimulation strategies, we performed a subgroup analysis comparing ipsilesional and contralesional rTMS, as shown in Figure 9. However, only three studies in this meta-analysis have included the effect of on ipsilesional and contralesional rTMS. The results showed that ipsilesional stimulation led to a significant improvement in upper limb motor function compared to the control group (MD = 4.32, 95% CI: 0.49 to 8.14, P = 0.027), whereas the contralesional subgroup did not reach statistical significance (MD = 1.93, 95% CI: −0.62 to 4.48, P = 0.139). Although the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (P = 0.308), these findings suggest a trend favoring ipsilesional stimulation, which may reflect its potential to more directly enhance cortical excitability in the affected hemisphere. This distinction supports previous reports highlighting the functional benefits of targeting the lesioned hemisphere, particularly in patients with preserved corticospinal tract integrity. Future studies should further explore how lesion location, corticospinal tract condition, and stroke chronicity interact with stimulation laterality to guide individualized rTMS protocols.

Neurophysiological Mechanisms of rTMS

Cortical excitability modulation is one of the primary mechanisms involved in rTMS-induced recovery. HF-rTMS applied to the ipsilesional primary motor cortex (M1) enhances cortical excitability, while LF-rTMS suppresses excitability in the contralesional M1. This balance of cortical activity is essential for restoring motor function in the affected upper extremity. For example, in the study by Jing Ni et al. (2023), HF-rTMS significantly improved FMA scores by 6.1 points (95% CI: 5.3–7.0), indicating the efficacy of HF-rTMS in modulating cortical excitability for motor recovery. Additionally, (Zhang et al., 2022) provide compelling evidence that rTMS restores the disrupted balance between cortical excitation and inhibition. Their findings revealed increased ipsilesional motor thresholds, reduced MEP amplitudes, and prolonged cortical silent periods (CSP) following rTMS treatment, highlighting the potential of rTMS to modulate the excitability of motor-related cortical areas and promote functional network reorganization (Gao et al., 2022).

Interhemispheric inhibition is another pivotal mechanism in rTMS's effects on stroke rehabilitation. After a stroke, the interhemispheric balance is often disrupted, with the contralesional M1 becoming hyperexcitable. LF-rTMS over the contralesional M1 restores this balance by reducing excitability in the unaffected hemisphere, thereby promoting the activation of the affected hemisphere. Petruseviciene et al. (2023) demonstrated that LF-rTMS significantly improved motor function in the affected upper extremity, with participants showing a 5.3-point improvement in FMA scores. This highlights the importance of restoring interhemispheric balance to improve motor function and facilitate recovery in stroke patients (Ding et al., 2022).

Finally, rTMS also promotes neuroplasticity, which is essential for long-term motor recovery. By facilitating the formation of new neural connections and enhancing synaptic strength, rTMS encourages cortical reorganization and enables the brain to compensate for lost function. Wu et al. (2023) observed significant changes in brain network parameters alongside functional recovery, with participants showing a 4.5-point improvement in FMA scores. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of rTMS, as it harnesses the brain's ability to reorganize itself and repair damaged motor pathways, thereby contributing to sustained recovery following stroke.

Safety and Tolerability of rTMS

“Several clinical trials investigating rTMS in stroke patients have reported that rTMS is generally well-tolerated, with minimal adverse events. In the study by Gao et al. (2022), no severe adverse events were observed, with only mild and transient side effects, such as headache and scalp discomfort, reported by a small percentage of participants. These side effects did not lead to any treatment discontinuation, suggesting that rTMS is a safe and tolerable intervention for post-stroke rehabilitation. Similarly, Wu et al. (2023) reported that both high-frequency rTMS and cervical nerve root magnetic stimulation were well-tolerated in stroke patients, with no severe adverse effects observed during the treatment period. Importantly, all participants completed the study without requiring treatment discontinuation, further confirming the safety of rTMS as a noninvasive neuromodulation technique.

In addition, de Freitas Zanona et al. (2023) and Chang et al. (2024) observed similar results, with mild side effects, such as tingling sensations or transient discomfort at the stimulation site, but no significant adverse events that impacted patient safety or led to discontinuation. These studies emphasize the importance of proper safety monitoring during rTMS treatment, especially in post-stroke patients, but also highlight the overall favorable safety profile of rTMS.

To enhance patient safety, this review recommends rigorous safety monitoring during rTMS intervention, including regular assessments of cognitive function, motor responses, and any potential adverse effects. Although rTMS has demonstrated a favorable safety profile, continued monitoring is essential to ensure patient well-being throughout the treatment process, particularly for patients with severe motor impairments.

Quality of Evidence by GRADE Approach

The quality of evidence for each outcome was rigorously evaluated using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) framework. A Summary of Findings (SoF) table was developed to provide a concise overview of the evidence quality for both primary and secondary outcomes (refer to the Supplementary Materials). Data, including MD and 95% confidence intervals (CI), were extracted from forest plots alongside heterogeneity measures (I² statistic) and subgroup differences. Each outcome was evaluated across five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias.

Risk of bias was evaluated through a detailed examination of the included studies, with downgrades applied for deficiencies in randomization, blinding, or selective reporting. Inconsistency was determined based on heterogeneity (I²), with single-study cases appropriately marked as “Not Applicable.” Imprecision was assessed by analyzing confidence intervals; wide intervals including the null effect warranted downgrades. Indirectness was addressed by ensuring the studies were directly relevant to the target population, intervention, and outcomes, with no concerns identified. Publication bias was excluded based on symmetrical funnel plot analyses.

The findings revealed high-quality evidence for the MBI, WMFT, and FMA-UE in subacute stroke patients, demonstrating robust benefits of rTMS. Moderate-quality evidence was observed for FMA-UE in subacute to chronic stroke due to minor concerns about risk of bias. Conversely, low-quality evidence was identified for FMA-UE in chronic stroke and ARAT outcomes, primarily due to wide confidence intervals and null effects, reflecting uncertainty. These assessments emphasize the strong evidence supporting rTMS efficacy in subacute stroke recovery while underscoring the need for further investigation in chronic stroke populations.

Discussion

Summary of Findings

This meta-analysis investigated the effectiveness of rTMS on upper limb recovery in post-stroke patients by analyzing FMA-UE, WMFT, MBI, and ARAT outcomes across 10 studies involving 382 participants. The selection of studies was driven by a focus on motor function recovery, primarily assessed using FMA-UE. However, the inclusion of additional measures such as WMFT, MBI, and ARAT allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of rTMS's broader impact, encompassing motor performance, daily functional independence, and fine motor skills.

The results affirm that rTMS significantly enhances upper limb motor function and task-specific outcomes, with the magnitude of improvement varying by stroke stage, session length, and the outcome measure used. Subgroup analyses revealed the strongest effects during the subacute and subacute-to-chronic stages, where heightened neuroplasticity supports more substantial recovery (Gao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). FMA-UE, as the primary motor recovery measure, demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in these stages (Cunningham et al., 2022; Di Pino et al., 2014). WMFT and MBI provided further evidence of rTMS's effectiveness, with notable gains in functional task performance and daily independence (Lv et al., 2023). Although ARAT showed less consistent improvements, it highlighted the challenges of achieving fine motor skill recovery in chronic stroke populations (Chang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022). Ipsilesional rTMS showed significant improvement in upper limb motor recovery (MD = 4.32, P = 0.027), while contralesional stimulation did not. Although between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.308), a trend favored ipsilesional targeting. These results support the potential benefits of stimulating the lesioned hemisphere in stroke rehabilitation.

The GRADE assessment revealed high-quality evidence for MBI, WMFT, and FMA-UE in subacute stroke patients, demonstrating robust benefits of rTMS. Moderate-quality evidence was found for FMA-UE in subacute-to-chronic stages, while low-quality evidence was noted for ARAT and FMA-UE in chronic stroke due to imprecision and null effects. These findings highlight strong support for rTMS in subacute recovery but call for further research.

Comparison with Previous Studies

The findings align with and expand upon previous studies, which have consistently supported rTMS as an effective intervention for enhancing upper limb motor function in stroke rehabilitation. Earlier research emphasized the role of rTMS in modulating cortical excitability, with high-frequency stimulation targeting the ipsilesional hemisphere and low-frequency stimulation applied to the contralesional hemisphere (Chen et al., 2023; Di Pino et al., 2014; Grefkes & Fink, 2014). However, most prior reviews focused predominantly on FMA-UE as the primary outcome, overlooking other dimensions of recovery.

This meta-analysis adds to the literature by integrating additional measures such as WMFT, MBI, and ARAT, providing a more holistic view of rTMS's impact. WMFT outcomes demonstrated significant improvements in time-based task performance, aligning with previous evidence of task-specific gains (Du et al., 2022; Hoyer et al., 2020a). MBI, a measure of functional independence, further corroborated the broader benefits of rTMS, highlighting its potential to improve daily living outcomes alongside motor recovery (Cunningham et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). ARAT findings were less conclusive, particularly in chronic stroke populations, where shorter session protocols (e.g., 10 sessions) may have limited efficacy. This aligns with prior observations that fine motor skill recovery is more challenging and requires more intensive or targeted interventions (Peurala et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2022).

Mechanisms of Recovery

The therapeutic effects of rTMS on motor recovery are underpinned by mechanisms of neuroplasticity and cortical reorganization. High-frequency rTMS enhances cortical excitability in the ipsilesional hemisphere, facilitating synaptic potentiation and motor recovery, while low-frequency rTMS mitigates interhemispheric inhibition by reducing contralesional excitability (Di Lazzaro et al., 2008; Murase et al., 2004). These mechanisms are particularly effective during the subacute stage, when neuroplasticity is heightened and recovery potential is maximized.

Neuroimaging studies support these findings, demonstrating post-rTMS changes in functional connectivity within motor networks that correlate with improved performance on FMA-UE, WMFT, and ARAT (Arai et al., 2009; Grefkes & Fink, 2014). The inclusion of MBI outcomes further highlights rTMS's broader impact, as improved motor function translates into gains in functional independence, suggesting enhanced integration of motor recovery into daily activities.

Clinical Implications

The results of this meta-analysis carry important implications for clinical practice. FMA-UE consistently emerged as the most reliable and sensitive measure of motor recovery, making it an essential tool for evaluating rTMS effectiveness in stroke rehabilitation (Gao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). WMFT, as a task-specific measure, provided insights into improvements in functional task performance, particularly in subacute and subacute-to-chronic stroke populations (Hoyer et al., 2020a; Lv et al., 2023). MBI, measuring functional independence, underscored the broader implications of rTMS on quality of life, reinforcing its utility as a comprehensive rehabilitation tool (Petruseviciene et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). ARAT findings suggest the need for tailored protocols targeting fine motor recovery, particularly in chronic stroke populations. This analysis supports the prioritization of longer session lengths, such as 20 sessions, to maximize outcomes, especially during the subacute stage. Clinicians should integrate rTMS with task-specific rehabilitation exercises to further enhance recovery, leveraging its benefits across different outcome measures (Cunningham et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2022).

Limitations

This meta-analysis has some limitations. First, heterogeneity in rTMS protocols, session lengths, and outcome measures may have influenced the results, despite subgroup analyses addressing variability (Lv et al., 2023). However, variability in other stimulation parameters—such as session duration and total number of pulses—remained across studies and could not be fully controlled. This heterogeneity limits firm conclusions regarding the optimal dosing strategy and highlights the need for standardized protocol designs in future trials. Second, the predominance of studies using 10-session protocols limits the generalizability to longer interventions, which have shown greater efficacy in previous research (Di Pino et al., 2014; Peurala et al., 2008). Third, small sample sizes in some studies, particularly for ARAT, reduced the statistical power of the findings (Zhang et al., 2022). Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up restricted insights into the sustained effects of rTMS. Future studies should prioritize standardizing protocols, using larger cohorts, and extending follow-up durations to enhance the reliability and applicability of results. Another limitation is the inability to stratify the effects of rTMS based on stroke severity (e.g., mild, moderate, severe) using FMA or ARAT scores, as most included studies reported stroke stage (e.g., subacute or chronic) without detailed baseline motor severity classifications. This limited our ability to perform more granular subgroup analyses. Additionally, most included trials were conducted in China, which may limit generalizability to health-system contexts with different rehabilitation pathways, demographics, or care standards. Finally, inadequate reporting of lesion location (e.g., cortical vs. subcortical, corticospinal tract integrity) and handedness (dominant vs. nondominant side affected) precluded subgroup analyses, and these factors may moderate rTMS response.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis validates the effectiveness of rTMS in enhancing upper limb motor function and promoting independence in stroke patients. Significant benefits were observed across key measures, including FMA-UE, WMFT, and MBI, particularly in the subacute stage with longer protocols (15–20 sessions). While chronic stroke patients showed modest gains, tailoring rTMS protocols to recovery stages remains essential. Future research should refine protocols, investigate long-term effects, and explore mechanisms underlying differential responses to establish rTMS as a key tool in stroke rehabilitation.
