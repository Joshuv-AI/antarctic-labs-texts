Characteristics of kidney stone-modulatory proteins decoded from proteins identified in stone matrix and urine of stone formers and non-stone subjects
Authors: Niracha Koeipudsa, Supatcha Sassanarakkit, Paleerath Peerapen, Visith Thongboonkerd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kidney stone formation is a sophisticated pathogenic process regulated by several stone-modulatory proteins, namely inhibitors and promoters. However, the characteristics of these proteins that determine their modulatory activities have remained largely unknown. Herein, we investigated potential characteristics of inhibitors and promoters involved in the stone-forming process by comparing amino acid contents, various physico-chemical properties, and calcium- and oxalate-binding properties of all proteins identified from normal urine (NU), stone former urine (SFU), and stone matrix (SM). Simple comparisons among the three groups showed that SM proteins apparently differed from NU and SFU proteins for almost all parameters, whereas no significant differences were detected between NU and SFU proteins. We then compared the proteins that were uniquely found in NU&SM with those uniquely found in SFU&SM, as it was hypothesized that they preferentially acted as stone inhibitors and promoters, respectively. The analyses revealed that proteins rich in amino acids with negative charges, normalized van der Waals volume of 2.95–4.0 and exposed solvent accessibility, acidic proteins, high molecular weight (MW) proteins, stable proteins, and hydrophilic proteins tended to be the stone inhibitors. On the other hand, proteins rich in aromatic and polar amino acids, basic proteins, low-MW proteins, unstable proteins, and hydrophobic proteins tended to be the stone promoters. These findings may help predict the stone-modulatory activities of proteins in urine and SM, and may inform the molecular design of kidney stone therapies.

Introduction

Kidney stone disease is a common health problem with high rates of occurrence and recurrence [1], [2]. Among many types of this disease, calcium oxalate (CaOx) stones are most prevalent in humans [3], [4]. CaOx stones consist mainly of CaOx monohydrate (COM) and CaOx dihydrate (COD) [4]. COM is more common because of its thermodynamic stability, whereas COD is metastable [5]. CaOx stone formation is a complex process with many steps, which generally include crystal nucleation, growth, aggregation and adhesion to renal epithelial cells [6]. Urine supersaturation of stone-generating minerals and imbalance of stone modulators (inhibitors and promoters) strongly influence the stone formation [6], [7]. For example, the urine rich in inhibitors inhibits stone formation, whereas that rich in promoters enhances the stone development [8], [9], [10], [11].

In general, a kidney stone contains both inorganic and organic components [12], [13]. While the majority are inorganic components, organic constituents (including proteins, lipids, glycosaminoglycans, carbohydrates, etc.) can also be found inside the stone mass [8], [14]. Proteins are the most predominant organic constituents in the stone and take part in stone-forming mechanisms [12], [13], [15], [16]. Previously, various proteomics approaches have been applied to identify proteins that are involved in stone-forming mechanisms [12], [13], [17], [18], [19]. Modulatory roles of some specific proteins in kidney stone formation have been validated, e.g., osteopontin (OPN) [15], [20], prothrombin fragment 1 (PTF1) [15], [20], calcineurin B [21], fibronectin [22], heat-shock protein 90 [23], and Tamm-Horsfall protein [24]. Interestingly, some proteins have dual modulatory roles (i.e., act as inhibitors in some steps, while promoting other steps) in the stone-forming process [10], [11]. Interestingly, previous reports have suggested that the modulatory roles of these proteins may be determined by their amino acid contents and properties [22], [25], [26], [27], [28]. However, the characteristics of proteins that determine their stone-modulatory activities remained largely unknown. Therefore, the analysis of amino acid contents and physico-chemical properties of proteins may lead to a better understanding of stone-modulatory roles of proteins.

In the present work, we conducted, for the first time, a large-scale comparative analysis of all proteins identified from normal urine (NU), stone former urine (SFU), and stone matrix (SM). We investigated potential characteristics of inhibitors and promoters involved in the stone-forming process by comparing amino acid contents, various physico-chemical properties, and calcium- and oxalate-binding properties of all proteins identified from NU, SFU and SM. Amino acid contents from these proteins were determined and categorized into different groups according to their sequences and physico-chemical properties, including type, charge, polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, solvent accessibility, isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), instability index, and grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) score. Moreover, the proportion of calcium- and oxalate-binding proteins and the number of calcium- and oxalate-binding sites per protein were also analyzed.

Materials and methods

Data collection

All proteins identified from normal urine (NU), stone former urine (SFU), and stone matrix (SM) were retrieved from previous large-scale proteomics studies [18], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36] that were relevant to our study. The duplicate entries in each group identified from different studies were merged into one, whereas protein isoforms were not included. All these proteins were summarized (Supplementary Tables S1-S3) and analyzed as follows.

Type and charge of amino acid contents

Identifiers or accession numbers of all proteins retrieved as mentioned above (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3) were submitted to the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org) to obtain their amino acid contents. These sequences were then submitted to the ProtrWeb tool (http://protrweb.scbdd.com) to categorize amino acids into five groups according to their chemical structure of side chains, including aromatic, polar, non-polar, positively charged, and negatively charged amino acids. These characteristics were then compared among groups.

Polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, and solvent accessibility

Amino acid sequences of proteins retrieved from NU, SFU and SM (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3) were further stratified based on their polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, and solvent accessibility, which were quantified based on the main clusters of amino acid indices described previously [37], [38], [39]. For each property, all amino acids were classified into three levels according to their attribute types [37], [38], [39]. These properties were compared across groups.

Other physico-chemical properties

In addition, amino acid sequences of proteins retrieved from NU, SFU and SM (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3) were submitted to the Expasy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) to obtain their other physico-chemical properties, including isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), instability index, and grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) score. They were then compared.

Calcium- and oxalate-binding proteins

Finally, the proportion of calcium-binding proteins and the number of calcium-binding sites/protein of all the proteins identified from NU, SFU and SM (see Supplementary Tables S1-S3) were determined using the UniProt database. Additionally, the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins and the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein of all these proteins were analyzed by using the OxaBIND database (https://www.stonemod.org/oxabind.php) [40], and then compared among groups.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were compared among groups (NU vs. SFU vs. SM). In addition, the proteins uniquely found in NU&SM were compared with those uniquely found in SFU&SM, as they preferentially acted as stone inhibitors and promoters, respectively. The Brown-Forsythe test was performed to determine the equality of group variances. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons of equal variances, whereas the Welch’s ANOVA with the Games-Howell post-hoc test was applied when the variances were unequal. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was performed when the variances were equal, whereas the Welch’s t-test was performed when the variances were unequal. The statistical significance was considered when p value was less than 0.05.

Type and charge of amino acid contents of proteins in NU, SFU and SM

Type and charge of individual amino acids of all proteins identified in NU, SFU and SM were compared among groups (Fig. 2A). There were no significant differences in individual amino acid contents found between NU and SFU groups. However, the individual amino acid contents of proteins in the SM group significantly differed from those of NU and SFU proteins. SM proteins contained lower proportions of tryptophan (W), tyrosine (Y), glutamine (Q), threonine (T), leucine (L) and proline (P) than both NU and SFU proteins, and lower proportions of cysteine (C) and serine (S) than SFU proteins. On the other hand, SM proteins had greater proportions of isoleucine (I), methionine (M), lysine (K), arginine (R), aspartic acid (D), and glutamic acid (E) than NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 2A).

In addition to individual amino acids, they were also grouped into aromatic, polar, non-polar, positively-charged, and negatively-charged residues (Fig. 2B-2F). Of all these groups, there were no significant differences found between NU and SFU proteins. Interestingly, NU, SFU and SM proteins contained mainly non-polar amino acids (accounting for > 40 %) with comparable proportions (Fig. 2D). However, SM proteins contained lower proportions of aromatic and polar amino acids (Figs. 2B and 2C) but greater proportions of positively and negatively charged amino acids (Figs. 2E and 2F) than NU and SFU proteins.

Polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, and solvent accessibility of proteins in NU, SFU and SM

Physico-chemical properties of individual amino acids were analyzed and exhibited in terms of polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, and solvent accessibility. For each property (except for solvent accessibility), the individual amino acids were categorized into three levels (i.e., low, medium and high) by their numerical values (Fig. 3A). For the polarity, the data showed significant differences at all levels (Fig. 3B). SM proteins contained lower proportions of amino acids with low and medium polarity but a greater proportion of those with high polarity than NU and SFU proteins that might be due to the higher contents of charged amino acids (K, R, D and E) (Fig. 3B).

Polarizability of amino acids can determine electrical charge distribution, which results from a distortion of the electron cloud under an external electrical field [41]. In general, polarizability is proportional to atomic radius but inversely proportional to the electronegativity of atoms [41]. In this study, NU, SFU and SM proteins had amino acids with medium polarizability as their major components, followed by low and high polarizability, respectively (Fig. 3C). For the low polarizability, there were no significant differences among groups. For both medium and high polarizability groups, SM proteins contained a lower proportion of amino acids with medium polarizability but a greater proportion of amino acids with high polarizability than NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 3C).

Normalized van der Waals volume is the intrinsic volume of proteins that relates to the size of amino acid side chains [42], [43]. Herein, amino acids with low normalized van der Waals volume were the majority in NU, SFU and SM proteins, followed by medium and high normalized van der Waals volume, respectively (Fig. 3D). Among groups, SM proteins showed a lower proportion of amino acids with low normalized van der Waals volume than SFU proteins. By contrast, SM proteins showed a greater proportion of amino acids with high normalized van der Waals volume than NU and SFU proteins. There were no significant differences among groups for amino acids with medium normalized van der Waals volume (Fig. 3D).

Solvent accessibility is determined by the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of proteins in a polar solvent [44]. It is used to quantify the interaction between protein surface and polar solvent (generally water), and can be classified into buried, exposed and intermediate structures [44]. In this work, the majority of amino acids in NU, SFU and SM proteins had buried structure with no significant differences found among groups (Fig. 3E). However, SM proteins contained a greater proportion of amino acids with exposed structure but a lower proportion of amino acids with intermediate structure than NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 3E).

For all these parameters, there were no significant differences found between NU and SFU groups (Fig. 3B-3E).

Other physico-chemical properties of proteins in NU, SFU and SM

Other physico-chemical properties of proteins were also analyzed and exhibited in terms of theoretical isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), instability index, and grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) score (Fig. 4). The average theoretical pI of proteins in SM was higher than that of NU and SFU (Fig. 4A). As a result, SM proteins contained a lower proportion of acidic proteins and higher proportion of basic proteins than NU and SFU groups (Figs. 4B and 4C). Unlike the pI, average MW and instability index as well as their related parameters showed no significant differences among groups (Fig. 4D-4I). It is well known that the GRAVY score represents the hydropathy of proteins – the more negative value indicates the greater hydrophilicity of proteins. Herein, the average GRAVY score of SM proteins was more negative than NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 4J). As a result, SM proteins contained a lower proportion of hydrophobic proteins (Fig. 4K) but a slightly greater proportion of hydrophilic proteins (Fig. 4L) than NU and SFU proteins. There were no significant differences found between NU and SFU groups (Fig. 4A-4L).

Calcium- and oxalate-binding proteins in NU, SFU and SM

SM proteins contained a lower proportion of calcium-binding proteins compared with NU and SFU proteins, but there was no significant difference found between NU and SFU groups (Fig. 5A). For the number of calcium-binding sites/protein, the proportion of oxalate-binding proteins, and the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein, there were no significant differences among groups observed (Fig. 5B-5D).

Comparisons of the proteins that were uniquely found in NU&SM with those uniquely found in SFU&SM

As there were no significant differences found between NU and SFU groups, we then compared the proteins that were uniquely found in NU&SM with those uniquely found in SFU&SM, as they might serve as stone inhibitors and promoters, respectively. There were 34 and 29 proteins uniquely found in NU&SM and SFU&SM, respectively (Fig. 6A). Type and charge of individual amino acids were then compared between these two groups. The results showed that W and C were more abundant in proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM, whereas D and E were more abundant in proteins uniquely found in NU&SM (Fig. 6B). Under grouping of amino residues, the main amino acid components in the two groups of unique proteins were non-polar amino acids. Proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM had greater proportions of aromatic and polar amino acids, whereas proteins uniquely found in NU&SM had a greater proportion of negatively charged amino acids (Fig. 6C).

For polarity and polarizability, there were no significant differences observed at all three levels (Figs. 7A and 7B). However, proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM had lower proportions of amino acids with medium normalized van der Waals volume (Fig. 7C) and those with medium exposed structure of solvent accessibility (Fig. 7D).

For other physico-chemical properties, average values of pI, MW, instability index and GRAVY score showed no significant differences between the two groups (Figs. 8A, 8D, 8G and 8J, respectively). However, proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM had lower proportions of acidic (Fig. 8B), high-MW (Fig. 8F), stable (Fig. 8H) and hydrophilic (Fig. 8L) proteins. On the other hand, proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM had greater proportions of basic (Fig. 8C), low-MW (Fig. 8E), unstable (Fig. 8I) and hydrophobic (Fig. 8K) proteins.

Finally, proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM had a greater proportion of calcium-binding proteins (Fig. 9A) but a lower proportion of oxalate-binding proteins (Fig. 9C). However, the two groups had no significant differences in the number of calcium-binding sites/protein (Fig. 9B) and the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

Kidney stone-forming process is regulated by a large number of proteins. However, the characteristics of these proteins that determine their modulatory activities remained largely unknown. In this work, we investigated the potential characteristics of inhibitory and promoting proteins that might determine their stone-modulatory activities. Types, charges and various physico-chemical properties of amino acid contents in proteins identified from NU, SFU and SM, as well as their calcium- and oxalate-binding properties, were examined and compared. We initially expected that proteins identified from NU would exhibit some characteristics unique to stone inhibitors, whereas those from SFU would exhibit characteristics unique to stone promoters. However, proteins identified from SM might be involved in stone modulation in either way (inhibiting or promoting) or might not be involved in stone formation at all [14]. Surprisingly, the first part of our results (simple comparisons among the three groups, i.e., NU vs. SFU vs. SM) showed no significant differences in all parameters between NU and SFU groups, whereas SM proteins obviously differed from the other two groups. This might be due to the large overlap of proteins identified in the individual groups, particularly NU and SFU groups (231 proteins were common in NU and SFU, 144 were common in SFU and SM, 149 were common in NU and SM, and 115 were common in all three groups) (Fig. 1). Therefore, the second part of this study focused on 34 proteins that were uniquely found in NU&SM compared with 29 proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 6A), as it was hypothesized that they preferentially acted as stone inhibitors and promoters, respectively. It should be emphasized that the further discussion is assumption-based and requires further experimental validation.

Since proteins can influence all steps of stone formation, the key factor in CaOx stone modulation is probably the interaction between proteins and calcium or oxalate ions and between proteins and CaOx crystal faces. In the beginning, crystallization occurs when urine is supersaturated with calcium and oxalate ions [6], [45]. Moreover, crystallization in the urine can occur by heterogeneous nucleation together with organic components (i.e., proteins, lipids and cell debris) as crystallization templates [46]. After crystallization, crystal nuclei are enlarged by the addition of free calcium and oxalate ions (crystal growth) and the formation of microcrystal aggregates (crystal aggregation) [47], [48]. Crystal growth and aggregation can occur concomitantly during the stone-forming process [46]. Crystal aggregation is a crucial process for stone formation because crystal growth alone may be too slow to enlarge the stone nuclei to retain before they are eliminated [15], [46], [47], [48]. Additionally, retention of COM crystals in renal tubules is caused by the adhesion of COM crystals onto renal tubular cells. Therefore, crystal-cell adhesion is another crucial step of stone formation.

Modulatory effects of proteins on crystallization may be affected by their binding affinities with calcium and oxalate ions that depend largely on electrostatic forces [21], [49]. Proteins rich in negatively charged amino acids would favor binding with calcium ions, which most preferably bind to atoms with low polarizability, such as oxygen and nitrogen [21], [49]. By contrast, those rich in positively charged amino acids would favor binding with oxalate ions. The previous experimental evidence has shown that negatively and positively charged amino acids (D and R, respectively) exert inhibitory effects on COM nucleation by increasing nucleation time, but the effect of R is less pronounced than that of D [50]. Our findings showed that both positively and negatively charged amino acids (K, R, D and E) were most abundant in SM proteins, followed by NU and SFU proteins, respectively (Fig. 2A). Moreover, proportions of D and E in proteins uniquely found in NU&SM were greater than those uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 6B). Since D and E are negatively charged and preferentially bind with calcium ions, proteins uniquely found in NU&SM probably exhibited potential characteristics of crystallization inhibitors. In addition, the results showed that proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM contained greater proportions of W (aromatic amino acid) and C (sulfur-containing amino acid) than those uniquely found in NU&SM (Fig. 6B). Some previous studies have revealed that uncharged (or neutral) amino acids M (sulfur-containing) and F (aromatic) promote COM nucleation by increasing the number of crystal nuclei [50], [51]. Therefore, the higher contents of W and C in proteins uniquely found in SFU&SM might relate to the promoting effect on COM crystallization.

The arrangement of calcium and oxalate ions in the crystal lattice is different on each COM crystal face [52]. The growing COM crystal surface has a total positive charge because calcium ions are physically exposed on the crystal surface, while oxalate ions are located deeper inside the crystal surface [20]. Proteins rich in negatively charged amino acids (D and E) can specifically bind to the calcium-rich surface of COM crystals due to electrostatic interaction [20], thus hindering the addition of free ions to the growing crystals. Face-specific binding on COM crystals of some proteins, e.g., OPN [53], PTF1 [54], human serum albumin (HSA) [54] and transferrin [55], has previously been reported to inhibit crystal growth on that particular crystal face. These proteins contained high percentages of negatively charged amino acids, consistent with the greater proportion of negatively charged amino acids in proteins uniquely found in NU&SM (Fig. 6C). However, the inhibitors having face-specific binding ability to COM crystals are possibly incorporated into the SM because they adsorb only on the specific crystal face, while the other faces can further grow and develop into a stone [27]. On the other hand, positively charged proteins can weakly bind to the COM surface [56] or promote crystal growth (e.g., lactoferrin and lysozyme) [55].

Urinary proteins can be trapped inside the inorganic matrix during the stone-forming process and become part of the SM [57]. Some proteins found in SM possibly promote crystal aggregation [56]. Our findings showed that SM contained the greatest proportions of both positively and negatively charged amino acids (Figs. 2E and 2F). It has also been suggested that protein-protein interaction plays an important role in this stage of stone formation [56]. Higher relative supersaturation in SFU might increase protein aggregation because of a highly supersaturated environment that facilitates protein aggregation [58].

Concerning polarity, polarizability, normalized van der Waals volume, and solvent accessibility, we found that potential characteristics of inhibitors included high contents of amino acids with medium normalized van der Waals volume and exposed structure (due to their higher proportions observed in proteins uniquely identified in NU&SM) (Figs. 7C and 7D). Amino acids with exposed structure tended to expose their side chains to the environment. Moreover, amino acids with medium normalized van der Waals volume contained linear and polar side chains. Due to these properties, they might facilitate protein binding with ions and/or crystals, leading to inhibitory effects on stone formation. In the case of SM proteins, they contained greater proportions of amino acids with high polarity, high polarizability, high normalized van der Waals volume, and exposed structure than NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 3B-3E). Some of these properties might be potential characteristics of the stone promoters.

It has been reported that protein-crystal binding and protein-protein interactions are involved in stone enlargement [56]. The greatest proportions of charged amino acids (Figs. 2E and 2F) and those with high polarizability (Fig. 3C) in SM proteins suggested the presence of potential crystal-binding domains within these proteins. Also, the greatest proportions of amino acids with high polarity (Fig. 3B) and exposed solvent accessibility (Fig. 3E) in SM proteins might enhance the interactions between these domains and the crystal surfaces. These properties might facilitate protein-crystal binding, thereby initiating crystal aggregation. Due to the large number (877) of SM proteins identified, it was plausible that some proteins might be passively incorporated into the SM without active stone-modulatory roles. In concordance, SM proteins contained the greatest proportion of amino acids with high normalized van der Waals volume (Fig. 3D), a characteristic that might facilitate the entrapment of non-specific proteins into the SM by physical entanglement.

Considering other physico-chemical properties, we found that SM proteins had a higher average theoretical pI (Fig. 4A) and a greater proportion of basic proteins (Fig. 4C) than NU and SFU proteins. Previous evidence has shown that matrix-preferring proteins generally have extreme pI values (<5 or >9) [56]. However, weakly acidic proteins have a strong inhibitory effect on crystal aggregation and/or crystal-cell adhesion [59]. This agrees with a greater proportion of acidic proteins in those uniquely found in NU&SM compared with those uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 8B). Highly hydrophilic polypeptides have previously been reported to preferentially interact with COM crystals rather than forming a complex with calcium ions [59]. This characteristic might exhibit an inhibitory effect on crystal aggregation or crystal growth. Our results showed that proteins uniquely found in NU&SM contained a greater proportion of hydrophilic proteins than those uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 8L). In addition, proteins uniquely found in NU&SM had a greater proportion of high-MW proteins than those uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 8F). By contrast, low-MW proteins were more abundant in the latter group (Fig. 8E). It has been reported that low-MW proteins are generated from reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by oxidative stress during stone formation [60], [61]. Moreover, oxidized side chains in amino acid residues may be the cause of protein destabilization and aggregation [58]. Our results also showed a greater proportion of unstable proteins in those uniquely found in SFU&SM (Fig. 8I). Taken together, acidic, high-MW, stable and hydrophilic proteins might be potential stone inhibitors, whereas basic, low-MW, unstable and hydrophobic proteins might be potential stone promoters.

Calcium-binding proteins also play an important role in the inhibition of CaOx stone formation [27]. It was initially expected that proteins in NU should contain a higher proportion of calcium-binding proteins and a greater number of calcium-binding sites/protein than those of SFU and SM proteins. Our data showed that NU proteins had a greater proportion of calcium-binding proteins compared with SM, but there was no obvious difference observed between NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the NU proteins tended to have the greatest number of calcium-binding sites/protein than the other two groups. However, such a modest difference was marginal and did not reach a statistically significant threshold (Fig. 5B). In the case of oxalate-binding proteins, they mainly contain positively charged amino acids in the binding sites [62]. Our group has previously reported that the number of oxalate-binding sites/protein inversely correlates with crystallization inhibitory activity [63]. This corresponded to the present results showing that proteins uniquely found in NU&SM tended to contain a slightly lower number of oxalate-binding sites/protein than those uniquely found in SFU&SM although such a difference was marginal and did not reach a statistically significant threshold (Fig. 9D).

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge that all proteins analyzed in this study were from the secondary dataset that was retrieved from 9 previous independent proteomics studies [18], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]. Note that none of them offered the complete list of proteins identified from all three samples (NU, SFU and SM). And there were several variable factors in this secondary dataset, including differences in sample sizes of individual sample groups within and across studies, selected population, sample preparation techniques, extraction methods, and analytical platforms. All urinary proteome samples were subjected to in-gel tryptic digestion, whereas SM proteome studies employed both in-gel and in-solution tryptic digestion methods. Additionally, technical differences, such as nanoLC gradient, MS/MS modality, acquisition parameters for MS/MS analyses, search engines, and search parameters, also differed. All these variables could affect the sensitivity of protein detection and the type of proteins being identified. These inconsistencies across studies and sample groups could introduce biases in comparative analyses.

Another important issue of this study was that all the data were computational/theoretical, based on the information provided in databases. And we assigned proteins as potential inhibitors or promoters based on the type of samples they were present. There was no experimental validation to address whether proteins were indeed inhibitors, promoters, or just present without any stone-modulatory impact. Therefore, the interpretation of the results was entirely assumption-based and needed further experimental validation.

In addition, we performed analyses of proteins according to their amino acid sequences (which rely only on the protein primary structure) and paid attention to the interaction between proteins and calcium ions, oxalate ions, or COM crystals. Therefore, only the effects of chemical interaction between functional groups were considered. Some other parameters, e.g., secondary and tertiary structures and post-translational modifications, were not included in the analyses in this study. A previous study has shown a relationship between the phosphorylation level of OPN and its inhibitory effect on COM crystallization [64]. Moreover, the binding ability of proteins on the COM crystal surfaces can depend on protein secondary and tertiary structures [20]. Therefore, further elucidations of the effects of these protein characteristics or parameters on stone modulation are required.

Summary

In the present study, we investigated potential characteristics of inhibitors and promoters involved in the stone-forming process by comparing amino acid contents, various physico-chemical properties, and calcium- and oxalate-binding properties of all proteins identified from NU, SFU and SM. Simple comparisons among the three groups showed that SM proteins apparently differed from NU and SFU proteins for almost all parameters, whereas no significant differences were detected between NU and SFU proteins (Fig. 10A and 10B). We then focused our attention on the proteins that were uniquely found in NU&SM compared with those uniquely found in SFU&SM, as they might serve as stone inhibitors and promoters, respectively. The analyses revealed that proteins rich in amino acids with negative charges, medium normalized van der Waals volume and exposed solvent accessibility, acidic proteins, high-MW proteins, stable proteins, and hydrophilic proteins tended to be the stone inhibitors (Fig. 10C). On the other hand, proteins rich in aromatic and polar amino acids, basic proteins, low-MW proteins, unstable proteins, and hydrophobic proteins tended to be the stone promoters (Fig. 10D).
