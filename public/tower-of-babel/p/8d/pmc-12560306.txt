A data quality study investigating the fieldworker’s impact on the reporting discrepancies of pre-marital sexual relationships among unmarried men and women in India
Authors: Saurabh Singh, Laxmi Kant Dwivedi, Neha Shri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGenerating unbiased and accurate assessments of sexual behavior among unmarried individuals is a major challenge, as it is governed by many social, moral, legal conventions and restrictions. These estimates are particularly susceptible to Fieldworker-induced social desirability bias. Accurate estimation of pre-marital sexual behavior is crucial for evaluating many aspects of sexual and reproductive health. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS) is the only reliable source of information on pre-marital sexual behavior in India, especially in the absence of any other comparative large-scale survey. This study provides us with an opportunity to understand the Fieldworker-level bias in the reporting of pre-marital sex among unmarried men and women.Data and methodsThis study uses nationally representative data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5). The analysis included 2950 female and 2073 male fieldworkers, as well as all unmarried women and men of reproductive age. Bayesian cross-classified multilevel modelling was performed to examine the Fieldworker’s effect on the reporting of premarital sex among unmarried respondents. Additionally, Multivariate decomposition analysis was used to quantify the effect of various fieldworker characteristics in reporting pre-marital sex.ResultsThe fieldworker effect accounted for around 31.7% of total variation in the reporting of intimate partner violence for females and 24.6% for IPV reporting among males. Results from the decomposition analysis indicated that age and residence of fieldworker were associated with a 2.4% increase and 1.3% decrease in the likelihood of reporting pre-marital sexual activity, respectively. After adjusting fieldworker and individual-level characteristics, an association between an age difference of greater than ten years and higher reporting of ever having had sexual intercourse was observed for both sexes.ConclusionAlthough we believe that NFHS surveys are a potentially valuable source of information on pre-marital sexual behavior, especially in the absence of proven alternatives, the evidence from this study suggests that fieldworker effects may bias reporting of ever-had sexual intercourse. Therefore, estimates from these surveys should be evaluated carefully and interpreted in the context of other available information, given their wide use in policy formulation and monitoring in India.

Introduction

The generation of unbiased and accurate assessments of behavioural patterns in individuals and populations is a major difficulty for all sex survey research. The quality and type of data generated during the research can be influenced by particular traits of both respondents and fieldworkers, as shown by a vast body of literature [1, 2]. Both social desirability and recall bias can affect survey data in general and sexual behaviour data in particular [3]. Sexual behaviour is essentially a private matter that is governed by many social, cultural, religious, moral, and legal conventions and restrictions. In a review of the methodological problems, Catania described the situation of misreporting as “Most of the sex research is based on self-reported sexual behaviour of unknown validity” [4]. Underreporting of such behaviours may cause the relative risk associated with that behaviour to be skewed (usually, attenuated) [5]. Evidence suggests that questions intended to extract information on sensitive subjects, such as intimate partner violence, the number of sexual partners, abortion, and contraceptive use are more likely to produce non-responses or bigger measurement errors than less sensitive questions [6, 7]. Employing methods to reduce the measurement error introduced by participation bias, recollection and comprehension issues, and respondents’ willingness to express delicate and occasionally socially taboo views or behaviours is crucial [8, 9].

The quality of data collected in a survey design can have an impact on the accuracy of the results. For example, methods that make the respondent appear more anonymous are more likely to elicit a response to sensitive subjects [10, 11]. Age, education, colour, sex, and other characteristics of the fieldworker have all been linked to non-responses to questions about sexual behaviour [12]. Additionally, prior research has demonstrated that the environment in which the interview is conducted might affect how sensitive behaviours are reported [2, 13]. In both national and subnational surveys, a growing amount of research shows varied degrees of fieldworker effects [14, 15]. Given that having sex before a legal union is considered inappropriate for girls in many, if not most, countries in the region, it is reasonable to assume that a sizable fraction of girls would have a lot of difficulty confessing to having sex outside of a socially acceptable relationship [16]. Furthermore, it is likely to enhance the discomfort that people—even young adults—feel when asked by other adults about sexual topics when older fieldworkers try to gather first-hand information from teenagers about their sex life.

Although DHS(Demographic and Health Surveys) data has long been considered the gold standard, particularly in low and middle-income countries, DHS staff have acknowledged that women may be underreporting premarital sex [17] and that the reporting is particularly susceptible to fieldworker-induced social desirability bias [18]. According to a recent study, when models considered the cross-classified structure of interviews among communities and interviewers, sensitive and complex questions were linked to higher interviewer-induced variance [19]. Despite being interviewed about the identical pairings, previous studies have found disparities in the number of opposite-sex sexual partners reported by men and women [20], with males typically reporting more than women. It is difficult to assess the statistics because sexual behaviour is underreported, as has been seen (particularly in adolescent girls; there is also limited evidence that boys may exaggerate their experiences) [21–23]. Measurement of sexual behaviour presents significant challenges because the consequences of such behaviour might be severe (e.g., expulsion from school or home). This under-reporting is especially important because it compromises programme assessment due to poor data quality and incorrect perceptions of HIV risk and STDs, which are used to measure the success of interventions in reducing sexual risk-taking.

Accurate sexual behavior reporting is useful as a standalone public health indicator as well as a launching pad for additional inquiries and indicators about the use of contraception and sexual health. Bias in respondents’ sexual activity reporting may spread to other factors that are crucial for evaluating many aspects of sexual and reproductive health. Moreover, past studies have highlighted the need for attention to quality in data collection especially in the context of measuring sexual behaviour [24]. In a country like India, where premarital sexual activity is highly stigmatized, the absence of any other comparative large-scale data source (other than NFHS) on premarital sexual activity makes the quality of survey data questionable. A limited number of studies based on DHS data have reported the presence of fieldworker level effect in reporting of issues such as Intimate partner Violence, and maternal and child health indicators in India [25–27]. While our understanding of fieldworker influences on survey data in case of sexual behavior is limited in India and in abroad (for exceptions, see [28, 29], methodological inquiries on gathering sensitive data are important. Despite recommendations for research, interviewer impacts on the reporting of sexual behavior from the DHS are not yet documented in the literature [30].

In light of this, the current study hypothesized that there is higher variability in quality of data collected on sensitive behavior like pre-marital sex across different fieldworker. Furthermore, the reporting of pre-marital sex among unmarried males and females is significantly influenced by the fieldworkers’ diverse backgrounds. Building upon these hypotheses, this study seeks to accomplish three primary objectives: Firstly, to provide a comprehensive profile analysis of both male and female fieldworkers who were involved in the implementation of NFHS-5 using fieldworker data. This study is the first of its kind to provide the background information of both male and female fieldworkers in a large-scale survey. Secondly, study utilized a cross-classified multi-level model to determine the influence of fieldworker on the reporting pre-marital sex after adjusting for the PSUs, fieldworker and individual-level covariates. Lastly, study aims to assess the effects of fieldworker’s characteristics in reporting pre-marital sex as well as decomposes the men-women differences in reporting of pre-marital sex.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the body of literature by investigating the relationship between fieldworker’s characteristics and respondents’ reporting of pre-marital sex in the NFHS. It underscores the critical roles that survey implementation methods and fieldworkers plays in shaping survey’s overall data quality.

Data and methods

This study uses nationally representative data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21). It is a cross-sectional, large-scale sample survey that was carried out by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), the survey’s nodal agency across each state and union territory of India under the direction of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), Government of India. The reliable unit-level data from 636,699 households including 724,115 women and 101,839 men were sampled using a face-to-face survey.Details of the sample size and sampling procedure are available in NFHS-5 national reports [31]. NFHS data is collected through computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) using handheld electronic tablets, following the standardized data collection protocols of the DHS program. Field investigators underwent rigorous training in interview techniques, questionnaire administration, and electronic data entry procedures to ensure consistency and minimize errors. Real-time data entry using tablets allowed for immediate validation checks, reducing data entry errors and enhancing data quality. The Fieldworker Characteristics file (FW file), Individual file (IR file) and Male file (MR File) were used for analytical purposes. The fieldworker data was merged with female (age 15–49 years) and male individual files (age 15–54 years) separately after dropping 2,010 duplicate fieldworkers (2,010 out of 7,127 fieldworkers). A total of 2,950 female fieldworkers and 2073 male fieldworkers for female file and male file respectively were used in the analysis. Fieldworkers provide information about themselves at the beginning of the fieldwork via a self-administered questionnaire. After completing the training, a numeric identification code (ID) was assigned to each fieldworker that was put in the household and individual data file. This numeric ID code was used to link the fieldworker’s data with the respondent’s data. The study excludes those respondents who reported being married and those who didn’t know their age at first sex or first union.

Dependent variables

The outcome variable of interest is whether a never-married and never-in-union respondent (male/female) has reported ever having sexual intercourse. It was measured by using a standard NFHS question: “How old were you when you had sexual intercourse for the very first time?”, further the response to this question was dichotomized as “never had intercourse” and “ever had intercourse”.

Independent variables

The independent variable for the study was categorized into two levels: the fieldworker’s level and the respondent’s level. Fieldworker’s characteristics such as place of residence (rural and urban), age (categorized into 15–20 years, 21–25 years, 26–30 years and above 30 years), education level (primary/secondary or higher), religion (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh and others) and caste (Scheduled Caste [SC], Scheduled Tribe [ST], Other Backward Classes [OBC], and others). Additionally, the study considered survey experience variables, including whether the fieldworker had previously worked in the NFHS or any other survey (both recorded as yes/no), and employment status at the time of being hired for NFHS (employed with the agency and not employed). Linguistic and regional factors were also included, such as whether the fieldworker’s mother tongue was the same as the respondent’s and whether the fieldworker was from the same state as the respondent (both dichotomized as yes/no). Furthermore, the number of interviews conducted by each fieldworker was clubbed into three quantiles: low (mean of 16.6 interviews), medium (mean of 28.4 interviews), and high (mean of 41.3 interviews).

Respondent characteristics like education level (No education, primary, secondary, and higher), mass media exposure (assessed based on whether the respondent read newspapers, listened to the radio, or watched television—each recorded as yes/no), alcohol consumption(yes/no) and smoking history (ever smoked-yes/no). The place of residence was categorized as rural or urban. Respondent’s time of interview was classified into three phases i.e., Phase 1, pre-covid Phase-2 and post-Covid Phase-2. This was categorised based on phase of data collection. NFHS-5 round data was collected in two phases expanding from 2019 to 2021 including the period of Covid Epidemic. (Note: Please refer to the NFHS − 5 report for clarification on data collection phases (National Family Health Survey (NFHS − 5), 2019–21:India: Volume I: IIPS, 2021)) [31]. Additionally, respondent’s states and union territories were grouped into six geographical regions namely North, Central, East, North-East, West and South region.

Statistical analysis

The study employed univariate analysis to delineate the characteristics of both respondents and the fieldworkers. Bivariate analysis was utilized to understand the reporting of pre-marital sex by various socio-economic backgrounds of respondents and fieldworkers. Further, the chi-square test was used to check the significance of association identified in the bivariate analysis for each group. Logistic Regression was performed with pre-marital sex and various characteristics of both fieldworkers and respondents. The fieldworker variation on reporting of pre-marital sex was investigated using the Bayesian cross-classified random intercept multivariable four-level logit model. The fieldworkers had worked across many PSUs and districts, and each PSU and district had employed more than one fieldworker, therefore fieldworker and PSU-district are cross-classified. Since, unlike PSUs-Districts, the fieldworkers with PSUs-Districts do not adhere to a rigid hierarchical structure. As a result, a cross-classified multilevel model was employed to disentangle the non-hierarchical effects [32].

Table 3 presents the results of a bivariate analysis examining the reporting of premarital sex across different socio-economic groups, comparing both women and men. Prevalence of pre-marital sexual intercourse were significantly lower when FW were from rural areas (5.1% overall, 13.6% male, 2.8% female) compared to those from urban area (5.3% overall, 17.4% male, 3.8% female). Overall, the reporting of premarital sex showed an upward trend as fieldworkers aged, rising from 3.7% among fieldworkers aged 15–20 to 6.7% among those aged 30 and older. For men, the prevalence was 17.5% among primary/secondary educated FW, compared to 14.3% among those interviewed by FW with higher education, though only the difference among women was statistically significant. Fieldworkers who ever had children reported the highest overall prevalence of premarital sex (8.2% for those with one or more children), compared to unmarried fieldworkers (4.8%). When interviewed by FW from Scheduled Tribes (ST), a total prevalence of 5.9% was observed, with male (16.4%) and female (4.0%),showing notable differences. Fieldworkers who had prior experience with NFHS or any other surveys had higher overall reporting (5.8% vs. 4.9% for not having NFHS experience and 6.2% vs. 4.7% for not having any survey experience), though this trend was not statistically significant for male respondents. In contrast, female respondents reported slightly lower prevalence when fieldworkers had prior NFHS experience (2.7% vs. 3.3% for not having NFHS experience). Fieldworkers employed by an agency or those from a different state than the respondent reported higher prevalence of premarital sex (5.9% and 6.1%, respectively) compared to their counterparts (4.9% and 5.0%). Additionally, the number of interviews conducted by fieldworkers showed a clear pattern where fieldworkers with higher numbers of interviews had overall prevalence of 5.9%, compared to 4.4% for those with low no. of interviews. Among female respondents, the prevalence increased from 2.7% for low-interview fieldworkers to 4.1% for high-interview fieldworkers.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study conducted in India that leverages the reporting of sexual activity using fieldworker and respondents’ characteristics and contributes to the understanding of reporting bias in pre-marital sex within the Indian context. The findings of this analysis suggests that this bias is likely to be mitigated by external factors such as fieldworker characteristics, particularly the fieldworker’s age, education, prior experience, number of interviews conducted by the fieldworker, and media exposure of the respondent. When reporting sexual behavior prior to a union, response bias is well-established in numerous settings [12, 21]. Women are less likely to report ever having had sex when the fieldworker is older, the fieldworker has higher educational attainment,have same language and same religion. The other fieldworker characteristics included in this analysis such as previous survey experience in NFHS and other surveys, appears to have little association with reporting ever having sexual intercourse among men and women. In contrast, the reporting of ever having sexual intercourse was significantly lower among men with different language, same state as of fieldworker, with secondary or no formal education. According to Mensch and colleagues’ boys are more inclined to exaggerate their sexual activity [21] while girls typically tend to underreport sexual activity in face-to-face interviews [21, 24]. Researchers emphasize that while cooperation is often more likely when fieldworkers and respondents are similar, cooperation does not necessarily imply condor [21, 33, 34]. A study based on trends in the age of first sex in DHS surveys indicated that there are disparities in reporting within birth cohorts across surveys that are consistent with young women reporting lower and young men disclosing higher levels of their sexual activity [35]. Our findings align with the finding from Rozelle and colleagues who found that women and men were less likely to report ever having sexual activity if the fieldworker was ten or more years older than them, adjusting for potential confounders when analysed for reporting of sexual activity using DHS data from 21 countries [29]. As seen in higher-income countries, older interviewers elicit more conservative responses to queries about sexual behaviour [36]. Significant variations observed by educational attainment of respondents and interviewer are in contrast with findings by Yang and Yu [37] who reported that less educated respondents provided more detailed responses than more educated respondents just to conform to the expectations and perceived educational background of the fieldworker.

The ICC for reporting of ever had sexual intercourse at the fieldworker level was around 31.7% and 24.6% (women and men respectively) which highlights the substantial fieldworker effect in the outcome of interest. This is consistent with studies that have reported fieldworker effect in in reporting of issues such as Intimate partner Violence (ICC 44%) using Indian DHS [18]. Additionally, the levels of reporting varied substantially among men and women with higher reporting among men than women. However, the differences in reporting behaviour were observed among men and women despite similar fieldworker characteristics. Previous research has also identified differences between reporting behaviour of men and women. Upchurch et al. found that both men and women can be inconsistent in their reporting of ever having sexual activity, although men were more likely to be inconsistent [38]. These discrepancies emphasize how crucial it is to comprehend fieldworker traits in connection to the validity and reliability of self-reported outcomes. Audio Computer-Assisted Self-Interviewing (ACASI) a technique used in surveys in developed countries to collect data on sensitive behaviors [11, 39] has been proved to yield better estimates while higher reports of sexual activity, particularly for females was observed in developing countries like Thailand [40] and Kenya [21]. However, its suitability to developing nations, including India is widely argued where “overcrowded living conditions typically prevail, where literacy is relatively low, and where some of the behaviours in question may be particularly pronounced” [41]. Nevertheless, in a semi-rural area of South India, the usage of ACASI and C-ACASI (Color-coded ACASI) has shown potential for gathering private information from uneducated men and women [42].

In line with this study findings, researchers emphasize that other demographic measurements that include sexual activity as a numerator or denominator component will be affected by the biases, as observed in fieldworker effects [29]. The use of family planning and behaviours related to HIV/AIDS prevention among young or never-married populations are two examples of common indicators in this category. Overall, Although we believe that NFHS surveys are potentially a valuable source of information on sexual behaviour, particularly given the absence of proven alternatives, the evidence from this study provides sufficient grounds for concern regarding the presence of fieldworker effect in reporting of ever-had sexual intercourse and thus the estimates must be evaluated carefully and interpreted in the context of other available information given the wide use of these surveys in policy formulation and monitoring in India.

Moreover, it is important to check the fieldworker age and other background characteristics as this study reported a significant number of fieldworker aged below 20. This raises concern about their experience in administering sensitive questions. Although, in large scale surveys, fieldworkers are recruited as per the eligibility criteria and proper training, younger interviewer might not poses same level of experience that could influence rapport building and disclosure.

The findings underscore the importance of recognizing how fieldworker characteristics may influence the reporting of sensitive behaviors, such as pre-marital sex. Although geographic region was controlled in the analysis, this study recognizes that regional categories may oversimplify India’s rich socio-cultural diversity, particularly regarding attitudes toward sexuality. However, these results must be interpreted in the broader context of data collection practices. Data quality is shaped not only by fieldworker demographics but also by rigorous training, emotional preparedness, and ethical integrity of survey staff. Ensuring consistent interviewer training protocols that stress non-judgmental interviewing, confidentiality, and respondent rapport-building can mitigate biases introduced by interviewer effects. While this study focuses on observable characteristics, future research could incorporate qualitative assessments to better understand the mechanisms through which interviewer effects emerge.

To improve the quality and accuracy of data collected through NFHS and similar surveys, several steps can be taken. First, fieldworker training should be strengthened. Training sessions should include not only how to ask sensitive questions but also focus on building trust, maintaining neutrality, and protecting respondent privacy. A well-trained fieldworker who behaves in a respectful and non-judgmental manner can help respondents feel more comfortable and reduce underreporting or misreporting. Second, wherever possible, survey teams should try to match the gender of the fieldworker with that of the respondent when asking questions related to personal or sensitive issues. Third, it is essential to make sure that interviews are conducted in a private and safe environment. Regular supervision and monitoring of fieldworker performance can help ensure that interviews are being conducted properly. Providing feedback to fieldworkers during data collection can further improve the quality of the process. Also, the results should be interpreted primarily within the methodological context of DHS-type, face-to-face household surveys in India, and further replication is necessary across different survey environments.

While interviewer characteristics may inadvertently influence responses, particularly on stigmatized topics like pre-marital sex, innovative approaches in data collection can help mitigate these biases. Integration of technology-assisted data collection tools such as Audio Computer-Assisted Self-Interviewing (ACASI) or self-administered mobile or tablet-based surveys can offer greater privacy, reduce social desirability bias, and minimize the influence of the interviewer, especially when respondents are asked about sensitive behaviors. Further, ensuring that fieldworkers are adequately trained not only in technical procedures but also in ethics, confidentiality assurance, and empathy can improve the overall trust and comfort of respondents. Future surveys may consider setting a higher minimum age threshold or providing specialized training modules for younger fieldworkers to ensure consistent handling of sensitive topics.

Conclusion and Recommendations

In conclusion, this study addresses the complexities of reporting bias in sexual activity in India. It highlights the need for context-specific approaches to data collection. However, there are limitations to the study that should be acknowledged. Given the sensitive nature of the topic and absence of a definitive benchmark for assessing the accuracy of prevalence, it is not possible to verify the ‘truth’ of responses. Although some significant background effects on reporting discrepancies were examined in this study, unavailability of information on potential mediators, such as fieldworker attitudes, beliefs, or behaviours could limit the observed reporting bias. Additionally, this approach does not establish what is real. Although surveys are consistent enough that the possibility of age discrepancy-induced response bias should be regarded seriously, replication across contexts would be beneficial for this research. We acknowledge that our findings may only partially represent the broader spectrum of fieldworker influence, and encourage future surveys to incorporate structured assessments or qualitative tools to better capture these dimensions Last but not least, we want to emphasise that this study is exploratory.

The study could benefit from a more comprehensive examination of the cultural and regional variations in India, as these factors can significantly impact reporting behavior. Future research could employ longitudinal studies to track changes in reporting behaviour over time. Comparative studies across different countries or regions with similar challenges related to sexual behavior reporting could help identify effective strategies and techniques for minimizing bias. Exploring the feasibility of more advanced data collection methods, such as ACASI and C-ACASI, in different settings and demographics within India could be beneficial. A thorough training concerning ethical guidelines, community engagement and data security can help fieldworkers build trust and collect data more accurately. We recommend that future rounds include such mechanisms, along with qualitative audit trails or field observations, to enhance the credibility of sensitive disclosures. Additionally, technology integration to combat reporting bias in surveys on sensitive topics like sexual behaviour and data triangulation by comparing self-reported data with external sources may also facilitate better data quality.
