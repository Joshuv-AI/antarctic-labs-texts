Biosynthesis of benzylisoquinoline alkaloids and its evolution in plants
Authors: Ya Tian, Zhoujie An, Qi Li, Ge Bai, Qiyue Zhang, Yuzhuo Dong, Yong Chen, Shilin Chen, Zhichao Xu, Ranran Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIAs) represent a large class of plant specialized metabolites that enhance plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses while also exhibiting diverse pharmacological activities, such as the analgesic effects of morphine and the antibacterial properties of berberine. We systematically review the biosynthetic pathways of diverse BIA scaffolds and the key enzymes involved. These enzymes, including methyltransferases, cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, berberine bridge enzymes, demethylases, and acyltransferases, collectively mediate structural diversification of BIAs through reactions such as methylation, oxidation, ring formation, demethylation, and acetylation. Additionally, we discuss how lineage-specific gene duplication events, neofunctionalization, and/or the assembly of biosynthetic gene clusters have driven structural diversification and adaptive evolution across taxa. Despite significant progress, challenges remain in fully characterizing pathway gaps, enzyme promiscuity, and regulatory networks. We recommend that integrating multi-omics data with protein engineering and synthetic biology offers promising avenues for sustainable production of high-value BIAs and the discovery of novel bioactive compounds.Key words: benzylisoquinoline alkaloids, structural diversification, biosynthesis, gene duplication, neofunctionalization

Introduction

Benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIAs) constitute one of the most medicinally significant classes of plant natural products, encompassing over 2500 known structures. BIAs can be structurally classified into nine major categories, including simple 1-benzylisoquinolines (1-BIAs), aporphines, protoberberines (BBRs), benzo[c]phenanthridines (BZPs), phthalideisoquinolines (PQs), morphinans, protopines, bisbenzylisoquinolines (bisBIAs), and pavines (Beaudoin and Facchini, 2014). BIAs are predominantly distributed in early-diverging eudicots (Ranunculales and Proteales) (Liu et al., 2021b; Yang et al., 2025), magnoliids (including Magnoliales, Laurales, and Piperales) (Tan et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2022), and the Zanthoxyloideae (such as Phellodendron and Zanthoxylum) within the order Sapindales (Azonsivo et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2023). Notably, certain species, such as Papaver somniferum (Papaveraceae, morphinans), Coptis chinensis (Ranunculaceae, BBRs), Litsea cubeba (Lauraceae, aporphines), and Phellodendron amurense (Rutaceae, BBRs) produce distinct profiles of these BIA subtypes (Tian et al., 2024). The occurrence of specific structural types often correlates with phylogenetic position, reflecting both conserved biosynthetic pathways and lineage-specific evolutionary processes.

BIAs exhibit remarkable biological and pharmacological activities, making them a continuous focus of natural product research and drug discovery (Dang et al., 2012). Biologically, BIAs play crucial adaptive roles in plants, enhancing resistance against microbial pathogens and herbivorous insects, improving thermotolerance, and promoting pigment accumulation. Pharmacologically, BIAs exhibit a broad spectrum of therapeutic properties, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antitumor, cardioprotective, and analgesic activities (Dang et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020; Jiao and Yang, 2025). Notable members include the potent analgesic morphine, the antimicrobial berberine, the antitumor agent sanguinarine, and the anti-inflammatory compound tetrandrine.

The biosynthetic pathway of BIAs begins with the condensation of dopamine and 4-hydroxyphenylacetaldehyde (4-HPAA) to form (S)-norcoclaurine, the universal precursor of all BIAs, catalyzed by (S)-norcoclaurine synthase (NCS) (Minami et al., 2007). The intermediate (S)-norcoclaurine then undergoes a series of methylation and hydroxylation, catalyzed by enzymes such as O- and N-methyltransferases (O/NMTs) and cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450s), leading to the formation of the key intermediate (S)-reticuline (Vadhel et al., 2023). Subsequently, C–C and C–O coupling reactions mediated by CYP450 enzymes and berberine bridge enzyme (BBE) lead to the biosynthesis of various structural scaffolds. The structural diversity of BIAs arises from enzymatic modifications of different BIA scaffold precursors through methylation, hydroxylation, methylenedioxy bridge formation, demethylation, and acetylation, catalyzed by MTs, CYP450s, BBE and BBE-like, demethylases, and acyltransferases (ATs). Recent advances in genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics have significantly accelerated the elucidation of BIA biosynthetic pathways and evolution. Nevertheless, numerous enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of many BIAs remain to be explored, and the enzymatic mechanisms controlling BIA accumulation are poorly understood.

This review discusses the pharmacological significance and biological roles of naturally occurring BIAs, their biosynthetic pathways, the enzyme-catalyzed diversification of their chemical structures, and the evolutionary mechanisms driving the diversity of BIA production in plants. We highlight the enzymatic basis of structural diversification, discuss recent omics-driven discoveries, and explore the evolutionary relationships that have shaped the distribution and diversity of BIAs across plant lineages.

The pharmacological value and biological functions of natural BIAs

Plants rich in BIAs yield extracts that hold significant importance in traditional medical applications. In contemporary medical research, BIAs show a wide range of pharmacological properties, primarily including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antitumor, and analgesic activities. BIAs also play multifaceted roles in plants, including enhancing antimicrobial defense, improving resistance to diseases and insects, and increasing tolerance to abiotic stresses (Figure 1).

Pharmacological properties

The anti-inflammatory properties of BIAs are associated with a range of conditions, such as rheumatism and other autoimmune diseases. For instance, tetrandrine, a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid and the active component of tetrandrine hydrochloride injection, is a potent anti-inflammatory agent. Its mechanisms involve suppressing macrophage activation induced by β-glucan and reducing inflammatory mediator release, primarily via inhibition of the nuclear factor (NF)-κB, ERK, and STAT3 pathways (Xue et al., 2008; He et al., 2011; Kang et al., 2014; Bao et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2016). Other BIAs, including noscapine (Nourbakhsh et al., 2022), pseudocoptisine (Yun et al., 2009), berberine (Cui et al., 2024), neferine (Ozal et al., 2020), and cepharanthine (Liu et al., 2023), are also capable of targeting NF-κB. Apart from that, BIAs, such as sinomenine, have potential as a specific GBP5 antagonist and aryl hydrocarbon receptor modulator (Jiang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). Furthermore, noscapine has demonstrated significant efficacy in mitigating skin inflammation associated with psoriasis (Nourbakhsh et al., 2022).

The antibacterial and antiviral activities of BIAs might be related to their unique molecular structures, particularly the quaternary nitrogen atom and the methylenedioxy bridge located at the C-2 and C-3 positions. Notably, berberine (Wojtyczka et al., 2014) and sanguinarine (Hamoud et al., 2015) exhibit potent inhibitory effects against diverse pathogens, including Helicobacter pylori (Chen et al., 2023), Staphylococcus spp. (Zhou et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025), Providencia rettgeri (Zhang et al., 2020), and Salmonella enterica (Zhang et al., 2018b). Berberine targets respiratory syncytial virus to treat pediatric bronchiolitis (Jiao and Yang, 2025) and exhibits anti-human immunodeficiency virus activity (Shao et al., 2020). Recent studies reported that cepharanthine (Zhang et al., 2022) and its bisBIAs analogs possess potential broad-spectrum anti-coronavirus activity (Yang et al., 2021b, 2025; Dong et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Leng et al., 2024).

The analgesic properties of BIAs have been extensively documented, including morphine, codeine, and rotundine. While morphine and codeine belong to potent opioid analgesics (Beaudoin and Facchini, 2014), they are limited by high addiction risk. Rotundine (levo-tetrahydropalmatine) acts through a distinct mechanism; it can inhibit the overexpression of Clec7a, which plays a vital role in mediating neuroinflammation by regulating the MAPK and NF-κB (Wu et al., 2023). Additionally, rotundine shows anti-addictive effects by blocking morphine-induced dopamine D2 receptor downregulation and α4β2-nAChR function (Jiang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022).

The antiarrhythmic effect represents another significant therapeutic property of BIAs, which primarily exert their antiarrhythmic effects by modulating the Na+, K+, and Ca2+ channels of myocardial cells, such as berberine and tetrandrine (Chen et al., 2001; Li et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2012). Furthermore, BIAs, including papaverine and berberine, contribute to cardiovascular protection via mechanisms such as vasodilation, inhibition of platelet aggregation, attenuation of myocardial ischemia, and antioxidant activity (Sanchez et al., 2018; Paul et al., 2019; Levi et al., 2025). Additionally, BIAs display a diverse array of bioactivities, including antioxidant (Shirwaikar et al., 2006; Kostic et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025), antimalarial (Wangchuk et al., 2012; Uche et al., 2021; Muema et al., 2023), and antifungal activities (Gupta et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024).

Until now, several BIAs have achieved clinical relevance, exemplified by morphine (analgesic agents) (Dowell et al., 2016) and codeine (analgesic agents) (Manglik et al., 2012), and berberine (an antibacterial agent) (Kong et al., 2004). The clinical application of these drugs relies on the extraction and isolation from medicinal plants, which is time consuming and consumes a great deal of plant resources. The elucidation of the biosynthetic pathway and heterologous synthesis of bioactive BIAs have become the current and future development trends.

Biological functions

Owing to the antimicrobial activity, BIAs are frequently developed as bactericidal agents in agriculture. For instance, berberine is extensively recognized as an excellent microbicide, with studies demonstrating its potent inhibition of Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (the causative agent of rice bacterial leaf streaks) (Peng et al., 2025). Furthermore, the combined application of berberine and phage LPRS20 against Ralstonia solanacearum strains has been shown to alleviate tobacco bacterial wilt significantly (Liu et al., 2024).

BIAs also contribute to defense against insect herbivores. Extracts derived from Ocotea spixiana and Zanthoxylum schreberi, which are abundant in berberine, berberrubine, and chelerythrine, have exhibited substantial insecticidal properties, achieving efficacy rates surpassing 90% (Conceicao et al., 2020; Rincon et al., 2024). Sanguinarine could indirectly enhance insect resistance by promoting the growth of the entomopathogenic fungus Beauveria bassiana, suggesting a novel insect-plant-fungus interaction-based pest-management strategy. These findings highlight the potential of BIAs in the development of eco-friendly botanical pesticides (Lash et al., 2020). Additionally, BIAs exhibit significant cytotoxicity, rendering them promising candidates for novel herbicidal agents. Experimental studies reveal that berberine induces cytotoxic effects in tobacco BY-2 cells by triggering necrotic cell death, with efficacy comparable to the widely used herbicide glyphosate (Zhang et al., 2018a). Beyond biotic stress responses, BIAs also play a role in abiotic stress adaptation. Sanguinarine could enhance heat tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana by regulating the chaperone activities in the way to promote the heat shock response (Hara and Kurita, 2014; Matsuoka et al., 2016).

Nevertheless, early-diverging eudicots and magnoliids synthesize a vast and structurally diverse array of BIA compounds, yet the biological functions and specific ecological roles remain largely unexplored. Investigating the uncharted biological roles will provide important molecular insights into plant stress resistance, developmental processes, and environmental adaptation.

Core biosynthesis and key enzymatic modifications of BIAs

The biosynthesis of BIAs begins with a conserved upstream pathway that converts L-tyrosine into the key intermediate (S)-reticuline. Utilizing (S)-reticuline and (S)-N-methylcoclaurine as precursors, various CYP450 subfamilies (including members of the CYP80A/B/G/Q, CYP719B, and CYP71BG) and flavin-dependent oxidases (e.g., BBE) catalyze regio- and stereospecific intramolecular or intermolecular phenolic couplings. These reactions lead to the formation of distinct BIA structural scaffolds, including aporphines, BBRs, BZPs, PQs, morphinans, protopines, and bisBIAs.

Conserved upstream biosynthetic pathway

Extensive studies have demonstrated that the biosynthetic pathway from shikimate/chorismate-derived L-tyrosine to (S)-reticuline is universally conserved across all characterized BIA biosynthetic routes. L-tyrosine is converted by tyrosine aminotransferase (TyrAT) to form 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, which is then decarboxylated by 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate decarboxylase (4HPPDC) to yield 4-HPAA (Lee and Facchini, 2011). Alternatively, L-tyrosine can be decarboxylated by tyrosine decarboxylase (TYDC) to produce tyramine, which is subsequently oxidized by polyphenol oxidase (PPO) to form dopamine (Bilka et al., 2003; Frontiers Editorial, 2017). Dopamine serves as the precursor for the isoquinoline moiety, while 4-HPAA, derived from tyramine via deamination, acts as the benzyl component. 4-HPAA and dopamine are condensed by NCS in a Pictet–Spengler reaction to produce (S)-norcoclaurine, which is the core scaffold of structural diversity BIAs (Figure 2) (Minami et al., 2007).

(S)-Norcoclaurine was further modified by O-methylation, N-methylation, and hydroxylation to generate the key intermediate (S)-reticuline, which serves as a central node in BIA biosynthesis. First, the biosynthesis of begins with the methylation of (S)-norcoclaurine at the C-6′ position, catalyzed by norcoclaurine 6-O-methyltransferase (6OMT), yielding (S)-coclaurine. This step has been characterized in multiple BIA-producing species, including LcOMT3 in Liriodendron chinense (Cheng et al., 2022), Cc6OMT in C. chinensis (He et al., 2018), and PaOMT4/5 in P. amurense (Xu et al., 2024). 6OMT function was conserved across diverse plant lineages. Subsequently, coclaurine N-methyltransferase (CNMT) methylates the N-position of (S)-coclaurine to form (S)-N-methylcoclaurine (Hu et al., 2025). In magnoliids, Ranunculales, and lotus, O- and N-methylation are typically carried out by distinct enzymes, OMT and NMT, respectively. However, in the Zanthoxyloideae (P. amurense and Zanthoxylum armatum), a specialized branch of OMTs, termed NOMT, has evolved to catalyze N-methylation in BIA biosynthesis (Xu et al., 2024). Thirdly, the CYP450 gene members catalyze the hydroxylation of (S)-N-methylcoclaurine at the 3′ position to generate (S)-3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine. In Ranunculales, this reaction is mediated by CYP80B subfamily enzymes (e.g., CYP80B1 in Eschscholzia californica) (Pauli and Kutchan, 1998). Notably, recent study has identified CYP71BG subfamily members from P. amurense responsible for this same 3′-hydroxylation step, revealing lineage-specific evolutionary adaptation. Finally, 4′-O-methyltransferase (4′OMT) methylates the 4′-hydroxy group of (S)-3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine, yielding (S)-reticuline, such as Cj4′OMT in C. japonica and PaOMT3 in P. amurense (Morishige et al., 2000).

Key scaffold biosynthesis for diverse BIA categories

Diverse BIA scaffolds arise from the intermolecular or intramolecular catalytic coupling of (S)-N-methylcoclaurine and (S)-reticuline, along with their chiral isomers. These reactions, mediated by oxidation enzymes (e.g., CYP450 and FAD-dependent oxidases) form diverse C–C linkages and C–O phenol coupling to generate the structural diversity of BIAs (Figure 2). For instance, the formation of proaporphines (e.g., glaziovine) from (S/R)-N-methylcoclaurine is catalyzed by enzymes of the CYP80Q subfamily, such as NnCYP80Q1 and AcCYP80Q8, through a C1′–C8 coupling (Menendez-Perdomo and Facchini, 2023; Pyne et al., 2023). In contrast, the biosynthesis of aporphines (e.g., corytuberine) from (S)-reticuline involves a C2′–C8 coupling catalyzed by members of the CYP80G subfamily, including CjCYP80G2 and AcCYP80G7 (Ikezawa et al., 2008; Meng et al., 2024).

BBR, predominantly accumulated in Ranunculales (e.g., Coptis and Berberis) and Phellodendron species. The BBR scaffold is formed through an intramolecular C–C coupling reaction catalyzed by the FAD-dependent BBE in Ranunculales. This enzyme directs the formation of distinct alkaloid scaffolds via two oxidative coupling pathways utilizing (S)-reticuline as the substrate: (1) N-methyl carbon to benzyl C2′ coupling produces (S)-scoulerine, the direct precursor to berberine; or (2) alternative N-methyl carbon to C6′ coupling yields (S)-coreximine, the biosynthetic intermediate for phellodendrine formation (Gaweska et al., 2012). Notably, PaCYP71BG29 from P. amurense has been identified as an NADPH-dependent CYP450 enzyme that catalyzes the formation of the berberine bridge, converting (S)-reticuline into (S)-scoulerine (Xu et al., 2024).

(S)-Methylcanadine, generated from (S)-scoulerine by 9OMT, CYP719A, and tetrahydroprotoberberine N-methyltransferase (TNMT), is the biosynthetic precursor for PQs, protopines, and BZPs. (S)-cis-N-methylstylopine 14-hydroxylase (CYP82N4) catalyzes the conversion of (S)-methylcanadine and (S)-methylstylopine into protopine-type alkaloids, which are subsequently transformed into BZPs by protopine 6-hydroxylase (CYP82N3) (Beaudoin and Facchini, 2013). Meanwhile, (S)-methylcanadine undergoes a series of enzymatic modifications, including hydroxylation by CYP450s (CYP82X1, CYP82X2, and CYP82Y1), acetylation by acetyltransferase (AT1), and ester hydrolysis by carboxylesterase (CXE) to form PQ-type alkaloids (Li et al., 2018).

Morphine and sinomenine are chiral enantiomers and representative constituents of morphinan alkaloids, exhibiting distinct distribution patterns across plant species. The R-configured morphinans, such as morphine, are predominantly accumulated in species of the genus Papaver (Papaveraceae), and their complete biosynthetic pathway has been elucidated in opium poppy (P. somniferum) (Yang et al., 2021a). The pathway is initiated by chiral isomers of (S)-reticuline. The initial discovery of these isomerization reactions was reported in opium poppy, mediated by a unique fusion protein, reticuline epimerase (DRS-DRR), which consists of a CYP450 domain coupled with an aldo-keto reductase domain (Winzer et al., 2015; Diaz-Barcena et al., 2023). Comparative genomics analyses revealed that the DRS-DRR gene is uniquely present in opium poppy, which has been functionally characterized for its role in catalyzing the isomerization of (S)-reticuline to generate (R)-reticuline—the precursor of morphinans. The salutaridine synthase (SalSyn) encoded by PsCYP719B1 in opium poppy stereospecifically converts (R)-reticuline into salutaridine through C2′–C4a coupling. In contrast, S-configured morphinans, such as sinomenine, occur more broadly across several plant families, including Menispermaceae (Liu et al., 2025) and Papaveraceae (He et al., 2017). Sinoacutine, the enantiomer of salutaridine, serves as a key biosynthetic precursor for sinomenine, its biosynthesis from (S)-reticuline is catalyzed via C2′–C4a phenol coupling by MdCYP80G10 in Menispermum dauricum (Gesell et al., 2009; An et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the complete biosynthetic pathway of sinomenine has not yet been fully elucidated.

Additionally, 1-benzylisoquinoline monomers, (S)-N-methylcoclaurine, and/or (R)-N-methylcoclaurine can undergo intermolecular single or double C–O/C–C phenolic coupling to form bis-BIA scaffold, which may exhibit either isostructural configurations or differential spatial arrangements. The reported single C–O/C–C coupling reactions are mediated by CYP80A/Q family members. These enzymes have been functionally characterized in several bisBIA-accumulating plant species, including Berberis (BsCYP80A1), M. dauricum (MdCYP80Q12), and Nelumbo nucifera (NnCYP80Q2) (Payne et al., 2021; Pyne et al., 2023; An et al., 2024). Although CYP450 enzymes responsible for the single C–O phenolic coupling step in bisBIA biosynthesis have been partially identified, the enzymes and catalytic mechanisms underlying double C–O phenolic coupling remain unclear.

Certain BIA scaffold categories exhibit distinct lineage specificity. For instance, morphine accumulates predominantly in Papaver species, whereas berberine is mainly found in Coptis. The lineage-specific distribution of BIA diversity is driven by enzymes that catalyze regio- and stereospecific coupling reactions. A prominent example is CYP719B, which has been reported only in opium poppy and catalyzes the formation of the morphinan scaffold—a characteristic metabolite of the Papaver. Despite considerable advances in BIA biosynthesis research, the biosynthetic pathways of sinomenine, bisBIAs, and pavine-type alkaloids remain largely unexplored. Future studies aim to reveal the biosynthesis of the unknown pathways of BIA, which is crucial for revealing the evolution of the structural diversity within BIA biosynthesis.

Enzyme-mediated BIA structural diversification

The structural diversity of each BIA category originates from functional group additions or substitutions on the precursor scaffold. These modifications are primarily mediated by (1) S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-dependent O-methyltransferases (OMTs) and N-methyltransferases (NMTs), (2) CYP450 oxidoreductases (CYP450s), and (3) FAD-linked oxidoreductases (BBE, BBE-like). Additionally, demethylation and acylation mediated by 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases (2OGDs) and acetyl-CoA-dependent acyltransferases (ATs) are also involved in the BIA structure diversity.

MTs

Methyltransferases (MTs) are a widespread class of enzymes found across animal, plant, and microbial life. They perform methylation reactions of natural products, thereby modulating the chemical properties, stability, and bioactivity of metabolites (Liscombe et al., 2012). MTs can be classified according to the substrate atom that accepts the methyl group, such as O-, N-, C-, S-, and Se- (Lashley et al., 2022), among which O- and N-methyltransferases are the main types involved in the BIA biosynthetic pathway. The MTs involved in BIA biosynthesis were initially isolated and characterized primarily from species within the Ranunculales, including Coptis and poppy. In recent years, advances in omics technologies have facilitated the discovery and functional identification of BIA biosynthetic enzymes in a broader range of plant taxa, such as Magnoliales (e.g., L. chinense) (Cheng et al., 2022), Piperales (Aristolochia manshuriensis and Houttuynia cordata) (Hu et al., 2025; Shan et al., 2025), Nelumbonaceae (N. nucifera) (Menendez-Perdomo and Facchini, 2023), and members of the Zanthoxyloideae (e.g., P. amurense and Z. armatum). The major OMTs identified so far include 6OMT, 4′OMT, reticuline 7-O-methyltransferase (7OMT), norreticuline 7-O-methyltransferase (N7OMT), scoulerine 9-O-methyltransferase (SOMT), and columbamine O-methyltransferase (CoOMT). Among these, several enzymes demonstrate broad substrate promiscuity, significantly contributing to the structural diversification of BIA metabolites. This functional versatility is especially evident at the C6, C7, and C4′ positions of 1-BIAs as well as at the C2, C3, and C9 sites of BBRs (Figure 3A). For example, CyOMT5 from Corydalis yanhusuo effectively catalyzes C7-O-methylation of (S)-3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine and also accepts several protoberberine alkaloids as substrates, including (S)-scoulerine, (S)-tetrahydrocolumbamine, and tetrahydrojatrorrhizine, mediating methylation at their C2 and C3 positions, respectively (Bu et al., 2022). CyOMT2 catalyzes both C6-O and C4′-O-methylation of (S)-norcoclaurine and (S)-3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine but exhibits a stronger catalytic preference for the 4′-O-methylation. In Glaucium flavum, GFLOMT6 efficiently methylates C7-O and C3′-O positions of 1-BIAs such as reticuline, laudanine, and codamine (Chang et al., 2015). Similarly, Se6OMT3 from Stephania epigaea demonstrates catalytic activity toward multiple 1-BIA compounds at the C6, C7, and C4′ sites (Gan et al., 2025).

The crystal structures of two key BIA OMTs have been reported: norlaudanosoline 6-OMT from Thalictrum flavum (Tf6OMT; PDB: 5ICE) and scoulerine 9OMT from P. somniferum (PsSOMT1; PDB: 6I6K) (Robin et al., 2016; Cabry et al., 2019). BIA OMTs share a conserved domain architecture, consisting of an N-terminal dimerization domain connected to a C-terminal substrate-binding domain that accommodates both SAM donor and BIA substrates. Catalytically, BIA OMTs employ a conserved mechanism similar to plant SAM-dependent OMTs: a fully conserved histidine residue (His256 in Tf6OMT; His296 in PsSOMT1) acts as a general base to deprotonate the target hydroxyl group. The resulting oxyanion then performs a nucleophilic attack on the labile methyl group of SAM, resulting in methyl transfer.

NMTs mainly participate in the N-methylation of various BIAs, including 1-BIAs, BBRs, and aporphines (Figure 3A). In magnoliids and early-diverging eudicots, these enzymes are typically named according to their preferred substrates, such as coclaurine N-methyltransferase (CNMT), TNMT, reticuline N-methyltransferase (RNMT), and pavine N-methyltransferase (PavNMT). CNMT, TNMT, and PavNMT exhibit distinct substrate specificities, preferentially catalyzing the methylation of 1-BIAs, BBRs, and pavines, respectively (Liscombe and Facchini, 2007; Torres et al., 2016). In contrast, RNMT (PsRNMT) exhibits broader substrate specificity, catalyzing N-methylation of both reticuline (1-BIA) and corytuberine (aporphine) (Morris and Facchini, 2016). Most plant NMTs catalyze a single methylation at the acceptor nitrogen atom, as exemplified by AmCNMT from A. manshuriensis (Hu et al., 2025). However, certain enzymes such as HcNMT2/3/4 from H. cordata are capable of mediating di-N-dimethylation (Shan et al., 2025).

In contrast to OMTs, structural and functional studies of BIA NMTs have garnered significant attention, including TfPavNMT from T. flavum (PDB: 5KOK), CjCNMT from C. japonica (PDB: 6GKV) (Bennett et al., 2018), and GfTNMT from G. flavum (PDB: 6P3O) (Lang et al., 2019). BIA NMTs adopt a conserved Rossmann-like fold for SAM binding, complemented by a C-terminal substrate-binding domain. A distinctive structural feature is an N-terminal extension composed of three helices that wrap around the substrate-binding domain and contribute to dimerization. Substitution of His206 in TfPavNMT and His208 in CjCNMT with alanine resulted in a sharp reduction in mutant activity (Torres et al., 2016; Bennett et al., 2018). However, unlike in BIA OMTs, none of these residues appears strictly essential for catalysis, suggesting a more complex and redundant mechanistic framework. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the catalytic strategy employed by BIA NMTs.

Notably, a novel branch of O-methyltransferase-like enzymes termed NOMT was identified in P. amurense (Rutaceae, Sapindales) (Xu et al., 2024). NOMT exhibits unexpected N-methyltransferase activity within the BIA biosynthetic pathway, along with broad substrate promiscuity. It can catalyze both mono- and di-N-methylation of various 1-BIAs and even mediates N-methylation of the BBR compound (S)-scoulerine. Structural comparisons indicate that PaNOMT shares a similar protein fold with Tf6OMT but differs significantly from CjCNMT and possesses a more open and hydrophobic substrate-binding cavity. Crucially, the NH group of the substrate was positioned closer to the SAM donor (4.5 Å) in the ternary complex PaNOMT9/(S)-coclaurine/SAM (PDB: 8ZN0), while the C-6 OH group was positioned closer to the SAM donor (3.4 Å) in the Tf6OMT complex, facilitating N-methylation. Although CjCNMT also catalyzes N-methylation, PaNOMT exhibits distinct substrate orientation and enhanced conformational flexibility around key hydrophobic residues, enabling broader substrate promiscuity toward both 1-BIA and BBR alkaloids. The structural and functional diversification of MTs has been instrumental in driving the chemical diversity of BIAs. Future research should prioritize harnessing the catalytic plasticity of these enzymes through protein engineering and synthetic-biology approaches to design novel methylated alkaloids with improved bioactivities or new properties.

CYP450s

CYP450s represent one of the most crucial enzyme families involved in plant secondary metabolism, playing an indispensable role in the structural diversification of BIAs. The remarkable structural complexity of BIAs largely stems from various CYP-catalyzed oxidative modifications, including hydroxylation, C–C/C–O coupling, and cyclization reactions. Key CYP450 genes implicated in these processes include members of the CYP80A/B/G/Q, CYP719A/B, CYP82X/Y/N, and CYP71BG subfamilies. Among these, CYP80A/G/Q, CYP719B, CYP82N, and CYP71BG significantly expand BIA scaffold diversity through reactions such as ring coupling and backbone rearrangement. In parallel, CYP80B, CYP82X/Y, CYP719A, and CYP71BG enzymes contribute to structural elaboration via hydroxylation and methylenedioxy bridge formation (Figure 3B), thereby enriching the chemical diversity of BIAs and enhancing their biological activities and pharmacological functions.

Currently, hydroxylation reactions within the BIA biosynthetic pathway have been primarily characterized in two major classes: 1-BIA alkaloids (e.g., (S)-N-methylcoclaurine) and BBR-type alkaloids. (S)-N-methylcoclaurine undergoes regioselective hydroxylation at the 3′ position, a reaction primarily catalyzed by CYP80B in many BIA-producing plants such as opium poppy (Frick et al., 2007), H. cordata (Shan et al., 2025), E. californica (Pauli and Kutchan, 1998), C. japonica (Ikezawa et al., 2003), T. flavum (Samanani et al., 2005), C. yanhusuo (Liu et al., 2021a), and M. dauricum (An et al., 2024). However, in the phylogenetically distant species Phellodendrons, members of the CYP71BG subfamily have been identified to catalyze the 3′-hydroxylation of (S)-N-methylcoclaurine to form 3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine, demonstrating functional conservation or convergent evolution of this key biosynthetic step across taxonomically divergent plant lineages (Xu et al., 2024). On the other hand, hydroxylation of BBR-type alkaloids at C1, C8, and C13 is catalyzed by members of the CYP82 family and contributes critically to the biosynthesis of noscapine. CYP82Y1 mediates C1-hydroxylation, CYP82X1 catalyzes hydroxylation at C8, and CYP82X2 is responsible for C13-hydroxylation. To date, these enzymes have been characterized only in noscapine-producing poppy species (Winzer et al., 2012; Li and Smolke, 2016; Li et al., 2018). The CYP719A subfamily is particularly notable for catalyzing intramolecular C–O coupling reactions, specifically constructing the characteristic methylenedioxy bridge (–O–CH2–O–). CYP719A enzymes from diverse plant species, including C. japonica (Ikezawa et al., 2003), C. chinensis (Liu et al., 2021b; Wu et al., 2024), E. californica (Ikezawa et al., 2007; 2009), Argemone mexicana (Diaz Chavez et al., 2011), and P. somniferum (Winzer et al., 2012; Li et al., 2018), catalyze the formation of methylenedioxy bridges between C2–C3 or C9–C10 positions across various BBR scaffolds, significantly enhancing structural diversity. For instance, EcCYP719A3 from E. californica converts (S)-cheilanthifoline, (S)-scoulerine, and (S)-tetrahydrocolumbamine into (S)-stylopine, (S)-nandinine, and (S)-canadine, respectively, via C2–C3 bridge formation (Ikezawa et al., 2007). Similarly, CcCYP719A1 from C. chinensis exhibits versatile activity by catalyzing methylenedioxy bridge formation at either C2–C3 or C9–C10 in substrates such as (S)-tetrahydrocolumbamine, (S)-scoulerine, (S)-cheilanthifoline, and 2-methylscoulerine, yielding (S)-canadine, (S)-cheilanthifoline, (S)-stylopine, and (S)-tetrahydroepiberberine, respectively (Wu et al., 2024). In addition, NnCYP719A22 from N. nucifera catalyzes the formation of a methylenedioxy bridge between C1 and C2 in aporphine-type alkaloids, converting caaverine to anonaine and lirinidine to roemerine (Menendez-Perdomo and Facchini, 2023).

The functional diversity of CYP450s has served as a key evolutionary driver of chemical complexity in BIAs. Through catalytic reactions including hydroxylation, methylenedioxy bridge formation, and C–C/C–O coupling, these enzymes generate core structural scaffolds and introduce functional groups that enable downstream enzymatic modifications. Nevertheless, our current understanding of CYP450-mediated BIA diversification remains fragmented. Advancing knowledge of CYP450 diversity and mechanistic underpinnings will not only shed light on evolutionary innovations in plant metabolism but also offer transformative tools for the biomanufacturing of pharmacologically active alkaloids.

BBE

The BBE represents a class of flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent oxidases that play a central role in the structural diversification of BIAs through a unique four-electron oxidation mechanism. Phylogenetic and functional studies classify BBE into two major groups: classical BBE and BBE-like enzymes. Classical BBE specifically catalyzes the intramolecular C–C coupling between the N-methyl group and the benzylic carbon of (S)-reticuline, yielding (S)-scoulerine, which features a distinctive bridged-ring scaffold. This transformation is achieved via a concerted four-electron transfer process, involving simultaneous oxidation and rearrangement of two carbon centers in the substrate.

BBE-like enzymes primarily catalyze two- or four-electron oxidation reactions in the BIA biosynthetic pathways, ultimately yielding BZP-type and BBR-type alkaloids with diverse pharmacological activities (Figure 3C). In C. chinensis, a BBE-like gene CcTHBO with broad substrate promiscuity could catalyze the four-electron oxidation of demethyl-tetrahydrocolumbamine, (S)-tetrahydrojartrorrhizine, (S)-canadine, demethyl-canadine, (S)-tetrahydroepiberberine, (S)-stylopine, and (S)-tetrahydroberberine to produce demethyleneberberine, jartrorrhizine, berberine, thalifendine, epiberberine, coptisine, and groenlandicine, respectively (Wu et al., 2024). The genome of Corydalis tomentella reveals the presence of a species-specific tandemly repeated gene cluster on chromosome 6, which comprises 25 BBE-like (BBEL) genes. Within this cluster, CtBBEL8 and CtBBEL31 catalyze the conversion of cavidine and cheilanthifoline to dehydrocavidine and dehydrocheilanthifoline, respectively, whereas CtBBEL22 specifically mediates the oxidation of stylopine to coptisine (Xu et al., 2022). These coordinated enzymatic activities collectively generate distinct oxidized intermediates that significantly enhance the chemical landscape of protoberberine alkaloids. Furthermore, in Macleaya cordata (Liu et al., 2017) and P. somniferum (Hagel et al., 2012), dihydrobenzophenanthridine oxidases (DBOX) have been functionally characterized to catalyze a two-electron oxidation process that mediates aromatic ring formation. This reaction constitutes the final step in the biosynthesis of BZPs, such as sanguinarine and chelerythrine.

BBE and BBEL enzymes play an indispensable role in expanding the structural diversity of BIAs, particularly BBR and BZP types, through their unique two- or four-electron oxidative reactivities. However, the heterologous expression of functional BBE enzymes remains challenging, primarily due to poor solubility, incorrect folding, and inefficient incorporation of the FAD cofactor. These limitations have significantly hindered the reconstruction of efficient BIA biosynthetic pathways in microbial or plant systems. Advanced protein-engineering strategies, such as directed evolution and rational design, hold great potential to overcome these barriers and enable efficient synthetic production of high-value BIAs.

Others

The enzymes thebaine 6-O-demethylase (T6ODM), codeine O-demethylase (CODM), and Cc6DM belong to the 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase (2OGD) superfamily (Figure 3D). Despite sharing a conserved catalytic mechanism dependent on Fe2+ and 2-oxoglutarate, they exhibit distinct substrate specificities and functional roles in BIA biosynthesis. T6ODM and CODM, characterized primarily in opium poppy, catalyze O-demethylation at specific sites of thebaine and codeine, respectively, critical for morphine biosynthesis (Thodey et al., 2014). In contrast, Cc6DM2/3 from C. chinensis acts on BBR-type alkaloids and mediates regioselective O-demethylation at either the C3 or C10 position (Wu et al., 2024). For instance, it converts (S)-scoulerine to 10-demethyl-scoulerine via C10-O-demethylation and (S)-tetrahydrocolumbamine to demethyl-tetrahydrocolumbamine through C3-O-demethylation. This functional divergence, driven by variations in their substrate-binding pockets, highlights how conserved enzyme frameworks can evolve to support species-specific alkaloid profiles and expand structural diversity within BIAs.

The enzymes salutaridinol 7-O-acetyltransferase (SalAT) and 1,13-dihydroxy-N-methylcandine 13-O-acetyltransferase (AT1) are pivotal acyltransferases in BIA biosynthesis that catalyze the regioselective acylation of specific alkaloid substrates (Figure 3D). SalAT, first characterized in opium poppy, mediates the acetylation of salutaridinol at the C7 position—a critical step in morphine biosynthesis (Allen et al., 2008). Meanwhile, PsAT1 acetylates the C13-hydroxy group of the BBR-type alkaloid 1,13-dihydroxy-N-methylcanadine, yielding 1-hydroxy-13-O-acetyl-N-methylcanadine (Dang et al., 2015). These modifications significantly alter the lipophilicity, bioactivity, and metabolic stability of the resulting compounds, thereby expanding the structural and functional diversity of BIAs and contributing to their wide spectrum of pharmacological effects.

Omics progress of BIA-producing plants

Genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics have collectively provided an unprecedented, systems-level view of the genetic and biochemical underpinnings of BIA metabolism. Comparative genomics has been instrumental in tracing the evolutionary history of BIAs, identifying genetic innovations, and linking genomic events to chemical diversity. High-quality chromosome-level genomes have been critical for accurate gene annotation and evolutionary inference. To date, such assemblies are available for several key BIA-producing species across divergent lineages (Figure 4), including Liriodendron chinense (Magnoliales) (Cheng et al., 2022), Litsea cubeba (Laurales) (Chen et al., 2020), Aristolochia species (Piperales) (Cui et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2025), and others within Ranunculales (e.g., P. somniferum, C. tomentella, C. chinensis, Stephania cephalantha, and M. dauricum) and Rutaceae (e.g., P. amurense), all with Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) scores exceeding 90% (Yang et al., 2021a; Liu et al., 2021b; Xu et al., 2022, 2024; An et al., 2024; Leng et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). However, many BIA-producing species, such as Cryptocarya (pavines producing) and Sinomenium (sinomenine accumulating), are still unsequenced or lack high-quality genomes, limiting the elucidation of their complex BIA pathways. Even within well-studied groups, BIA profiles can vary significantly between closely related species (e.g., cepharanthine in Stephania and sinomenine in Sinomenium), underscoring the need for further genomic studies to expand the phylogenetic breadth of comparative analyses (Supplemental Table S2).

Polyploidy and whole-genome duplication (WGD) events have played a pivotal role in providing the genetic foundation for metabolic diversity and adaptive evolution (Qiao et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020). Many BIA-producing plants have undergone lineage-specific WGDs after the ancient WGD event shared by angiosperms (Figure 4) (Wu et al., 2020). Among the magnoliids, the orders Magnoliales and Laurales share a common ancient WGD event. Subsequently, the Laurales lineage experienced additional independent WGDs, with distinct events occurring separately within the Lauraceae and Calycanthaceae families (Chen et al., 2020; Lv et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021). In contrast, the Magnoliales exhibit no evidence of a shared, order-wide WGD following the initial ancient duplication (Dong et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025). For early-diverging eudicots, the evolutionary history of WGD has long been a subject of active debate. Initial studies proposing a shared ancient WGD between early-diverging eudicots and core eudicots were based on genomic synteny and KS analyses of species such as Aquilegia coerulea compared to Vitis vinifera (Akoz and Nordborg, 2019). However, this hypothesis of a common polyploidy origin has been challenged by increasing evidence from more extensive phylogenomic analyses. A key counter-argument comes from the analysis of N. nucifera, whose genome lacks the characteristic KS peak corresponding to the core eudicot γ-event, arguing against a shared whole-genome triplication (WGT) event (Shi and Chen, 2020; Shi et al., 2020). Furthermore, ancestral karyotype reconstructions suggest that certain observed syntenic patterns between specific lineages are likely the result of independent rearrangements, pointing to a complex history of independent chromosomal rearrangements rather than a simple shared polyploidy event (Zhong et al., 2022). An emerging consensus now supports the viewpoint that WGD events in early-diverging eudicots are lineage specific. For instance, the Ranunculales experienced a shared ancient WGD event (named Rα). Recent genomic analyses further reveal that this Rα event in likely involved allopolyploidization, originating from the hybridization and duplication of two distinct ancestors—one with six chromosomes (pre-Rα ancestor1) and the other with seven (pre-Rα ancestor2) (An et al., 2024). Meanwhile, Nelumbo underwent an independent recent WGD (named λ) (Ming et al., 2013). These findings suggest that early-diverging eudicots and core eudicots have likely followed independent genomic evolutionary trajectories since their divergence, with distinct WGD events occurring at different times and contributing differentially to their genomic architectures. Future studies incorporating high-quality genomes from key lineages and employing more elaborate comparative genomic approaches will help resolve lineage-specific diversity and complexity of BIA metabolism in genome evolution.

Despite their remarkable structural diversity, all BIAs originate from a common precursor, norcoclaurine, which is formed by the Pictet–Spengler condensation of dopamine and 4-HPAA catalyzed by NCS. This reaction represents the pivotal, first committed step in establishing the BIA backbone. Phylogenetically, NCS was identified as a member of the pathogenesis-related 10 (PR-10)/Bet v1-like protein family, which is typically associated with plant defense and ligand binding rather than catalysis (Morris et al., 2021). Phylogenetic analyses of PR-10 proteins further reveal a complex evolutionary history for these Pictet–Spenglerases (Supplemental Figure S1). Interestingly, NCS enzymes from early-diverging eudicot BIA producers (e.g., Papaveraceae, Ranunculaceae) form a distinct clade. However, NCSs from the magnoliid Aristolochia contorta (AcNCSs) cluster more closely with norbelladine synthase (NBS) from Amaryllidaceae monocots than with the NCS clade from Ranunculales (Singh et al., 2018), indicating that they might share a more recent common ancestor. This pattern suggests that the Bet v1-like protein superfamily represents a pre-existing, versatile “toolkit” in plants. Its inherent ability to bind phenolic compounds and potentially stabilize reaction intermediates may have provided a latent catalytic promiscuity. Therefore, the emergence of NCS is not an isolated event but part of a broader evolutionary narrative where common protein scaffolds are recruited to generate chemical diversity. The recruitment of a PR-10 protein as NCS provided the core chemical architecture (the norcoclaurine scaffold). Once this step was established, the subsequent evolution of BIA diversity largely proceeded through the expansion and functional diversification of downstream enzyme families (e.g., OMTs and CYP450s), which acted upon this common precursor. This establishes a clear evolutionary logic: a key innovation of PR-10 genes (the emergence of NCS enzyme activity) created the entry point, while modular modifications of the intermediate reticuline led to the radiation of BIA structural classes.

Currently available genome resources have revealed both conserved and lineage-specific gene expansions linked to BIA diversification. Conserved upstream genes (e.g., 6OMT, 4′OMT, CNMT, and CYP80B) are widely retained in BIA-producing magnoliids and eudicots, facilitating the biosynthesis of core BIAs (Figure 4). In addition, lineage-specific gene expansions have played a major role in the evolution of specialized BIA pathways. Specially, the massive expansion of genes such as CYP82Y (13 copies), COR (19 copies), and 2OGDs (14 copies) in opium poppy is directly correlated with its unique capacity to produce morphinans and noscapine (Supplemental Table S1; Supplemental Figures S5, S10, and S11) (Winzer et al., 2012). In the Menispermaceae, expansion of the CYP80Q subfamily (e.g., 15 copies in S. cephalantha) is linked to bisBIA diversification (Supplemental Table S1; Supplemental Figure S4), while taxon-specific genes including CYP80G10 and dechloroacutumine halogenase (DAH) enable the synthesis of sinomenine and halogenated acutumines (Kim et al., 2020; An et al., 2024). Notably, members of the Zanthoxyloideae (e.g., P. amurense, Z. armatum) have independently evolved an alternative BIA biosynthetic pathway through the expansion of Zanthoxyloideae-special BIAs OMTs, such as PaOMT4/5 (6OMT), PaOMT3 (4′OMTs), PaOMT1 (9OMT), and PaNOMT2/7/8/9 (NOMTs) and the multifunctional CYP71BG29 (Supplemental Figures S2 and S7). These duplicated genes together facilitate protoberberine production via a distinct pathway absent in typical BIA-producing plants, illustrating a unique evolutionary trajectory for BIA biosynthesis in this group. Recent studies have confirmed that the independent gene losses involved in BIA biosynthesis in monocots and most core eudicots—a distribution consistent with BIAs–-—are largely absent in other clades (Hu et al., 2025). These discoveries highlight the differential retention of ancient gene duplication events across angiosperm lineages and the complex evolutionary dynamics of the BIA biosynthetic pathway.

The omics era has accelerated our understanding of BIA evolution, enabling a shift from studying single enzymes to analyzing entire pathways systematically. Co-expression network analysis, which clusters genes with similar expression patterns, has efficiently uncovered unknown catalytic enzymes involved in BIA pathways. For example, the discovery of the noscapine and morphine gene clusters in opium poppy was greatly facilitated by the strong co-expression of their constituent genes (Winzer et al., 2012). Thus, multi-omics integration not only facilitates the discovery of novel catalytic enzymes but also enables the elucidation of BIA biosynthetic gene evolutionary diversity through comparative genomics, with insights gained from genome duplication-driven gene expansion and sub-/neo-functionalization.

Evolutionary drivers of BIA structural diversity

The structural diversity of BIAs is closely related to their distribution in various plant lineages, reflecting their important role in plant adaptive evolution. This diversity, encompassing simple precursors (e.g., norcoclaurine) to complex bioactive ingredients (e.g., morphine, berberine, and cepharanthine), is directly induced by the evolution of the biosynthetic mechanism. Herein, we summarize the key molecular mechanisms, including gene duplication and neofunctionalization and the assembly and evolution of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), that have acted as powerful drivers in BIA structural complexity and distribution across plant lineages.

Biosynthetic gene cluster

BGC is the physical clustering of non-homologous biosynthetic genes into genomic loci, facilitating the co-inheritance and co-regulation of enzymatic steps, providing a selective advantage by ensuring the functional integrity of a pathway (Smit and Lichman, 2022). The discovery of BGCs for BIAs has been a pivotal advancement in understanding the evolution of their biosynthesis.

The most renowned BIA BGC is the super-gene cluster located on chromosome 11 of P. somniferum (opium poppy), which consists of 10 noscapine synthase genes and five morphine synthase genes (Figure 5A) (Winzer et al., 2012). However, this BGC is unique to opium poppy. The morphine cluster co-localizes key genes encoding enzymes such as PsSTORR (CYP82Y2-oxidoreductase fusion gene), PsSalSyn (PsCYP719B1, encoding salutaridine synthase), PsSalR (encoding short-chain dehydrogenase), PsSalAT (encoding acyltransferase), and PsTHS (encoding thebaine synthase), which catalyze consecutive five steps in the morphine pathway from (S)-reticuline to morphine to produce intermediate thebaine. The tight linkage of these genes allows for coordinated transcriptional control, explaining how the opium poppy is the sole natural source of morphine analgesics. In addition, wide diversity of BIA gene clusters has been identified across phylogenetically divergent BIA-producing plants. The dynamic and evolutionarily plastic 6OMT-CYP80B-4′OMT clusters have been identified across divergent BIA-producing plants, including in magnoliids (e.g., L. chinense and H. cordata) and Ranunculales (e.g., opium poppy and S. japonica) (Leng et al., 2024; Shan et al., 2025). These clusters exhibit a dynamic distribution across phylogenetically distant angiosperms, contrasting with the majority of BGCs that are typically confined to specific evolutionary lineages. In addition, a similar PaOMT1-PaCYP71BG29 gene cluster was also found in P. amurense (Sapindales) (Figure 5A) (Xu et al., 2024). This suggests that cluster assembly events occur repeatedly and independently through gene relocation and recruitment in different plant lineages. The extensive structural diversity among BIA BGCs underscores a complex and species-specific evolutionary history, which may involve intricate genomic variations and selection processes.

Gene duplication events

Gene duplication serves as the primary source of genetic novelty, providing the raw genetic material for evolutionary innovation. Duplication events, whether through WGD or tandem gene duplication (TGD), generate redundant gene copies. These copies are then free to accumulate mutations without compromising the original function, potentially leading to neofunctionalization or subfunctionalization (Engel et al., 1970). This process is paramount in the expansion and diversification of enzyme families critical for BIA biosynthesis, such as CYP450s, MTs, and BBEs.

Genomic analyses across BIA-producing plants have revealed that key biosynthetic genes are frequently amplified through TGD. In opium poppy, the 6OMT, 4′OMT, and 7OMT genes, which are involved in central BIA pathways, exist as multiple tandemly arrayed copies (Hu et al., 2025). Although these paralogs produced by TGD share high sequence homology, their enzymatic activities have diverged; some preferentially methylate specific positions on the BIA backbone (e.g., 6-O or 4′-O) with distinct regiospecificity. Similarly, the 2OGD and CYP82X subfamily members, responsible for noscapine biosynthesis, have expanded via TGD in opium poppy (Figure 5A) (Guo et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2021a). In C. tomentella, lineage-specific TGD events of the BBEL genes are linked to functional diversification, driving the structural diversity of cavidine and its analogs (Figure 5B) (Xu et al., 2022). A recent study has discovered that a specific plant halogenase, DAH, undergoes neofunctionalization through TGD event of flavonol synthase gene, enabling it to produce halogenated acutumines (Kim et al., 2025). Moreover, the occurrence frequency of WGD events is vastly lower than that of TGD, yet they also play a crucial role in diversifying the structural diversity of BIA. For instance, M. dauricum exhibits significant TGD- and WGD-driven specific expansion of CYP80G and CYP80Q genes, facilitating functional diversification and resulting in the specific distribution of its morphinans and bisBIAs (An et al., 2024). Coincidentally, the genetic redundancy provided by WGD and subsequent genome rearrangements in opium poppy allowed duplicate copies to neofunctionalize, contributing to the evolution of the complex morphine and noscapine pathways (Yang et al., 2021a). These repeated patterns of gene family expansion through both TGD and WGD underscore the prevalence and significance of duplication-driven evolution in shaping the structural diversity and lineage specificity of BIAs.

WGD and TGD have also driven the convergent evolution of BIA biosynthesis in distantly related plant lineages. A notable example is the independent emergence of the berberine biosynthesis between Rutaceae and Ranunculaceae. In C. chinensis (Ranunculaceae), berberine biosynthesis requires two specialized enzymes: CYP80B catalyzes 3′-hydroxylation, while BBE mediates berberine bridge formation (Liu et al., 2021a; Jiao et al., 2024). In contrast, P. amurense (Rutaceae)—which underwent recent lineage-specific WGD—employs one multifunctional CYP71BG29 to efficiently catalyze both oxidative steps (Figure 5B) (Xu et al., 2024). These CYP71BG subfamily members showed obvious lineage-specific expansion via WGD and TGD events. Subsequently, a duplicated copy derived from a conserved tyrosine hydroxylase gene underwent positive selection and neofunctionalization, thereby acquiring the dual catalytic capabilities of 3′-hydroxylation and berberine bridge formation (Figures 5B and 5C). This case exemplifies how nature can accumulate identical secondary metabolites through distinct biosynthetic pathways.

Concluding remarks and outlook

The well-characterized pharmacological activities of BIAs underscore their significant therapeutic potential in modern medicine, driving continued interest in their biosynthesis and production. These pharmacological effects are not merely incidental from an evolutionary perspective. In fact, they represent the functional manifestation of sophisticated chemical defense mechanisms honed by plants over millennia. Key enzyme families, including MTs, CYP450s, BBEs, demethylases, and ATs, generate diverse bioactive compounds by cooperatively modifying the core BIA scaffold. Consequently, the remarkable structural diversity of BIAs, underpinned by these lineage-specific enzymatic expansions, is not a random evolutionary outcome. It likely reflects direct adaptive responses to diverse and dynamic ecological pressures.

The specific biological and pharmacological activities of BIAs, such as neurotoxicity to insect herbivores, antimicrobial effects, and significant cytotoxicity, provide crucial clues to understanding the selective forces that may have shaped their biosynthetic pathways. For instance, in P. somniferum, a key adaptation driven by natural selection is the extensive duplication and functional diversification of the CYP82 gene subfamily, as well as the recruitment of multiple genes to form morphine BGC and noscapine BGC. This genetic innovation facilitates the efficient production of potent alkaloids, favoring the amplification and refinement of a chemical defense arsenal. Similarly, the independent evolution of berberine biosynthesis in Ranunculaceae and Berberidaceae, involving specific expansions of CYP719A/CYP71BG and other genes, likely conferred a selective advantage through the compound’s broad-spectrum antimicrobial and antifungal properties, possibly in response to soil-borne pathogens in their respective habitats. Conversely, the loss or simplification of certain BIA pathways in some lineages could also be adaptive. If the metabolic cost of producing a particular alkaloid outweighs its benefit in a new environment (e.g., absence of specific herbivores), gene loss or pseudogenization may occur. Future research integrating comparative genomics, metabolomics, and ecological studies within a phylogenetic framework will be key to testing these hypotheses and pinpointing the specific biotic/abiotic factors that drove the diversification of major BIA classes such as morphinans, protoberberines, and benzophenanthridines in their respective plant lineages.

Despite significant advances in BIA biosynthesis, substantial gaps remain in our understanding of key pathways, such as those underlying sinomenine and pavine alkaloid biosynthesis. Furthermore, the functional promiscuity of many BIA-modifying enzymes complicates in vivo pathway elucidation. To address these challenges, it is essential to adopt integrated and forward-looking strategies. By combining multi-omics data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, with artificial intelligence (AI) and machine-learning technologies, predictive models of gene function, metabolic flux, and regulatory networks can be constructed, thereby accelerating the identification of unclear biosynthetic steps (Liao et al., 2025; McConnachie et al., 2025). For instance, the integration of genomic, transcriptomic, and spatial metabolomic data from P. amurense has successfully predicted and validated multiple key genes involved in berberine biosynthesis (Xu et al., 2024). Furthermore, AI-based tools such as DeepBGC (Hannigan et al., 2019) enable efficient mining of potential BGCs by identifying patterns in genomic data, while AlphaFold (David et al., 2022) offers transformative potential for predicting enzyme structures and clarifying catalytic mechanisms in plant biosynthetic pathways. Moreover, the Gene-encoded Natural Diverse Components Repository leverages AI to predict key intermediates and structural analogs in natural product biosynthesis, further expanding the resource base for natural product research and development (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). In enzymatic mechanisms and protein engineering, the elucidation of crystal structures of key enzymes, such as norcoclaurine synthase (NnNCS, PDB: 8HO2), BBE (EcBBE, PDB: 3D2D), and various methyltransferases (e.g., Tf6OMT, PDB: 5ICE; PaNOMT9, PDB: 8ZN0), has revealed critical residues in catalytic pockets and substrate recognition mechanisms. These structural insights provide a foundation for rationally engineering substrate selectivity and enhancing catalytic efficiency. Moreover, the heterologous reconstruction of complete BIA pathways in microbial hosts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Escherichia coli has been achieved, including opioids such as thebaine and hydrocodone (Galanie et al., 2015), the antimicrobial alkaloid berberine (Han and Li, 2023), and the antitussive agent noscapine (Li et al., 2018). However, achieving sustainable and green production still faces several hurdles, such as the effective integration of plant-derived genes and sophisticated host engineering, including codon optimization, metabolic flux balancing, compartmentalization of toxic intermediates, and enhancement of cofactor supply.

In summary, the integration of multi-omics, AI, structural biology, and synthetic biology provides a powerful framework for elucidating and engineering BIA biosynthesis. Continued advances in these interdisciplinary approaches will not only bridge existing knowledge gaps but also pave the way for the sustainable production of valuable BIAs and the creation of novel analogs with potential applications in medicine and agriculture.
