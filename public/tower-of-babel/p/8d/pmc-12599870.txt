Why rat oral cavity tumors should not be the basis of quantitative cancer risk assessment for oral exposure to hexavalent chromium
Authors: Deborah Proctor, Xiaohui Jiang, Heidi Reichert, Chad Thompson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral regulatory agencies have developed threshold-based drinking water guidelines for hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] protective of nonneoplastic and neoplastic lesions in rodents using small intestine tumor data in mice. However, in 2024, the US Environmental Protection Agency Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) set an oral cancer slope factor based on oral cavity tumors in rats following chronic exposure to up to 180 ppm Cr(VI) in drinking water. Herein, we review previously published in vivo mechanistic data in rat oral cavity tissue indicating the absence of mutation responses in oral cavity tissue of transgenic Big Blue® rats and the absence of transcriptomic responses in F344 rats indicative of toxicological or homeostatic changes in the oral cavity following exposures up to 180 ppm Cr(VI). In addition, we extended an IRIS meta-analysis of gastrointestinal cancers by including oral cavity cancers, using the same epidemiological studies and approach as IRIS. We observed a significantly decreased meta-relative risk (meta-relative risk: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.69 to 0.95 and 0.74, 95% CI: 0.068 to 0.81 using random and fixed effect models). Given the lack of evidence for genotoxicity or even homeostatic responses to Cr(VI) in the rat oral cavity and the absence of oral cancer risk in humans, oral toxicity criteria for Cr(VI) should not be based on oral cavity tumors in rats. Many agencies have instead developed threshold-based toxicity criteria using nonneoplastic intestinal lesions observed in mice due to strong evidence for a cytotoxicity/regenerative proliferation mode of action for intestinal tumors, which were observed at lower drinking water concentrations than rat oral tumors.Keywords: risk assessment, mouth neoplasms, humans, rats, United States Environmental Protection Agency, drinking water

For nearly a century, hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] has been considered a lung carcinogen based on occupational epidemiology data (IARC 1990, 2012); however, the oral carcinogenicity of Cr(VI) was first demonstrated in 2008 in a 2-year drinking water cancer bioassay where mice developed tumors in the small intestine and rats developed tumors in the oral cavity. Intestinal tumors were accompanied by extensive tissue damage and regenerative cell proliferation, whereas nonneoplastic or preneoplastic lesions were not observed in the oral cavity (NTP 2008). For a decade, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) draft Toxicological Reviews for Cr(VI) have proposed oral cancer slope factor (CSF) values based on the intestinal tumors in mice (US EPA 2010, 2022). In 2023, EPA’s Science Advisory Board (SAB) recommended that IRIS further evaluate the low-dose kinetics of Cr(VI) and potential overestimation of cancer risk (SAB 2023). In 2024, EPA finalized the Toxicological Review and based the oral CSF on the oral tumors in rats—without the opportunity for public or SAB review and comment on the altered basis of the CSF (US EPA 2024a). To our knowledge, IRIS is the only regulatory agency to date to propose toxicity criteria based on oral cavity tumors in rats. Several organizations reviewing the same literature developed threshold-based cancer criteria for Cr(VI) protective of intestinal damage in mice as a precursor to intestinal tumor formation (Health Canada 2016; TCEQ 2016; FSCJ 2019; WHO 2020). Evidence supporting a threshold mode of action (MOA) for intestinal tumors can be found elsewhere (Thompson et al. 2017, 2021, 2023). This article highlights critical animal and human data that regulators and risk assessors should consider before deriving or adopting a CSF based on rat oral cavity tumors.

The data discussed herein includes published literature demonstrating an absence of early molecular changes in the rodent oral cavity and a meta-analysis of oral cavity tumors that expands on the IRIS meta-analyses of gastrointestinal cancers at other sites. Due to space limitations, this article is not meant to be an exhaustive review of all Cr(VI) literature but rather a focused summary of in vivo animal studies and human epidemiology data that directly informs why the rat oral cavity tumors should not be the basis of an oral CSF for Cr(VI).

Previously published animal data on Cr(VI) relevant to the oral cavity

Carcinogenicity of Cr(VI) in the oral cavity following drinking water exposures was demonstrated in F344 rats in the NTP (2008) chronic drinking water cancer bioassay. The NTP study authors stated that the tumors typically appeared to originate in the oral mucosa of the palate adjacent to the upper molar teeth (NTP 2008; Stout et al. 2009). An independent review of the NTP slides concluded that most tumors “appeared to arise from oral mucosa surrounding the molar teeth (gingival epithelium) and/or from the region of the buccal mucosal fold (above the maxillary molars or below the mandibular molars)” (Long 2012). The combined rat oral tumor incidence was significantly increased only in the highest treatment group (180 ppm Cr(VI) in drinking water) of both sexes; the dose–response on a mg/kg-day dose is shown in Fig. 1A (Thompson et al. 2018). Notably, the 95% lower confidence limit of the benchmark dose on the 10% extra risk (BMDL10) of oral tumors in rats is 4.4 mg/kg-day [US EPA (2024a, p. 4–45) derived similar BMDL10 values of 6.0 and 4.3 mg/kg-day for male and female rats, respectively] and estimates of adult human Cr(VI) exposure are 0.065 μg/kg/day (Health Canada 2016), indicating a margin of exposure (MOE) of 68,000, well in excess of the 10,000 that is widely regarded as indicating low concern for mutagenic carcinogens (EFSA 2005; Chepelev et al. 2023). Given the lack of evidence for genotoxicity in target tissues (see below), this MOE indicates low concern for carcinogenicity.

Given the apparent absence of nonneoplastic and preneoplastic lesions, molecular investigations into the MOA were conducted, including transcriptomic analyses and in vivo mutation analyses. Agilent whole-genome oligonucleotide microarray-based transcriptomic analyses in palate tissue were analyzed following 7 or 90 days of exposure to up to 180 ppm Cr(VI) in drinking water (Thompson et al. 2016). No statistically significant changes in differentially expressed genes were observed after 7 or 90 days of exposure using standard filters (/2/-fold change, ANOVA P < 0.05 with false discovery rate (FDR) corrected q < 0.05). Therefore, filters were relaxed (/1.5/-fold change, ANOVA P < 0.05 with no FDR), resulting in a small number of gene changes in each group that lacked dose–response concordances (Fig. 1B). The minimal gene changes precluded pathway enrichment analysis (Thompson et al. 2016). Similar analyses were conducted in the oral tissues of mice to compare molecular responses in rodents that did not develop oral tumors; however, minimal and non-dose concordant results were also observed (data not shown; see Thompson et al. 2016). Overall, the lack of transcriptional response in both species is consistent with the absence of nonneoplastic or preneoplastic lesions at NTP study doses and together indicates a lack of cellular toxicity or homeostatic perturbation.

The potential for mutagenicity in the oral cavity was examined using Big Blue transgenic F344 (TgF344) rats in a study design informed by OECD Test Guideline 488 (OECD 2022). Only 180 ppm Cr(VI) was investigated because it was the highest and only carcinogenic concentration (see Fig. 1A) in rats, and higher concentrations resulted in stomach ulceration (NTP 2007). Assessment of the ability of the Tg344 model to detect mutations in the palate/gingival and palate/buccal regions of interest was first demonstrated using a known oral mutagenic carcinogen, 4-nitroquinoline-1-oxide (Young et al. 2015). After confirming/qualifying the Tg344 model for detecting mutation in these tissues, a separate study measured mutant frequency (MF) following 28 days of exposure to 180 ppm Cr(VI) in drinking water (Fig. 1C) (Thompson et al. 2015). These results clearly indicate an absence of increased MF in 2 regions of the rat oral mucosa.

Taken together, the previously published animal data indicate an absence of phenotypic (histopathological) or molecular (transcriptomic or mutagenic) response to Cr(VI) following exposures up to 180 ppm Cr(VI) for 90 days. As such, the oral cavity tumors in rats are unlikely the result of early genotoxic events that might support a mutagenic MOA and derivation of a linear non-threshold CSF.

Human data

The US EPA (2024b) conducted its own meta-analysis of gastrointestinal cancers, which included oral cavity cancers as a stated outcome in its Population Exposure Comparison Outcome; however, results for oral cavity cancers were not provided (US EPA 2024b). We sought to fill this data gap by identifying the oral cancer data from the papers included in EPA’s analysis following its systematic review (US EPA 2024b). Using those studies, we extracted reported risk measures for oral cavity cancers, including counts, Standardized Incidence Ratios (SIRs), Standardized Mortality Ratios (SMRs), Odds Ratios (ORs), and Proportionate Mortality Ratios (PMRs) to conduct the meta-analysis. EPA’s methods for study selection are summarized in Supplementary Material S1.

Of the 28 studies used in EPA’s meta-analysis (see Supplementary Material S1), 24 reported SIRs or SMRs and were included in our meta-analysis. Three studies were excluded because they reported only PMRs (Silverstein et al. 1981; Veyalkin and Gerein 2006; Dalager et al. 1980), and 1 study was excluded because it only reported an OR (Garabrant and Wegman 1984). EPA analyzed ORs separately from SIRs and SMRs and excluded PMR results. None of the excluded studies reported a significantly increased risk measure, and each reported only 1 to 5 cases, which, if included, would not contribute significantly to the meta-relative risk. A total of 365 cases of oral cancer were counted among the 24 included studies, resulting in a robust sample size for this relatively uncommon cancer.

We applied both fixed and random effects models using Rstudio (version 2022.2.3.492). For the random effects meta-analysis, the DerSimonian–Laird method for between-study variance estimation was used. For both models, the meta-relative risks were statistically significantly decreased at 0.74 (CI: 0.68 to 0.81) for the fixed effect model and 0.81 (CI: 0.69 to 0.95) for the random effects model, with an I2 value for heterogeneity of 28% (Fig. 2). None of the studies included in the meta-analysis reported a significantly increased risk of oral cavity cancers (Fig. 2). These results further support the lack of an oral cavity cancer risk in humans exposed to Cr(VI).

Two earlier meta-analyses, which included oral cavity cancer, were also reviewed (Gatto et al. 2010; Deng et al. 2019), both of which used inclusion criteria that differed from those of EPA. Using a random effects model, Gatto et al. (2010) conducted a meta-analysis of 18 occupational epidemiology papers published from 1950 to 2009 and did not observe an increase in oral cavity cancers (meta-SMR: 1.02, CI: 0.77 to 1.34). Gatto et al. (2010) focused on industries with the highest levels of potential exposure, specifically chromate and pigment production, metal plating, ferrochromium production, and stainless steel welding.

By comparison, Deng et al. (2019) conducted a meta-analysis of studies published from 1985 to 2016 and, using a fixed effects model, reported increased incidence (meta-SIR: 1.3, CI: 1.1 to 1.5), but not mortality (meta-SMR: 0.91 CI: 0.75 to 1.1) for oral cavity cancer among worker cohorts. Deng et al. (2019) reported that the increased SIR was significant for cement workers and welders; however, only 4 of the SIR studies reporting oral cavity cancer included in Deng et al. (2019) were also included in the EPA meta-analysis, and none of the 4 reported a significantly increased oral cavity risk. Overall, EPA excluded 6 of the 16 SIR studies included in Deng et al. (2019), primarily because Deng et al. included multiple follow-up publications of the same cohort, thus double counting cases. Also relevant to the oral cavity meta-SIR, we uncovered several data extraction errors during our review. In short, the findings of Deng et al. (2019) regarding oral cavity cancer SIR results are not reproducible and are considered unreliable.

To further understand whether workers with evidence of oral cavity exposures to Cr(VI) experienced increased oral cavity cancer risk, we reviewed the findings from the 1953 US Public Health Service study of chromate production industry workers. Direct evidence of oral cavity exposures included reports and pictures of yellowish discolored teeth and tongues, indicating oral cavity tissue highly exposed to Cr(VI) (PHS 1953). This study included the evaluation of contemporaneous workers of 6 active US plants at that time and approximately 1,600 airborne samples for the purpose of studying the high rates of lung cancer observed in this industry (PHS 1953). Cr(VI) exposures ranged from <50 to >3,000 µg/m3, with average exposures of 200 to 300 µg/m3 reported in the 1950s from 2 of these plants, which were studied subsequently by others (Braver et al. 1985; Proctor et al. 2003). However, neither this report nor subsequent epidemiological studies of workers from 2 of the facilities studied since have indicated a significant increase in oral cavity cancer. Specifically, among Painesville, OH, chromate production workers, the oral cavity SMR was 0.67 (CI: 0.02 to 3.75, based on 1 case), and among the Baltimore, MD, workers, the oral cavity SMR was 1.20 (CI: 0.52 to 2.36, based on 8 cases) (Gatto et al. 2010).

Finally, regarding human evidence for Cr(VI) genotoxicity in the oral cavity of workers, US EPA (2024b) cited 3 studies reporting micronuclei (MN) formation in buccal cells of Cr(VI)-exposed workers (Benova et al. 2002; Dana Devi et al. 2004; Muller et al. 2022). The first 2 were rated as low confidence by the EPA due to methodological and confounding issues, and Muller et al. (2022), which applied current analytical methods and confirmed exposure to chromium using biomonitoring data, reported no difference in buccal cell MN counts among exposed workers and unexposed controls.

How other agencies have addressed the oral carcinogenicity of Cr(VI)

Since the release of the NTP (2008) cancer bioassay, agencies around the world have reviewed the available data for Cr(VI) and many have concluded that the intestinal tumors in mice are the result of a threshold MOA and set safety guidelines protective of precursor effects, rather than a CSF (Health Canada 2016; TCEQ 2016; FSCJ 2019; WHO 2020). Although we have previously summarized these agencies’ conclusions about the threshold MOA for intestinal tumors in mice (Thompson et al. 2023), Table 1 provides relevant quotes from these agencies regarding oral cavity tumors in rats. Overall, these agencies concluded that the weight of evidence (WOE) supports that the MOA for alimentary tract tumors in rodents is nongenotoxic and that protection against intestinal toxicity protects against the oral carcinogenicity of Cr(VI).

US EPA (2024a) concluded that the WOE supports that all tumors related to Cr(VI) exposure are the result of a mutagenic MOA and therefore assumed a linear dose–response and applied age-dependent adjustment factors to the oral CSF. The US EPA (2005) Guidelines for Carcinogen Risk Assessment state that linear low-dose extrapolation should be performed “when there are MOA data to indicate that the dose–response curve is expected to have a linear component below the POD,” such as with “agents that are DNA-reactive and have direct mutagenic activity”. Linear extrapolation is also EPA’s default approach when “the weight of evidence evaluation of all available data are insufficient to establish the mode of action for a tumor site and when scientifically plausible based on the available data” (emphasis added). The negative TGR results in the rat oral cavity tissues (Fig. 1C) provide strong support that the MOA for these tumors is not mutagenic, whereas the lack of transcriptomic responses and the nonlinear tumor dose–response in the oral cavity (Fig. 1A and B) do not support a default linear approach because it is not “scientifically plausible based on the available data”. The robust meta-analysis of workers exposed to Cr(VI) herein, including data from 24 cohort studies and 364 cases, which found a significant decrease in oral cavity tumors (Fig. 2), and the studies of workers with extremely high exposures (PHS 1953; Gatto et al. 2010), wherein no oral cavity cancer risk was observed, indicate either a lack of concordance with the rat tumor data or a requirement of even higher exposure.

Conclusion

Multiple lines of evidence from animal data support that oral cavity tumors in rats are not caused by a mutagenic MOA, and the use of a linear low-dose extrapolation is not scientifically supportable. Epidemiological data do not support a concern for oral cavity cancer following even very high levels of Cr(VI). We have previously shown that low-dose toxicokinetics in the mouse small intestine can be quantified using sophisticated Cr(VI) PBPK models capable of predicting target tissue dose (Kirman et al. 2017). These models have been used to derive threshold-based cancer toxicity criteria for Cr(VI) based on precursor lesions in the mouse intestine (Health Canada 2016; Thompson et al. 2018) and can be used to derive similar values using linear low-dose extrapolations based on the mouse small intestine tumor data for agencies with policies requiring use of linear approaches (Thompson et al. 2023). However, agencies seeking to derive or adopt existing oral toxicity criteria for Cr(VI) using oral cavity data are encouraged to carefully review the evidence cited herein. Based on the totality of animal and human data, oral cavity tumors should not be used for human health risk assessment.
