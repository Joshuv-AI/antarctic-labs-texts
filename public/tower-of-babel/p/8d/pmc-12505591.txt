The mitochondrial genome sequence of the Bornean orang-utan (Pongo pygmaeus) malaria parasite, Plasmodium pitheci

Taufiq Purna Nugraha, Sugiyono Saputra, Arif Nurkanto, Agus Fahroni, Agus Irwanto, Din Syafruddin, Iskandar Alisyahbana Adnan, Jessica R. Saragih, Farah Novita Coutrier, Wuryantari Setiadi, Neil F. ABSTRACT
This study presents the complete mitochondrial genome sequence of Plasmodium pitheci, a malaria parasite that infects Bornean orang-utans (Pongo pygmaeus). The mitochondrial genome spans 5871 bp in length encoding all essential mitochondrial genes in a conserved arrangement typical of Plasmodium species. Using Bayesian phylogenetic analyses, the evolutionary relationships of P. pitheci with other Plasmodium species that infect non-human primates were investigated. Findings of this study confirm that the previously identified Plasmodium sp. VM and VS and Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade A and clade B from orang-utans correspond to P. pitheci. However, the taxonomic classification of Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade C remains unresolved. The detection of P. pitheci infections in orang-utans across Borneo provides insights into the parasite’s geographic distribution. This genomic information contributes to a deeper understanding of P. pitheci and its evolutionary relationship with other malaria parasites. Comparative genomics with other Plasmodium species, including those infecting humans, will deepen the general understanding of malaria's evolutionary pathways. Furthermore, the availability of the complete mitochondrial genome of this parasite provides a foundation for developing species-specific molecular diagnostic tools. These tools will improve malaria diagnosis in orang-utans, assist in conservation efforts, and potentially aid in zoonotic malaria control strategies for humans in regions where cross-species transmission is a concern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05487-3.

Background

Malaria is caused by a diverse group of parasites belonging to the genus Plasmodium which includes over 250 species that infect a wide range of hosts, including humans and non-human primates (NHP) [1, 2]. Plasmodium parasites are considered to have originated from avian hosts and switched to mammal hosts between 13 and 78 million years ago [2]. NHP Plasmodium phylogenetic studies have given place to a number of evolutionary theories pointing to these species as the possible origin of human malaria parasites [3–5]. Unfortunately, while phylogenetic and evolutionary studies have focussed mostly on Plasmodium species from African NHP, same studies are still lacking for the Asian NHP species [6, 7]. Amongst the Asian NHP species, two species morphologically identified in the twentieth century, have been found to infect orang-utans (Pongo spp.), the only great ape species in Asia: Plasmodium pitheci and Plasmodium sylvaticum [8–10].

Evolutionary studies have demonstrated that orang-utan malaria parasite lineages may date to as early as 1.8 million years ago [7]. Molecular studies conducted in orang-utan samples have described possibly up to three Plasmodium genetic lineages [6, 11]. Though the previously described mitochondrial (mt) DNA molecular sequences [6] were suspected to be from P. pitheci, a completed genome sequence with validated morphological association to this specific species had not been obtained. Consequently, until present, orang-utan Plasmodium species have not been molecularly characterized, and no molecular probes are available for PCR-based and other studies that would require species specific sequences.

The Bornean orang-utan (Pongo pygmaeus), a global conservation icon, is classified as critically endangered due to declining population numbers [12, 13]. Orang-utans, both in the wild (“in situ”) as well as wild rescued orang-utans at Orangutan Rehabilitation Centres (ORCs) (“ex-situ”) across Borneo have long been found to harbour malaria infections, including morphologically identified P. pitheci [9, 14–17] which was discovered in 1907 [8]. The clinical and parasitological aspects of natural infections of P. pitheci in Bornean orang-utans have recently been documented at one of these ORCs, describing malaria symptoms similar to human malaria [17]. This study revealed the presence of endemic P. pitheci infecting up to 68% of the orang-utans, and causing moderate to severe illness in up to 14% of infected individuals, providing evidence that this parasite can be pathogenic in orang-utans as their natural host [17, 18]. Furthermore, the study investigated aspects of orang-utan malaria immunity which seemed to be influenced by age and exposure, suggesting the existence of a naturally acquired non-sterilizing immunity resembling that in humans—thereby similarly affecting malaria infection, diagnosis and treatment outcomes [18]. As such, interruption of natural exposure to malaria in orang-utans has been considered to impair protection from serious illness based on acquired immunity [18]. Changes in behaviour and ecology of orang-utans in disturbed/human-modified habitats or due to effects of climate change which may affect malaria exposure could contribute to an increased risk of potential detrimental malaria health effects in their populations. Furthermore, some Bornean orang-utan populations are considered to be at serious risk [12]. Conservation strategies for orang-utan populations might need to include consideration of vulnerability to P. pitheci, its treatment in ORCs, and also possibly prior to release into the wild. Therefore, increasing understanding of the transmission dynamics and pathology of this Plasmodium species in both the wild and in ORCs would be essential to the protection of this NHP species.

In recent years, there is a growing concern regarding the potential zoonotic transmission of NHP malaria species to humans [19], particularly since the geographic distribution of NHP Plasmodium species overlaps with areas of human malaria endemicity [2, 20]. Evidence indicates that NHP Plasmodium species have crossed species barriers, acted as reservoirs, and zoonotically infect humans [21–24]. Over the past few decades, Plasmodium knowlesi, a species that naturally infects macaque species, has emerged as a significant zoonotic threat in South East (SE) Asia, becoming a global public health concern [25, 26]. This parasite is now recognized as the fifth Plasmodium species capable of infecting humans [27], with documented infections in both monkeys and humans [28]. Similarly, the anthroponotic potential of human malaria species infecting NHPs has also been reported [2, 4]. The documented cases of malaria in orang-utans not only highlight the health risks for these primates but also raise concerns about the broader implications for human health—especially with the increasing human-NHP interface [19]. Monitoring malaria infections in orang-utans becomes especially important when considering their close proximity to humans—towards the protection of both species.

This study expands on the morphological identification of P. pitheci conducted by Sanchez et al. [17] in blood samples collected from infected orang-utans, with the molecular characterization of the P. pitheci mitochondrial genome. In addition, this study includes the comparison of sequences from different sites across Kalimantan, Indonesia and Sabah, Malaysia, where wild Bornean orang-utans are distributed.

Methods

Genome sequencing

The mt genome sequence of P. pitheci was obtained in two stages.

Stage 1: Assembly of raw reads from malaria infected orang-utans

Genomic DNA extraction: Genomic DNA (gDNA) was obtained from blood samples from orang-utans infected with Plasmodium morphologically identified as P. pitheci using the MagAttract l kit (QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen) was used to estimate gDNA concentration.

Library construction, barcoding and sequencing: Approximately 332 ng of processed gDNA from each sample was used to construct a sequencing library with the ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK109, Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Barcoding was performed using the EXP-NBD104 and EXP-NBD114 kits (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Sequencing was carried out on a MinION FlowCell R9.4.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Raw reads basecalling: Base calling using high accuracy mode to get single reads accuracy as high as possible (~ 97%) was performed with guppy basecaller v6.0.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) on the outputted raw sequence 47 Gb (Fast5) file resulting in 2.7 Gb (Fastq) of basecalled sequence.

The minimum nanopore quality threshold (Q) was set at 7. Parameter used for basecalling included -x auto -c dna_r9.4.1_450bps_hac.cfg –num_callers 4 –gpu_runners_per_device 8 –chunks_per_runner 1024 –chunk_size 1000 –compress_fastq –min_qscore 7. Statistical evaluation of the Fastq file was conducted using NanoStat v1.5.0 [29].

De novo assembly and annotation: Assembly was performed using Flye assembler v2.9 [30] with four iterations of polishing. Subsequently, further polishing was performed with Medaka v1.4.3 (https://nanoporetech.com) using the r941_min_high_g360 model. Assembly statistics were calculated using QUAST v5.1.0 (https://quast.sourceforge.net/) (in supplementary file). The resulted draft assembly was annotated for mt genome content using GeSeq [31] (utilising Plasmodium mt genome references available on NCBI. The resulting annotated fragmented Plasmodium mt genome was further analysed with BLASTx (NCBI) for homolog proteins. The cytB from the GeSeq annotation was used for BLASTx calculations, with refseq_protein as the database and organism limited to Plasmodium (taxid:5820).

Stage 2. Sequencing and assembly of the complete P. pitheci mitochondrial genome

Primers previously used by Pacheco et al. [6] were applied on all 23 DNA samples mixed with LongAmp® Taq 2X Master Mix (NEB) to amplify the ~ 5.8 kb genome (Fig. 2). The PCR conditions began with an initial partial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min. This was followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, and annealing/extension at 68 °C for 7 min. The process concluded with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min.

Nanopore-generated sequences were assembled as before with additional mapping targeting only Plasmodium mt genome. Raw reads were mapped with Minimap2 [32] to reference mt genomes of Plasmodium species (from Pongo clades only). Sorting and extraction of mapped reads were performed with Samtools [33]. To ensure uniform coverage, aligned reads were subsampled to a maximum of 250 × coverage using Rasusa [34]. These targeted and subsampled mapped reads were then de novo assembled for each genome construction.

Genome annotation: A consensus sequence of the P. pitheci mt genome, generated from all 23 individual genomes using Seqman (DNASTAR), was used for annotation. The mt genome was annotated with GeSeq, a specialized tool for genome annotation [31].

Plasmodium pitheci mt-COI sequence generation: A 670 bp region of the mt cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (mt-COI) gene was amplified using primers PlasmoCOI-FDB 5ʹ ATACAAATTGTAATCATAAAACTTTAGG 3ʹ and PlasmoCOI-RDB 5ʹ ACTTCAGGATGTCCAAAAAACCA3ʹ [35]. The PCR amplicons were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and directly sequenced using the BigDyeTM Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit on a 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Phylogenetic trees

The optimal model for sequence data sets was selected via jModelTest-2.1.10 [36, 37] based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Bayesian inference was employed to construct phylogenetic trees for both whole mitochondrial genomes and mt-COI sequences, using the General Time Reversible (GTR) model with gamma-distributed rate variation and a proportion of invariant sites (GTR + G + I) in MrBayes version 3.2.7an [38], implemented within Geneious Prime® 2023.0.4 (www.geneious.com), using a chain length of 1,000,000, a subsampling frequency of 200, and a burn-in of 100,000. The resulting consensus tree, with posterior probabilities representing the statistical support for each clade, was visualised in FigTree version 1.4.4 [39].

Geographically diverse mt-COI sequences were obtained from multiple locations across Kalimantan (Fig. 1), comprising: (1) all 23 samples utilised for obtaining the P. pitheci mitochondrial sequence, (2) sequences (n = 145) from malaria-infected orang-utans at the Nyaru Menteng ORC, Central Kalimantan, (3) sequences (n = 26) from individual malaria infected orang-utans from Samboja Lestari ORC, East Kalimantan, and published sequences from GenBank from, (4) Orangutan Care Center and Quarantine (OCC&Q) in Pasir Panjang, Central Kalimantan [6], (5) the Sepilok ORC (SORC) in Sabah, Malaysia, and (6) a satellite facility of the Sabah Wildlife Department in Sabah, Malaysia [11]. Only mt-COI sequences (from the mt-COI study or from GenBank) that mapped to the P. pitheci assembled genome with over 99% identity were included in this analysis (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Haplotype analysis and genetic divergence: Haplotype analysis was performed using DnaSP [40] on 48 mitochondrial DNA sequences which included 23 sequences obtained in this study, and 25 sequences downloaded from GenBank: 5 sequences from Pacheco et al. [6] and 20 sequences from Muehlenbein [11]. The Tajima’s Neutrality Test and genetic distance between Plasmodium was calculated using MEGAX software [41].

Results

Sequencing assembly and annotation

Sequencing and assembly resulted in 23 whole mt genome assemblies representing infections in 20 orang-utans infected with malaria parasite [17]. There were two P. pitheci infected blood samples obtained from different time points from three orang-utans each—Opan, Udin and Utu. These were labelled Opan1 and Opan2, Udin1 and Udin2, and Utu1 and Utu2. Assembly demonstrated that the infections from each orang-utan had the same sequence (100% identical) and though each one was utilized in the phylogenetic trees, only a single representative consensus sequence (from each) was submitted to the DNA Data bank of Japan (DDBJ). The entire genome P. pitheci was 5871 bp with a GC content of 35%. Structural characteristics of the mitochondrial genome

The mt genome structure of P. pitheci (Fig. 2) was conserved, similar to the gene sequence structures of other Plasmodium species and consisting of five Coding DNA Sequences (CDS) and 13 ribosomal RNAs (rRNA) [42].

Phylogenetic tree

Bayesian phylogenetic trees were constructed with two types of datasets—whole mitochondrial genomes and the mt-COI sequences—both showing similar topologies (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Data demonstrates that P. pitheci samples form a monophyletic group along with another clade also identified in orang-utans—Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade B [11].

Haplotype analysis

Given that blood samples were collected from multiple orang-utans across Kalimantan, a diverse range of Plasmodium haplotypes was expected to be present. To assess this diversity, a haplotype analysis was conducted using DnaSP v6 [40] on 23 P. pitheci sequences generated in this study, along with 25 sequences obtained from GenBank. The GenBank sequences originated from Central Kalimantan [6] and Sabah [11].

Haplotype analysis among the 48 sequences (Supplementary Table 2) identified 27 distinct haplotypes (H1 to H27). The majority of samples from West Kalimantan (e.g., P. pitheci 01, 02, 05) were associated with haplotype H₂, suggesting that this is the most prevalent haplotype in that population. In contrast, sequences from Central Kalimantan and Sabah exhibited greater haplotype diversity, indicating more complex parasite population structures in these regions.

To further explore population-level variation, Tajima’s Neutrality Test was performed on all 48 sequences. The dataset contained 113 segregating sites, corresponding to a proportion of segregating sites (Ps) of 0.0196. The estimated Theta (Θ), based on the number of segregating sites, was 0.0044. Nucleotide diversity (π), representing the average number of pairwise differences per site, was low at 0.0032. Tajima’s D was calculated at − 0.959, suggesting an excess of low-frequency polymorphisms potentially indicative of population expansion or purifying selection. Haplotype diversity (Hd) was high, at 0.925 (Table 2). Discussion

In this study, the complete mitochondrial DNA sequence of Plasmodium pitheci from Bornean orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus) was successfully constructed. The molecular sequencing of previously morphologically described P. pitheci infections [17] enabled the associations of sequences generated to this species. Initial sequence efforts were unable to amplify enough DNA for whole genome sequencing, and an adaptive approach utilising species-specific primers allowed for the generation of the mitochondrial genomes of P. pitheci. The close relationship between these genomes point to a possible founder effect based on geography—all whole mt genomes were isolated from a single location and all sequences were > 99% identical with only a few SNPs separating individual infections. Whole genome sequencing of these (and other) P. pitheci samples would allow for a greater understanding of population level Plasmodium diversity, and the possible impacts of movement or migration on malaria infections as well as translocation and release of rescued orang-utans.

Plasmodium silvaticum described by Peters et al. [9], another Plasmodium species of orang-utans, was not detected morphologically in any of the samples from West Kalimantan analysed in the whole mt genome sequencing, opening the question as to whether this species is distributed in the same areas as P. pitheci. While multi-species infections have commonly been reported in African great apes [20, 21, 43, 44], and have also been reported in orang-utans [9, 15] none of the YIARI samples indicated the presence of any other species of malaria, either when morphologically or molecularly analysed. Other authors have also reported morphologically identified mono-infections of P. pitheci in orang-utans without P. silvaticum being present [15, 16]. The existence of Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade B and C [11], which were not found on any of the sequenced samples, indicate the possible presence of P. silvaticum or any other yet to be known Plasmodium species. The association of these sequences with morphology would be essential to identifying them to species.

In Plasmodium species, the mitochondrial genome exhibits remarkable conservation across the genus. It typically encodes three protein-coding genes [cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) and subunit 3, (cox3), and cytochrome b (cytb)] involved in oxidative phosphorylation, along with small and large subunits of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. This conservation is crucial for maintaining the functionality of the parasite's mitochondrial electron transport chain, which is essential for energy production during its complex life cycle. The observed similarity in gene sequences between different Plasmodium species suggests a strong evolutionary pressure to preserve these vital functions. Studies on mitochondrial genomes of various Plasmodium species, such as Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, and Plasmodium knowlesi, have consistently shown this conserved structure and gene content as seen in the P. pitheci genome presented here [45, 46]. Given that mutations in mitochondrial genes are often deleterious, the evolutionary stability of these sequences may reflect their critical role in parasite survival and adaptability.

The conserved nature of the mitochondrial genome also makes it a valuable tool for phylogenetic studies. It enables researchers to trace evolutionary relationships among Plasmodium species, providing insights into their speciation and geographical spread. Despite the genetic stability of the mitochondrial genome, variations in non-coding regions and synonymous mutations within coding regions may still provide useful markers for distinguishing between closely related species as well as for tracking drug resistance patterns [47].

Bayesian phylogenetic analyses were used to investigate its taxonomic status and relationships with other primate Plasmodium species. Phylogenetic tree reconstruction and genetic divergence analysis of P. pitheci provides and confirms crucial insights into the evolutionary relationships among malaria parasites infecting primates. Phylogenetic analysis of the whole mitochondrial genome demonstrates that P. pitheci is about 99% identical to previously described as P. sp VM and P. sp. VS [6] and to P. sp. Pongo clade A and P. sp. Pongo clade B [11]. Close genetic relationship is clearly reflected in the Bayesian phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2), where clade P. pitheci including P. sp. VM and P. sp. VS, P. sp. Pongo clade A, and P. sp. Pongo clade B form a strongly supported monophyletic group. These results reinforce the hypothesis that these lineages share a recent common ancestor and may currently be undergoing active speciation or diversification. Plasmodium sp. Pongo A and B clades may represent geographically structured variants of P. pitheci.

In contrast, Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade C exhibits divergence levels from the P. pitheci and Plasmodium sp Pongo clade (0.0214–0.0229) that are comparable to those between P. pitheci and other well-established species, such as Plasmodium inui (0.0194–0.0208). This level of genetic distinction—paired with its distinct phylogenetic placement—may represent other Plasmodium species, e.g. P. sylvaticum [9, 10] or a new unknown species. Morphological identification of these parasites along with molecular data that includes whole genome sequencing would enable the identification of these samples to Plasmodium species and further elaborate on these relationships being either adaptive radiation or geographic speciation [11]. Muehlenbein’s study identified Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade A, B and C as distinct lineages infecting orang-utans (Pongo pygmaeus). By confirming that P. pitheci belongs to Plasmodium sp. identified by Pacheco et al. [6] as well as Plasmodium sp. clade A and Plasmodium sp. clade B identified by Muehlenbein et al. [11] provide genetic evidence suggesting that these parasites may have a unique evolutionary history, distinct from human-infecting Plasmodium species. The phylogenetic tree analysis also aligns with broader research on the diversity of malaria parasites infecting great apes, evidence confirming that orang-utans harbour malaria parasites that are genetically distinct from those infecting humans and other African primates [6, 48].

This supports the view that Southeast Asia serves as an important reservoir for diverse Plasmodium lineages, some of which may provide key insights into the origins and evolutionary pressures shaping the genus as a whole. Overall, the identification of P. pitheci as Plasmodium sp. VM, Plasmodium sp. VS [6], Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade A and Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade B [11] reinforces the need for further studies on NHP malaria, with a special focus on Asian species. Understanding these relationships not only deepens our knowledge of Plasmodium evolution but also has potential implications for the zoonotic transmission of malaria and the broader ecological dynamics of the parasite in primate hosts.

The Bayesian Tree obtained from P. pitheci mt-COI sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1a and b) obtained from across Kalimantan demonstrate similar tree topology as above. Since only those that were greater than 99% identical to the P. pitheci genome were included, all sequences mapped to the P. pitheci, Plasmodium sp. VM, Plasmodium sp. VS or Plasmodium sp. Pongo clade A branch.

In a haplotype analysis of P. pitheci mitochondrial genomes from ORC-based orang-utan populations, the identification of 27 distinct haplotypes with haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.925) across 48 samples suggests a substantial genetic diversity within the parasite population. Such variation is remarkable given the relatively small sample size. This diversity could be attributed to several factors including the local introduction and consequent concentration of multiple parasite lineages from across Indonesia based on the origin of these rescued orang-utans—it is notable that samples used in this study originated from orang-utan populations belonging to the sub-species of Pongo pygmaeus wurmbii and P. p. morio distributed across the Southern part of West Kalimantan and Central Kalimantan, and East Kalimantan (Indonesia) and Sabah (Malaysia) respectively-, and the potential for frequent recombination events within the parasite population with localized ORC-based transmission [49, 50]. Micro-evolutionary processes such as genetic drift or past bottleneck events in isolated populations may also shape this genetic structure [1]. Additionally, the observed negative Tajima’s D value indicates that the population may not be evolving under neutral expectations, suggesting recent population expansion or positive selection [51, 52]. This is supported by the combination of high haplotype diversity and low nucleotide diversity—a pattern typically seen in expanding populations—characterized by numerous distinct haplotypes with minimal nucleotide differences, consistent with recent divergence from a common ancestor, bottleneck recovery, or selective.

Comparing the mitochondrial haplotype diversity in Plasmodium from orang-utans with that found in human malaria parasites, such as P. falciparum and P. vivax, reveals similar patterns of diversity in regions where transmission is endemic [53]. The fact that this local orang-utan population also harbours diverse parasite haplotypes suggests that primates in Southeast Asia could serve as important reservoirs for sustaining malaria diversity in the region. Furthermore, the identification of these haplotypes provides an essential baseline for understanding the evolutionary dynamics of Plasmodium in orang-utans and may have broader implications for conservation efforts. It also raises questions about the potential for zoonotic spill-over, given the genetic diversity and adaptability of the parasite in non-human primates.

Non-human primate malarias are important from an evolutionary perspective as predecessors of human malaria parasites as well as for their zoonotic potential. This draft genome sequence provides a foundation for comparative genomics with other Plasmodium species enhancing our understanding of the evolutionary pathways and diversity of malaria parasites in orangutans and other primates, including humans. Furthermore, it will facilitate the development of diagnostic tools specifically for P. pitheci infections, improving the accuracy of malaria diagnosis and treatment strategies in Bornean orangutans, a critically endangered species.
