SARS-CoV-2 Breakthrough Infections and Reinfections in Patients with Severe Mental Illness during Three Omicron Outbreaks
Authors: Chih-Chia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with severe mental illness (SMI) in psychiatric facilities face unique vulnerabilities to severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 infection. We conducted an study in a female-only, closed psychiatric ward from February 2020 to December 2023, documenting three coronavirus disease 2019 outbreaks during BA.2, BA.5, and XBB-predominant periods. During the first outbreak, 98.0% of residents were infected, including 97.7% of vaccinated individuals. The second outbreak resulted in 28.6% infections, with 25.5% experiencing presumed reinfection. The third outbreak affected 78.0% of residents. All infections were mild with no hospitalizations or deaths. These findings highlight the vulnerability of institutionalized SMI populations.

Patients with severe mental illness (SMI) face heightened vulnerability to infectious diseases, including coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [12]. This vulnerability stems from multiple factors: biological alterations affecting immune function [34], potential immunomodulatory effects of psychotropic medications [5], cognitive or behavioral challenges that limit adherence to preventive measures, and structural factors in long-term psychiatric facilities, including shared living spaces and close quarters that facilitate transmission [6]. While COVID-19 vaccines have demonstrated effectiveness in preventing severe disease, evidence regarding their protective effects in institutionalized psychiatric populations remains limited [789]. Taiwan maintained low COVID-19 case numbers through early 2022 via strict border controls. The emergence of Omicron variants in April 2022 led to increased community transmission, with the first outbreak in a Taiwanese psychiatric facility occurring in May 2022. We report three consecutive COVID-19 outbreaks in a closed psychiatric ward, each during periods when different Omicron subvariants predominated, to describe infection patterns and outbreak dynamics in this vulnerable population.

All residents of the ward during the study period (February 2020 to December 2023) were included. Patients were diagnosed with schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder, or major neurocognitive disorder with behavioral disturbance according to DSM-5 criteria. During the three outbreaks, 54 individual patients were admitted to the ward at various times. The cohort was exclusively female due to the institutional ward organization.

As part of Taiwan’s national vaccination program, residents received prioritized access to COVID-19 vaccines. Among 54 patients admitted during the study period, 48 (88.9%) received multiple doses, while 6 (11.1%) remained unvaccinated. Vaccine types included ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, mRNA-1273, NVX-CoV2373, and bivalent mRNA-1273.214 and mRNA-1273.222 boosters. The individual who remained uninfected during the first outbreak received the fourth dose on July 22, 2022. Individuals infected in the first outbreak but uninfected in the second outbreak received the fourth dose between October 17 and October 24, 2022. Those infected in both the first and second outbreaks received the fourth dose between December 29, 2022 and January 10, 2023. The fifth dose was administered on May 3, 2023. Three distinct outbreaks occurred: June 21–28, 2022; September 25–29, 2022; and July 16-27, 2023 (Fig. 1). Variant classification was based on national epidemiological surveillance data during periods when BA.2, BA.5, and XBB were predominant in Taiwan, respectively, rather than on individual genomic sequencing. When any patient or staff member displayed COVID-19 symptoms or tested positive, comprehensive testing was implemented for all ward residents, either immediately or at least every three days until all tested negative. Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection was defined as a positive result on the PANBIO™ COVID-19 Antigen Self-Test (Abbott, Sejong, Korea) or the cobas SARS-CoV-2 reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assay (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Breakthrough infection was defined as a positive test ≥14 days after vaccination completion. Presumed reinfection was defined as two positive tests separated by ≥90 days. Without genomic sequencing, we could not definitively distinguish true reinfection from prolonged viral shedding; however, the 90-day interval and distinct outbreak timing support reinfection.

De-identified data included demographics (age, body weight, and height), psychiatric diagnoses, psychotropic medications, and physical comorbidities. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a documented diagnosis with ongoing treatment. Liver disease included chronic hepatitis (4 hepatitis B virus carriers, 1 hepatitis B/C virus co-infection); none had progressed to cirrhosis. Renal disease included nephrotic syndrome (n=2) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 3 (n=1). Two patients had diabetes insipidus. No patients had cancer. One patient with systemic lupus erythematosus received chronic immunosuppressive therapy.

Descriptive statistics characterized outbreaks and patient characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test; continuous variables using Mann-Whitney test. All tests were two-sided with significance at P<0.050 (version 12, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

The ward remained free of infection during the initial pandemic waves but subsequently experienced three distinct outbreaks following the emergence of Omicron variants (Fig. 1). During the first outbreak (BA.2-predominated period), infection occurred in 49/50 (98.0%) residents within 8 days. Among 44 vaccinated residents (all with 3 doses), 43 (97.7%) had breakthrough infection. All 6 unvaccinated residents were infected. Three months later, 14/49 residents (28.6%) were infected over 5 days during the second outbreak, which occurred in the BA.5-predominant period. This included 12 presumed reinfections among 47 previously infected individuals (25.5%) and 2 primary infections. Among 47 residents with prior infection, those with presumed reinfection (n=12) had lower vaccination rates (66.7% vs. 97.1%, P=0.012), lower body mass index (BMI) (19.4 vs. 22.9 kg/m2, P=0.021), and higher diabetes mellitus prevalence (50.0% vs. 8.6%, P=0.005) compared to those without reinfection (Supplementary Table 1). Among 44 vaccinated residents, 10 had breakthrough infection. These patients had a lower rate of prior infection (80.0% vs. 100%, P=0.048), lower body weight (44.4 vs. 53.8 kg, P=0.022), lower BMI (19.5 vs. 23.0 kg/m2, P=0.018), and higher diabetes mellitus prevalence (40.0% vs. 5.9%, P=0.018) compared to those without breakthrough infection. After a ten-month gap, the third outbreak occurred (XBB-predominant period), infecting 39/50 (78.0%) residents over a 12-day period. Among 48 residents with prior infection, 37 (77.1%) were presumed reinfected: 26 experienced a second infection and 11 a third. By the third outbreak, vaccination coverage was nearly universal (90% with 5 doses). Among vaccinated residents, 75.6% (34/45) experienced breakthrough infection. All 5 unvaccinated residents (100%) were also infected. This difference was not statistically significant. Notably, residents with two prior infections had a higher rate of a third infection than those with one prior infection (P=0.048) (Supplementary Table 2). All infections were asymptomatic or mild. Most symptomatic patients presented with mild upper respiratory symptoms (fever, cough, sore throat, and rhinorrhea). No hospitalizations or deaths occurred. In the first outbreak, 43/49 cases were diagnosed by rapid antigen test, and 6 by RT-PCR. In the second outbreak, all 14 cases were diagnosed by RT-PCR. In the third outbreak, 29/39 cases were diagnosed by rapid antigen test, 10 by RT-PCR. Diabetes control was adequate among affected patients. Median HbA1c levels were 6.35% (interquartile range [IQR], 5.65–6.98%), 6.05% (IQR, 5.65–6.80%), and 6.25% (IQR, 5.5–6.8%) for patients with diabetes in the first, second, and third outbreaks, respectively. BMI did not differ significantly between patients with and without diabetes (first outbreak: 22.21 vs. 21.88 kg/m2, P=0.833; second outbreak: 22.21 vs. 21.88 kg/m2, P=0.838; third outbreak:22.17 vs. 21.21 kg/m2, P=0.477).

This study documents three COVID-19 outbreaks in a closed psychiatric ward during periods when BA.2, BA.5, and XBB subvariants predominated. Despite high vaccination coverage and prior exposure, attack rates remained substantial. All infections were mild, with no hospitalizations or deaths. These findings underscore the unique vulnerabilities of institutionalized populations with severe mental illness [10].

The nearly universal infection rate (98.0%) during the first outbreak occurred despite 88% of residents having received at least three vaccine doses, highlighting persistent vulnerability in this population. Several factors may contribute: SMI patients may exhibit impaired immune responses to vaccination [341112], psychotropic agents affect immunological markers and SARS-CoV-2 binding [5], behavioral challenges reduce adherence to preventive measures, and closed ward environments facilitate rapid transmission. The three outbreaks demonstrated distinct patterns: rapid widespread transmission initially, lower attack rate (29.0%) three months later despite 25.5% presumed reinfections, then resurgence (78.0%) ten months later suggesting waning immunity or XBB immune evasion [13]. Notably, residents with two prior infections had higher third infection rates, raising questions about individual immune differences. Similar patterns have been observed in elderly long-term care populations [14].

Diabetes mellitus and low BMI were risk factors in the second outbreak, with two-thirds of diabetic residents experiencing presumed reinfection. These associations suggest specific susceptibilities in the psychiatric population, though the small sample size warrants cautious interpretation. The limited sample size (n=54) and very small number of unvaccinated residents (n=6) limit statistical power and preclude definitive conclusions about vaccine effectiveness. The female-only cohort limits generalizability. Variant assignment was based on surveillance data rather than individual sequencing. Without sequencing, definitive distinction between reinfection and prolonged viral shedding is not possible, although the 90-day interval and distinct outbreak timing make prolonged shedding unlikely. The universal mildness of cases prevented assessment of vaccine effectiveness against severe outcomes, and immunological measurements were unavailable to directly assess immune responses. The small number of patients with immunosuppressive conditions (n=2: one with CKD stage 3, one with systemic lupus erythematosus on immunosuppressants) precluded analysis of immunosuppression as a confounder. While the primary goal of vaccination was achieved, as the majority of COVID-19 cases remained mild, these findings suggest that vaccination alone was insufficient to prevent all infections among patients in SMI facilities. Comprehensive infection control strategies should address multiple levels, including tailored vaccination approaches such as more frequent boosters, enhanced infection prevention education, improved ventilation and cohorting capacity, and careful consideration of the psychosocial impacts of containment measures. Future research should involve larger studies with immunological assessments to optimize vaccination strategies for this vulnerable population.
