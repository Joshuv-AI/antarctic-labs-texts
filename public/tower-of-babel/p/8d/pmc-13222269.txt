Evaluation of Frequency, Severity and Spectrum of Cognitive Impairment and Its Associated Risk Factors in Elderly Individuals with Diabetes in India: A Prospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Anu Gupta, Yashdeep Gupta, Vineeta Garg, Roopa Rajan, Venugopalan Y Vishnu, Nikhil Tandon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCognitive impairment in type 2 diabetes mellitus affects self-management of the disease, creating a vicious cycle of worsening cognitive and glycaemic control. Low- and middle-income countries are underrepresented in research focusing on this association. This study aimed to evaluate the frequency, severity, spectrum and risk factors associated with cognitive impairment in elderly individuals with diabetes in India.MethodsElderly participants aged 60 years and above underwent a comprehensive clinical, biochemical and neuropsychological evaluation (cognitive concerns, neuropsychological testing and functional activity assessments).ResultsA total of 832 participants were included: 698 with diabetes and 134 without diabetes. Participants with diabetes had a mean age of 65.2 ± 4.5 years and average HbA1c level of 7.8 ± 1.8%. Cognitive impairment (NIA-AA 2018 criteria for cognitive staging) was identified in 75 participants (9.2%), with a higher prevalence in the group with diabetes (10.7%) compared to the group without diabetes (1.5%) [adjusted odds ratio (OR) 9.1 (95% CI 2.1–39.4)]. Gender-based subgroup analysis did not reach statistical significance [female participants, OR 3.7 (95% CI 0.76–18.4), p = 0.11]. Risk factors for cognitive impairment included increasing age [OR 1.1 (95% CI 1.02–1.14)] and unemployment [OR 2.5 (95% CI 1.2–5.4)], while protective factors included higher education [OR 0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.92)] and physical activity [OR 0.99 (95% CI 0.99–0.99)].ConclusionCognitive impairment affects one in ten elderly Indians with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Factors affecting cognitive reserve are a crucial, modifiable determinant of cognitive function in these individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03549-9.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Cognitive impairment, Elderly Indians, MCI

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Diabetes and dementia represent significant contributors to the chronic illness burden in aging populations. Globally, 20% of individuals aged 65 and older, approximately 135.6 million people, are living with diabetes, a figure projected to rise to 195.2 million by 2030 and 276.2 million by 2045 [1]. Similarly, the prevalence of dementia increases markedly with age, currently affecting around 55 million people worldwide, with estimates suggesting this number could reach 153 million by 2050 [2].

Over the past decade, the recognition of cognitive dysfunction as a comorbidity of type 2 diabetes mellitus has gained substantial attention. Cognitive dysfunction in diabetes spans from diabetes-related subtle cognitive deficits to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, due to vascular and/or Alzheimer’s disease. Research indicates that the incidence of dementia is nearly double in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus compared to those without [3]. MCI is reported in nearly one in two or three individuals with diabetes [4, 5], with executive functions (critical for goal setting, motivation and adaptability in daily routines) being disproportionately affected. A decline in executive functions in people with diabetes occurs at a rate six times faster than normal aging [6], which can severely impair diabetes self-management skills, creating a vicious cycle where cognitive impairment exacerbates further cognitive decline. Studies have also shown that diabetes accelerates the progression of MCI to Alzheimer’s dementia within the first year of MCI diagnosis [7].

Most cohort studies and meta-analyses examining the relationship between diabetes and cognition have been conducted in high-income countries (HICs) or East Asia, particularly China [4–6]. However, risk associations, sociodemographic factors and cultural influences vary across regions, underscoring the need for research that promotes equity and diversity. India faces a dual challenge: a large population with diabetes [8] and a demographic shift toward aging, accompanied by a rising prevalence of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease [9]. This issue is particularly relevant given the advent of biological treatments for early Alzheimer’s disease [10] and the growing body of work on dementia prevention following the Lancet Commission’s identification of 14 modifiable risk factors for reducing dementia risk [11]. Against this backdrop, the present study was designed to evaluate the frequency, magnitude, spectrum and risk factors associated with cognitive impairment in elderly individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus in India.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The cross-sectional study was conducted prospectively between December 2019 to December 2022 at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, a tertiary care public hospital in New Delhi, North India. Elderly participants aged 60 years and above were enrolled and stratified into groups based on diabetes status. The study was approved by the institute’s ethics committee (Ref. No. IEC-485/02.08.2019, AA-6/04.10.2019) and all participants provided written informed consent. Ethical standards were adhered to in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This is the second in a series of papers from our cohort study. We described detailed methodology in our previous publication [12] and have also provided relevant details here. The first paper assessed the validity of Montreal Cognitive Assessment scale to detect cognitive impairment in individuals with diabetes [12].

Objective

The objective of this study was to assess the frequency, type, magnitude and associations of cognitive deficits in elderly patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and to compare them with an age- and gender-matched control group without diabetes.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included elderly participants (aged ≥ 60 years) with or without type 2 diabetes mellitus (control group) who attended the endocrinology and neurology outpatient departments of the institute. Participants unwilling to participate, those with major depressive disorder or psychosis, overt hypothyroidism (TSH > 10 with subnormal T4), established dementia, disabling stroke, active delirium, significant visual, language, or hearing disabilities interfering with neuropsychological testing, active malignancy, or Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scores ≥ 15 (moderately severe or severe depression) were excluded. The participants in the control group were either people seeking care (enrolled from the same clinics which provided cases) or were spouses of the cases. The same exclusion criteria applied to controls as well.

Measurements

Clinical Interview

Participants underwent a clinical interview to collect data on sociodemographic parameters, medical history (e.g. diabetes duration, hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke, transient ischemic attack, smoking, alcohol use, medications, diabetes-related complications), depression (PHQ-9) [13], sleep quality [Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [14]], physical activity [Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [15]], followed by anthropometric (weight, height and blood pressure) and biochemical assessments (plasma glucose, HbA1c and lipid profile). The details of assays, instruments and methods used for measuring anthropometric and biochemical parameters have been provided in our earlier publication [16].

Psychological Testing

A detailed assessment was conducted, including a history of cognitive concerns, evaluation of global cognition, objective tests for different cognitive domains, and functional activities, using standardised, culturally validated tools, as mentioned below.

Cognitive Concerns: The Informant Questionnaire for Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) [17] was used to assess cognitive concerns based on informant responses. A score > 3.15 differentiates cognitive impairment from normal cognition with a sensitivity of 0.75, specificity of 0.54, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.71 [18].

Detailed Neuropsychological Assessment: The Indian Council of Medical Research Neurocognitive Toolbox (ICMR-NCTB), a culturally validated tool, was administered by certified psychologists [19]. We obtained written permission to use ICMR-NCTB from the Indian Council of Medical Research. We assessed multiple cognitive domains including attention & executive functions [Trail Making A & B and Phonemic fluency (ka, ma, pa)], memory [Verbal learning test (total learning, learning over trials, delayed recall, delayed recognition)] and language [Picture naming test, category fluency (animals, vegetables, food)]. An individual’s test score was categorised as abnormal if their performance on the test was below the 5th percentile, as per available norms. A domain was categorised as abnormal if at least three tests for that domain were available and two out of these three tests were abnormal (except for the memory domain, where at least two subtests were to be available and both should be abnormal). If an individual’s performance was abnormal on one or more domains (as per the aforementioned criteria), the subject was classified as having ‘objective cognitive impairment’. To classify ‘normal performance on neuropsychological testing’, the subject should have had evaluation of at least two cognitive domains and both should have been normal. Subjects who underwent tests for only one domain (and had normal results) were classified under ‘missing data’. This information is also summarized in Table S1.

Cognitive Staging: Participants were categorised into cognitively normal (no cognitive concerns, normal performance on neuropsychological tests, independent in instrumental activities of daily living), subjective cognitive decline (presence of cognitive concerns, but normal performance on neuropsychological tests and independent in instrumental activities of daily living), MCI (presence of cognitive concerns, impaired performance on neuropsychological tests, but independent in instrumental activities of daily living) or dementia (presence of cognitive concerns, impaired performance on neuropsychological tests and loss of independence in instrumental activities of daily living) using the NIA-AA 2018 syndromal cognitive staging scheme [21] (Table S2).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to evaluate and compare the frequency of MCI in people with diabetes, compared to people without diabetes. The secondary outcomes included the type/magnitude of cognitive deficits in the two groups and associations of cognitive deficits in the group with diabetes.

Sample Size

The study initially proposed to enrol 500 subjects (age ≥ 60 years) with diabetes and 500 age -and gender-matched subjects without diabetes (controls) over 3 years (December 2019 to December 2022), with the aim of establishing a large cohort of elderly with risk factors for dementia. We had planned to calculate a formal sample size based on the primary outcome after completion of one-third of the aforementioned numbers in both groups. However the recruitment was significantly affected by the COVID pandemic as the footfall of elderly to outpatient departments reduced at that time. Also, since the study was clinic based we had difficulty recruiting the control group. Hence a convenience sampling approach was used to enrol consecutive participants till the study period ended. On post hoc power calculation the study had > 90% power for observed difference in the primary outcome (frequency of MCI) between the two groups.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using Stata 15.0 (Stata-Corp LLC, TX, USA) and presented as number (%), mean ± SD, or median (Q25–Q75), as appropriate. Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for qualitative variables. For quantitative variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. ​ Student’s t test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Logistic regression was done to calculate the odds of cognitive impairment in the group with diabetes, keeping the subgroup of participants without diabetes, and having no subjective or objective cognitive deficits as the reference group. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using models that considered variables known to affect cognition, as well as baseline differences between participants with and without diabetes. Univariate logistic regression was performed to explore the risk associations of cognitive impairment. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 832 participants were enrolled in the cohort, comprising 698 individuals with diabetes and 134 without diabetes. The mean age of participants was 65.2 ± 4.5 years, with 65.5% being male and having an average education level of 11.5 ± 4.5 years (Table 1). The majority were married (84.8%), retired (48.9%) and had an monthly family income of less than 50,000 INR (86.7%). Hypertension was prevalent in 626 participants (75.3%), active smoking in 175 (21.1%), alcohol consumption in 104 (12.5%) and overweight/obesity in 458 (55.3%). Among comorbidities, retinopathy was observed in 100 participants (38.0%), nephropathy in 204 (25.8%), neuropathy in 330 (46.0%), coronary artery disease in 159 (19.1%) and a history of non-disabling stroke in 41 (4.9%).

The diabetes group had a higher proportion of male participants (67.8% vs. 53.7% in the non-diabetes group, p = 0.002), individuals who were employed (23.8% vs. 16.4%, p = 0.04), those with pre-existing hypertension (69.3% vs. 42.9%, p < 0.001), people who smoked (22.4% vs. 14.2%, p = 0.03), consumed alcohol (13.9% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.005) and with vascular comorbidities. The median duration of diabetes was 10 years (IQR 5–16 years), with the majority (69.0%) of participants on oral hypoglycaemic agents. The mean HbA1c in the group with diabetes was 7.8 ± 1.8% (Table 1).

Cognitive Performance and Syndromic Constructs

While raw scores on cognitive tests were generally similar between the group with diabetes and without diabetes (Table S3), the group with diabetes had a higher frequency of participants performing below normative levels in one or more domains (38% vs. 28.4%, p = 0.03) (Table 2). This was particularly evident in domains of executive function (21.8% vs. 15.8%, p = 0.12) and memory (22% vs. 16.9%, p = 0.19). Individual tests such as TMT-A (23.3% vs. 17.7%, p = 0.11) and TMT-B (41.5% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.006) also showed higher impairment rates in the group with diabetes (Table S4).

Examining syndromal cognitive constructs, cognitive impairment was identified in 75 participants (9.2%) across the cohort (Table 3). MCI was found in 62 participants (7.6%), with a higher prevalence in the group with diabetes (8.8% vs. 1.5% in the group without diabetes). Dementia was observed in 13 participants (1.6%), exclusively in the group with diabetes. Subjective cognitive symptoms (normal neuropsychological test performance and functional activities) were reported by 70 participants (8.6%), with a slightly higher frequency in the group without diabetes (10.6% vs. 8.2% in the group with diabetes). Nearly 27.3% (n = 223) of participants demonstrated impaired performance on neuropsychological tests in at least one domain, despite normal functional activities and no subjective cognitive concerns.

When participants with normal cognition in the group without diabetes were used as a reference, the odds of cognitive impairment (MCI and dementia) were 7.9 times higher (95% CI 1.9–32.9, p = 0.004) in the group with diabetes (Table 4). After adjustment for age, gender and education, the odds increased to 9.2 (95% CI 2.2–39.1, p = 0.003). Further adjustments for baseline differences between the groups did not significantly alter the results. We also did a gender-based subgroup analysis: the odds of cognitive impairment were 3.7 times higher (95% CI 0.76–18.4, p = 0.11) in female participants with diabetes (adjusted for age, education and baseline differences) compared to female participants without diabetes. In male participants with diabetes true odds could not be calculated because of ‘perfect failure’, as the control group had no outcome.

Associations of Cognitive Impairment

Univariate analysis revealed that increasing age [OR 1.1 (95% CI 1.02–1.14), p = 0.003] and unemployment [OR 2.5 (95% CI 1.2–5.4), p = 0.02] were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment in the group with diabetes (Table 5). Conversely, higher education [OR 0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.91), p < 0.001] and physical activity [OR 0.99 (95% CI 0.99–0.99), p = 0.02] had protective effects. We did not find a significant association of diabetes-specific factors such as duration of diabetes [OR 1.02 (95% CI 0.99–1.05), p = 0.16] and history of hypoglycaemia [OR 3.0 (95% CI 0.85–10.52), p = 0.086] with cognitive dysfunction.

Discussion

In this cohort of 832 elderly Indian subjects (698 with diabetes, 134 without diabetes), clinically significant cognitive impairment was observed in 75 participants (9.2%). MCI was present in 7.6%, while dementia was seen in 1.6% of the entire cohort. Participants with diabetes exhibited a higher frequency of cognitive impairment, with 8.8% having MCI and 1.9% having dementia, compared to only 1.5% with MCI in the group without diabetes. Increasing age and unemployment were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment, whereas higher education and physical activity were associated with protective effects. Notably, more individuals with diabetes showed impairment on the TMT-B test (41.5% vs. 27.8% in the group without diabetes), which assesses complex executive functions, including inhibition control, set shifting, working memory, and visuo-perceptual processing speed.

Globally, the prevalence of MCI in the general population is reported to be approximately 23.7%, based on a systematic review and meta-analysis of 51 studies involving 287,689 elderly individuals [22]. The Harmonized Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia for the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (n = 4096, mean age 69 years, 51% women, 49% with no formal education, 56% urban) reported a population prevalence of 17.6% for MCI and 7.2% for dementia [23]. In comparison, our cohort showed a lower frequency of both MCI and dementia, likely due to sociodemographic differences and exclusion of known dementia cases at baseline. The participants in our cohort had a lower mean age (65 years), included more men (65.5%) and only educated participants. Cognitive decline is known to increase with age, lower education levels and among female individuals [24].

When comparing individuals with diabetes to those without, the risk of developing MCI (up to 60%) and dementia (50–100%) is significantly higher in people with diabetes [25]. A meta-analysis of 144 prospective studies found that diabetes was associated with a 49% increased risk of MCI (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.26–1.77) and a 43% increased risk of all-cause dementia (RR 1.43, 95% CI 1.33–1.53) [26]. Another meta-analysis by You et al. reported a high prevalence of MCI in people with diabetes, particularly in Asia (46.4%, 95% CI 36.2–56.6, with a significant number from China) compared to Europe (36.6%, 95% CI 26.3–46.9) [4]. Zhao et al. also found a high prevalence of MCI (44.1%) in a meta-analysis that included 30 studies (26 from China, 16 of which used a cognitive screening tool to define MCI) involving 10,469 participants [5]. Varghese et al. studied cognitive impairment in 800 participants in India (40–60 years old, 400 with diabetes, 400 without diabetes, diagnosis based on Addenbrooke’s cognitive examination III) and found cognitive impairment in 63.8% of patients with diabetes compared to 10.8% of participants without diabetes, with an odds ratio of 8.78 (CI 4.47–17.22) [27]. Chakraborty et al. reported frequencies of 43.7%, 57.1% and 75% in age groups 61–70, 71–80 and 81–90 years, respectively (1278 participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus, MCI diagnosis based on MoCA) [28]. Our study aligns with these findings, showing higher frequency (people with diabetes 10.7%, people without diabetes 1.5%) and odds [OR 9.1 (2.1, 39.4)] of cognitive impairment in the group with diabetes. However, the overall figures of cognitive impairment in our cohort were lower than previous Asian and Indian studies, likely due to differences in study settings, sampling approaches, sociodemographics and the stringent algorithm used to define MCI and dementia.

Among risk factors, increasing age and unemployment were inversely associated with cognitive impairment, while higher education and physical activity showed protective effects. Lower education levels are a significant modifiable risk factor for dementia, with a population attributable fraction (PAF) of 13.6% in India, nearly double the global rate of 7.5% [29]. Education builds cognitive reserve, enabling the brain to maintain function despite age-related or neuropathological damage. Occupational cognitive stimulation and physical activity also contribute to this reserve [11]. Physical activity reduces vascular risk factors and neuroinflammation, as evidenced by a systematic review and meta-analysis (58 studies, n = 257,983), which reported decreased risk of all-cause dementia (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.77–0.84), Alzheimer’s disease (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93) and vascular dementia (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.95) over a follow-up of at least 10 years [30]. Increasing age remains a non-modifiable risk factor for dementia, with incidence doubling approximately every 5 years and reaching as high as 41% per year in centenarians [31]. We did not find a significant association between diabetes-specific factors such as duration of diabetes, hypoglycaemia and cognitive dysfunction. Unfortunately we did not assess other important biomarkers related to glucose metabolism such as HOMA-IR and glycaemic variability.

In our cohort, the cognitive domains most affected were executive functions (TMT-B test) in the group with diabetes. Antal et al. analysed neurocognitive effects of type 2 diabetes mellitus in the UK Biobank cohort (n = 20,314, comprising 1012 with type 2 diabetes mellitus and 19,302 matched healthy controls, aged 50–80 years) and found marked deficits in executive functions and processing speed, along with accelerated grey matter atrophy. Their meta-analysis of 34 cognitive studies (n = 22,231) confirmed these findings, showing a 13.1% decrease in executive domain performance in the group with diabetes, exceeding the decline expected with normal aging (1.9% per year) [6].

Our study has significant clinical and research implications. For endocrinologists/diabetologists, it highlights the burden of cognitive issues in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (approximately one in ten individuals in our cohort) and identifies domains/tests (executive functions, trail making test) that can detect early abnormalities for timely referral to memory clinics. Executive functions are crucial for effective diabetes management, involving goal-setting, motivation, flexibility and medication adherence. Future studies could explore the impact of cognitive rehabilitation on diabetes self-management skills. Further, research could investigate the association between different patho-biologies and cognitive deficits using novel fluid biomarkers of Alzheimer’s pathology (amyloid and phosphorylated tau), other fluid biomarkers associated with cognitive decrements in diabetes (glycogen synthase kinase 3β, inflammatory molecules, advanced glycation end products, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, specific microRNAs and adipokines), neuroimaging (vascular burden and hippocampal/neocortical atrophy on magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, white matter microstructural integrity using diffusion tensor imaging, functional and metabolic imaging) and genetic factors especially APOE ε4 expression [32, 33]. Future studies could also assess the role of multimodal intervention strategies in limiting progression in early-detected cases. Approximately one-third of our cohort (27.3%) had impaired test performance in at least one neuropsychological domain, with normal functional activities and no significant cognitive concerns, while one in ten participants reported subjective cognitive symptoms despite normal test performance and functional activities. Understanding the trajectory of these subgroups could also provide valuable insights.

The study’s strengths include a large sample size of people with diabetes, well-characterized risk associations, a comprehensive psycho-diagnostic approach, and detailed diagnostic constructs along the cognitive continuum. It provides data from a region with the highest number of people living with diabetes globally, but limited representation in cognitive research. We used stringent criteria for MCI definition, incorporating neuropsychological test performance, cognitive concerns and instrumental activities of daily living. In contrast, most studies rely solely on screening tools such as MMSE or MoCA, which may lead to false positives [12, 34].

The limitations of our study include lack of neuroimaging data or biomarkers to confirm the aetiology of the cognitive deficits. Exclusion of established cases of dementia reduces the numbers with cognitive impairment, underestimating the disease burden. There was significant missing data for some predictor variables such as microvascular complications and lipid profile, making study of associations less reliable. Also, causal inferences cannot be made as the study was cross-sectional in design. Even though most factors were adjusted for, we cannot exclude residual confounding by unknown or unmeasured factors such as genetics, inflammatory markers, etc. The results cannot be generalized to either gender (subgroup analysis led to smaller numbers in the two categories, losing statistical significance).

Despite these limitations, the study highlights the extent, types and risk factors of cognitive impairment in Indian individuals with diabetes. These participants need a follow-up analysis to understand better the importance of the cognitive constructs and their biological underpinnings.

Conclusion

Elderly Indians with diabetes carry a high burden of cognitive impairment, particularly executive dysfunction. Factors affecting cognitive reserve are an important modifiable determinant. Future studies should test the utility of preventive and rehabilitation strategies in these individuals.
