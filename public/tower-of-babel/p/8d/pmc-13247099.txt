CBP/p300 is critical for the expansion and maintenance of functional pancreatic α cell mass
Authors: Shushu Wang, Tianjiao Li, Chunxiang Sheng, Jialin Tan, Yuling Yang, Xiaoqin Ma, Yun Liu, Rui Wei, Feiye Zhou, Libin Zhou, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic α cell senses amino acid availability to adjust secretion function and proliferation, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Here, α cell-specific deletion of CBP/p300 in mice leads to hypoglucagonemia and hyperaminoacidemia, along with decreased functional pancreatic α cell mass due to impaired cell proliferation, dedifferentiation, and cell loss. The knockout of CBP/p300 blocks glucagon receptor antibody-stimulated α cell proliferation and mTORC1 signaling in mice. In CBP/p300-deficient α cells, single-cell RNA sequencing reveals upregulated autophagy-related genes and downregulated α cell identity genes and amino acid transporters, including Slc7a2. Slc7a2 is involved in regulating α cell identity gene expression through lysine-facilitated H3K27 acetylation. In addition, Slc7a2 downregulation compromises arginine-stimulated mTORC1 signaling, thereby suppressing α cell proliferation and triggering autophagy. Collectively, our findings uncover CBP/p300 as central regulators of functional α cell mass partially by orchestrating Slc7a2-mediated amino acid sensing.

Introduction

As structural elements and energy sources for normal cell growth and function, amino acids are essential to human health1. The disturbance of amino acid homeostasis has been closely linked to metabolic diseases. Branched-chain amino acids and their metabolites have been identified as the strongest biomarkers of obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)2. 2-Aminoadipic acid (2-AAA), a catabolic metabolite of lysine, could even predict the risk of developing T2DM3. It is widely recognized that impaired pancreatic β-cell function is the core problem in T2DM4. However, accumulating evidence suggests that α-cell dysfunction, characterized by inappropriate glucagon secretion, also contributes to hyperglycemia in diabetes5. Notably, α cell function and number is tightly coupled to changes in plasma amino acid levels6. However, the response mechanism of α cells to amino acids remains largely unclear.

Pancreatic α cells are essential to maintaining glucose homeostasis primarily through secreting glucagon, which promotes hepatic glucose production during fasting or hypoglycemic states7. Glucagon binds to the glucagon receptor (GCGR), activating PKA-CREB signaling cascades that stimulate hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis8. In addition, glucagon facilitates hepatic amino acid uptake and catabolism, thereby supplying substrates for glucose production9. Thus, glucagon secreted from α-cell plays a critical role in the turnover of amino acids to glucose in hepatocytes, resulting in decreased circulating amino acid concentration and increased blood glucose level. In turn, changes in plasma glucose and amino acid levels modulate glucagon secretion in an opposite direction6. In T2DM, α-cell dysregulation is characterized by excessive basal glucagon secretion that occurs independent of glycemic status, contributing to hyperglucagonemia and increased hepatic glucose output10,11.

Recent studies have highlighted the role of α cells as key amino acid sensors12. In humans, the ingestion of whey protein induces a significant rise in circulating amino acids and glucagon levels13. Several amino acids, including arginine, glutamine, alanine, and glycine, have been shown to acutely stimulate glucagon secretion14. Prolonged elevations of amino acids also drive excessive α-cell proliferation, leading to its hyperplasia and hyperglucagonemia15–18. Increased circulating glutamine has been shown to be causative to amino acid-induced α-cell hyperplasia in GCGR signaling interference models, involving the upregulation of SLC38A5, a transporter of glutamine, and stimulation of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway. However, whether or how α cells sense other types of amino acids, especially those related to T2DM, remains elusive.

It has been demonstrated that amino acids provide substrates for several protein post-translational modifications such as acetylation and crotonylation, which link amino acids catabolism to the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in cell function or proliferation19,20. Besides cell number, the maintenance of α cell identity is also critical for their function21,22. Epigenetics plays a crucial role in the maintenance of cell type-specific features and lineage stability22,23. ATAC-Seq analysis shows that α cells possess more poised and accessible chromatin at genes related to identity and diabetes risk22. Histone acetylation is an important epigenetic modification involved in regulating cell type-specific gene expression patterns and determining cell fate24. The lysine acetyltransferases type 3 (KAT3) family members CBP (Crebbp) and p300 (Ep300) are key transcriptional co-activators with similar structure and function23. Of interest, acetylation of H3 lysine 27 (H3K27Ac) mediated by CBP and p300 is reported to be a biomarker of cell type-specific promoters and enhancers related to cell fate25. However, the role of CBP/p300 in controlling α cell identity remains poorly understood.

In this study, we generated α cell-specific Crebbp/Ep300 knockout mice to explore the role of two acetyltransferases in maintaining α cell identity. Dual deletion of CBP and p300 led to a significant reduction in functional α cell mass and glucagon secretion due to impaired α cell proliferation, α cell dedifferentiation, and cell loss. Single-cell RNA sequencing and in-vitro experiments revealed the involvement of SLC7A2 in CBP/p300-governed α cell identity, proliferation, and autophagy through lysine-promoted histone H3K27 acetylation and arginine-stimulated mTORC1 activation. These findings uncover a critical role for CBP/p300 in linking amino acid sensing with the maintenance of α cell identity and function.

Results

Pancreatic α cell-specific double knockout of Crebbp and Ep300 mice exhibit low plasma glucagon level and high amino acid level

To investigate the role of CBP and p300 in pancreatic α cells, we generated α cell-specific CBP/p300 knockout (CBP/p300αDKO) mice by crossing Gcg-Cre mice with Crebbpflox/flox and Ep300flox/flox mice (Fig. 1a). The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining within 1 day after birth, showing negative CBP and p300 staining in more than 90% Gcg-positive α cells of CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 1b, c). The potential off-target effects of Gcg-Cre in intestinal L-cells and brain were also evaluated. Intestinal co-expression of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and tdTomato in Gcg-Cre-driven tdTomato lineage-tracing wild-type (WT) and CBP/p300αDKO mice confirmed that Gcg-Cre was indeed active in the intestinal L-cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a). However, neither the average number of GLP-1+ cell per intestinal section nor circulating level of active GLP-1 differed between control and CBP/p300αDKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). In addition, CBP/p300αDKO mice exhibited normal oral glucose tolerance (Supplementary Fig. 1d), further indicating preserved L-cell function. We also confirmed Gcg-Cre-mediated recombination in neurons of the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Interestingly, fasting blood glucagon level was significantly reduced in 8-week-old CBP/p300αDKO mice, but not in single-gene knockout (CBPαKO, p300αKO) or triple-allele knockout (CBPαKO; p300HE, CBPHE; p300αKO) mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 1d). However, fasting blood glucose and plasma insulin levels remained unchanged in 8-week-old CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1h), accompanied by normal hepatic glucose output (Fig. 1f) and mRNA levels of key gluconeogenic genes, including Pck1, Fbp1, and G6pc (Fig. 1g). This was the case for the levels of PEPCK protein and phosphorylated CREB (Fig. 1h). In contrast, total plasma amino acid level was significantly increased in fasted CBP/p300αDKO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 1i). We further performed metabolomic assays on plasma samples from 8-week-old WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice. A total of 193 metabolites were identified, with univariate analysis revealing 53 upregulated and 2 downregulated metabolites in CBP/p300αDKO mice (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1j). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted that differential metabolites were mainly associated with amino acid metabolism, including arginine and proline metabolism, as well as alanine, aspartate, glutamate, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Fig. 1k). Of the 20 basic amino acids examined, 16 were significantly increased, including several glucogenic amino acids such as alanine, glutamine, and serine (Fig. 1l). These results suggest that CBP and p300 deletion in α cells causes hypoglucagonemia and significant disturbance in amino acid homeostasis, without affecting plasma glucose levels.

CBP/p300 is necessary for the expansion and maintenance of postnatal α cell number

During murine islet endocrine cell development, glucagon (Gcg)-positive cells first appeared at embryonic day 9.528. We next examined whether CBP/p300 deletion-mediated hypoglucagonemia is due to impaired development or postnatal maintenance of Gcg-positive α cells. Morphometric analysis showed that Gcg-positive cell number in CBP/p300αDKO mice was normal at birth (0 w) (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2a), indicating that CBP/p300 are not essential for α cell development. With the elongation of postnatal time, significant difference was observed in Gcg-positive cell number between WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice. In WT mice, α cell numbers increased rapidly during the first 2 weeks, with the fastest growth occurring between 1 and 2 weeks. Afterward, the rate of α cell expansion slowed and stabilized from 4 weeks onward (Fig. 2b). Whereas, α cell number was decrease by 37% at 2 weeks and 70% at 8 weeks in CBP/p300αDKO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2b, c). We further analyzed additional morphometric indicators. At 2 weeks, pancreatic weight and the average number of islets per section were similar between two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2b). However, the α cell area as a percentage of the total islet area was significantly reduced in CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 2d), with similar α cell size between two groups (Fig. 2e). We also examined α cell proliferation during the neonatal period, which plays a key role in postnatal α cell mass establishment29. Ki67 staining revealed a significant decrease in α-cell proliferation in 1-week-old CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 2g, h), consistent with the reduced α-cell expansion observed in these mice (Fig. 2i). However, the apoptosis rates of α-cell were comparable in 4 and 8-week-old WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice as shown by cleaved-Caspase 3 staining (Supplementary Fig. 2d), indicating that other forms of cell loss may contribute to the decline in α-cell numbers. At 8 weeks, although pancreatic weight and the average number of islets per section were similar between WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2e), the α cell area as a percentage of total islet area was even further reduced in CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 2j), with no significant change in α cell size (Fig. 2k). The number of Gcg-positive α cells per islet remained significantly lower in CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 2l), while the β cell area was still unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Together, these results demonstrate that CBP/p300 are essential for the postnatal proliferation and survival of α cells.

Double deficiency of CBP and p300 leads to α-cell identity loss and population reduction

Building on our previous findings that HAT activity of CBP/p300 are critical for the transcription of β cell identity genes30, we next investigated their role in α cell fate maintenance using a lineage-tracing system based on ROSA-LSL-tdTomato reporter mice crossed with Gcg-Cre to label glucagon-expressing (Gcg+) α cells (Fig. 3a). The labeling efficiency was confirmed in 2-week-old ROSAfl/wt; Gcg-Cre mice, in which over 90% of Gcg+ cells co-expressed tdTomato (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). We then examined islet sections at postnatal weeks 1, 2, 4, and 8, and found that nearly all tdTomato+ cells consistently co-expressed Gcg in WT mice (Fig. 3b). However, CBP/p300αDKO mice showed a progressive and significant reduction in the number of tdTomato+ cells per islet section starting from 2 weeks of age (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3c), indicating tdTomato+ cell loss. Although the percentage of tdTomato+Gcg+/tdTomato+ cells in the knockout mice was similar to WT at 1 week, it dropped to 79% at 2 weeks, 55% at 4 weeks, and 32% at 8 weeks (Fig. 3d), indicating a gradual loss of α cell identity. Immunofluorescence staining revealed no significant transdifferentiation into β or δ cells in CBP/p300αDKO mice when α-cell lost its identity. To further investigate the role of CBP/p300 in adult α cells, we generated tamoxifen-induced α cell-specific CBP/p300 knockout mice (iCBP/p300αDKO) by crossing Gcg-CreERT2 with Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/flox mice, and ROSA-LSL-tdTomato reporter mice (Fig. 3g). Tamoxifen administration in 8-week-old mice resulted in efficient tdTomato labeling of Gcg+ cells ( > 90%) (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 3d). In line with findings from CBP/p300αDKO mice, iCBP/p300αDKO mice exhibited decreased fasting plasma glucagon level and elevated plasma amino acid level, without changes in fasting blood glucose level (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). Histological analysis revealed that iCBP/p300αDKO mice had significantly fewer tdTomato+ cells and Gcg+ α cells compared to WT mice (Fig. 3i, j), as well as a reduced proportion of tdTomato+Gcg+/tdTomato+ cells (Fig. 3k), further supporting the occurrence of α cell loss and dedifferentiation. Similar to the developmental model, no transdifferentiation of α cells into β or δ cells was observed, but tdTomato+ Ppy+ cells were also diminished (Fig. 3l, m). Together, these results confirm that CBP/p300 are indispensable for maintaining α cell survival and identity throughout the postnatal life.

Expressions of α-cell identity genes are compromised in CBP/p300αDKO mice

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying α-cell identity loss in CBP/p300αDKO mice, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on islet cells from 4-week-old WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice using tdTomato as a lineage tracer (Fig. 4a). Intact islets were isolated from two groups of mice, dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and subjected to scRNA-seq analysis. After filtering out low-quality cells and doublets, 11,797 high-quality cells, including 4993 from WT mice and 6804 from CBP/p300αDKO mice were retained for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis identified seven major cell types, including β, α, δ, and PP cells, as well as stellate, endothelial, and immune cells, based on canonical markers (Fig. 4b, c). To characterize α-cell state transitions in the absence of CBP/p300, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis on α cells from CBP/p300αDKO mice due to tdTomato marking biologically distinct α-cell states in WT versus CBP/p300αDKO mice, which would otherwise confound joint trajectory interference. The trajectory showed a temporal progression from tdTomato-negative (tdTomato−) to tdTomato-positive (tdTomato+) α cells, with tdTomato− cells mainly located at early to intermediate stages, while tdTomato+ cells enriched at intermediate to late stages, suggestive of a differentiation trajectory toward identity loss following Cre-mediated CBP/p300 deletion (Fig. 4f, g). Gene expression dynamics along pseudotime identified four co-regulated modules with temporal expression patterns, including a decline in α cell identity genes (e.g., Gcg, MafB, and Ttr) and a concurrent upregulation of autophagy-related genes (e.g., Ctsf, Ctsl, and Tmem59) (Fig. 4h). Consistent with the downregulation of α-cell identity genes, islets isolated from CBP/p300αDKO mice exhibited markedly impaired ex vivo glucagon secretion in response to basal glucose, low glucose, and arginine, indicating functional impairment of α cells following CBP/p300 ablation (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

We next investigated the transcriptional disruptions in α cells by performing UMAP co-visualization of Gcg and tdTomato expression in α cells from CBP/p300αDKO mice. This revealed two major tdTomato+ subsets. One retained high Gcg expression and another lacked Gcg expression, indicative of heterogeneous α cell states (Fig. 4i, j). Based on the expression levels of Gcg and tdTomato, four α cell subpopulations were defined: (i) Gcg+tdTomato+ cells (Both, 42%), representing functional α cells; (ii) Gcg− tdTomato+ cells (tdTomato, 39%), denoting identity-compromised α cells; (iii) Gcg+tdTomato− cells (GCG, 14%), likely α cells escaping recombination; and (iv) Gcg− tdTomato− cells (NONE, 5%), potentially immature α cells (Fig. 4k).

To further dissect molecular changes across these α cell subpopulations, we analyzed the expression of key markers associated with known markers, identity, autophagy, transdifferentiation, dedifferentiation, glucose-responsiveness, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Gcg and MafB expressions were diminished in the tdTomato group, confirming identity erosion. Autophagy-related genes, such as Map1lc3a, Map1lc3b, Lamp1, Lamp2, Atp6v0c, and Ctsf, were upregulated in the tdTomato group, while the expressions of Ins1, Mafa, and Ucn3 remained unchanged, excluding transdifferentiation into β cells. In the tdTomato group, Ngn3 was undetectable, and other progenitor-related genes showed no significant changes, arguing against persistence of progenitor-like cells. Several glucose-responsive genes, such as Gck and G6pc2 were significantly downregulated in the tdTomato group. Notably, expressions of amino acid transporter genes (Slc16a10, Slc38a5, Slc38a4, and Slc7a2) were profoundly altered in identity-lost tdTomato group, implicating impaired amino acid sensing in the progression of α cell dysfunction.

CBP/p300 are known to regulate lineage-specific gene expression through histone H3K27 acetylation30,32. Consistent with this, H3K27Ac level was significantly reduced in α cells of CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 5d). Structurally, CBP/p300 possess a histone acetyltransferase (HAT) domain that catalyzes acetylation of lysine residues and a bromodomain that recognizes acetylated lysines to facilitates chromatin targeting33. To determine the contribution of each domain to transcriptional regulation of α cell genes, we treated primary islets from WT mice with either I-CBP112 (a bromodomain inhibitor34, 5 μM) or A-485 (a selective HAT inhibitor35, 3 μM). Only A-485 markedly suppressed the expressions of Gcg, Mafb, and Slc7a2 (Fig. 5g), highlighting the crucial role of CBP/p300 HAT activity in maintaining α cell identity. This was further validated in α-TC1-6 cells, where A-485 treatment led to similar decreases in the expressions of these genes (Fig. 5h). ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that A-485 markedly lowered H3K27Ac enrichment at their promoter regions (Fig. 5i–k), a finding consistent with our previous H3K27Ac ChIP-seq data from A-485-treated rat islets (Supplementary Fig. 5e).

Amino acid metabolism has been implicated as a key regulator of α-cell identity and function36. In this study, we found that amino acid starvation conditions (HBSS) significantly reduced the mRNA expressions of Gcg, MafB, Pcsk2, and Slc7a2 in α-TC1-6 cells (Fig. 5l). Slc7a2 knockdown produced a similar effect, supporting the essential role of Slc7a2-mediated amino acid transport in maintaining α-cell identity. On the contrary, lysine supplementation significantly upregulated the expression of these identity genes and partially rescued the inhibitory effects of Slc7a2 knockdown (Fig. 5m), suggesting that Slc7a2-mediated lysine uptake supports α-cell function. Ex vivo islet secretion assays further demonstrated that lysine pretreatment enhanced both basal and arginine-stimulated glucagon secretion (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Mechanistically, lysine catabolism yields acetyl-CoA, which serves as a substrate for histone acetylation37. Supporting this, ChIP-qPCR analysis showed that lysine treatment significantly increased H3K27Ac levels at the promoters of Gcg and MafB (Fig. 5n, o). We further tested whether the catabolite of lysine 2-AAA, a known biomarker of T2DM, elicits similar effects. Indeed, 2-AAA treatment robustly elicited the expression of Gcg, Mafb, and Pcsk2 (Fig. 5p), with elevated H3K27Ac levels at their promoters (Fig. 5q, r), linking amino acid metabolism to CBP/p300-dependent epigenetic regulation of α cell identity genes.

Together, these results suggest that Slc7a2-mediated lysine uptake fuels acetyl-CoA production, thereby promoting CBP/p300-dependent histone acetylation and expression of α cell identity genes (Fig. 5s).

To determine whether amino acid availability regulates autophagic activity in α cells, we treated α-TC1-6 cells with HBSS. This treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of autophagy-related genes (Fig. 6g). Importantly, knockdown of Slc7a2 in α-TC1-6 cells also increased the expression of autophagy markers (Fig. 6h), suggesting that Slc7a2 modulates autophagic activity. CBP/p300 inhibition by A-485 elicited a similar response (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, Slc7a2 knockdown in α-TC1-6 cells led to elevated level of LC3-II, a marker of autophagosome accumulation (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 6b). This was the case for A-485 treatment (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 6c), further supporting the roles of Slc7a2 and CBP/p300 in amino acids-regulated autophagy.

CBP/p300 deficiency blocks glucagon receptor antibody-stimulated α cell proliferation

Arginine activates the mTORC1 signaling pathway to regulates cell growth and proliferation42. Since Slc7a2 mediates arginine uptake, it is reasonable to suppose that Slc7a2 influences α-cell proliferation by activating mTORC1. Under starvation condition, mTORC1 activation in islets α-cells was significantly reduced as shown by low phosphorylation of S6 (pS6), a key downstream target of mTORC143. Conversely, arginine supplementation enhanced mTORC1 activation (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Slc7a2 knockdown blunted the ability of arginine to activate S6 phosphorylation in α-TC1-6 cells (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 7b), whereas Slc7a2 overexpression potentiated mTORC1 activation in response to arginine (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 7c), indicating that Slc7a2 is indeed a key arginine sensor in α cells. The link between hyperaminoacidemia and α-cell proliferation has been well established in models with disrupted glucagon signaling44. To assess the impact of CBP/p300 loss on α cell responses to amino acids, WT and CBP/p300 knockout mice were treated with a monoclonal antibody (REMD 2.59) targeting GCGR (Fig. 7f). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that GCGR antibody treatment strongly increased the number of tdTomato+ α cells in WT mice, whereas this effect was abolished in CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 7g). Quantification confirmed a significant expansion of tdTomato+ cells per islet in WT mice following GCGR signaling blockade, without significant change in knockout mice (Fig. 7h). Proliferative activity was further assessed by co-staining for Ki67. In WT mice, GCGR mAb robustly increased the proportion of Ki67+ tdTomato+ α cells, while CBP/p300-deficient mice failed to mount a proliferative response (Fig. 7i), indicating that CBP/p300 are required for α cell proliferation in response to glucagon receptor inhibition. We next examined mTORC1 signaling by assessing phosphorylation of S6. GCGR antibody significantly increased the proportion of pS6⁺ tdTomato⁺ cells among tdTomato⁺ cells in WT islets, but this induction was markedly blunted in CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 7j, k), indicating impaired mTORC1 activation.

To validate these findings in an inducible model, we administered GCGR antibody to iCBP/p300αDKO mice. Similarly, GCGR signaling blockade promoted α cell expansion in WT mice, but failed to increase tdTomato+ cell numbers in iCBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 7l, m). Ki67 staining also showed enhanced α-cell proliferation in WT mice, but not in knockout mice (Fig. 7l, n). pS6 staining was significantly lower in iCBP/p300αDKO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7o, p). Collectively, these findings suggest that CBP/p300 are necessary for α-cell proliferation and proper mTORC1 signaling in response to glucagon receptor blockage.

Discussion

Pancreatic α cells sense changes in amino acid availability and dynamically adjust glucagon secretion and proliferation to maintain systemic nutrient homeostasis45; yet the molecular mechanisms governing these adaptive responses remain unclear. Using embryonic and tamoxifen-inducible α cell-specific knockout mouse models, we demonstrated that CBP/p300 deficiency seriously dampened the establishment and maintenance of functional α cell mass with Slc7a2 downregulation, resulting in hypoglucagonemia and hyperaminoacidemia. Mechanically, CBP/p300 and its effector Slc7a2 link lysine availability to epigenetic control of α-cell identity genes. In addition, this regulatory axis is critical for maintaining α cell number and function via governing cell proliferation and autophagy, involving arginine-stimulated mTORC1 signaling (Fig. 8). Glucagon was first identified as the substance responsible for raising blood glucose in pancreatic extracts46. For over 50 years, elevated glucagon secretion has been recognized as a hallmark of hyperglycemia in both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes47. Disruption of glucagon signaling, either through GCGR antagonists or genetic deletion of Gcgr, leads to significant hypoglycemia48. However, we did not observe a decrease in fasting glucose levels in CBP/p300αDKO mice, despite hypoglucagonemia. Similar findings have been reported in other models: (i) α cell-specific ablation using diphtheria toxin in embryos and adult mice minimally affects fasting glucose49–51, and (ii) Gcg knockout mice maintain normal fasting glucose level52. We speculate that the lack of hypoglycemia in CBP/p300αDKO mice may be attributed to residual glucagon production and compensatory factors, which maintain hepatic glucose production and glucose homeostasis.

A 1974 study published in Lancet reported that four patients with glucagonomas had elevated plasma glucagon levels and low blood amino acid concentrations53. Additionally, amino acid supplementation was shown to reverse skin lesions (necrolytic migratory erythema) in glucagonoma syndrome patients54. In contrast, patients who underwent pancreatectomy experienced severely elevated blood amino acid levels, and glucagon administration alleviated hyperaminoacidemia symptoms55. Elevated blood amino acid levels have been observed in mouse models with Gcg knockout, Gcgr knockout, or GCGR antibody blockade. In the present study, CBP/p300αDKO mice also exhibited hyperaminoacidemia due to hypoglucagonemia, with significant upregulation of various L-type amino acids (Fig. 1l). Interestingly, the pattern of plasma amino acid levels in CBP/p300αDKO mice resembles that seen in other mouse models with disrupted glucagon signaling, indicating that circulating amino acids are more sensitive to the change of plasma glucagon level compared to glucose.

The ability of organisms to modulate cellular processes in response to nutrient availability is essential for maintaining energy homeostasis. Amino acid-dependent cellular processes, collectively referred to as “amino acid sensing”, encompass a wide range of activities, including amino acid transport, processing, metabolic control, and signal transduction56. These pathways are integrated to regulate various biological functions, such as cell growth, differentiation, gene expression, and overall cellular function. Pancreatic α cells display high sensitivity to changes in amino acid levels. Chronic hyperaminoacidemia in various models of GCGR blockade leads to excessive α-cell proliferation, a phenomenon also observed during neonatal development15,18,57. Solloway et al.17 showed that postnatal plasma amino acid levels in Gcgr−/− mice fluctuated in correlation with α-cell proliferation and mass. mTORC1 is a key regulator that links amino acid availability to cell growth40. Notably, α cell-specific deletion of the mTORC1 regulator Raptor in mice led to a significant reduction in neonatal α cell proliferation58. Conversely, conditional deletion of the mTORC1 inhibitor TSC2 in α cells stimulated α cell proliferation and mass expansion58. Additionally, two studies confirmed that rapamycin, an mTORC1 inhibitor, significantly blocked GCGR neutralizing antibody-induced α cell proliferation15,16. These results highlight mTORC1 signaling as a critical link between amino acids and α-cell proliferation. Our study shows that the fastest rate of α cell expansion in WT mice occurs during postnatal 1–2 weeks. CBP/p300αDKO mice failed to normally expanse their α cell mass during this period. Moreover, dual loss of CBP and p300 significantly inhibited α-cell proliferation induced by GCGR antibody. Reduced mTORC1 activation was observed in both neonatal and GCGR antibody-treated CBP/p300αDKO mice. Therefore, CBP/p300 activity is crucial for amino acid-induced mTORC1 activation and subsequent α-cell proliferation. In the study of Wong et al.59, Neurog3-Cre-driven pancreatic islet-specific p300 knockout mice displayed reduced proliferation of neonatal α-cells and β-cells. Mice lacking all four copies of p300/CBP in pancreatic endocrine progenitors failed to establish α- and β-cell mass postnatally, which was attributed to impaired cell proliferation. In our study, the decrease of postnatal α-cell mass in CBP/p300αDKO mice arises from a combination of impaired proliferation, dedifferentiation, and cell loss.

It is currently believed that L-glutamine is causative for hyperaminoacidemia-induced α-cell proliferation in glucagon signaling inhibition models, but high levels of glutamine alone are insufficient to stimulate this process15,16. Kim et al.15 found that the expression of the glutamine transporter Slc38a5 was upregulated in α cells under disrupted glucagon signaling conditions. Pancreatic α cell mass was reduced in Slc38a5−/− mice treated with GCGR antibody, suggesting that Slc38a5 is partially required for α cell expansion in response to GCGR inhibition-induced hyperaminoacidemia. However, RNA sequencing studies revealed that Slc38a5 was highly expressed in embryonic α cells, but significantly decreased after birth60. In adult mice, Slc38a5 was expressed in only about 1% of α cells, and Slc38a5−/− mice exhibited normal α cell proliferation and mass16. These findings suggest that Slc38a5 may be not a dominant regulator in neonatal α cell proliferation. Our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that Slc38a5 was expressed only in a small subset of α cells and at much lower levels (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with prior studies. In contrast, our study demonstrated that Slc7a2, a transporter of lysine and arginine, was broadly and highly expressed in α cells among islet endocrine cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing of islets revealed that Slc7a2 expression was significantly downregulated in α cells from CBP/p300αDKO mice (Fig. 5a). Inhibition of CBP/p300 acetyltransferases activity with A-485 also reduced Slc7a2 expression in α cells. Furthermore, both A-485-treated and CBP/p300 double-knockout α cells exhibited impaired mTORC1 activation in response to arginine. These results suggest that downregulation of Slc7a2 and subsequent impairment of arginine-activated mTORC1 signaling may contribute to α-cell dysfunction in CBP/p300αDKO mice.

To maintain intracellular metabolic homeostasis, cells use autophagy, a self-degradation process to remove unwanted components and recycle nutrients, as a critical tool in balancing the amino acid pool. Several amino acids, including arginine, leucine, glutamine, and methionine, serve as signals to regulate autophagy61. The mTORC1 pathway converts amino acid signals into autophagic signals that regulate each step of the autophagic process, such as nucleation, autophagosome extension, autophagosome maturation, and termination62. Additionally, amino acids regulate autophagy through transcriptional control of autophagy-related genes expression63. Although α cells are established as amino acid sensors, the role of autophagy in α cells remains elusive. Himuro et al.64 reported that in an α-cell-specific Atg7 knockout model, autophagy in α cells was significantly reduced, and a multilayered structure of glucagon-positive cells was observed, resembling α-cell hyperplasia seen in GCGR-blocked mice. On the contrary, excessive autophagy has been reported to be associated with autophagic cell death65. However, whether autophagic cell death contributes to the regulation of α-cell number homeostasis is unclear. In our study, a significant increase was observed in the expression of autophagy-related genes in α cells of CBP/p300αDKO mice and A-485-treated α-TC1-6 cells. More LC3 puncta and lysosomes were detected in CBP/p300-deficient α cells, suggesting elevated autophagic activity. Interestingly, CBP/p300 deletion in α cells led to cell loss, characterized by decreased tdTomato-positive cell numbers, but no increase in apoptosis or necrosis was detected. It is likely that autophagic cell death is responsible for the loss of CBP/p300-deficient α cells. Further evidence is needed to confirm this hypothesis. Several studies indicate that mTORC1 signaling regulates autophagy in α cells. In α-cell-specific Raptor knockout mice, autophagy was significantly increased58. Additionally, in the αTC9 cell line under amino acid deprivation, decreased lysosomal localization of mTORC1, reduced mTORC1 activity, and increased crinophagic degradation of glucagon were observed66. Collectively, our data support the hypothesis that CBP/p300 deficiency impairs amino acid sensing and mTORC1 activation, leading to reduced α cell proliferation and increased autophagy.

Another notable change in CBP/p300αDKO mice is the loss of α-cell identity. Several α-cell identity and functional genes, including Gcg, MafB, and Pcsk2, are significantly downregulated in CBP/p300-deficient α cells and A-485-treated α-TC1-6 cells, accompanied by decreased H3K27Ac levels at the promoters of Gcg and MafB. These findings highlight the dominant role of CBP/p300 HAT activity-mediated epigenetic modification in maintaining α cell identity and functional gene expression. Interestingly, our study found that chronic treatment of lysine enhanced glucagon secretion from mouse islets. The treatment of lysine and its catabolic metabolite 2-AAA, both of which can metabolize into acetyl-CoA, increased H3K27Ac occupancy at the promoters of Gcg and MafB, leading to their upregulation (Fig. 5m–r). These results indicate that chronic amino acid exposure could stimulate α-cell function through epigenetic modification-mediated gene expression, which provides clue for exploring the link between disturbance of circulating amino acids and hyperglucagonemia in T2DM.

Overall, our study provides key insights into the role of α cells as amino acid sensors using α cell-specific CBP/p300 knockout mice. CBP/p300 are essential for the maintenance of α-cell identity, mass, and function, which is involved in Slc7a2-governed amino acid sensing. Slc7a2 stimulates the expressions of α-cell identity-related genes via promoting lysine-derived acetylation. mTORC1 signaling mediates Slc7a2/arginine-dominated α cell proliferation and autophagy. Our findings highlight the critical role of Slc7a2 in CBP/p300-maintaining α-cell identity and function through amino acids-elicited epigenetic modifications and mTORC1 signaling. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that CBP/p300 deficiency contributes to profound alterations in the epigenome and transcriptome of α cells. Beyond histone acetylation, non-histone protein acetylation and other types of acylation modifications are likely to be involved in the regulation of CBP/p300 on α cells. Our findings probably represent only the tip of the iceberg in understanding how amino acid signaling modulates the functional capacity of pancreatic α cells, which need further in-depth investigations.

Methods

Mice

Crebbp-Loxp (Crebbpflox/flox) and Ep300-Loxp (Ep300flox/flox) mice were generated by Gempharmatech CO. using the CRISP-Cas9 system to insert LoxP sites flanking exon 2 of the Crebbp or Ep300 genes. GcgiCre knock-in (Gcg-Cre) mice (Stock No. 030663) were acquired from the Jackson Laboratory, while Gcg-IRES-CreERT2 knock-in (Gcg-CreERT2) mice were generated via CRISPR-Cas9 by Gempharmatech CO. ROSA-LSL-tdTomato reporter mice (Stock No. 007909) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. For generating conditional Crebbp and Ep300 knockout mice, Crebbp/Ep300flox/flox mice were crossed with Gcg-Cre mice. The resulting F1 progeny (Crebbpflox/wt/Ep300flox/wt; Gcg-cre) were interbred with Crebbpflox/wt/Ep300flox/wt mice. This breeding strategy yielded the following specific genotypes: Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300wt/wt; Gcg-cre (CBPαKO) mice, Crebbpwt/wt/Ep300flox/flox; Gcg-cre (p300αKO) mice, Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/wt; Gcg-cre (CBPαKO/p300HE) mice, Crebbpflox/wt/Ep300flox/flox; Gcg-cre (CBPHE/p300αKO) mice, and Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/flox; Gcg-cre (CBP/p300αDKO) mice. Cre-negative littermates from each mating combination were used as WT controls. To generate inducible α-cell-specific Crebbp and Ep300 double knockout (iCBP/p300αDKO) mice, Gcg-CreERT2 mice were crossed with Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/flox mice. For induction of Cre-mediated recombination, mice aged 6–8 weeks received three intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich #T5648) at 100 µg/g body weight, with each injection spaced 24 h apart. For lineage tracing studies, ROSA-LSL-tdTomato reporter mice were further bred with CBP/p300αDKO or iCBP/P300αDKO mice to facilitate tracking of Crebbp or Ep300 deletion in specific cells. Both male and female mice were used in this study. All mice were kept on a C57BL/6J background and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, following a standard 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food (normal chow) and water. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., Shanghai, China.

Genomic PCR for mouse genotyping

Mouse genotype was detected with genomic DNA prepared from tail using One Step Mouse Genotyping Kit (Vazyme, PD101-01). Multiple pairs of primers were used to identify different genetic modifications. For Crebbp/Ep300 floxed alleles, Crebbp-F1/Crebbp-R1 and Ep300-F1/Ep300-R1 primer pairs were used. The Crebbp-F1 and Crebbp-R1 primers were used to distinguish WT (147 bp) or floxed Crebbp allele (245 bp), whereas the Ep300-F1 and Ep300-R1 primers were used to distinguish WT (416 bp) or floxed Ep300 allele (521 bp). For Cre recombinase detection, three different Cre systems were genotyped: Gcg-Cre was detected using Gcg-Cre-P1, Gcg-Cre-P2, and Gcg-Cre-P3 primers; Gcg:CreERT2 was identified using Gcg:CreERT2-F1 and Gcg:CreERT2-R1 primers; and Rosa26 reporter alleles were genotyped using Rosa-P1, Rosa-P2, Rosa-P3, and Rosa-P4 primers to distinguish between different Rosa26 allelic states. The primer sequences used for genotyping mice are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Metabolic studies

Random and 16 h-fasting blood glucose concentrations were measured using a glucometer (Accu-Chek, Roche, Mannheim, Germany) by tail vein sampling. For pyruvate tolerance test, the mice in an overnight-fasting state received an i.p. injection of sodium pyruvate (2 g/kg body weight, Sigma-Aldrich), blood glucose concentration was measured before and 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after pyruvate injection. Fasting plasma glucagon level was quantitated using homogenous time-resolved FRET assays (Cisbio/PerkinElmer). Fasting plasma insulin level was detected by mouse insulin Elisa kit (Mercodia). The level of total fasting plasma amino acids was measured using a commercially available Total Amino Acid assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute).

We performed the metabolomics analysis by Q300 Kit according to a detailed procedure (Metabo-Profile, Shanghai, China). A ultraperformance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) system (ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ-S, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) was used to quantitate all targeted metabolites in this study by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The volcano plot integrated the log2 (Fold Change) and P-value. variable importance in projection (VIP) was obtained based on the OPLS-DA model. Differentially expressed metabolites were selected with VIP ≥ 1 and P-value < 0.05. The Z-score indicates standard deviations concerning the mean of the control group. The raw data are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

For GCGR mAb treatments, the animals were treated weekly with 5 mg/kg of a humanized monoclonal anti-GCGR antibody (REMD 2.59) intraperitoneally for 4 weeks via intraperitoneal administration as previously reported67.

Immunofluorescence staining and image analysis

Mouse whole pancreas (including head, body, and tail) was dissected, fixed in 4% formalin, and processed for paraffin embedding. For the immunostaining analysis, entire pancreatic tissues were continuously sectioned at 5-μm thickness. The primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Then, tissue sections were treated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody and stained with DAPI. For cell quantification in the immunofluorescent staining, six to eight equally spaced sections (which covered the entire pancreas) per pancreas were imaged using a TissueGnostics microscope or a ZEISS LSM880 confocal microscope, and the total numbers of positive staining cells from three to five mice per group were counted manually. The α cell area from the glucagon antibody-stained sections and β cell area from the insulin antibody-stained sections were measured using HistoQuest software (TissueGnostics).

Ex vivo islet assays

Mouse pancreatic islets were isolated as described in our previous study68. For amino acid starvation experiment, islets from 4 weeks old C57BL/6J mice were overnight recovered in RPMI-1640 containing 16.7 mM glucose and 10% FBS. They were then cultured in RPMI-1640 containing 16.7 mM glucose and 0.2% BSA or Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing 16.7 mM glucose and 0.2% BSA for 50 min. For arginine re-stimulation experiment, starved islets were then stimulated with 400 μM arginine for 10 min. At the end of the culture, these islets were fixed in 4% formalin for 24 h and subsequently blocked in 1% agarose gel and embedded in paraffin. These islets paraffin blocks were continuously sectioned at 5-μm thickness. Then immunofluorescence staining was performed on the tissue section to detect the phosphorylated level of S6 in Gcg+ cells.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis

For the single-cell RNA-seq, islets were pooled from three Gcg-Cre;ROSAflox/+ mice and three CBP/p300αDKO;ROSAflox/+ mice at 4 weeks of age. Each group contained 300–400 islets, which were digested with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min at 37 °C. Digestion was terminated with an equal volume of ice-cold RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and the sample was centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min. The suspension was then sieved and the pellet was resuspended in ice cold PBS containing 0.2% BSA. Cell viability and number were assessed by Trypan blue staining and the Eve automatic cell counter, respectively. Approximately 10,000–15,000 cells for each sample were loaded into the 10× Chromium Chip B according to the 10× Genomics Single Cell 30 Reagent Kit v3 GEM protocol. The sequencing strategy used was the Illumina NovaSeq PE150. The scRNAseq data were subsequently processed using Cell Ranger v. 5.0.1 (10× Genomics) to make fastq files for each sample. Cell Ranger Count aligned reads to the mm10 reference genome via STAR. To enable mapping of tdTomato-derived transcripts, the sequence for tdTomato was added to the reference genome and GTF file. Cell filtration was performed with Seurat v4.0.0, removing low-quality sequencing data (parameters: nFeature > 200, nUMI > 1000, log10GenesPerUMI > 0.7, mt <30%, percent of rbc <0.05) and potential doublets were removed by DoubleFinder69. Count matrices were normalized and scaled using the NormalizeData function with standard parameters. Dimensionality reduction was performed via PCA to enable cell clustering. Cell clustering was done using the FindClusters method with variable-feature 2000 and resolution 0.4. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (u-MAP) was performed. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)70 was used to complete Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG term enrichment analysis with the Molecular Signatures Database C5 GO gene sets and C2 KEGG gene sets (version 7.2) separately. Slingshot71 could provide the lineage structure and pseudotime variables inferences for cells along each lineage. The slingshot analysis was performed by slingshot R package (version 1.8.0). Initially, the dimensionally reduced Seurat object was transformed into a SingleCellExperiment object by as.SingleCellExperiment function. Pancreatic α cells from WT mice were selected as a starting cluster for the trajectory inference. Then, FitGAM function in tradeSeq package72 (version 1.4.0) was used to fit the nonlinear function between gene expression and pseudotime using negative binomial generalized additive model. By using the associationTest function, the top 100 genes whose expression levels significantly differed from pseudotime were chosen to create a heatmap.

To classify hormone-positive and hormone-negative cells, Gcg+ and Gcg− cells were defined using the Pareto density-based cutoff strategy described by Perez-Frances et al.73. Normalized Gcg expression values were fitted with Pareto density functions, and cells above the Pareto-derived threshold were classified as Gcg+, whereas those below were classified as Gcg−. The same approach was applied to define tdTomato+ and tdTomato− cells. The cutoff values used for classification were Gcg: 4.384858 and tdTomato: 2.281258.

Transmission electron microscopy

For ultrastructure observation, islets from 3 to 4-week-old WT and CBP/p300αDKO mice were immediately hand-picked without filtration after digestion. Each group contained 200 islets. Then the purified islets were chemically fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer at real temperature for 2 h. After washing, specimens were osmicated with 1% osmium tetraoxide in the same buffer. Then, specimens were washed and dehydrated in ethanol, embedded into Epon 812. After polymerization, 50–100 nm sections were cut and stained. Micrographs were taken by Transmission Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Cell culture and treatment

α-TC1-6 cells (a mouse glucagonoma cell line) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #10567-014) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 10 mM HEPES, and 100 IU/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Empty plasmids and shRNA interference plasmids targeted Slc7a2 were designed and synthesized from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Plasmid transfection was carried out by Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For inhibiting CBP/p300 HAT activity, 3 μM A-485 was used to treat α-TC1-6 cells. After infection or A-485 treatment, cells were harvested for RNA isolation or protein extraction and further analysis. For amino acid starvation and arginine re-stimulation experiments, α-TC1-6 cells were incubated with HBSS buffer containing 1 g/L glucose and 0.2% BSA for 50 min (amino acid starvation) and then treated with 400 μM arginine for 10 min. The target sequences of shRNA plasmids are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

qPCR and Western blotting analysis

For qPCR, total RNA was extracted from α-TC1-6 cells using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme). cDNA was synthesized using a HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The relative mRNA level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−△△Ct method. The △Ct was computed with normalization to 18 s rRNA or beta-actin, which served as the internal control. Primer sequences can be obtained in Supplementary Table 3.

α-TC1-6 cells were treated with RIPA lysis buffer (Aksmics, Shanghai, China) for total protein extraction. Histone was extracted using the EpiQuik Total Histone Extraction Kit (EpiGentek, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibodies are listed as following: rabbit anti-S6 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-pS6Ser240/244 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-CREB (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-pCREB (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), mouse anti-HSP90 (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Rabbit anti-H3K9Ac, rabbit anti-H3K18Ac, rabbit anti-H3K27Ac, rabbit anti-H4K12Ac, rabbit anti-H4K8Ac, and rabbit anti-Histone H4 were all purchased from PTM Bio (1:1000 dilution). HSP90 or Histone H4 was used as an internal control to normalize band intensity. Horseradish peroxidase-coupled goat-anti-rabbit IgG or goat-anti-mouse IgG (1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were used as a secondary antibody. The primary antibodies used in the experiments are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

ChIP-qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed using an EZ-ChIP Chromatin Immunoprecipitation kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, α-TC1-6 cells were fixed using the formaldehyde with 1% formaldehyde and homogenized in cell lysis buffer.

DNA was sheared to fragments at 100–1000 bp using sonication. Chromatins were incubated and precipitated with anti-H3K27Ac (Cell Signaling Technology) or IgG (negative background control) antibodies. Precipitated DNA was analyzed by real-time qPCR, using primers listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each experiment was replicated at least three times for each condition. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. We used two-tailed Student’s test for comparison between groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD for comparisons among three or more groups. P < 0.05 is used to declare statistical significance. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001. All data were presented as means ± SEM (standard error). Data from representative experiments, including micrographs and Western blots, are confirmed in at least three independent biological replicates with consistent and reproducible outcomes.
