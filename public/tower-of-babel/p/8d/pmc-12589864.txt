Anti-Thymocyte Globulin Treatment of Steroid-Refractory T-Cell Hepatitis in Children: A Case Series
Authors: Rebecca Farr, Abigail Russi, Liver Care Center, Reinaldo Garcia, Anas Bernieh, Anna L. Peters
Published: 2025

Pediatric severe acute hepatitis (SAH) carries a high risk of progression to acute liver failure (ALF), an unpredictable condition with significant mortality.1,2 Emerging evidence suggests that a subset of indeterminate SAH is caused by T-cell-driven immune dysregulation, labeled T-cell hepatitis (TC-hep), with reported aberrant T-cell activation in peripheral blood and liver tissue.3, 4, 5, 6 In clinical practice, pediatric gastroenterologists have empirically used steroids for these patients; however, data on the effectiveness of this remain limited.7,8 Given this, there is growing interest in a T-cell-targeted therapy. Notably, the TRIUMPH (TReatment for ImmUne Mediated PathopHysiology, NCT04862221) trial is investigating the efficacy of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) in pediatric indeterminate ALF.9 However, ATG has not been studied prior to critical liver injury with failure.

We present a single-center case series exploring a novel role for ATG in treating TC-hep. We report eight children with steroid-refractory TC-hep who were treated with ATG between June 2017 and December 2024, with a median follow-up of 15 months. None of the children developed ALF and therefore would not have met inclusion criteria for the TRIUMPH trial.9

All patients were previously healthy White, non-Hispanic children, with 7/8 being male. The median age at presentation was 10 years. They presented with new-onset jaundice and abdominal pain. Initial evaluation revealed severe cholestatic hepatitis, characterized by a median alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 2075 U/L and a total bilirubin level of 9.7 mg/dL, along with mildly impaired synthetic function (median international normalized ratio 1.42) (Table 1). Routine investigations for autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson’s disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, and viral hepatitis (including adenovirus, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein–Barr virus) were negative. Two patients had elevated antinuclear antibody titers but negative anti-smooth muscle and anti-liver/kidney microsomal antibodies. After excluding these more common etiologies, all underwent liver biopsy prior to the initiation of treatment. Biopsies revealed severe mixed lymphocytic portal and parenchymal inflammation with a paucity of plasma cells. Immunohistochemistry, performed on six patients, revealed dense CD3+/CD8+ positivity, with minimal CD20+ detection (Figure A1). All had evidence of T-cell activation, including an elevated plasma soluble IL-2 receptor (median soluble IL-2R 6313 ng/mL, normal range 137–838 ng/mL). Taken together, the biochemical and histological findings support TC-hep as the etiology in this cohort.

Patients were initially treated with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1–10 mg/kg/day), but biochemical improvement was limited with a median reduction in ALT of 17.5% after 5 days. This contrasted with a separate cohort of four patients who had similar clinical presentations, liver histology, and immune markers but responded to steroid therapy with a median reduction of 37% in ALT after 5 days (Table A1). Given the limited response in the initial cohort, therapy was escalated after a median of 9 days of methylprednisolone to rabbit ATG at 1.5mg/kg for 7 days, using a dosing regimen adapted from our center’s liver transplant steroid-resistant rejection treatment protocol. By the final day of ATG, patients showed significant improvement in liver enzymes and total bilirubin, with a median ALT of 342 U/L and total bilirubin of 3.6 mg/dL (median reductions of 79% and 71%, respectively, from ATG initiation) (Figure 1). Immediately following, they began maintenance immunosuppression with prednisone, with either tacrolimus (5/8 patients) or azathioprine (3/8 patients). Six of 8 patients had sustained normalization of total bilirubin and ALT at a median of 17 and 65 days post-ATG, respectively (Figure 1). One patient required an additional 5-day course of ATG due to rising liver enzymes 29 days after initial ATG treatment, and one had a recurrence of hepatitis 3.5 years later, which was treated with high-dose methylprednisolone and tacrolimus.

Five of 8 patients experienced side effects and/or adverse events following ATG. Two were admitted within 10 days after the last ATG administration for sepsis—one with an unidentified source and one with rhinovirus/enterovirus. Two developed aplastic anemia, a condition reported in patients with immune-mediated hepatitis.10 Two other patients were admitted within 10 days post-ATG for self-limited serum sickness. Lastly, one patient developed herpes simplex virus gingivostomatitis and viremia after a single dose of ATG, which delayed further doses. He completed a course of acyclovir before resuming ATG. Notably, re-evaluation of his liver biopsy revealed no evidence of herpes simplex virus. Serum viral monitoring was not routinely obtained for the other patients.

None of the patients progressed to ALF or required liver transplantation. For immunosuppression, the patients are currently maintained with low-dose prednisone and azathioprine (3 patients), low-dose prednisone and tacrolimus (2 patients), or without any immunosuppression (3 patients). As exemplified by this cohort, there is no clear consensus on the optimal choice or appropriate duration of maintenance immunosuppression for patients with TC-hep.

Our study mirrors other studies of children with more advanced liver injury secondary to TC-hep.3,4 It is not known whether our patients would have progressed to ALF without escalation to ATG. This uncertainty underscores the need for prospective studies to evaluate the safety and efficacy of ATG in patients with SAH due to TC-hep.

Our findings suggest that early identification of immune dysregulation and timely escalation of therapy may prevent further hepatic injury and reduce the need for liver transplantation. Routine immunophenotyping is now common at our center for patients presenting with acute cholestatic hepatitis, and we recommend broader adoption of this approach to aid in diagnosis and guide immunosuppressive management. Future research should focus on identifying biomarkers that predict treatment response and disease trajectory, optimizing steroid dosing and protocols, and defining the ideal timing and duration of second-line therapies like ATG. In addition, longer-term studies are needed to evaluate relapse risk, maintenance immunosuppression strategies, and whether rebiopsy prior to immunosuppression withdrawal is helpful.

Finally, it is crucial that general pediatric gastroenterologists are also aware of TC-hep, as many of these children who maintain normal liver function will likely not need care at a transplant center, again highlighting the need for a standardized approach to diagnosis and treatment.
