Evidence of internal structure validity of the nursing outcome “Mechanical Ventilation Weaning Response: Adult (0412)” for critically ill COVID-19 patients
Authors: Aline Batista Maurício, Viviane Martins da Silva, Agueda Maria Ruiz Zimmer Cavalcante, Larissa Giardini Bruni, Sue Ann Moorhead, Elizabeth Swanson, Karen Dunn Lopez, Marcos Venícios de Oliveira Lopes, Alba Lucia Bottura Leite de Barros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the evidence of internal structure validity of the nursing outcome (NO) “Mechanical Ventilation Weaning Response: Adult (0412)” of the NOs classification for critically ill COVID-19 patients.MethodsA methodological study focused on internal structural validation, part of a multicenter study, was carried out in the intensive care units of two hospitals in Brazil, from 2020 to 2021. Patients in critical condition due to COVID-19 subjected to mechanical ventilation for more than 24 h and with an invasive device attached to the ventilator in the final phase of the weaning process were evaluated. The researchers used an instrument containing sociodemographic and clinical data and a physical examination of the clinical conditions based on the indicators of the NO. An exploratory factor analysis was performed, using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin method, in addition to other additional adjustment indexes. Reliability was assessed using McDonald's omega, Cronbach's alpha, and Gutmann's lambda 6 coefficients.FindingsAmong the 20 indicators evaluated, 6 were included in the final structure organized into two factors, the first being composed of spontaneous respiratory rhythm, spontaneous respiratory depth, discomfort, and difficulty breathing on own, and the second of anxiety and fear. This two-dimensional structure explains about 62.9% of the total variance. The final structure showed good internal consistency, suggesting that the indicators were measuring the same construct.ConclusionsThe structure proposed for the NO was shown to be reliable and valid for practice and research to evaluate patients with COVID-19 in the final phase of ventilatory weaning.Implications for nursing practiceThe findings are expected to provide robust evidence for clinical nursing practice. The proposal to organize this NO into dimensions could facilitate the nurse's clinical reasoning and make the monitoring of indicators more organized and faster for the evaluation of patients in ventilatory weaning.

INTRODUCTION

Mechanical ventilation (MV) plays a crucial role in sustaining the lives of critically ill patients, especially those with acute respiratory failure. This procedure involves the use of a mechanical ventilator to assist or completely replace the patient's respiratory function, providing oxygen and removing carbon dioxide (Duška et al., 2023). MV, when poorly managed, can cause severe complications that could increase the length of hospital stay, treatment costs, and morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. Therefore, it is crucial to reduce the time a patient is under invasive artificial ventilation, restoring spontaneous ventilation as soon as possible (Duška et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2020). COVID-19, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, can trigger a severe inflammatory response in the lungs, leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). ARDS impairs the lungs’ ability to exchange gases, necessitating MV in severe cases. Prolonged MV can cause additional lung damage and complicate the weaning process, delaying patient recovery (Grasselli et al., 2023; Kondili et al., 2021).

The process of transitioning from artificial to spontaneous ventilation in patients who have been on invasive ventilation for more than 24 h is called ventilatory weaning. Successful weaning corresponds to the maintenance of spontaneous ventilation for at least 48 h after the interruption of artificial ventilation (Pham et al., 2023). Nursing care for patients weaning from MV should be based on consistent data for decision-making regarding care needs, goals to be achieved, and the best care to obtain them (Silva et al., 2020). Nursing care with the goal of weaning can reduce costs, increase safety, and improve quality indicators in the intensive care units (ICU) (Hirzallah et al., 2019).

The nursing outcome (NO) “Mechanical Ventilation Weaning Response: Adult (0412)” (MVWR) is defined as the respiratory and psychological adaptation to the progressive removal of MV. It belongs to the domain Physiologic Health (II) and class Cardiopulmonary (E), and composed of 31 indicators, which are expressed in levels of magnitudes on the two types of 5-point Likert scale (Moorhead et al., 2024).

For measurement instruments that are used in clinical health and nursing practice, such as NOC outcomes, it is crucial to assess certain properties such as reliability and validity (Souza et al., 2017). Reliability is the ability of an instrument to measure what it proposes without errors in a homogeneous and stable way when replicated in different circumstances. Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support interpretations of a test's consequences for the test’ intended uses (American Educational Research Association et al., 2018; Souza et al., 2017).

The present study highlights the validity of the internal structure of this outcome, which aims to evaluate the organization of the items to measure the phenomenon, and in this instance, the response to ventilatory weaning. The validity of the internal structure reflects how the elements are dimensioned, the importance of each one in the measurement of the phenomenon, and the agreement with the theory that supports the instrument (American Educational Research Association et al., 2018). Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the evidence of internal structure validity of the NO “Mechanical Ventilation Weaning Response: Adult (0412)” of the NOs classification for critically ill COVID-19 patients.

METHODS

Design

This is A methodological study focused on internal structural validation that is part of a multicenter study carried out in ICU in two large, public teaching and research hospitals part of the Brazilian Universal Healthcare System and where nurses cared for patients with COVID-19 during the pandemic.

Sample

This study utilized a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method; however, the generalization of the sample adheres to standard recommendations for factor analysis, which suggest determining global sample size based on the number of items present in the instrument (Hair, 2019). In this study, 20 NO MVWR indicators were evaluated, as they had already undergone a content validation process with judges and suggested for the intern structure validation (Sá et al., 2024). They are the following: spontaneous respiratory rate, spontaneous respiratory rhythm, spontaneous respiratory depth, apical heart rate, PaO2 (partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood), PaCO2 (partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood), arterial pH, oxygen saturation, positive end-expiratory pressure, chest x-ray findings, respiratory secretions, anxiety, fear, impaired cough reflex, adventitious breath sounds, atelectasis, restlessness, discomfort, difficulty breathing on own, and difficulty communicating needs.

Thus, a number of five subjects was estimated for each indicator evaluated, resulting in a sample estimate of 100 subjects. During the data collection period, 106 patients who met the study's inclusion criteria were identified, corresponding to the final sample used in the research.

Population

Data collection took place from December 2020 to December 2021, and each patient was evaluated at a single time. Patients aged over 18 years, of both sexes, hospitalized in critical condition due to COVID-19, on MV for a period of more than 24 h, were included. The criteria for collecting patients weaning from MV were patients who were still using an invasive device (endotracheal cannula or tracheostomy) coupled to a mechanical ventilator in pressure support mode and who had a sedation level between −1 and +2 on the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale with at least one spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) to ensure better homogeneity of the sample.

Main research variables

In each hospital, two evaluators with more than 2 years of practical and theoretical experience in standardized nursing terminology and intensive care received standardized, hands-on training to ensure consistent clinical evaluation. The evaluators identified patients with the COVID-19 diagnosis, who were weaning from MV, and who met the criteria above.

Subsequently, the data collection instrument was completed, whereby information from the patient's medical record was initially collected. After, data collection was conducted at the bedside, either through observational evaluations, data from medical equipment, or physical examination, to contemplate the indicators that constitute the outcome.

Data collections were conducted using a form containing sociodemographic data (gender, age, race, and origin), clinical data (comorbidities, medical devices in use, laboratory and complementary tests, extubation attempts, Glasgow Coma Scale, and medications in use), and clinical indicators (data exposed in the instrument validated by the judges who define the indicators and their levels of magnitude).

For subjective data, such as anxiety and fear, we use patient self-reported Visual Analog Scale for Anxiety, a well-established tool for mechanically ventilated patients (Chlan, 2004; Chlan & Savik, 2011). For discomfort, interpreted as pain, was assessed via researcher-observed Brazilian version of the Behavioral Pain Scale, also validated for this population (Azevedo-Santos et al., 2017; Morete et al., 2014). In the present study, which began in 2020, the NOC sixth edition was used during the construction of the study protocol (Moorhead et al., 2018).

The bedside evaluation consisted of observing of the rhythm, depth, and frequency of breathing; pulmonary auscultation to identify breath sounds and adventitious sounds; observation of respiratory secretion in terms of type, quantity, and color; observation of the cough and swallowing reflex; questions about feelings of fear and anxiety; verification of the ability to communicate needs; and difficulties in breathing alone through the presence of respiratory distress within one minute observed.

Data analysis

The data were collected manually and later tabulated in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Statistical analyses were performed in the R package.

An exploratory factor analysis was used to identify the structure composed of factors and items that best represented the NO. A saturated solution (with all outcome indicators) was adjusted to identify relevant items and possible candidates for exclusion. These items were initially excluded when they presented factor loadings lower than 0.4 for all factors of the saturated solution. After the exclusion of these items, new exclusions were made for those who presented a measure of adequacy of the sample by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin method lower than 0.5 and who did not agree with the total score of the scale.

For each alteration of the structure evaluated, the Bartlett test was applied to verify the assumption of sphericity and the chi-square test to verify the global fit of the model. The analysis of the model was based on the correlation matrix, and the number of factors was defined considering those that presented eigenvalues >1. The varimax orthogonal rotation method was used with a factorization method based on minimum ranks.

Additional adjustment indices were presented, such as the root of the mean square error of approximation, which can vary from 0 to 1, with ideal values less than 0.05; the standardized residual mean square root (SRMR), which can vary from 0 to 1, with ideal values less than 0.08; the Comparative Adjustment Index (CFI) and the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), which can vary from 0 to 1 and with ideal values greater than 0.9.

A path diagram with the relationships between the indicators and the factors is also presented. In this diagram, the arrows indicate the direction of the relationship between items and factors, with wider arrows indicating greater uniqueness.

The scree plot with the eigenvalues of the factors and the data simulated by parallel analysis will also be presented in order to allow a visualization of the number of factors retained. McDonald's omega (ω), Cronbach's alpha (α), and Gutmann's lambda 6 (λ6) coefficients were used to evaluate reliability, and values above 0.7 were considered adequate.

RESULTS

A total of 106 patients were included, out of which 55.66% (N = 59) were male and predominantly white (N = 68; 64.15%), with a mean age of 52.92 years (median 53.5; SD 13.71). Regarding the presence of clinical comorbidities, 49 patients (46.22%) were hypertensive, 30 were diabetic (28.30%), 40 were obese or overweight (37.73%), 5 had a history of dyslipidemia, and 12 were smokers (11.32%). Two patients had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (2.83%), and two had asthma (1.88%). Most patients (89.96%) were diagnosed with COVID-19 through RT-PCR and were being ventilated by orotracheal cannula (72.64%). The median number of weaning attempts was 1.00 (IQR 0.750) at the time of data collection, and the median number of days to start weaning from the day they started MV was 7.00 (IQR 7.00).

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin index demonstrates that the factorial model was adequately adjusted to the sample data both for the general measure and for the indicators included in the final structure, all of which were above 0.5 (Table 1). The Bartlett's test rejects the null hypothesis of sphericity, demonstrating that the matrix of factor loadings is distinct from the identity matrix, denoting the existence of correlations between the indicators included in the final structure (p < 0.001). In addition, the global fit test of the model accepts the null hypothesis, indicating a good fit of the structure with the selected items (p = 0.270).

The final structure obtained from the rotated matrix was composed of two factors: the first being composed of spontaneous respiratory rhythm, spontaneous respiratory depth, discomfort, and difficulty breathing on own, which explained 33.7% of the total variance, and the second being composed of anxiety and fear, which explained 29.2% of the total variance. It should be considered that two indicators of factor 1 showed high singularity, reducing the explanatory capacity of this factor. DISCUSSION

Ventilatory weaning requires the direct participation of the nurse, which is decisive for the success of the event (Silva et al., 2020). The factors that influence intensive care nurses in weaning from MV show the complexity of this role (Lavelle & Dowling, 2011). Commonly, COVID-19 patients experience prolonged weaning with extubation failures, with many of them never completing an SBT due to high mortality (Dorado et al., 2023). A review study revealed that approximately half of the patients remained intubated for 15 days (Kondili et al., 2021). The identification of factors that predict weaning success and survival and the risks of failure can help nurses assess MV weaning readiness, improve outcomes, and reduce the mortality rate (Aldabayan et al., 2023).

When analyzing factor 1, composed of the indicators spontaneous respiratory rhythm, spontaneous respiratory depth, discomfort, and difficulty breathing on own, it is perceived that these four indicators express the patient's psychophysiological clinical manifestations that may be present with alterations and influence the ability to maintain spontaneous breathing during ventilatory weaning. Because nurses are closer to the patient, they are often the first to identify clinical alterations such as these. These conditions can be perceived when performing the nursing process in a patient with unfavorable respiratory conditions, when a complete pulmonary and respiratory physical examination should be conducted (American Nurses Association, 2021).

The depth of breathing is determined by the range of respiratory motion, which is the degree of movement or excursion in the chest wall. Ventilatory movements can be deep or superficial, normal or difficult. Respiratory rhythm, on the other hand, refers to the sequence, form, and interval of respiratory incursions, which should be observed by the movement of the chest and abdomen for a period (Jarvis & Eckhardt, 2020). Both indicators are controlled by the respiratory center, located bilaterally in the medulla oblongata and pons in the brain medulla. The respiratory center can be divided into three major groups of neurons that stimulate inspiration, promote expiration, and control respiratory rate and amplitude (Hall & Hall, 2021).

It is recommended that this aspect of spontaneous ventilation be explored more deeply in research to help nurses understand and standardize this assessment. It is noteworthy that the indicators have not only proven to be significant for the outcomes, but it has also garnered attention in studies involving nursing diagnosis in diverse populations. One study demonstrated that the depth of breathing in children was pointed out as one of the characteristics that best helps in the differentiation of respiratory diagnoses. The evaluation of 136 children with respiratory infections over a 10-days revealed that this defining characteristic was the most common from the first (77.9%) to the eighth (48.1%) day (Pascoal, 2011; Pascoal et al., 2012).

In another study that analyzed nursing diagnoses in children with respiratory symptoms in Brazil, it pointed to changes in respiratory rhythm (96.7%) and changes in respiratory depth (73.3%) as one of the defining characteristics of higher prevalence in this population (Chagas et al., 2011). Another study that evaluated the temporality of the ND dysfunctional ventilatory weaning response (DVWR) (034) revealed that shallow breathing is a clinical sign that is found in a late period, denoting worsening of the ND and deterioration of the patient's clinical condition, thus demonstrating the relevance of being aware of its occurrence and urgency in actions (Cerqueira et al., 2012).

Additionally, it is known that discomfort, which we can also understand as pain, another indicator part of factor 1, can influence the rate and rhythm of breathing, making breathing shallower. In fact, there is a bidirectional relationship between pain and breathing. It is possible to observe changes in the breathing pattern in response to pain, as well as the use of breathing techniques to relieve and control pain (Jafari et al., 2017).

Pain commonly occurs in adult ICU patients, regardless of their admission diagnoses. Often, pain can hinder certain care in the ICU, such as early mobilization and weaning from MV (Barr et al., 2013; Pota et al., 2022). In addition, when a patient weans from ventilation, they also wean from sedation and adjust to sedoanalgesia drugs, which can affect their responsiveness, level of agitation, and comfort (Adams et al., 2020).

A medium-range theory that sought to establish a theoretical basis for the ND DVWR (034) brings in one of its propositions, based on clinical relationships pointed out in the literature, that pain acts as a disabling factor, which can lead to failure of ventilatory weaning, most likely due to the increase in ventilatory demand (Lemos et al., 2020). The literature points to favorable effects of systematic pain assessment; however, conclusive results are still lacking due to the lack of strong evidence from controlled clinical trials (Georgiou et al., 2015). A prospective cohort study showed an increase in the chance of weaning from the ventilator (OR = 1.40) and a decrease in the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia (OR = 0.75) in the group of patients routinely evaluated for pain (Payen et al., 2009).

The indicator “difficulty breathing on own” included, in its definition, signs and symptoms that, in the literature, are related to respiratory distress or dyspnea (Sá et al., 2024). The evaluation of the difficulty in maintaining spontaneous ventilation in the present study revealed that a significant portion of the patients were able to maintain spontaneous breathing without great discomfort. This observation suggests that respiratory function was preserved sufficiently to allow spontaneous ventilation.

A review study showed that although it is not a primary indicator of concern for the initiation of weaning from MV, respiratory distress is described as one of the main stressful symptoms during weaning by patients. In fact, dyspnea is recognized as one of the sensations that has the greatest effect on the patient's mood and that plays a role in the development of feelings of anxiety or loss of control, shown as predisposing factors for weaning failures. This review also identified dyspnea as a good predictor and indicator of changes in the patient's respiratory status during weaning (Merchán-Tahvanainen et al., 2017).

The literature also points to a strong association between dyspnea and anxiety. Patients on MV with dyspnea are more likely to have anxiety than non-dyspneic patients (71% and 24%, respectively). For example, interventions that relieve anxiety reduce dyspnea, and adjustments to ventilator settings thought to improve dyspnea alleviate anxiety, suggesting a causal relationship. In the patient-centered literature, patients reported how the sensation of dyspnea made them feel anxious, afraid, and panicked. This would worsen the dyspnea, which in turn would increase the panic, leaving them trapped in a cycle of shortness of breath and fear (Demoule et al., 2024).

When evaluating factor 2, composed of the anxiety and fear indicators, it is noticed that these two items converged the two highest factor loadings from the factorial structure obtained (0.970 and 0.866, respectively). Anxiety, however, showed less uniqueness among the items of the structure, being notably an indicator that most influenced the others and the final structure. Anxiety during ventilatory weaning has harmful effects on the process, such as agitation and potential accidental extubation, increased oxygen consumption, need for increased sedation, or restrictions include immobility, changes in the level of consciousness, and loss of protective reflexes (Tate et al., 2012).

A recent study with 247 patients who were on MV for more than 24 h demonstrated a negative relationship between those who felt anxiety during the MV period and a higher rate of weaning failures (r = −0.207; p = 0.002), as well as feeling fear during SBT (r = −0.127; p = 0.046). However, family support during MV demonstrated a positive relationship with weaning success (r = 0.144; p = 0.023) (Elsehrawy & Saleh, 2024).

A study that sought to examine the physiological and psychological factors that can predict the risk of repeated weaning failures and prolonged MV developed a model based on Pavlov's theory of association in which contiguous pairings between a conditional stimulus and an unconditional aversive stimulus produce conditional fear. From this, significant causal pathways were found between fear and anxiety (r = 0.77; p < 0.001), anxiety and respiratory function (r = 0.24; p < 0.05), and respiratory function and weaning outcomes (r = 0.42; p < 0.001) (Chen et al., 2011). Because the patient's mental and emotional state affects their functional status, these changes may be related to the success or failure of MV removal. The reverse is also valid, and the feeling of excessive respiratory effort can evoke an emotional response (e.g., fear and anxiety) as strong as that associated with shortness of breath (Demoule et al., 2024).

The nursing team plays a fundamental role in improving communication with patients and anticipating their needs. Physical, psychological, and technological care is important to provide holistic care that avoids procedural failure and makes the weaning process effective (Merchán-Tahvanainen et al., 2017). Thus, the importance of intensive care nurses in verifying the presence and measuring of these indicators in weaning patients is emphasized, to monitor their evolution through the NOC outcome and thus verify the effectiveness of their actions.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations of the study can be pointed out. One of the issues concerns the criterion for selecting a sample of patients with a sufficient level of consciousness to obey commands, which limited the spectrum of weaning patients who could participate. Also noteworthy is the difficulty in establishing communication with relatives to obtain acceptance of participation, which occurred due to restrictive measures and social distancing, resulting in losses of patients in the sample.

Our study's findings, particularly regarding the structure of NO, are primarily derived from a specific population of mechanically ventilated. Consequently, the generalizability of these results to other patient populations, different clinical settings, or those with varying pathologies should be approached with caution.

Furthermore, although our retained indicators demonstrated robust clinical representativeness within this context, other factors or dimensions not included in our final structure, such as respiratory secretions, impaired cough reflex, and adventitious breath sounds, when consider patient's airway clearance ability and guide nursing interventions like suctioning frequency, might be highly relevant in broader clinical applications and warrant consideration in future research.

It is recommended that the study be repeated in different populations to compare the results and verify whether the structure can be applied in other clinical situations.

Implications for nursing practice, research, and education

The proposal to organize this NO into dimensions can facilitate the nurse's clinical reasoning, making the monitoring of indicators more organized, faster for the evaluation of patients in ventilatory weaning during their phases. It provides nurses with a more accurate assessment of patients’ experience in ventilatory weaning, enhancing their clinical decision-making.

This study also has a positive impact on the development of standardized language, being the first to evaluate the evidence of the internal structure validity of the NO MVWR (0412), providing robustness based on best practices. Similar studies may contribute to the development of knowledge in the profession by generating visibility with documentation when using standardized nursing language, highlighting the importance of the nursing process in health care.

CONCLUSION

Among the 20 indicators evaluated, 6 were part of the final structure organized into two factors. This two-dimensional structure explains about 62% of the variance of the NO. The indicators belonging to the structure are spontaneous respiratory rhythm, spontaneous respiratory depth, discomfort, and difficulty breathing on own, integrating factor 1 and anxiety and fear to factor 2. Of these, anxiety was the indicator with the lowest value of uniqueness, revealing greater influence on the others. It is noteworthy that there is a moderate amount of variability in the data that is not being captured by the identified factors. This indicates that other variables or dimensions not included in the analysis may be relevant and should be considered in future research or clinical evaluations.

The values of indices that measure reliability (McDonald's omega, Cronbach's alpha, and Gutmann's lambda 6) were above 0.7, demonstrating good internal consistency of the NO MVWR, showing that the indicators measure the same construct.

Therefore, a valid internal structure was found for the NO MVWR (0412) for patients in critical condition due to COVID-19, especially those in the last phase (pre-extubation).
