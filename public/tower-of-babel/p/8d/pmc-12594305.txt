Outcome of Prepectoral Direct-to-Implant Breast Reconstruction With the Poly-4-hydroxybutyrate Wrap
Authors: Thomas J. Sorenson, Carter J. Boyd, Kshipra Hemal, Jenn J. Park, Chris Amro, Nicholas Vernice, Alexis Lakatta, Oriana Cohen, Mihye Choi, Nolan Karp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Prepectoral direct-to-implant (DTI) breast reconstruction has gained popularity for reducing postoperative pain, animation deformity, and the number of surgical procedures. However, the limited vascularized tissue overlying the implant presents challenges. This study evaluated mean 18-month outcomes in prepectoral DTI patients using a poly-4-hydroxybutyrate (P4HB) wrap designed to optimize reconstructive results.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed all consecutive patients who underwent prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction with our P4HB-implant construct. Data were collected via chart review.Results:There were 50 patients with 87 breasts included. The mean (± SD) age of patients was 52.7 (± 11.7) years, and the median (interquartile range) body mass index (BMI) was 23 (5) kg/m2. The mean (± SD) follow-up time was 18.1 (± 5.1) months. Thirty (30 of 50, 60%) patients did not require further procedures beyond the index operation. No patients had implant malposition/dystopia. Of patients requiring a subsequent operation, the majority (12 of 20, 60%) of operations were for aesthetic optimization. Eight (8 of 87, 9.2%) breasts required a subsequent operation due to complications with 4 (4 of 87, 4.6%) of these breasts requiring removal of the construct. Increased BMI and age were found to significantly decrease the odds of rippling (odds ratios 0.73 and 0.89, respectively), and increased BMI was also found to significantly increase the odds of major complications (odds ratio 1.21).Conclusions:This is the first study reporting mean 18-month P4HB outcomes in prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction at full hydrolysis. Most patients did not require revisional procedures during the follow-up period, and reoperations were primarily for aesthetic concerns. These findings suggest that P4HB is an effective adjunct for implant stabilization in breast reconstruction.

Takeaways

Question: Is poly-4-hydroxybutyrate a safe and effective material for soft tissue support and implant stabilization in prepectoral direct-to-implant (DTI) breast reconstruction, particularly during the long-term period of 18 months?

Findings: Sixty percent of patients did not require further surgery beyond the DTI operation. No patients had implant malposition or dystopia. Of the patients requiring a subsequent operation (n = 12), 60% of operations were for aesthetic optimization. Four breasts required implant removal.

Meaning: This study found that using poly-4-hydroxybutyrate in prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction is safe and effective, with most patients avoiding reoperation during an 18–month follow-up and experiencing few complications after the material fully resorbs.

INTRODUCTION

Prepectoral direct-to-implant (DTI) breast reconstruction has regained significant traction as a viable alternative to subpectoral approaches, offering advantages such as a decreased number of surgical procedures, reduced postoperative pain, elimination of animation deformity, and preservation of full pectoral muscle function.1–4 By placing the implant entirely in the prepectoral space, this technique seeks to optimize aesthetic outcomes while enhancing patient satisfaction and recovery. However, concerns surrounding soft tissue support, vascularized implant coverage, and long-term aesthetic outcomes remain key considerations.

To address these challenges, the use of biological and synthetic meshes has emerged as the standard of care when soft tissue support is needed in breast reconstruction. Although all products are approved for soft tissue support by the Food and Drug Administration, none have a specific breast indication, so the use of these products is currently considered off-label. Nonetheless, they represent a promising adjunct in prepectoral breast reconstruction, and although various options are available for use, no superior modality has emerged.5 In hopes of addressing some of the limitations encountered with other types of soft tissue scaffolds or acellular dermal matrix (ADM), our group has started using poly-4-hydroxybutyrate (P4HB; GalaFLEX LITE) as a promising adjunct in prepectoral breast reconstruction. P4HB, a monofilament, knitted, bioresorbable soft tissue scaffold, provides temporary structural reinforcement while promoting host tissue integration and collagen deposition, ultimately resorbing within approximately 18 months.6,7 Initially introduced for soft tissue reinforcement in aesthetic surgery, P4HB has since demonstrated broad utility in breast reconstruction, particularly in improving implant support and mitigating complications such as implant malposition and contour irregularities.8–10

We use P4HB in our practice as a novel wrap that can be secured within the breast pocket and creates a temporary pseudotextured implant.11 Despite its increasing adoption, extended clinical data evaluating the outcomes of prepectoral DTI reconstruction with P4HB remain limited. In this study, we presented our institutional experience and mean 18-month outcomes in a series of patients who underwent prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction with our novel breast implant–P4HB wrap.

METHODS

Study Design and Patient Selection

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis of all patients who underwent prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction with our novel P4HB wrap at NYU Langone Health between September 2022 and January 2024. Eligible patients were identified through a review of institutional medical records. Inclusion criteria included patients undergoing prepectoral DTI reconstruction with our breast implant–P4HB wrap by one of the senior authors of the study (N.K., M.C., O.C.). Exclusion criteria consisted of tissue expander–based breast reconstruction or cases using an alternative method of prepectoral DTI reconstruction. Indications for prepectoral DTI reconstruction included well-perfused mastectomy skin flaps with low concern for mastectomy flap ischemia or necrosis, nonradiated chest wall, small breast volume and/or grade II ptosis or less, appropriate breast volume expectations, and an overall healthy patient profile (body mass index [BMI] < 30 kg/m2, no history of tobacco use, no major vascular comorbidities). Contraindications for prepectoral DTI reconstruction included thin mastectomy flaps with threatened vascularity, a radiated chest wall, large breast volume and/or grade III ptosis requiring substantial skin redraping, patient desire for a substantial increase in size of breasts, or an overall unhealthy patient profile with high-risk comorbidities (obesity, uncontrolled diabetes, active tobacco use). All patients who are planned to undergo prepectoral DTI reconstruction with these authors receive the P4HB wrap. If patients are not perfect candidates or may require adjuvant radiation after new information is uncovered during the oncological resection, we will modify our plan intraoperatively and not perform prepectoral DTI reconstruction. The type of mastectomy performed at our institution is a multifactorial decision made collaboratively by breast surgeon, plastic surgeon, and patient and includes breast size, breast ptosis, tumor size and location, patient comorbidities, and patient desire.

Surgical Technique

All patients underwent skin-sparing or nipple-sparing mastectomy performed by 4 distinct breast surgeons. Prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction was conducted by 3 plastic surgeons using a standardized P4HB technique, as previously published.11 Implant selection was based on patient-specific anatomical considerations and surgeon preference, with a range of implant sizes and profiles used. Drains were placed in all patients and removed when output was between 20 and 30 mL for 2 consecutive days.

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

All data were manually extracted from electronic medical records by the authors. As DTI reconstruction is intended to be the definitive breast reconstruction procedure, the primary outcome was the number of surgical procedures to reach the final reconstruction. The secondary outcomes were the incidence and type of unexpected postoperative complications (seroma, hematoma, incision dehiscence, skin flap necrosis), the incidence of known postoperative complications (rippling, capsular contracture, animation deformity, implant flipping, implant rupture), and the incidence of reconstruction revisions. Major complications were defined as requiring greater than 24-hour admission to the hospital or return to the operating room. Minor complications were defined as those manageable without hospital admission and/or return to the operating room.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and surgical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as means ± SDs or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses were performed using Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. Mann-Whitney U test (for 2 groups) was applied for the P value on the median row, and 1-way ANOVA was used for the P value on the mean row for continuous variables. Multivariate regression was conducted to identify independent risk factors associated with patient outcomes. Independent risk factors included in the multivariate analysis include plastics attending, breast attending, time of operation, BMI, age, and length of follow-up. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted under the supervision of a biostatistician.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board at NYU Langone Health, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were de-identified and handled in compliance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations to ensure confidentiality.

RESULTS

Demographics

There were 50 patients with 87 breasts included in the study. Thirteen patients received unilateral reconstruction; 37 patients received bilateral reconstruction. All patients underwent DTI reconstruction in the prepectoral plane. The mean (± SD) age of patients was 52.7 (± 11.7) years, and the median (interquartile range) BMI was 23 (5) kg/m2. No patients were current smokers or had diabetes mellitus. No patients had prior breast surgery (lumpectomy, breast reduction, or breast augmentation). Four distinct breast surgeons performed the mastectomies; 3 distinct plastic surgeons performed the reconstructions. Most (78%) patients underwent skin-sparing mastectomy; the remainder underwent nipple-sparing mastectomy. The mean follow-up time of our cohort was 18.1 (± 5.1) months. All patients had follow-up longer than 1 year; the shortest follow-up time in this cohort was 14 months. Demographics are summarized in Table 1.

Outcomes

Thirty (30 of 50; 60%) patients did not require further procedures beyond the index operation. Of the cases requiring a subsequent operation, 12 (12 of 20, 60%) were for aesthetic optimization due to rippling. Eight (8 of 87, 9.2%) breasts required a subsequent operation due to postoperative complications with 4 (4 of 87, 4.6%) of these breasts requiring removal of the breast implant–P4HB construct due to incision dehiscence (n = 2), hematoma (n = 1), and infected seroma (n = 1). Two (2 of 50, 4%) patients experienced minor complications during the follow-up period, including mild incisional dehiscence (n = 2), and 1 patient had lower extremity deep vein thrombosis requiring therapeutic anticoagulation. Additionally, 2 patients experienced a flipped implant that was addressed in the office, and 1 patient experienced Baker grade II capsular contracture. No patients experienced implant malposition or dystopia during the follow-up period. All complications occurred in discreet breasts and patients (ie, no bilateral reconstruction patient experienced a complication in more than 1 breast). These outcomes are summarized in Table 2.

Multivariate Regression

On multivariate regression, an increase in BMI was found to significantly decrease the odds of rippling (odds ratio [OR], 0.73; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.54–0.92; P < 0.05). An increase in age was also found to significantly decrease the odds of rippling (OR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.79–0.97; P < 0.05). An increase in BMI was also found to significantly increase the odds of major complications (OR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.03–1.51; P < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

We present the first series of patients undergoing prepectoral DTI reconstruction using P4HB support, with a mean follow-up of 18 months. In this cohort, the initial prepectoral DTI reconstruction was the definitive reconstructive procedure for most patients (Fig. 1). Of those requiring subsequent operations, most additional procedures were for mild aesthetic improvements through fat grafting. Increased BMI and age were found to significantly decrease the odds of rippling, and increased BMI was also found to significantly increase the odds of major complications in our patients. These data suggest that P4HB used as a wrap around the breast implant can be used in a safe and effective manner when performing prepectoral DTI reconstruction while providing excellent aesthetic outcomes.

Though prepectoral DTI reconstruction has been established to have relative safety,1 this approach is not without challenges and limitations.2,12,13 Although useful for preventing animation deformity and reducing postoperative pain, prepectoral breast reconstruction involves a nonnegligible major complication profile with rates approaching 25% in some instances.14–19 To address some of the shortcomings of prepectoral DTI reconstruction with other types of soft tissue scaffolds or ADMs, we designed and used a P4HB wrap for the breast implant. The major complication rate with this approach (9%) is comparable to other methods of performing prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction, as reported by Avila et al16 (7.6%) and Sigalove et al17 (9%). These complications were largely related to soft tissue insults from the mastectomy, which suggests that damage to the skin flaps during the mastectomy cannot be overcome even with optimized breast implant positioning and soft tissue support, as previously suggested.20,21 In these cases, if there is any concern for future mastectomy flap necrosis or skin edge viability that may lead to dehiscence, subpectoral reconstruction or tissue expander placement should be considered in lieu of prepectoral DTI reconstruction. If there is clinical concern, handheld SPY indocyanine green angiography is a valuable tool for objectively assessing skin flap perfusion. Of note, patients with higher BMI demonstrated higher complication rates, underscoring the importance of appropriate patient selection when performing prepectoral DTI reconstruction.

With some published reports of high rates of successful 1-stage reconstruction,22 prepectoral DTI reconstruction is often discussed with patients as a single-stage operation. This “one and done” approach may be an alluring option for patients seeking to minimize the time to definitive reconstruction, but the literature demonstrates that this “one and done” paradigm is often not the case with DTI breast reconstruction, as patients frequently require additional surgeries.23 Further investigations that have evaluated whether the use of soft tissue support may decrease the need for subsequent operations also describe an approximate 50% reoperation rate for reconstructions with and without soft tissue support.24,25 Although more than half of our patients did not require a second operation, 25% of our cohort did elect for additional operations to address aesthetic concerns. These revision procedures predominantly consisted of autologous fat transfer to address superior pole rippling.26 Interestingly, in our study cohort, lower BMI patients more commonly required secondary fat grafting to the superior pole to address implant–chest wall step-offs and rippling of the implant. Reduced soft tissue and thinner mastectomy flaps in these patients likely contribute to this finding.27,28 This information can be appropriately used to counsel patients who may be at heightened risk of requiring subsequent operations. When counseling patients preoperatively, the strong possibility of future minor operations to address aesthetic concerns must be discussed. Moreover, DTI reconstruction should be more aptly described as 1 fewer operation, not only 1 operation.

P4HB, a synthetic soft tissue scaffold, has previously been found to be useful in optimizing breast reconstruction outcomes both alone and alongside biological soft tissue supports, though most studies are in 2-stage or revision breast reconstruction.8,29–33 Notably, Sigalove et al8 investigated the use of an implant wrap made of AlloDerm and P4HB, and Karp and Salibian34 have also previously reported on the use of a biological plus synthetic hybrid wrap. As an extension of this idea, we eliminated the biological mesh from the wrap and used only P4HB to create a temporary pseudotextured implant that reduces microshifting between the breast implant and parenchyma and provides functional tabs to reliably secure the device into position within the breast pocket.11 We feel that minimizing microshifting of the breast implant within the breast pocket is crucial for reducing dead space and risk of subsequent seroma, which is a known risk for reconstruction loss.

In the first study investigating the sole use of a breast implant–P4HB wrap in primary prepectoral DTI reconstruction, we report a low rate of breast seroma development (1 of 87, 1.2%) during the follow-up period and feel that our construct is effective at minimizing seroma in conjunction with the standard use of postoperative drain tubes. In our cohort, the patient with an infected seroma underwent a skin-sparing mastectomy and was a daily “vape” user with an elevated BMI (30 kg/m2). However, P4HB cannot overcome complications related to decreased vascularity of the mastectomy skin flaps, and these patients still progressed to reoperation and/or loss of their reconstruction. The stability of the implant position in the breast pocket with the P4HB construct may also contribute to lower capsular contracture rates, though long-term follow-up studies are required in the reconstructive literature before this can be determined.35 However, this is not a perfect strategy. We feel anecdotally that ADM does create a marginally better plane for fat grafting than P4HB, as the ADM is slightly thicker than the P4HB. However, we still prefer the P4HB for its primary purpose of cost-effectively resuspending the implant on the chest wall in a fixed position before resorption, as fat grafting is still possible with this construct if necessary. However, comparative prospective studies are needed to directly assess this potential benefit.

Although not directly evaluated in this study, standardized postoperative care protocols likely also contribute to favorable outcomes.36 In our practice, all patients receive closed-suction drains, which are removed once drainage drops to 20–30 mL/d for 2 consecutive days. We use sterile, nonadherent dressings with gentle compression garments to minimize shear and support the breast pocket. Consistent application of these postoperative protocols may help reduce seroma formation and implant migration.

This is the first study to report mean 18-month outcomes of P4HB use in prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction with follow-up at the point that P4HB is fully hydrolyzed. Nevertheless, there are limitations. This study is retrospective, which has certain inherent potential biases, including selection bias and missing data. Though the technique aims to be identical, the study includes 4 different breast surgeons and 3 different reconstructive surgeons. We also did not have any non-P4HB or subpectoral plane control groups included in the study for comparison. Further follow-up is also needed to assess longer term outcomes, such as the development and/or progression of capsular contracture, as well as to investigate the association that we identified between age and risk of rippling. In addition to favorable clinical outcomes, our group has previously demonstrated that use of a hybrid wrap configuration can reduce costs compared with biological mesh alone, but future studies are needed to formally assess the cost-effectiveness of P4HB-based prepectoral reconstruction strategies.34 Finally, as plastic surgery increasingly adopts technologies for objective pre- and postoperative assessment,37–39 future studies may benefit from incorporating these modalities into outcome evaluation. Moreover, validated Patient Reported Outcome Measures such as the BREAST-Q, SCAR-Q, and BODY-Q provide critical insight into the patient’s perspective and would enhance future investigations of satisfaction and quality of life in patients undergoing prepectoral reconstruction with P4HB.

CONCLUSIONS

This is the first study reporting mean 18-month P4HB outcomes in prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction at full hydrolysis. Most patients receiving prepectoral DTI reconstruction with this novel breast implant–P4HB construct have not required subsequent revisional procedures during our mean 18-month follow-up. Of the patients requiring additional procedures, most were elective procedures for mild aesthetic concerns, with a higher likelihood in patients with lower BMI. Additional operations for postoperative complications are uncommon but can result in construct explantation. These data suggest that P4HB is a safe and efficacious adjunct for implant control and soft tissue support when performing prepectoral DTI breast reconstruction.
