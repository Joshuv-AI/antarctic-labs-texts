Racial Discrimination and Hwa-byung, a Korean Medicine-Based Anger Syndrome, among Foreign Residents in South Korea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chan-Young Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesResearchers have traditionally viewed Hwa-byung (HB) as a culture-bound syndrome specific to Korea. However, no studies have examined how racial discrimination experienced by foreigners and naturalized citizens contributes to HB in Korea’s increasingly multicultural society. The present study investigated how experiences of racial discrimination influence HB among foreigners and naturalized Korean citizens living in Korea and offers a new perspective on the concept of culture-bound syndromes.MethodsIn June 2025, a web-based cross-sectional survey collected responses from 601 foreigners and naturalized citizens aged 19 years or older who had lived in Korea for at least one year. The analysis used multivariate binary logistic regression with HB presence as the dependent variable.ResultsThe overall prevalence of HB was 23.8%. Individuals who consistently experienced racial discrimination had 10.581-fold higher odds of HB (95% CI 1.920-58.308), representing the strongest association in the analysis. Poor subjective health status (OR = 2.602), higher HB trait scores (OR = 1.134), higher depression scores (OR = 1.147), and higher stress scores (OR = 1.130) also significantly increased the likelihood of HB.ConclusionHB among foreigners and naturalized citizens residing in Korea shows preliminary associations with experiences of racial discrimination, raising the theoretical question of whether HB’s etiological mechanism may extend beyond its original cultural context. The finding that those who consistently experience racial discrimination showed more than a 10-fold higher odds of HB presence represents a preliminary finding that warrants replication in larger samples.

INTRODUCTION

Korean society has rapidly shifted toward multiculturalism as internationalization has accelerated over the past few decades [1, 2]. By 2024, the number of foreign residents had surpassed 2.5 million, and the population of naturalized Korean citizens continued to grow [2]. This demographic change has introduced new social challenges, including rising concerns about the mental health of foreigners and naturalized citizens [3]. For example, Kim et al. reported that multicultural families, particularly those with lower socioeconomic status, experienced significantly higher levels of perceived stress and extreme sadness or despair than monocultural families, underscoring the need to strengthen social support networks for this vulnerable group [3]. Stress experienced during migration and cultural adaptation processes can lead to various mental health problems [4]. Language barriers, cultural differences, social discrimination, and economic difficulties are known to affect immigrants’ psychological adaptation negatively [4].

Hwa-byung (HB) is an anger-related syndrome rooted in Korean medicine and was designated as a Korea-specific culture-bound syndrome in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) by the American Psychiatric Association in 1996 [5]. This recognition in the DSM system has evolved significantly with the transition to DSM-5, which replaced the term “culture-bound syndrome” with a more nuanced framework of “Cultural Concepts of Distress (CCD)” [6]. This shift reflects a move away from viewing these conditions as exotic, localized phenomena toward understanding them as specific ways that different cultural groups experience, understand, and communicate suffering [7]. Within the CCD framework, HB functions not as a relic of traditional society but as a dynamic idiom of distress that adapts to contemporary social stressors. It provides a culturally meaningful way to express psychological pain that might otherwise remain unspoken during migration and settlement [7, 8].

HB is understood to result from accumulated suppressed anger and stress, characterized by physical and psychological symptoms including chest tightness, heat sensations, indigestion, depression, and anxiety [5, 9]. Although HB is known to occur mainly in middle-aged women in the sociocultural context of Korea, shaped by Confucian culture [9], a recent study found a high prevalence of HB in the MZ generation, which is socio-culturally distinct from this classically recognized vulnerable group [10]. This shift in the affected demographic suggests a broadening of HB vulnerability beyond traditionally recognized groups. While the Kim et al. study addresses family-level dynamics, the mental health vulnerabilities identified therein are likely to extend to individual foreign residents and naturalized citizens who face comparable socioeconomic and acculturative stressors [3].

Research on mental health among foreigners and naturalized citizens in Korea is still in its early stages. Existing studies have primarily focused on depression and adaptation problems among married immigrant women [11, 12], with very limited research on HB. On the other hand, previous HB research has mainly been conducted among Koreans, identifying socioeconomic stress, HB traits, and interpersonal conflicts as major risk factors [10, 13]. However, research on HB among foreigners and naturalized citizens is virtually nonexistent, leaving an insufficient understanding of HB prevalence and related factors in these populations. Among the mental health problems immigrants may face, HB warrants particular attention for theoretically grounded reasons. HB develops when individuals chronically suppress anger in response to perceived injustice and accumulated stress [5, 9]. Immigrants in Korea face these conditions directly, including persistent racial discrimination, social marginalization, and the structural powerlessness experienced by non-native residents in a predominantly mono-ethnic society. The overlap between HB’s core pathogenesis and the psychosocial burdens of immigration suggests that HB may not be exclusively a Korean cultural phenomenon, but may also emerge in other cultural groups exposed to similar social stressors.

As Korea’s multiculturalization accelerates, a systematic understanding of mental health problems among foreigners and naturalized citizens is needed [3]. HB, as a mental health problem occurring within Korea’s unique cultural context, may become an important health issue for foreigners adapting to Korean society. Therefore, this study aims to examine the prevalence of HB among foreign residents and naturalized Korean citizens residing in Korea, analyze factors associated with HB presence, including sociodemographic, health-related, and psychosocial characteristics, and contribute to the theoretical understanding of whether culture-bound syndromes such as HB may manifest across diverse cultural groups exposed to similar social stressors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design and setting

This cross-sectional survey examined factors associated with HB among foreigners and naturalized Korean citizens in Korea and adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines (Supplementary File 1) [14].

2. Study participants

Study participants were foreign nationals or naturalized Korean citizens aged 19 years or older who had resided in Korea for at least one year. The one-year minimum residence requirement was based on acculturation research indicating that meaningful acculturative stress, such as discrimination, cultural conflict, and identity negotiation, typically emerges and becomes clinically observable after at least one year of sustained residence in a host country [4].

3. Data collection

The research team collected data through a web-based survey administered to Hankook Research panel members. The survey ran for 10 days, from June 9 to June 18, 2025. Of the 11,126 individuals who received e-mail invitations, 3,372 accessed the survey link, and 1,530 began the questionnaire. A total of 601 respondents met the eligibility criteria and completed the survey, yielding a 39.3% completion rate. Hankook Research verified and coded all responses in accordance with its standard procedures, and the researchers excluded four respondents identified as insincere from the final analysis (Fig. 1).

4. Measurement instruments

Sociodemographic characteristics included sex, age, Korean-language ability (beginner, intermediate, advanced), naturalization status, nationality (Western countries, Asian countries, other regions), area of residence, purpose of residence (marriage/family, employment, study/other), and marital status. Health-related characteristics included subjective health status, measured on a 5-point scale (very healthy to very poor), and the presence of chronic disease, assessed through diagnoses of 14 conditions: hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive diseases, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lower respiratory disease, joint disease, thyroid disease, cancer, mental illness, and dementia. Psychosocial characteristics included racial discrimination experiences, measured on a 5-point scale (never to always); HB traits and symptoms, assessed using standardized HB scales, with HB defined as a symptom-scale score ≥ 30 points [15]; mental health status, measured using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [16]; and anger, assessed using the State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory (STAXI) state-anger and trait-anger scales [17].

5. Statistical analysis

The research team analyzed the collected data using SPSS 18.0. The analysis summarized participants’ general characteristics as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. The analysis compared characteristics between foreigners and naturalized citizens using chi-square tests and independent t-tests. Univariate analyses examined differences in each variable by HB status, using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables.

Multivariate binary logistic regression identified factors associated with HB. The dependent variable was HB presence, and the independent variables included sociodemographic, health-related, and psychosocial characteristics. We set reference groups for categorical variables as follows: sex (male), naturalization status (foreigner), nationality (others regions), residence area (non-metropolitan), Korean language ability (beginner), residence purpose (study/other), marital status (unmarried/other), subjective health status (average), chronic disease presence (absent), and racial discrimination experience (sometimes). We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values. We reported results as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and considered p < 0.05 statistically significant. We generated forest plots in Python (matplotlib library) to visualize ORs and 95% CIs, highlighting significant predictors (p < 0.05) in red and non-significant predictors in black.

6. Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board of Dong-Eui University Korean Medicine Hospital approved this study (Number: DH-2025-04; Approval Date: May 28, 2025). All procedures followed relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The online survey platform obtained electronic informed consent from all participants before they participated in the study. Participants received detailed information about the study’s purpose and procedures, assurances of anonymity and confidentiality, and notification of their right to withdraw at any time. They provide electronic consent by clicking “I agree to participate” before accessing the survey questions. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants could discontinue the survey at any point without penalty.

RESULTS

1. General characteristics of study participants

Of the 601 study participants, 389 (64.7%) were foreigners, and 212 (35.3%) were naturalized citizens. The majority were women (396, 65.9%), lived in metropolitan cities (401, 66.7%), and had an overall mean age of 41.81 ± 10.93 years. Korean language ability was reported as advanced in 331 (55.1%), intermediate in 192 (31.9%), and beginner in 78 (13.0%), with significant group differences (p = 0.022). Regarding the purpose of residence, marriage/family was the most common reason (349, 58.1%), followed by employment (174, 29.0%) and study/other (78, 13.0%). Naturalized citizens reported significantly higher proportions of marriage/family residence (84.9% vs 43.4%) and being married (81.1% vs 62.0%) compared to foreigners (both p < 0.001). Most participants rated their subjective health as “average” (272, 45.3%), and 235 (39.1%) reported having at least one chronic disease. Experiences of racial discrimination were reported as “sometimes” by 232 (38.6%), “rarely” by 171 (28.5%), “never” by 127 (21.1%), “frequently” by 54 (9.0%), and “always” by 17 (2.8%) (Table 1).

2. HB and mental health characteristics

The mean HB trait score was 29.78 ± 9.22, and the mean HB symptom score was 21.24 ± 11.29. Based on a symptom cutoff score of ≥ 30, 143 participants (23.8%) were classified as having HB, including 85 foreigners (21.9%) and 58 naturalized citizens (27.4%), with a non-significant trend toward a higher prevalence in the naturalized group (p = 0.134). DASS-21 scores were as follows: depression 5.30 ± 4.70, anxiety 4.92 ± 4.51, and stress 6.52 ± 4.57. State anger and trait anger scores were 14.96 ± 6.07 and 17.61 ± 5.64, respectively. HB trait scores were significantly higher in naturalized citizens than foreigners (30.92 ± 9.40 vs. 29.16 ± 9.08, p = 0.026), while all other mental health and anger-related variables showed no significant group differences (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

3. Univariate analysis by HB presence

In the univariate analysis comparing participants with and without HB, the HB group had a significantly lower mean age (40.13 ± 11.90 vs. 42.34 ± 10.57 years; p = 0.034). Subjective health status also differed significantly between groups, with the HB group reporting substantially higher rates of “poor” or “very poor” health (p < 0.001). Chronic disease was more prevalent among individuals with HB (51.7% vs. 35.2%, p = 0.001). Experiences of racial discrimination were significantly more frequent in the HB group, particularly in the “always present” and “frequently present” categories (p < 0.001). All measured psychological variables—including HB trait scores, HB symptom scores, depression, anxiety, stress, state anger, and trait anger—were significantly higher in the HB group (all p < 0.001). There were no significant group differences in sex, naturalization status, residence area, nationality, Korean language ability, marital status, or residence purpose (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

4. Factors related to HB presence

Multicollinearity did not appear among the independent variables; VIF values ranged from 1.083 to 3.928 (all < 5), and tolerance values ranged from 0.255 to 0.924 (all > 0.2). The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several variables significantly associated with HB. Participants reporting poor subjective health had significantly higher odds of HB compared to those reporting average health (OR = 2.602, 95% CI: 1.171-5.779, p = 0.019). Experience of racial discrimination also showed a strong association with HB: those who reported “always” experiencing discrimination had 10.58 times higher odds of HB compared to those who reported “sometimes” (OR = 10.581, 95% CI: 1.920-58.308, p = 0.007). Among psychosocial variables, each 1-point increase in HB trait score was associated with a 13.4% increase in the odds of HB (OR = 1.134, 95% CI: 1.088-1.181, p < 0.001). Similarly, depression (OR = 1.147, 95% CI: 1.035-1.271, p = 0.009) and stress (OR = 1.130, 95% CI: 1.019-1.253, p = 0.021) scores were significantly associated with increased odds of HB. In contrast, sex, age, naturalization status, residence area, nationality, Korean language ability, residence purpose, marital status, chronic disease presence, as well as anxiety, state anger, and trait anger were not significantly associated with HB presence (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 2, Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study has significant meaning as the first research to investigate HB prevalence among foreigners and naturalized citizens residing in Korea. The overall HB prevalence of 23.8% is similar to or slightly higher than that reported in a recent study targeting Koreans [10], suggesting that unique stressors experienced by foreigners and naturalized citizens in Korean society may influence HB prevalence.

A noteworthy preliminary finding of this study is that individuals who consistently experience racial discrimination showed 10.581-fold higher odds of HB presence compared to those who sometimes experience discrimination. However, this finding relies on a small subgroup of participants (n = 17) who reported always experiencing discrimination, and the wide confidence interval (95% CI: 1.920-58.308) indicates substantial statistical uncertainty. Therefore, readers should interpret this result with caution and treat it as a hypothesis-generating finding rather than definitive evidence. Nevertheless, this suggests a possible association between social discrimination and HB presence that merits further investigation in larger samples [5, 9, 10]. This finding aligns with previous research showing that discrimination profoundly affects mental health outcomes among immigrant populations [3, 18, 19], although no prior studies have documented its specific relationship with HB. The significant impact of racial discrimination identified in this study aligns with Meyer’s Minority Stress Theory [20]. This framework suggests that marginalized individuals face chronic distal stressors, such as objective discrimination, which subsequently trigger proximal stressors like the internal expectation of rejection [20]. For immigrants in Korea, constant exposure to racialized distal stressors likely triggers chronic physiological arousal and sustained suppression of anger, both core features of HB. Unlike typical depression, the somatic manifestations of HB embody the physical consequences of structural social injustice. As a result, HB functions as a somatized indicator of the health inequities faced by non-native residents in a predominantly mono-ethnic society.

The relationship between racial discrimination and HB suggests a potential expansion of our understanding of culture-bound syndromes. Although HB has traditionally been described as a Korea-specific condition rooted in Confucian cultural norms and suppressed emotions [5, 9], the present findings indicate that the syndrome may emerge as a broader response to chronic social stressors, particularly discrimination. This interpretation challenges the conventional view that culture-bound syndromes are confined to specific cultural groups and suggests that HB may reflect a more universal human reaction to sustained social injustice and marginalization. Support for this universalist perspective appears in parallels between HB and other cultural idioms of distress, such as ataque de nervios in Latin American populations [21]. Like HB, these conditions often emerge in response to acute social stressors and domestic disruptions, serving as a culturally accepted “cry for help” when individuals feel overwhelmed by circumstances beyond their control [21]. However, while ataque de nervios often involves overt emotional outbursts, HB is traditionally characterized by the “suppression” of anger. The finding that HB occurs among immigrants regardless of gender or nationality suggests that the syndrome’s characteristic “suppression” of anger is not an inherently Korean cultural trait. Instead, it appears to function as an adaptive response used by marginalized individuals who lack the social power to express their grievances openly.

Poor subjective health status emerged as another significant risk factor, with individuals reporting poor health having 2.6 times higher odds of HB presence. This finding is consistent with the well-established bidirectional relationship between mental and physical health, where psychological distress can manifest as somatic symptoms and vice versa [9, 10]. The prominence of physical symptoms in HB, including chest tightness, heat sensations, and digestive problems [9], may reflect the psychosomatic nature of stress responses among individuals facing challenges of cultural adaptation. This somatic presentation distinguishes HB from other mental health conditions, such as major depressive disorder, where psychological symptoms typically predominate [5]. The embodied nature of distress in HB may be particularly relevant for immigrant populations who may express psychological suffering through physical complaints due to cultural factors or stigma associated with mental health issues.

Among psychological factors, higher HB trait scores, depression scores, and stress scores significantly increase the risk of HB. The association with HB traits suggests that certain personality characteristics or coping styles may predispose individuals to HB presence when exposed to stressors [15, 22]. The relationships with depression and stress align with previous research in Korean populations and underscore the interconnected nature of various mental health conditions [5, 10]. Interestingly, anxiety did not show a significant association in our multivariate model, which may reflect the specific manifestation of distress in this population or the particular nature of stressors they face. Similarly, neither state nor trait anger showed significant associations, despite anger being conceptually central to HB as an “anger syndrome” [5]. The pattern of results indicates that HB presence is more closely linked to internalized distress, reflected in depression and stress, than to overt anger expression. This association appears especially relevant in contexts of chronic discrimination, where social constraints may limit individuals’ ability to express anger openly.

The absence of significant associations with sociodemographic factors such as age and sex is noteworthy. In particular, while previous research has identified women as the primary vulnerable group for HB in Korean society [5, 10], our findings suggest that the risk factors may be different for immigrant populations. The equal vulnerability of both men and women to HB in our immigrant sample contrasts with the traditional understanding that women’s higher HB prevalence in Korean society stems from gender-based injustices within Confucian patriarchal structures [5]. Our findings indicate that HB presence may be driven primarily by experiences of unfair treatment and social injustice rather than by gender-specific discrimination alone. They further show that, whether the discrimination is based on gender or race, the core mechanism underlying HB involves the shared experience of systematic unfairness and powerlessness. This interpretation broadens the understanding of HB from a gender-specific response to discrimination to a more universal response to social injustice.

The finding that Korean language ability was not significantly associated with HB presence is somewhat surprising, as language barriers are commonly cited as major stressors for immigrant populations [3, 4], suggesting that while language proficiency facilitates practical adaptation, it may not necessarily protect against the psychological impact of discrimination and social exclusion. Alternatively, it may indicate that discrimination experiences transcend language proficiency levels, affecting both those with limited and those with advanced Korean skills. This finding implies that even individuals with advanced Korean language skills may continue to face discriminatory treatment based on their appearance, accent, or perceived foreign status, suggesting that linguistic assimilation alone is insufficient to prevent exclusion from mainstream Korean society. The persistence of discrimination regardless of language proficiency highlights the racialized nature of exclusion that immigrants face, where cultural markers beyond language continue to mark them as “other” and subject to differential treatment.

These findings have important implications for understanding the evolving nature of culture-bound syndromes in increasingly globalized societies. As populations become more mobile and multicultural societies develop worldwide, traditional conceptualizations of the culture-specific mental health condition (i.e., HB) may need to be revised. Our results suggest that HB may represent a more universal pattern of stress response that can manifest across different cultural groups when exposed to similar social stressors, particularly discrimination and marginalization. From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of screening for discrimination experiences among immigrant populations seeking mental health services. Healthcare providers should be aware that individuals reporting consistent experiences of racial discrimination may be at substantially elevated risk for developing HB and other mental health conditions. The strong association between discrimination and HB also highlights the need for culturally sensitive approaches to mental health care that acknowledge the role of social factors in psychological distress. From a public health standpoint, our findings suggest that addressing HB among immigrant populations requires interventions at multiple levels. While individual-level treatments focusing on stress management and coping skills remain important, our results indicate that broader social interventions addressing discrimination and promoting multicultural acceptance may be crucial for prevention. The development of anti-discrimination policies, cultural competency training, and community integration programs may be essential components of comprehensive mental health promotion strategies.

This study has several limitations to consider. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between discrimination experiences and HB presence. Longitudinal studies would be valuable for better understanding the temporal relationship between these factors. Second, the use of web-based surveys may have introduced selection bias, potentially excluding individuals with limited internet access or digital literacy. Third, the measurement of racial discrimination relied on self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects. Fourth, the study did not account for potentially protective factors such as social support networks, community connections, or resilience resources that might moderate the relationship between discrimination and HB presence. Fifth, this study defined HB presence using a threshold score of ≥ 30 on the HB Symptom Scale rather than structured diagnostic interviews or clinician-administered diagnostic criteria. Using a threshold-based classification may introduce misclassification and does not equate to a formal clinical diagnosis of HB. Throughout this manuscript, the term HB presence therefore refers specifically to a high symptom burden consistent with HB, and the findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Sixth, the assessment of racial discrimination relied on a single item measured on a five-point frequency scale. Racial discrimination is a multidimensional construct encompassing frequency, severity, and domain-specificity across contexts such as the workplace, housing, healthcare, and interpersonal interactions. Future studies should employ validated multidimensional instruments such as the Everyday Discrimination Scale [23] or the Racial and Ethnic Microaggressions Scale [24]. Domain-specific analyses examining which contexts of discrimination most strongly predict HB presence would provide valuable mechanistic insights. The present study, while limited in its measurement approach, provides important preliminary data to inform the design of such future investigations. Seventh, the study did not assess several potentially important confounders, including social support networks, community integration, length of exposure to discrimination, acculturation stress, and economic precarity. These factors may independently influence both discrimination experiences and HB presence, and their absence from the model limits the interpretability of the observed associations. Finally, the measurement instruments used in this study, including the HB Scale, DASS-21, and STAXI, were originally developed and validated primarily in Korean or Western populations. The study did not establish the cultural validity of these tools among the diverse nationalities represented in our sample. Future research should incorporate cultural validation procedures before applying these tools in multinational immigrant samples.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study represents the first systematic investigation of HB among foreigners and naturalized citizens in Korea, revealing that individuals experiencing consistent racial discrimination have a 10-fold increased odds of HB presence. Our findings suggest a preliminary association between consistent racial discrimination experiences and elevated odds of HB presence, raising the theoretical question of whether HB’s core etiological mechanism, the suppression of anger arising from perceived injustice, may extend beyond its original cultural context. However, this hypothesis requires replication in larger and more diverse samples before stronger conclusions can be drawn. The absence of significant associations with traditional demographic risk factors and the observed prominence of discrimination experiences suggest that social determinants may play an important role in HB among immigrant populations. However, longitudinal research is needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships. These results highlight the urgent need for comprehensive anti-discrimination interventions and culturally sensitive mental health services in Korea’s multicultural society, with broader implications for understanding how culture-bound syndromes manifest across diverse populations in an increasingly globalized world.
