The utility of spirometry and single breath gas transfer measurements to identify low total lung capacity
Authors: Ben Knox-Brown, Chara Alexiou, Sanja Stanojevic, Karl P. Sylvester
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeasurement of total lung capacity (TLC) requires large and expensive equipment. We aimed to investigate whether spirometric restriction and low alveolar volume measured by single breath gas transfer (VA) can be used to identify those with a low TLC.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed data from adults referred to Cambridge University Hospitals between January 2016 and December 2023. We investigated the utility of spirometric restriction (forced vital capacity (FVC) < lower limit of normal (LLN) with forced expiratory volume in 1 s /FVC ≥LLN) and reduced VA (<LLN), to discriminate low TLC measured by plethysmography (TLCpleth <LLN). We assessed agreement between definitions using the Cohen's kappa coefficient and the discriminative ability of spirometric restriction and reduced VA to identify TLCpleth <LLN.ResultsData from 7923 patients were included. The majority (94%) of patients were of European ancestry, 51% were female. Mean age was 58 years. 11% of patients had a TLCpleth <LLN, of which 27% also had spirometric restriction, and 95% had a VA <LLN. Agreement between spirometric restriction and TLCpleth <LLN was fair (κ=0.37). Spirometric restriction had low sensitivity (27%) but high specificity (99%) for discriminating TLCpleth <LLN. Agreement between VA <LLN and TLCpleth <LLN was substantial (κ=0.63). VA <LLN had good sensitivity (95%) and specificity (89%) for discriminating TLCpleth <LLN, except for in patients with the most severe airflow obstruction.ConclusionsA FVC in the healthy range is an effective tool for ruling out restriction. Low VA can be used to accurately identify those with a low TLC, negating the need for formal measurement of static lung volumes when identifying restrictive lung disease.

Background

Total lung capacity (TLC) is generally considered the gold standard measure of lung volume [1]. It is either assessed using body plethysmography or gas dilution techniques such as nitrogen washout and multi-breath helium dilution [2]. Both methods require large and expensive specialist equipment, not routinely available outside of secondary care centres. It is important, therefore, to investigate whether parameters derived from other lung function tests can be used accurately as a surrogate for TLC, and therefore negate the need for its measurement.

Spirometry is the most widely performed lung function test due to its relative simplicity, low cost and the availability of reference equations in a range of populations [3]. The forced vital capacity (FVC) derived from a spirometry manoeuvre reflects the maximum volume of gas that can be forcefully exhaled from the lungs. A low FVC, often referred to as spirometric restriction when in the presence of a normal forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/FVC, is widely used as a surrogate for low TLC [4], despite studies generally showing that spirometric restriction has poor sensitivity for this purpose [5–8]. The limitation of the FVC is that it can be reduced in obstructive lung disease because of gas trapping and airway collapse and also in respiratory muscle weakness due to incomplete emptying [1]. This means that the FVC can be low while the TLC may be normal or elevated. However, studies to date have not investigated whether agreement between the FVC and TLC differs depending on the disease present, and it may be that the FVC is a suitable surrogate for TLC in certain populations.

Single breath gas transfer is a lung function test that assesses the diffusing capacity of the lung [1]. It is not uncommon for diffusing capacity to be reduced even when lung function assessed using spirometry is normal [9]. This has led to the routine measurement of single breath gas transfer in secondary care. As part of the measurement process, the alveolar volume (VA) is determined. This is the volume of the lungs as assessed by an inert tracer gas, normally helium or methane, inhaled during the test [10]. The measurement of VA works in much the same way as the multi-breath gas dilution measurement of TLC. Therefore, it is plausible that the VA is also an accurate estimate of TLC. However, as with the FVC, the accuracy of the VA as a surrogate for TLC is impacted by the presence of obstructive lung disease, where ventilation inhomogeneity and delays in gas mixing can underestimate the VA in relation to TLC by a mean of 2.2 L [11–13]. An alternative approach is to consider VA and TLCpleth with respect to their lower limit of normal (LLN), and assess whether a low VA accurately discriminates a low TLCpleth. If so, this would negate the need for more detailed assessment of static lung volumes when screening for restrictive lung disease, saving time, money, and reducing patient burden. This could have particular impact in community-based diagnostic hubs, where the purpose is to identify physiological impairment before referral to a specialist centre for management.

In this study, we aimed to assess the ability of spirometric restriction and low VA to identify those with a low TLCpleth, both overall and stratified by severity of airflow obstruction. We also aimed to identify whether the level of agreement was different depending on the patient diagnosis, defined using international classification of disease codes (ICD-10).

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively analysed data from individuals referred for lung function testing at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS FT, for any referral reason, between the 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023. Data were included in this study if spirometry, single breath gas transfer and body plethysmography were performed on the same day, if patients were 18 years or older at the time of testing, and had acceptable and reproducible lung function measurements according to the most up-to-date American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standards [2, 10, 14]. Data were excluded if the patient had opted out of their data being used for research purposes, and if they were over the age of 85 years, due to the lack of suitability of the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference equations in this age group. For individuals with serial lung function measurements over time, only the first measurement was included in the analysis. Approval to use the data was provided by the EHR Research and Innovation (ERIN) database data access committee (reference: A096987).

Data collection

Spirometry, single breath gas transfer and body plethysmography were performed on the MasterScreen-PFT-Pro from Vyaire Medical. Trained respiratory physiologists instructed all tests, and only those equivalent to grades A or B according to the most up-to-date ATS/ERS quality standards were included in our analysis [2, 10, 14]. Data were extracted directly from the SentrySuite measurement software from Vyaire Medical, using the Sentry Data Cube. Demographic data included age at time of test, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity (white or non-white) and smoking history (never or ever). Spirometry measures extracted included the FVC (L), the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) (L) and the FEV1/FVC ratio. Single breath gas transfer measurements extracted included the VA (L), total lung gas transfer (mmol·min−1·kPa−1) and carbon monoxide transfer coefficient (mmol·min−1·kPa−1). Static lung volume measurements extracted included the TLCpleth (L), residual volume (RV) (L) and the inspiratory capacity (L). Raw values for spirometry were converted into per cent of predicted and z-scores using GLI race neutral reference equations [15]. Raw values for single breath gas transfer and plethysmography were converted into per cent of predicted and z-scores using the GLI equations limited to people of white European ancestry [16, 17]. The LLN was defined as −1.645 z-scores or the 5th percentile of the healthy population. The LLN for all parameters was calculated according to the GLI reference equations [15–17].

Where available, ICD-10 codes from hospital episode statistics collected at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS FT were extracted from the medical record and linked with patient data in our study database. Codes were collected from encounters, problem lists and medical histories recorded during inpatient and/or outpatient care, if any of the following codes were noted: Asthma: J45.0, J45.1, J45.8, J45.9. COPD: J40.0, J41.0, J41.1, J42.0, J43, J43.1, J43.2, J43.8, J43.9, J44.0, J44.1, J44.8, J44.9, E88.0; Interstitial lung disease (ILD): J60.0, J61.0, J62.0, J62.8, J63.0, J63.1, J63.2, J63.4, J63.5, J63.8, J64, J65.0, J66.0, J67.0, J67.1, J67.2, J67.3, J67.4, J67.5, J67.7, J67.8, J67.9, J70.2, J70.3, J70.4, J84.1, J84.8, J84.9.

Outcome measures

The primary analysis was the level of agreement between spirometric restriction (FVC <LLN with FEV1/FVC ≥LLN) and restriction defined by TLCpleth <LLN and the level of agreement between VA <LLN and TLCpleth <LLN. Secondary outcomes included the level of agreement stratified by ethnicity, severity of airflow obstruction and diagnosis.

Statistics

Normality of the data was determined through inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms. For normally distributed data, the mean and standard deviation (sd) were reported, for non-normal data, median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported. Categorical variables were summarised as the number and percentage in each group. Agreement between spirometric restriction, VA <LLN, and TLCpleth <LLN was calculated using Cohen's kappa coefficient, with level of agreement classified according to Landis and Koch [18]: below 0.0=poor, 0.0–0.2=slight, 0.21–0.40=fair, 0.41–0.60=moderate, 0.61–0.80=substantial, >0.81=almost perfect. The ability of spirometric restriction and VA <LLN to discriminate TLCpleth <LLN was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic analysis, reporting the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios. For both spirometric restriction and VA <LLN, analyses were stratified by ethnicity and by disease according to ICD-10 coding. For VA <LLN, analyses were additionally stratified according to severity of airflow obstruction classified using z-score and Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria [1, 19]. We further investigated the difference between the raw values (in litres) for TLC and VA by constructing Bland–Altman plots stratified by severity of airflow obstruction. We investigated the reasons for discordance and compared differences across discordant groups using ANOVA for comparison of means, Kruskal–Wallis test for comparison of medians and the Fisher's exact-test for comparison of binary variables. To explore causes of discordance, we re-ran analyses in white participants using GLI race-specific reference equations for Caucasians [3]. We also investigated the ability of spirometric restriction to discriminate a low VA. We considered results significant if the p-value was <0.05. Analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023, 14 282 consecutive lung function tests were performed where individuals completed spirometry, single breath gas transfer and body plethysmography on the same day. Of these, 1116 were excluded for not having test quality meeting ATS/ERS grade A or B for all measurements, 4905 were excluded due to being serial measurements on the same patients, 158 were excluded as patients were under the age of 18 years and 180 were excluded as patients were older than 85 years. Data from 7923 individuals were included in our analyses (figure 1). Table 1 summarises the characteristics of individuals included in this study. 94% (7486 of 7923) of patients were white, with females making up 51% (4037 of 7923) of the study population. Mean±sd age was 58.0±15.0 years, with mean BMI being 28.3±6.3 kg·m−2. 58% (4586 of 7923) reported a smoking history. Airflow obstruction classified as having an FEV1/FVC <LLN was present in 54% (4276 of 7923) of patients, while FEV1/FVC <0.70 was present in 61% (4803 of 7923). 11% (870 of 7923) of patients had a TLCpleth <LLN, of which 27% (231 of 870) also had spirometric restriction, and 95% (826 of 870) also had a VA <LLN.

Agreement between spirometric restriction and TLCpleth <LLN was fair, with a kappa coefficient of 0.37 (se: 0.01). Spirometric restriction had low sensitivity (27%) but high specificity (99%) for discriminating TLCpleth <LLN, with an area under the curve of 0.63 (0.61–0.65). Discrimination did not materially change when stratifying by ethnicity (table 2). In white individuals, when the GLI Caucasian spirometry reference equations were applied, discrimination improved compared to when race-neutral equations were used (kappa 0.47, sensitivity 47.8%, specificity 96.7%, supplementary table eTable 4). There was fair agreement between spirometric restriction and VA <LLN, with a kappa coefficient of 0.24, sensitivity of 17% and specificity of 99.7% (supplementary eTable 5).

The level of agreement between VA <LLN and TLCpleth <LLN was substantial, with a kappa coefficient of 0.63 (se: 0.01). VA <LLN had high sensitivity (95%) and specificity (89%) for discriminating TLCpleth <LLN, with an AUC of 0.92 (0.91–0.93). When stratifying by ethnicity, compared to white individuals, non-white individuals with a VA <LLN had a higher sensitivity (98%) but lower specificity (76%) to discriminate TLCpleth <LLN (table 3). When stratifying according to severity of airflow obstruction, there was a dose–response relationship between increasing severity of obstruction and a larger difference between raw values for TLC and VA (figure 2). Concurrently, the ability of VA <LLN to discriminate TLCpleth <LLN worsened (table 3). However, discrimination was acceptable in all but severe airflow obstruction according to z-score criteria and very severe airflow obstruction according to GOLD criteria.

In keeping with disease pathophysiology, individuals classified as COPD and asthma had more airflow obstruction than those with ILD, while those with ILD had lower lung volumes. Discussion

Using retrospective data from individuals referred for lung function testing at a large tertiary care centre, we have shown that spirometry is a poor diagnostic tool for identifying those with a low TLCpleth. Conversely, we have shown that low VA derived from single breath gas transfer can accurately identify those with a low TLC. The accuracy of low VA to discriminate low TLCpleth diminishes with increasing severity of airflow obstruction. However, it is unlikely that individuals with severe airflow obstruction will be investigated for only restrictive lung disease.

Overall, we found that spirometric restriction had low sensitivity but high specificity for discriminating a TLCpleth <LLN. Our results support the previous study by Aaron et al. [7] in patients referred to a university teaching hospital for lung function testing. They found that of those with spirometric restriction, only 41% a had a low TLC, but of those with a normal FVC, only 2.4% had a low TLC. Similar was also shown by Vandevoorde et al. [5] in over 12 000 consecutive patients referred to the pulmonary function laboratory of the Academic Hospital of the University of Brussels. They found that spirometry was useful for ruling out low TLC but had poor positive predictive value. In particular, they found that low FVC had a very high false positive rate in those with airflow obstruction, which as we demonstrate in figure 3b is likely explained by those with moderate–severe obstruction, where dynamic airway collapse and increasing residual volume reduce the FVC [20]. More recently, Torén et al. [6], in a Swedish general population sample aged between 50 and 64 years, found that spirometric restriction had a sensitivity of 34% and specificity of 98% to discriminate low TLC, very similar to the 27% and 99% we have found in our study. Our study is the first to repeat these analyses using the most up-to-date GLI reference equations for spirometry and TLCpleth [15, 16]. We have confirmed previous findings that spirometry can only be used to rule out the presence of a restrictive defect, not to identify those with restriction. We found that this recommendation should be consistent regardless of diagnosis, or whether TLC or VA is used as the measure reference of restriction.

We found that low VA assessed using single breath gas transfer had good sensitivity and specificity for discriminating low TLCpleth, with 95% of those with a VA <LLN also having TLCpleth <LLN. There are limited data for direct comparison; however, a retrospective analysis by Punjabi et al. [11], using data from two American lung function laboratories, investigated the agreement between VA assessed during single breath gas transfer and TLC estimated by helium dilution. They found that while the VA and TLC were closely correlated, the level of agreement decreased with increasing severity of airflow obstruction. They concluded that VA could be used as a surrogate for TLC in those with no–mild airflow obstruction, but not without applying a correction factor in those with moderate–severe obstruction. Similarly, Archer et al. [21], in a small sample of patients with confirmed COPD and IPF, found that VA could be used as a surrogate for TLC measured by helium dilution in those with IPF, but that in those with COPD VA underestimated TLC by a mean of 1.4 L, likely due to delays in gas mixing in poorly ventilated areas of the lung [12]. Similar results were shown by Milite et al. [13], who found in 55 patients with emphysema a mean difference of 2.2 L in the estimation of lung volume between single breath and plethysmography methods. Our study also demonstrated that agreement between VA and TLC decreases with increasing severity of airflow obstruction, with a mean difference of 2.9 L in those with severe airflow obstruction according to z-score criteria. The novelty of our study is that we used the LLN for VA and TLCpleth, rather than directly comparing the raw volumes obtained. As a result, we found that discriminative ability is acceptable in all but severe airflow obstruction, providing evidence that the LLN for VA can be used as a threshold to identify the presence of a genuine restrictive defect, without the need for formal measurement of static lung volumes. This appears to be the case irrespective of diagnosis, in all but those with the most severe airflow obstruction. This adds to previous research, by demonstrating the utility of the LLN for VA, which has not been previously recommended as a diagnostic cut-off to indicate restriction. With regards to ethnicity, our results suggest that the sensitivity and specificity of low VA to discriminate low TLCpleth in non-white individuals is acceptable. However, our study population is predominantly of a white background, making it difficult to generalise our findings to hospital-based populations with more ethnic diversity.

For both spirometric restriction and low VA, we found instances of discordance with TLCpleth in the classification of normal and abnormal physiology. <1% of the study population had spirometric restriction in the presence of a normal TLC. This group also had evidence of a raised RV in the absence of airflow obstruction. The prevalence of this nonspecific pattern (NSP) is lower than previously reported [7, 22, 23]. This is partly explained by our use of race neutral reference equations, as when we re-ran our analyses using GLI Caucasian equations, the prevalence of NSP increased to 3%. The alternative discordant pattern of normal FVC but low TLC was seen in 8% of the study population. This discordant group is harder to explain, as the high prevalence of airflow obstruction seen would normally be accompanied by a higher TLC not lower. One explanation is the use of race neutral spirometry reference equations for spirometry and the use of Caucasian standards for plethysmography, which we found resulted in a higher prevalence of this discordant pattern compared to when race-specific equations were used. 9% of the study population had a low VA in the presence of a normal or elevated TLC. As we have previously mentioned, this is likely due to delays in gas mixing in poorly ventilated areas of the lung [12]. <1% of the study population had a normal VA in the presence of a low TLC. This pattern is unusual from a pathophysiological perspective, as those with low TLC would also be expected to have a low VA. Interestingly, there was a large difference in the median raw values for VA (4.6 L) and TLC (4.1 L) in this group, which would suggest equipment or technician error not detected by the quality control criteria implemented in our analyses, such as a leak [10]. Of note, the inspired volume from single breath gas transfer and the expired volumes from spirometry and body plethysmography were within 10% of each other.

Our study has several strengths. It has a large sample size, which is representative of those patients referred to secondary and tertiary care centres for lung function testing. All lung function measurements were obtained by trained physiologists, and we only accepted manoeuvres of the highest quality. We also used the most up-to-date GLI refence equations to provide an updated view of this topic. Our study is not without limitations. Being a hospital-based study, our results cannot be easily extrapolated to the general population where the prevalence of physiological abnormality is lower. We only had data for a small number of non-white individuals, and due to GLI equations being derived from individuals from European ancestry, similar studies are needed in a diverse range of populations to confirm our findings. Finally, we did not have ICD-10 codes for our entire population, and therefore could only do analysis by diagnosis for a small subsample.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results show that spirometry cannot be used to accurately identify those with a low TLC, but is an effective tool for ruling out restriction when the FVC is in the healthy range. Furthermore, we provide evidence that when low, the VA derived during single breath gas transfer measurement can be used to accurately identify those with a low TLC, negating the need for formal measurement of static lung volumes when investigating for restrictive lung disease, which would save time, money and reduce patient burden.
