History of Presentation

A 23-year-old male former collegiate volleyball player and vigorous exerciser was found to have an aortic root aneurysm on a screening transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) during a return-to-play evaluation following COVID-19 infection. The TTE demonstrated an aortic root diameter of 4.4 cm at the sinus of Valsalva with a trileaflet aortic valve and normal dimensions of the remaining thoracic aortic segments (Figure 1). Aortic regurgitation and mitral valve prolapse were not present. Left ventricular size, wall thickness, and systolic function were normal. Blood pressure (BP) at the time of TTE was 121/70 mm Hg. Subsequent chest computed tomography angiogram measured the aortic root at 4.3 cm and ascending aorta 3.1 cm. Based on these findings, he was referred to our cardiology practice for further evaluation.

On presentation, he was without cardiovascular symptoms and maintaining an active lifestyle including high-intensity aerobic and isometric exercise. He was a competitive athlete since childhood and played collegiate volleyball until his graduation a few months prior. His BP was 132/89 mm Hg, and pulse 70 beats/min. His height was 1.88 m, weight 88 kg, and body surface area 2.07 m2. Physical examination was unrevealing. His arm span was 193 cm, with an arm span-to-height ratio of 1.03. His lower segment was 102 cm, with an upper-to-lower segment ratio of 0.84. His joint hypermobility score was 6/9 using the Beighton score.

Past Medical History

He had intermittent mildly elevated BP readings at past doctor's visits (systolic pressures 135 to 148 mm Hg and diastolic pressures around 80 mm Hg). Besides several orthopedic issues including herniated discs, knee tendinosis, ankle ligament tears, and a pubis symphysis fracture related to a benign tumor, he was without other medical issues. He had a prior magnetic resonance angiography of the head and neck to evaluate an episode of dizziness that did not show evidence of an intracranial aneurysm or vascular malformation.

Of note, his maternal grandfather had a type A aortic dissection at age 50 requiring emergency repair with subsequent redo at age 70. His maternal aunt was under surveillance for an aortic aneurysm; her 2 sisters (including his mother) had normal echocardiograms. He had a brother and a sister who had not been screened (Figure 2).

Differential Diagnosis

The differential for this athlete's aortic enlargement includes aortic remodeling due to athletic training plus increased body size, or aortic enlargement secondary to an aortopathy. Within the category of aortopathy, a wide range of potential etiologies exist including sporadic aortic aneurysm, atherosclerotic disease, bicuspid aortic valve, inflammatory aortic aneurysm, or heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD). HTAD can be subdivided into syndromic or nonsyndromic aneurysmal disorders. Syndromic HTAD includes diseases like Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, and vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Nonsyndromic HTAD (nsHTAD) includes familial thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAAs) for which several causative gene mutations have been identified, although the majority of nsHTADs do not have a currently identifiable gene (Figure 3).

Investigations

Repeat TTE performed 5 months after his initial study revealed unchanged findings. The aortic root diameter measured 4.4 cm with normal dimensions of the remaining thoracic aortic segments. Genetic testing utilizing a genetic aortopathy panel of 60 genes was performed and did not reveal any gene variants associated with aortopathy.

Management

He was initiated on atenolol 25 mg daily for BP management. Physical activity guidelines were discussed in detail, and he was counseled to continue moderate-intensity aerobic/dynamic exercise and avoid intense isometric exercise or exercises that require the Valsalva maneuver such as heavy lifting. Despite the negative genetic test results, clinical suspicion for nsHTAD remained high, and genetic testing will be repeated in 3 to 5 years with updated genetic aortopathy panels.

Outcome and Follow-Up

On return clinic visits, his BP has been well-controlled on atenolol. His surveillance TTEs for the past 3 years have shown an unchanged aortic root diameter at 4.3 to 4.5 cm with normal ascending aorta dimensions. He feels well and remains active while avoiding isometric exercise. He is unsure if his siblings have undergone screening. He will continue to follow up with annual surveillance TTEs and interspersed chest computed tomography angiogram.

Discussion

We present a case of a young former collegiate volleyball player with an incidentally detected TAA. Distinguishing physiologic remodeling from pathological dilation in an athlete can be challenging. The thresholds established in the 2022 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines defining aortic enlargement as ≥4.0 cm, with dissection risk significantly increasing at ≥4.5 cm, were primarily derived from nonathletic populations.1 Complicating the application of these guidelines to athletes is that aortic enlargement may be a component of cardiac athletic remodeling.2 Potential mechanisms underlying athletic remodeling of the aorta include endurance exercise-related increases in stroke volume and cardiac output that increase aortic wall stress and isometric exercise-related elevations of BP that promote an increase in aortic diameter. Despite these factors, several studies of athletic cohorts suggest that aortic root dimensions ≥4.0 cm in males and ≥3.4 cm in females are uncommon, occurring in approximately only 1% and 2% of highly trained athletes, respectively.3,4 Similarly, in male professional basketball players with extreme height and body size, aortic root diameters ranged from 2.5 to 4.2 cm (mean of 3.37 cm), and only 4.6% had dimensions between 4.0 and 4.2 cm.5 Notably, in this cohort, aortic root diameter reached a plateau at the uppermost body size metrics, suggesting that extreme aortic dilation should not be attributed to and cannot be explained by large body habitus alone.5 Our patient's aortic root of 4.4 cm with an aortic size index of 2.13 cm/m2 and Z-Score of 4.01 falls above the 99th percentile established in athletic populations, suggesting true pathologic dilation despite his athletic history and body size. Another approach to normalizing aortic size to height that can be useful in athletes is calculating the cross-sectional area of the aorta (cm) to the patient's height (m), with a threshold of ≥10 cm2/m as a threshold for intervention. In this athlete, his cross-sectional area to height ratio is 8.09 cm2/m, which is below the threshold predictive of increased risk.1

While guidelines recommend genetic testing in individuals with TAA and risk factors for HTAD, management becomes less clear when genetic testing yields negative results.1 Although this athlete's genetic testing was negative, his concomitant clinical data including joint hypermobility, abnormal segment ratio, and history of disproportionate orthopedic problems along with a family history of a type A aortic dissection suggest a potential genetic pathogenesis. Genetic testing identifies causative mutations in only about 30% of patients with familial TAAs, and therefore, negative genetic testing does not exclude HTAD.6 In such cases, patients should be managed as having presumed nsHTAD, with repeat genetic testing in 3 to 5 years as new genes and variants are discovered. In families with ascending aortic aneurysms or aortic dissection, the 2022 ACC/AHA guidelines recommend screening of at-risk biological relatives.1,7 Despite this patient's negative genetic testing, his family members require aortic screening.

Management of our patient with negative genetic testing but high clinical suspicion for nsHTAD required a tailored approach that balanced minimizing risk of aortic rupture while prioritizing quality of life for this young active individual. Medical therapy with beta-blockade was initiated to reduce aortic wall stress. The current TTE surveillance strategy has demonstrated stability over 3 years, so annual imaging was deemed an appropriate surveillance interval. Cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography or magnetic resonance angiography should be performed for comprehensive aortic assessment, particularly of the ascending aorta beyond the root to evaluate the entire thoracic aorta in athletes with aortopathy.8 The 2022 ACC/AHA guidelines recommend that for patients with nsHTAD and no identifiable genetic cause, surgical repair should be considered when the aortic diameter reaches ≥5.0 cm in the absence of high-risk features, or ≥4.5 cm in the presence of high-risk features such as positive family history of aortic dissection at ≤5.0 cm or rapid growth.1 Our patient's family history may constitute a high-risk feature. While his dimension of 4.4 cm does not meet surgical thresholds, continued close surveillance is warranted, and the patient has been counseled that surgical intervention may be necessary if progression occurs.

Exercise recommendations for athletes with aortic root dilation are one of the most challenging aspects of management, particularly in young competitive athletes for whom sports participation may be central to their quality of life and livelihood. Competitive athletes with HTAD are at higher risk of aortic emergencies than the general population.8 HTAD has considerable heterogeneity in risk by specific disease, and in the absence of a causative gene, there remains ambiguity in how best to counsel patients regarding their individual exercise risk. Given this heterogeneity, the 2025 AHA/ACC Scientific Statement on competitive sports participation recommends expert evaluation for competitive athletes with HTADs.8 Competitive sports participation for athletes with HTAD whose aorta are of normal size can be considered with shared decision-making. In competitive athletes with HTAD and aortic dilation, the risks likely outweigh the benefits of competitive sports participation depending on the underlying condition and sport type. Athletes with HTAD and aortic dimensions that meet surgical thresholds should not participate in competitive sports.8

As our patient was no longer playing volleyball competitively, discussions regarding exercise participation focused on recreational activity rather than the more challenging issue of disqualification. Exact exercise recommendations need to be tailored to each patient based on clinical risk profile and should involve shared decision-making. The 2020 European Society of Cardiology guidelines provide recommendations on exercise for individuals with aortopathy. Our patient is classified as intermediate risk given his degree of aortic dilatation, and therefore should engage only in skill (eg, golf, bowling) or mixed sports (eg, soccer, volleyball, tennis) or endurance sports at low intensity (eg, jogging, long-distance running). Power sports such as weightlifting that require sustained isometric contractions or Valsalva maneuver were discouraged. Activities with isometric components performed in a predominantly dynamic movement such as those during mixed sports or bodyweight exercises without isometric holds were permitted.9

Conclusions

This case highlights the diagnostic and management complexities of aortic enlargement in young athletes. Compared with normative data on aortic size in competitive athletes of similar body habitus, an aortic diameter of 4.4 cm, as seen in our patient, raised suspicion for pathologic dilation.5,10 Given his concomitant clinical data, genetic testing was pursued that was ultimately negative. Nevertheless, he is clinically presumed to have nsHTAD. Repeat genetic evaluation and continued surveillance will remain critical components of this patient's care.
