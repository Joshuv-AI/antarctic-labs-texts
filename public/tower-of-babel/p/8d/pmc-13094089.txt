Pan-genomic analysis and abiotic stress expression of eight TPS gene families in Brassica napus
Authors: Tianyuan Xue, Zixiang Liu, Huachuan He, Heping Wan, Xigang Dai, Changli Zeng, Xiangxiang Zhang, Shuai Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTPS transcription factors play crucial roles in plant growth, development, and responses to abiotic stress. In this study, we identified 90 BnTPS genes across the Brassica napus pan-genome comprising eight high-quality genomes, including 11 core, 2 near-core, 6 non-essential, and 3 private genes. Ka/Ks analysis revealed that BnTPS38, BnTPS41, and particularly BnTPS20 (frequently > 1), along with BnTPS19 (up to 11.38), showed strong signals of positive selection, suggesting adaptation during domestication. In contrast, BnTPS14 and BnTPS16 consistently exhibited Ka/Ks < 1, indicating purifying selection. Notably, BnTPS41 displayed moderate exon SNP variation causing missense mutations in the Gangan and Shengli cultivars, which correlated with distinct expression profiles. Expression analyses under different sowing stages and abiotic stresses showed that BnTPS17 and BnTPS45 maintained high expression in leaves across most stages and stresses except freezing, while BnTPS16 from the TPS-e subfamily was highly expressed in leaves and roots under stress and during various sowing stages (except in No2127). These findings deepen our understanding of the evolutionary dynamics and functional diversity of BnTPS genes and provide candidate genes for future genetic improvement of B. napus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08487-w.Keywords: TPS, pan-genome, SNP variation, positive selection, abiotic stress

Introduction

Trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) is a key signaling molecule linking sucrose availability with carbon metabolism [1, 2]. T6P plays a central role in crop growth and development by regulating sucrose utilization and allocation [3, 4]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, there are 11 TPS genes. Among them, overexpression of AtTPS1 leads to delayed flowering and increased floral branching [5]. AtTPS5 is involved in thermotolerance and ABA signaling [6, 7], while AtTPS6 regulates the morphology of epidermal pavement cells and trichome branching [8]. AtTPS11 participates in defense responses against aphids [9].

Studies have shown that overexpression of TPS genes generally enhances the tolerance of transgenic plants to abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and cold. For instance, rice lines overexpressing OsTPS2, OsTPS4, OsTPS5, OsTPS8, and OsTPS9 under the control of the 35 S promoter showed no differences in growth and development compared to wild type, but exhibited significantly improved tolerance to salt and cold stresses [10]. Moreover, using tissue-specific or stress-inducible promoters (such as Rubisco, AtRAB18, StDS2, and OsMads6) to drive overexpression of genes like ScTPS1 and ScTPS2 often avoids growth defects while markedly enhancing stress resistance [11–14].

Overexpression of TPS genes can also increase crop yield. For example, transgenic maize overexpressing OsTPP1 under the control of the floral organ-specific promoter MADS6 exhibited significantly reduced T6P levels in reproductive tissues and showed yield advantages under both drought and non-drought conditions [15]. Such transgenic lines displayed higher photosynthetic rates and delayed leaf senescence compared to wild-type plants [16]. In addition, exogenous application of plant-permeable T6P analogs has been shown to modify endogenous T6P levels, thereby promoting starch synthesis in wheat and enhancing grain yield potential [17]. Recent studies have revealed that the sugar-induced transcription factor OsNAC23 can repress OsTPP1 expression, leading to increased T6P accumulation and resulting in a 13–17% yield increase in rice [18]. This further supports the feasibility of improving crop yield by regulating T6P concentrations [4].

Collectively, these findings highlight the great potential of trehalose metabolism-related genes (TPS) in enhancing crop stress tolerance and growth. However, their diverse biological functions and complex regulatory mechanisms warrant further investigation. The TPS gene family is of medium size, typically comprising 30–100 genes per genome [19]. In recent years, with the increasing availability of whole-genome resources, members and functions of the TPS gene family have been extensively characterized in many species. For instance, 32 functional TPS genes have been reported in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana [20], 69 in grape (Vitis vinifera) [21], 34 in rice (Oryza sativa) [22], 14 in the non-seed plant Selaginella moellendorffii [23], 38 in poplar (Populus trichocarpa) [24], and 34 in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [25].

Brassica napus L. (rapeseed; also known as canola or oilseed rape) and Arabidopsis thaliana both belong to the family Brassicaceae, but they are classified into different genera. It is estimated that the divergence between Arabidopsis and the Brassica lineage occurred approximately 20 to 40 million years ago. Arabidopsis is a diploid model species with a relatively small genome (~ 135 Mb), whereas B. napus is an allotetraploid (AACC, 2n = 38) formed by interspecific hybridization between B. rapa (AA, 2n = 20) and B. oleracea (CC, 2n = 18), resulting in a much more complex genome structure [26, 27]. This also implies that the TPS (trehalose-6-phosphate synthase) gene family in rapeseed exhibits high complexity and diversity. In B. napus, the class II TPS gene BnaC02.TPS8 is specifically expressed in mature leaves and developing silique pericarps. Compared with the wild type, overexpression of BnaC02.TPS8 significantly enhances photosynthetic capacity, promotes the accumulation of sugars, starch, and biomass. More importantly, under both high- and low-nitrogen field conditions, overexpression of BnaC02.TPS8 not only increases seed yield but also improves seed oil content and optimizes fatty acid composition [28]. However, current studies on TPS genes in rapeseed are mostly limited to analyses of gene families based on a single genome, and there is still a lack of systematic understanding of the entire TPS gene family. In particular, whether sequence variations in TPS gene families among different rapeseed genotypes lead to functional differences has not yet been reported. It is worth noting that multiple genome assemblies of B. napus have already been released [27]. Yang et al. [29]. identified 35 TPS genes using the B. napus ZS11 genome, while this study identified 32 TPS genes. This discrepancy may be due to the HMMsearch threshold, which was set to e < 1E-5 in this study. Additionally, this study used Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles for the N-terminal (PF01397) and C-terminal (PF03936) domains, which differed from the above study. Hu et al. [30]. identified 26 TPS genes using the B. napus Darmor-bzh genome, possibly due to the presence/absence variations (PAVs) of TPS members in different materials. Another reason might be the different HMM profiles used, as that study employed TPS (Glyco-transf-20, PF00982) and TPP (Trehalose_PPase, PF02358), which are distinct from those used here. Unlike traditional gene family studies that rely solely on a single reference genome, pan-genome family analysis based on eight B. napus genotypes can capture a broader range of genetic diversity, including core, variable, and unique gene family members. This approach can identify PAVs and structural variations that are often overlooked in single-genome analyses.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive pan-genomic analysis of the TPS transcription factor family. By integrating comparative genomics, phylogenetics, and functional annotation approaches, we aimed to elucidate the evolutionary dynamics and functional diversity of TPS genes. Our analysis encompassed eight B. napus genotypes from different lineages, revealing patterns of conserved functions as well as lineage-specific roles of TPS transcription factors. Additionally, we investigated the expression patterns of BnTPS genes at different sowing stages and under various abiotic stresses in these B. napus genotypes, and further validated the expression of TPS transcription factors under abiotic stress conditions. This highlights their significance in both fundamental biological research and potential applications. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of the evolutionary and functional complexity of the TPS transcription factor family. Pan-genome-based analysis facilitates the identification of novel or rare alleles that may be critical for improving stress tolerance or yield, offering insights that cannot be achieved through analyses based solely on a single reference genome.

Results

Pan-genome-wide BnTPS identification

In this study, 90 BnTPS (terpene synthase) genes were identified in the Brassica napus genome, including 11 core genes, 19 non-essential genes, 14 near-core genes, 6 private genes, and 40 pan-genome dispensable genes (Fig. 1). Analysis of the reference genome identified 35 TPS genes, all containing conserved TPS N-terminal and C-terminal domains. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using BnTPS protein sequences from Song et al. [31]. Based on the classification system established for Arabidopsis thaliana TPS proteins, the BnTPS genes were classified into five subfamilies (Fig. 1): TPS-a (58 genes), TPS-b (19 genes), TPS-c (6 genes), TPS-f (5 genes), and TPS-e (2 genes). Notably, all genes in the TPS-e subfamily were near-core genes. Presence/absence variation analysis across eight B. napus accessions revealed accession-specific patterns (Fig. 2). TPS33 and TPS49 were exclusively present in No2127, while TPS10, TPS33 and TPS49 were uniquely found in Quinta. Similarly, TPS43 was specific to Tapdior, and TPS34 and TPS42 were uniquely distributed in Westar and Gangan, respectively. Among the 40 pan-genome dispensable genes, the distribution was as follows: Gangan (7), No2127 (8), Quinta (5), Shengli (1), Tapdior (6), Westar (8), Zheyou7 (3), and ZS11 (2). These results suggest that the observed accession-specific distribution patterns of BnTPS genes may be associated with unique phenotypic traits in different B. napus varieties.

BnTPS is subjected to different selection pressures among B.napus varieties

To evaluate the evolutionary constraints acting on the BnTPS gene family, we calculated the nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution rate ratios (Ka/Ks) of TPS genes across eight B. napus genomes (Fig. 3A). The results showed that the Ka/Ks ratios ranged from 0 to 11.38, with distinct peak variations among different genes (Fig. 3A). Notably, multiple BnTPS genes—including BnTPS38 and BnTPS41—consistently exhibited Ka/Ks values greater than 1 across several genomes, indicating widespread signatures of positive selection. Among them, BnTPS20 frequently showed Ka/Ks values exceeding 1 in all eight B. napus genomes, while BnTPS19 reached a particularly high Ka/Ks value of 11.38 in certain accessions, suggesting strong positive selection. Additionally, other genes (BnTPS5, BnTPS19, BnTPS23, BnTPS27, BnTPS28, BnTPS38, BnTPS39, BnTPS41, and BnTPS50) also exhibited a substantial proportion of Ka/Ks values above 1 (Fig. 3B), implying that these genes may have been subjected to selective pressures during the domestication of B. napus. In contrast, BnTPS14 and BnTPS16 consistently displayed Ka/Ks values below 1 across multiple B. napus genomes, reflecting purifying selection that may help maintain their functional stability during evolution. These findings provide critical insights into the adaptive evolution of the BnTPS gene family and highlight key candidate genes that may have contributed to the domestication and agronomic improvement of B. napus.

Expression and structure of BnTPS genes are affected by SNP variation

To investigate the SNP variations in the TPS genes exhibiting signals of positive selection across different genomes, we classified the SNP variants compared to the reference genome into high, moderate, low, and modified types (Fig. 4A). Notably, BnTPS41 exhibited moderate SNP variation in exon regions, resulting in missense mutations, in both the Gangan and Shengli cultivars relative to the reference genome. By calculating the Pearson correlation coefficients of expression levels between genes affected by SNP variations and those unaffected, we found that BnTPS41 was the gene showing significant differences (p < 0.05 and |r| > 0.5) between SNP-carrying and SV-free varieties, indicating that the SNP variation significantly altered BnTP41’s expression pattern (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, BnTP41 displayed substantial variation between the Gangan and Shengli cultivars, a phenomenon that may be attributed to their distinct geographic origins.

To investigate whether SNP variations affect the gene structure of BnTPS, we analyzed the architecture of BnTPS41 across four B. napus genomes (Fig. 5). The results showed that the domain types, exon count, and 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of BnTPS41 were conserved among the Gangan, No2127, and Shengli cultivars. However, ZS11 exhibited differences in both the 5′ and 3′ UTRs, each containing an additional repeat unit compared with Gangan, No2127, and Shengli.

TPS genes were widely expressed in B.napus

To investigate the correlation between the number of BnTPS genes and their total expression levels among different Brassica napus cultivars, we quantified the BnTPS gene numbers and total expression values for each cultivar (Fig. 6). The results showed that while the number of BnTPS genes was nearly identical across cultivars, their total expression levels exhibited significant variations, with the lowest total FPKM value at 12.26 (cultivar No2127) and the highest reaching 29.05 (cultivar Zheyou7). Our analysis revealed that the absence of certain BnTPS genes did not directly influence the total expression levels. For example, the Westar cultivar contained 45 TPS genes but displayed a total FPKM of only 14.65, whereas the Quinta cultivar, with 25 TPS genes, had a comparable total FPKM of 12.44. Additionally, some cultivars with fewer TPS genes also exhibited lower total expression levels, such as Shengli (29 TPS genes, total FPKM = 12.58). Pearson correlation analysis between TPS gene numbers and log2RPKM values across cultivars yielded a coefficient of r² = 0.106 (r = 0.31, p = 0.43), indicating no significant correlation between BnTPS gene copy number and total expression levels.

Collinearity analysis of the BnTPS gene family

To investigate the collinearity of TPS genes, a collinearity analysis was conducted among B. napus, Brassica rapa, and Brassica oleracea. The results showed that there were 41 pairs of collinear TPS genes between B. napus and B. rapa, and 37 pairs of collinear TPS genes between B. napus and B. oleracea. However, TPS26 and TPS90 showed no collinearity with either B. rapa or B. oleracea (Fig. 7 and Table S1).

Transcriptome expression pattern analysis of TPS under abiotic stress

To investigate the expression patterns of BnTPS genes under abiotic stress conditions, we utilized the reference genome ZS11 of Brassica napus to analyze the expression profiles of the TPS gene family across different abiotic stress treatments, time points, and tissues (leaves and roots). The abiotic stress types included control (CK), salt stress (200 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl), drought stress (natural air-drying), freezing stress (-4 °C treatment followed by recovery at 25 °C), cold stress (4 °C treatment followed by recovery at 25 °C), heat stress (38 °C treatment followed by recovery at 25 °C), and osmotic stress (300 mmol·L⁻¹ mannitol). The results were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 8). The results revealed that TPS genes could be classified into five subfamilies: TPS-a, TPS-b, TPS-c, TPS-e, and TPS-f, with each subfamily exhibiting distinct expression characteristics at various treatment time points. The TPS-a subfamily contained the largest number of gene members. Notably, BnTPS4, BnTPS20, BnTPS50, BnTPS45, BnTPS90, BnTPS5, and BnTPS3 already exhibited relatively high expression levels in the control group; under certain abiotic stress treatments, their expression levels slightly decreased following treatment. Nevertheless, BnTPS4 and BnTPS20 still showed significantly higher expression in roots under multiple abiotic stresses, while BnTPS50 maintained high expression under most abiotic stress conditions. Several members of the TPS-b subfamily, such as BnTPS17 and BnTPS45, displayed high expression levels in leaves under abiotic stresses other than freezing. Notably, BnTPS90 exhibited consistently high expression in leaves across different abiotic stress treatments. Interestingly, BnTPS5 and BnTPS30 not only showed high expression in roots at various time points under multiple stresses, but also exhibited upregulated expression in leaves at certain time points under different stresses, suggesting their potentially important roles in the abiotic stress response of B. napus. In contrast, members of the TPS-c, TPS-e, and TPS-f subfamilies generally displayed low expression levels, with upregulation observed only at a few time points. However, BnTPS16 from the TPS-e subfamily was highly expressed in both leaves and roots at different time points under abiotic stresses. These findings indicate that the TPS gene family exhibits diverse expression patterns in B. napus under abiotic stresses across different time points, implying potential functional divergence to meet various stress response requirements.

Analysis of expression patterns of BnTPS under abiotic stress treatments

Transcriptome expression pattern analysis of TPS at different time points after sowing in eight genotypes

Using the reference genome ZS11 of B. napus, the expression patterns of the TPS gene family under abiotic stress treatments were analyzed across different time points and tissues, and visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 9). The results showed that TPS genes exhibited distinct expression characteristics at different treatment time points. Among them, BnTPS11, BnTPS38, and BnTPS50 from the TPS-a subfamily showed high expression levels at certain sowing time points in different genotypes, while BnTPS89 displayed consistently high expression at the T0 stage across all eight genotypes. Several members of the TPS-b subfamily, such as BnTPS5 and BnTPS30, exhibited elevated expression levels at some sowing stages in the eight genotypes. Notably, BnTPS17 and BnTPS45 maintained high expression levels at most sowing stages in the eight genotypes, suggesting their potentially important roles in the response processes at different sowing times in B. napus. In contrast, members of the TPS-c, TPS-e, and TPS-f subfamilies generally showed low expression levels, with upregulation observed only at a few time points. However, BnTPS16 from the TPS-e subfamily exhibited high expression across different sowing stages in seven genotypes, except for No2127. These results indicate that the TPS gene family displays diverse expression patterns in B. napus at different sowing time points.

The expression of BnTPS in eight different materials at different growth stages

Detection of BnTPSs expression under various abiotic stresses by the qPCR technique

To further validate the transcriptome sequencing results, eleven BnTPS genes (BnTPS3, BnTPS11, BnTPS16, BnTPS17, BnTPS21, BnTPS22, BnTPS39, BnTPS41, BnTPS45, BnTPS46, and BnTPS50) were selected for qRT-PCR analysis under NaCl, NaHCO₃, low nitrogen (LN), and PEG treatments at different time points (0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h). The results revealed that these genes exhibited diverse expression patterns under various abiotic stress conditions (Fig. 10). Among them, BnTPS17 and BnTPS21 were significantly upregulated under NaCl, NaHCO₃, LN, and PEG stresses, reaching peak expression at 6–12 h, with levels markedly higher than the control. BnTPS22 also showed strong induction under all four stress treatments, peaking at 6 h but declining significantly at 12 h and 24 h, while still being notably higher than the control at 6 h. BnTPS45 was markedly upregulated under three stresses (excluding LN), with peak expression at 6–12 h. In contrast, BnTPS3, BnTPS39, and BnTPS50 exhibited relatively consistent expression patterns, showing varying degrees of repression at most time points under the four stresses (NaCl, NaHCO₃, LN, and PEG). However, at the specific early time point of 6 h, BnTPS39 was significantly upregulated under NaHCO₃ treatment relative to the control, while BnTPS50 was significantly upregulated under NaCl treatment relative to the control, indicating that these two genes exhibit stress-specific early responses. BnTPS16 also showed a significant increase at 6 h under NaHCO₃ treatment, whereas BnTPS11 was significantly induced at 24 h under NaCl and NaHCO₃ treatments, similarly displaying obvious temporal and stress-specific responses. BnTPS46 exhibited significantly elevated expression at certain time points under NaCl, NaHCO₃, and PEG stresses, with particularly notable upregulation at 24 h under NaCl treatment and at 6 h under NaHCO₃ and PEG treatments.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically identified 90 terpene synthase genes (BnTPS) at the pangenomic level in Brassica napus and categorized them into five groups—core genes, near-core genes, dispensable genes, and private genes—based on their distribution patterns across different accessions. Evolutionary analysis revealed that some genes, such as BnTPS19 and BnTPS20, exhibited Ka/Ks > 1 in multiple cultivars, suggesting that they may have undergone significant positive selection pressure. Additionally, we found that BnTPS41 harbors notable SNP variations among different varieties, affecting its expression levels and structure, indicating that this gene may play a key role in the adaptive evolution of rapeseed. Expression profiling further demonstrated that TPS family genes display diverse spatiotemporal expression patterns under various abiotic stresses (such as salinity-alkalinity, drought, and low nitrogen) and sowing dates, highlighting substantial functional differentiation. These findings provide an important foundation for elucidating the roles of TPS genes in stress responses and adaptive evolution in rapeseed.

Further analyses revealed that multiple genes, including BnTPS20, BnTPS19, and BnTPS41, consistently exhibited Ka/Ks > 1 across various B. napus cultivars, indicating that these genes may have undergone positive selection during domestication, closely associated with plant adaptation to specific ecological environments. Notably, BnTPS41 not only showed significant differences in expression levels (p < 0.05) among different accessions, but also exhibited insertion variations in its UTR regions, suggesting more complex regulatory mechanisms. This result is consistent with findings in maize, where several TPS genes (such as TPS25, TPS27, TPS20, and TPS19) showed widespread Ka/Ks values greater than 1 and strong selection pressures across 26 maize genomes [32]. Although this study inferred the important functions of some genes through expression profiles and Ka/Ks analyses, functional validation experiments (such as gene knockout, overexpression, or complementation tests) have not yet been conducted, and their precise biological functions remain to be elucidated.

In this study, multiple abiotic stresses—including salt, drought, low nitrogen, freezing, cold, heat, and osmotic stress—were selected to comprehensively represent the major pressures that plants may encounter in natural and agricultural environments. These stress conditions can trigger a variety of responses, including ion homeostasis [33], osmotic regulation, hormone signaling, and antioxidant defense, and have been shown in oilseed rape to significantly regulate the expression of gene families [34–36]. Therefore, they are suitable for systematically analyzing the spatiotemporal expression patterns and functional diversification of BnTPS genes under different environmental conditions.Given the growing influence of climate change on global food production, cultivating crops that are climate-resilient to cope with abiotic stress is a crucial task in agriculture [37]. Nitrogen is an essential macronutrient for plant growth and development, and its deficiency can significantly affect plant morphology and reduce yield. Previous studies have shown that plant responses to low-nitrogen stress are closely linked to trehalose and its metabolites. For example, in Arabidopsis, low-nitrogen treatment significantly increased the levels of T6P and sucrose [38], suggesting that the trehalose pathway may participate in plant adaptation to low-nitrogen environments by regulating carbohydrate metabolism. Our qRT-PCR results showed that under low-nitrogen stress, the expression levels of BnTPS3, BnTPS16, BnTPS39, BnTPS41, BnTPS46, and BnTPS50 were significantly downregulated. This is consistent with the finding that the expression of BnaC2.TPS8 in shoots was significantly lower under low-nitrogen treatment within 0–3 days compared to normal nitrogen conditions [28]. Similarly, during the jointing stage in maize, expression of genes such as ZmTPSII0.2.1, ZmTPSII0.4.2, and ZmTPSII0.4.3 in roots was also significantly suppressed 24 h after low-nitrogen treatment [39]. Moreover, in Arabidopsis, TPS genes exhibited distinct responses to short-term (6–24 h) and long-term (10 d) phosphate stress, with some genes rapidly restoring expression upon phosphate resupply [40]. Notably, in this study, freezing stress did not significantly alter the expression levels of most BnTPS genes. This suggests that the TPS/T6P pathway in Brassica napus may not primarily participate in cold or freezing stress responses at the transcriptional level. Previous studies have shown that T6P, as a key sugar signaling molecule, mainly regulates carbon metabolism, development, and adaptation to stresses such as drought, salinity, and low nitrogen in plants [41–45]. In contrast, transcriptional responses of T6P/TPS to cold or freezing stress have been reported less frequently and exhibit strong species- and gene-specific patterns, which may reflect a greater reliance on post-transcriptional regulation, enzyme activity, or metabolite-level modulation rather than direct transcriptional control under low-temperature adaptation [44, 45].These results indicate that different TPS genes exhibit specific expression responses under particular stress conditions, further corroborating the diverse expression patterns of BnTPS genes under multidimensional conditions of tissue, time, and environment as revealed in this study.

In summary, this study systematically analyzed the composition, evolutionary dynamics, expression patterns, and responses to abiotic stresses of the BnTPS gene family in Brassica napus from a pangenomic perspective for the first time, uncovering their potential roles in adaptive evolution and breeding improvement. Multiple BnTPS genes, especially BnTPS41, BnTPS20, and BnTPS50, demonstrated active involvement in positive selection, structural variation, and stress responses, providing important candidate genes for molecular breeding of rapeseed. In the future, integrating functional validation with metabolomics approaches may further elucidate the key roles of BnTPS genes in stress adaptation and quality regulation in rapeseed, offering new ideas and technical support for molecular design breeding in crops.

Materials and methods

Identification of TPS gene family in B. napus

In this study, bioinformatic analysis was conducted using eight B. napus accessions from three ecological types, namely ZS11, Gangan, Zheyou7, Shengli, Tapidor, Quinta, Westar, and No2127.The genomic data of eight B. napus accessions were obtained from the study by Song et al. [31]. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles for the N-terminal (PF01397) and C-terminal (PF03936) domains of TPS genes were retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [46]. HMMER 3.3.2 was used to search for TPS domains with an E-value threshold set to e < 1E-5 [47]. The candidate TPS genes were then submitted to the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [48] to confirm the presence of both the N-terminal and C-terminal TPS domains. Finally, genes showing collinearity with TPS genes across the eight B. napus accessions were identified as members of the TPS gene family.

Phylogenetic analysis and presence/absence variation of the BnTPS gene family

A phylogenetic analysis was performed using TPS protein sequences from both Arabidopsis and B. napus. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using Muscle, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed with MEGA 11 [49]. The final tree was visualized using Envolview v3 (https://itol.embl.de/) [50].

The presence/absence variation (PAV) information for TPS genes was obtained from the study by Song et al. [31]. A heatmap showing the presence or absence of each TPS gene across the eight B. napus accessions was generated using Rscript v4.0.3 with the ComplexHeatmap package [51].

Ka/Ks calculation

The protein sequences and coding sequences (CDS) of BnTPS genes from the eight B. napus genomes were obtained from the study by Song et al. [31]. Sequence alignment of BnTPS genes was performed, and Ka/Ks values were calculated using the KaKs Calculator [52]. Ridgeline plots of the Ka/Ks value distributions were generated using the R packages ggridges and ggplot2 (v4.0.3) [53], and a heatmap illustrating the proportion of BnTPS genes with Ka/Ks values greater than one was created using ComplexHeatmap (R v4.0.3) [51].

Expression analysis of TPS genes associated with SNP variations

The SNP loci information of the eight B. napus accessions, along with their gene expression data, were obtained from the BnVIR database (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnVIR) [54]. Accessions harboring the reference (Ref) allele were used as the reference genomes, while those containing the alternative (Alt) allele were regarded as genomes with variant alleles. If a TPS gene in a given accession contained SNP sites with moderate or higher functional effects, the TPS expression data for that accession were treated as the expression data of the SNP-variant gene; otherwise, they were considered as expression data of genes without SNP variants. Subsequently, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the presence of SNP variations in the gene and its expression level was calculated. TPS genes with p < 0.05 and |r| > 0.3 were considered to have expression levels significantly affected by SNP variations.

Expression analysis of TPS genes in B. napus

The genome annotation file in General Feature Format (GFF) was downloaded from http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/cgi-bin/rape/download_ext (accessed on 5 July 2025). The MEME tool was used to analyze motifs within the BnTPS protein sequences [55]. It was found that the genes encoding these proteins harbored SNP variations that significantly affected their expression levels. Subsequently, TBtools II v2.301 was employed to visualize the files generated from the previous analyses together with the GFF file, thereby constructing gene structure diagrams [56]. Gene expression data across different sowing stages for eight B. napus accessions were obtained from the study by Song et al. [27]. Using the ZS11 accession as the reference genome and transcriptome data from the T0 sowing stage (24 days after sowing), the FPKM expression levels of TPS genes in ZS11 were analyzed. A linear regression was then performed between the number of TPS genes and their expression levels, with r > 0.3 and p < 0.05 indicating that the expression levels of TPS genes were significantly influenced by TPS gene copy number.

Collinearity analysis of TPS family genes

This analysis used data from B. rapa (Chiifu) and B. oleracea (HDEM) obtained from the BRAD database (http://brassicadb.cn/#/), including the whole-genome files and GFF3 files for both Chiifu and HDEM. In TBtools, the Advanced Circos tool was used to perform intraspecific synteny analysis of the BnTPS genes in B. napus, while the MCScanX module was employed to analyze the interspecific synteny relationships between B. napus, B. rapa, and B. oleracea [57].

Expression pattern analysis of BnTPS under different abiotic stresses

To investigate the expression patterns of BnTPS genes under abiotic stress conditions, the sequences of BnTPS were uploaded to the BnIR database [58]. Based on TPM (Transcripts Per Million) data, the expression levels of BnTPS in leaves and roots under various stress treatments were retrieved, including control (CK), salt stress (200 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl ), drought (natural air-drying), freezing stress (-4 °C followed by recovery at 25 °C), cold stress (4 °C followed by recovery at 25 °C), heat stress (38 °C followed by recovery at 25 °C), and osmotic stress (300 mmol·L-1 mannitol ). The obtained TPM values were normalized using the formula log10(TPM + 1), and the resulting matrix was visualized using TBTools software [56].

Transcriptome expression pattern analysis of TPS at different time points after sowing in eight genotypes

To investigate the expression patterns of BnTPS genes in different Brassica napus accessions, gene expression data at different sowing stages from eight rapeseed genotypes were obtained from the study by Song et al. [27]. Using ZS11 as the reference genome, transcriptome data from five sowing stages—T0 (24 days after sowing), T1 (54 days), T2 (82 days), T3 (115 days), and T4 (147 days)—were selected to analyze the FPKM expression levels of TPS genes in ZS11. The resulting FPKM values were normalized using the formula log10(FPKM + 1), and the expression matrix was visualized using TBTools software [56].

Expression analysis of BnTPS genes under different abiotic stresses

B. napus cultivar “ZS11” was used for expression analysis. The seeds of B. napus ZS11(provided by the research group of Professor Shen Jinxiong at the National Rapeseed Engineering Technology Research Center, Huazhong Agricultural University, China). Seeds were germinated in distilled water for 4 days and uniformly growing seedlings were transferred to the Hoagland nutrient solution for 14 days. Seedlings were then subjected to 1.2% (w/v) NaCl, 0.2% (w/v) NaHCO3, low nitrogen (Figure S1), and 20% (w/v) PEG 6000 prepared with Hoagland solution. Samples were collected at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h for RNA extraction and expression analysis under salt and drought stress.

Primers were designed using the qPrimerDB-qPCR Primer Database (https://biodb.swu.edu.cn/qprimerdb/) [59], and an internal reference gene was used (see Table 1). Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit, and reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript II QRT SuperMix for qPCR kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. Each 20 µL reaction system contained: 2 µL cDNA, 0.4 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 10 µL SYBR, and sterile ddH2O up to 20 µL. PCR conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 22 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and the results were visualized using TBTools. Based on our preliminary experiments under the four abiotic stress conditions, ACT7 was selected as the reference gene [36, 60].

Conclusion

In conclusion, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the TPS gene family in the pangenome of B. napus, revealing significant variation in the number of TPS genes among different B. napus varieties. Compared to a single genome, the pangenomic analysis identified more TPS genes, which were subsequently categorized to distinguish core genes from private genes. At the pangenomic level, SNP variations within individual genes had a notable impact on gene expression. TPS genes exhibited distinct expression patterns across different subfamilies under varying sowing dates in eight B. napus accessions, suggesting their involvement in starch biosynthesis. Further analysis of responses to abiotic stresses, including low-nitrogen stress, highlighted the crucial roles of TPS genes in B. napus under low-temperature, drought, salt, and low-nitrogen stress conditions, with several genes (e.g., BnTPS17 and BnTPS21) showing significant upregulation under low-nitrogen treatment. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying B. napus responses to environmental stresses and provide valuable candidate targets for improving its tolerance to abiotic stresses.
