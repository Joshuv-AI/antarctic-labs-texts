Severity of Respiratory Syncytial Virus and Other Respiratory Viruses Versus Seasonal Influenza Among Hospitalized Patients With Cancer
Authors: Liang En Wee, Leicester Shawn Alcantara, May Kyawt Aung, Shalvi Arora, Jean Xiang Ying Sim, Dong Dong Ren, Zachary Allen Vincent, Veeramani Srivathsan, Tertius Tansloan Tuy, Shin Yeu Ong, Wei Chong Tan, Iain Beehuat Tan, Limin Wijaya, Indumathi Venkatachalam, Kelvin Bryan Tan, Benjamin W Teh, Ban Hock Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMore data are required to contextualize disease burden attributable to respiratory viral infections (RVIs) in hematology/oncology patients, including vaccine-preventable RVIs (eg, influenza, respiratory syncytial virus [RSV]) and other RVIs for which vaccines/therapeutics are not presently available (eg, human metapneumovirus [HMPV] or human parainfluenza virus [HPIV]).MethodsA retrospective cohort study included all adult patients hospitalized for RVI with diagnoses based on multiplex polymerase chain reaction testing from 2018 to 2024 in a large Singaporean cancer center. Severity was defined as a composite of multiple individual severe events (oxygen usage, intensive care unit [ICU] or high-dependency [HD] admission, and all-cause mortality events). Patients were followed up from admission until discharge or up to 1 month. The comparative severity of RSV and other non–vaccine-preventable RVIs (eg, HMPV/HPIV), versus seasonal influenza, was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression.ResultsIn total, 1823 patients hospitalized with RVIs were included; 24.0% had influenza (n = 437); 12.9% had RSV (n = 235), and the remainder had other RVIs (eg, HMPV [n = 128] and HPIV [n = 204]). A third (29.4% [536 of 1823]) had severe infection (defined as requirement for oxygen, ICU/HD admission, or death within 1 month); 26.9% (491 of 1823) required oxygen, 6.9% (126 of 1823) required ICU/HD admission, and 14.8% (269 of 1823) died. Other RVIs (eg, RSV, HMPV, or adenovirus) were more severe overall, compared with influenza (adjusted odds ratios [aORs] for RSV, HMPV, and adenovirus, 1.94 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.33–2.83], 1.77 [1.12–2.80], and 2.19 [1.33–3.63], respectively). Patients hospitalized with RSV had increased odds for ICU/HD admission (aOR, 2.41 [95% CI, 1.22–4.77]) and all-cause mortality events at 1 month (2.42 [1.42–4.12]), compared with seasonal influenza. Older age (aOR, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.03–1.64]), comorbid conditions (1.50 [1.18–1.90]), coinfections (1.70 [1.21–2.38]), and nosocomial transmission (1.53 [1.20–1.94]) were associated with greater severity.ConclusionsRSV was more severe than influenza in hospitalized patients with cancer, but higher odds of severity were also observed for other RVIs (HMPV and adenovirus), underlining the need for new vaccines and therapeutics. Given the significant morbidity/mortality rates associated with nosocomial RVI, infection prevention is crucial in preventing outbreaks in this immunocompromised population.Keywords: cancer, influenza, metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, RSV

Respiratory viral infections (RVIs) are a leading cause of disease and death in patients with cancer [1–6], given underlying immunocompromise. Comparative analyses on real-world outcomes of RVI hospitalizations in this at-risk population are limited, with existing studies focused on a limited number of RVIs for which vaccines or therapeutics are available (eg, influenza, coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19], and respiratory syncytial virus [RSV]) [1–3]. In a large multicenter registry of patients with hematological cancer and reported RVI (n = 1312), the overall morbidity/mortality rates were high; 13.5% required intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and 10.6% died [4]. A key limitation was that patients may not have been simultaneously tested for various RVIs [4], potentially introducing bias through preferential testing for treatable or preventable RVIs (eg, influenza or RSV). Multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing facilitates concurrent testing for various RVIs. However, most studies during the era of multiplex testing were limited to ICU-admitted patients with cancer [5, 6], specific higher-risk groups, such as hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients [7, 8], or patients with lymphoma or multiple myeloma [9].

Given the significant healthcare utilization attributable to RVIs among at-risk cancer survivors [10], comparative analysis of outcomes for different RVIs in this immunocompromised population provides an important context for prioritization of therapeutics/vaccine development, particularly as preventive options beyond influenza/RSV vaccination are currently limited. RSV is increasingly recognized as an important cause of severe RVI, especially in tropical countries where heat and humidity predispose to year-round RVI transmission [11]. Preexisting immunocompromise, in turn, contributes to increased risk of poor outcomes following RSV infection [12]. New RSV vaccines confer effective real-world protection among older adults with comorbid conditions, including immunocompromise [13], but comparative analyses of RSV's severity in patients with cancer are limited, despite the association of cancer with high mortality and morbidity rates [2, 3]. We assessed the comparative severity of various RVIs identified with multiplex PCR testing among adult hematology/oncology patients hospitalized at a large cancer center from 2018 to 2024, predating the availability of RSV vaccines. The severity of various RVIs was contrasted against influenza hospitalizations during the same period.

METHODS

Study Background, Period and Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Singapore General Hospital (SGH), a large tertiary hospital in Singapore, a multiethnic Southeast Asian city-state located on the equator. Given Singapore's humid and rainy tropical climate, year-round transmission of influenza and other RVIs (eg, RSV) has been described [11, 14]. SGH shares a campus with the National Cancer Centre, Singapore (NCCS); approximately 70% of all cancer cases in Singapore are seen at NCCS, with >9000 new cancer cases and >1 million patient visits yearly [15].

The study period spanned from 1 January 2018 to 31 May 2024, following the introduction of multiplex PCR testing for RVIs at our institution, which improved surveillance among hematology/oncology patients [16, 17]. This period overlapped with the emergence of COVID-19; imposition of public health measures from 2020–2021 during the pandemic resulted in unprecedented drops in community transmission of non–severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) RVIs and therefore reduced hospitalizations attributable to non–SARS-CoV-2 RVIs at our institution [18]. As such, the majority of RVIs were recorded only from 2022 onward. SARS-CoV-2, while an RVI, was not included in this analysis because substantial changes in diagnostic testing modalities during the pandemic (eg, rapid antigen testing vs PCR) [19] and testing criteria (eg, universal COVID-19 testing for all hospitalized inpatients) precluded direct comparison and analyses of comparative severity [20].

Cohort Construction and Definition of Exposure

Inclusion criteria included all adults (aged ≥21 years) who were hospitalized in SGH under the Department of Hematology and the oncology wards managed by the NCCS, with a concurrent RVI confirmed on multiplex PCR testing at any point during their hospitalization, over the study period (1 January 2018 to 31 May 2024). Case finding was conducted by SGH's Department of Infection Prevention and Epidemiology, which maintains hospital-wide surveillance of RVI cases via a combination of automated data extraction and manual record review, in order to facilitate subsequent isolation of positive cases. Exclusion criteria included age <21 years, hospitalization with a hematological condition not related to cancer (eg, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura), and viral coinfections (>1 RVI isolated). RVI coinfections were excluded because such cases made up a minority (<3%) of RVI hospitalizations in our local setting [21], and death or disease in these cases could not be attributed to a single virus. Case patients were followed up to the point of hospital discharge, patient death, or 1 month of hospitalization, whichever occurred latest.

Exposure (RVI) was defined as fulfilling a microbiological criterion, including an upper or lower respiratory tract specimen testing positive on a respiratory pathogen multiplex PCR panel for any viral pathogen. Throughout the study period, our institution used the Seegene Anyplex II RV16 Detection Multiplex PCR kit (Seegene) for RVI testing; testing for patients with acute respiratory symptoms was at the discretion of the primary physician [16–18, 21]. Consistency in the testing platform and criteria used throughout the study period facilitated comparative assessment of severity across various RVIs. The RVI testing assay simultaneously detects the following human respiratory viral pathogens: influenza A/B, RSV subtypes A/B, human parainfluenza virus (HPIV) 1/2/3/4, human metapneumovirus (HMPV), human coronavirus (HCoV) (229E/NL63/OC43), adenovirus, human bocavirus 1/2/3/4, and human rhinovirus (HRV)/enterovirus [16–18, 21]. Individuals could have ≥1 RVI hospitalization recorded during the study period; a consecutive RVI was included if the RVI detected was nonidentical or if it was identified ≥30 days after the previous episode with the identical RVI [7]. Chronological criteria, including diagnosis on or after a minimum number of days since hospital admission specific for each virus's maximum incubation period, were subsequently used to classify RVIs as healthcare-associated RVIs (HA-RVIs) [22]. Infections that did not fulfill chronological criterion for HA-RVIs (and therefore not potentially attributable to nosocomial transmission) were classified as community onset.

Outcomes

Disease severity was defined as a composite measure [23], including any of the following severe events: use of supplemental oxygen [9, 11], ICU/high-dependency (HD) admission [11, 23], and all-cause mortality events [4], occurring within 1 month [2, 9, 11]. Supplemental oxygen use was defined as the delivery of oxygen by any modality (eg, nasal cannula, mask, noninvasive ventilation, or mechanical ventilation) and was recorded if sustained for >4 hours [9]. Supplemental oxygen usage in the context of medication administration or baseline oxygen use was not included. Individual categories of severe events (oxygen usage, ICU/HD admission, all-cause mortality events) were assessed separately as secondary outcomes for the comparative analysis of severity in RSV versus influenza; the relatively larger proportion of patients hospitalized for influenza/RSV facilitated such a comparison.

Covariates

The following covariates were incorporated in regression models: type of RVI, age/sex/ethnicity; presence of comorbid conditions listed in the Charlson comorbidity index, using a look-back period of 5 years in the electronic medical record; current cancer (leukemia/lymphoma, other hematological cancer, or solid organ cancer); neutropenia, lymphopenia, or hypogammaglobulinemia; active chemotherapy (receipt within the preceding 30 days); receipt of B-cell–depleting therapy in the year before admission; infiltrates reported on chest imaging within 72 hours of positive test for RVI; whether infection was community onset or nosocomial; and the presence of coinfection. The presence of a copathogen was defined as a bacterial, fungal, or other nonrespiratory virus pathogen (eg, mycobacterial) isolated from a respiratory tract sample [7] around the time of the positive RVI test (from 2 days before to 14 days after diagnosis of the index viral infection) [24]. Antiviral treatment was included as a covariate only in regression models that compared severity of RSV versus influenza, as there were no targeted antiviral therapies routinely available for the majority of RVIs, except oseltamivir for influenza and ribavirin/immunoglobulin for RSV.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as proportions/percentages for categorical data and medians with interquartile ranges for skewed data. Factors associated with severity (as a composite outcome) during RVI hospitalization were evaluated using χ2 tests for univariate analysis and logistic regression models with multivariate analysis, adjusting for all available covariates listed above. Influenza was used as the reference category when comparing severity of (1) RSV and (2) other non–vaccine-preventable RVIs (HCoV, HPIV, HMPV, adenovirus, and HRV). For the comparison of RSV versus influenza, RSV severity was contrasted against influenza severity (1) in all patients hospitalized for influenza or (2) only in those unvaccinated against influenza, given that a minority (<15%) of the adult population received influenza vaccination during the study period [11]. Individuals with no prior record of influenza vaccination in our institution's electronic medical record, or who had received influenza vaccination ≥365 days before hospitalization, were designated as unvaccinated [11]. RSV vaccines were approved by the Singapore Health Sciences Authority only from June 2024 for adults aged ≥60 years; hence all patients hospitalized with RSV were unvaccinated for RSV. Receipt of treatment (oseltamivir for influenza or ribavirin/immunoglobulin for RSV) was included as an additional covariate in regression models to assess the comparative severity of RSV versus seasonal influenza.

The following sensitivity analyses were performed. First, as coinfections with other pathogens (eg, bacterial or fungal) were associated with increased RVI severity [4], potentially resulting in an overestimate of severity attributable to a single RVI, we excluded coinfections as a sensitivity analysis. Second, while HRV infections are generally self-limiting, persistent PCR positivity has occasionally been described in immunocompromised patients [25], the clinical significance of which is unclear [26, 27]; therefore HRV cases were also excluded from the analysis of factors associated with RVI severity. Third, given shifts in RVI trends during the COVID-19 pandemic among patients with cancer [17, 28], we restricted our analysis to RVI hospitalizations recorded during the pandemic period (1 January 2020 to 31 May 2024), to evaluate whether estimates changed. Finally, given that hematological cancers comprised the majority of patients, we restricted the study population to individuals with hematological cancer and included prior hematopoietic stem cell transplantation as an additional covariate. Analyses were conducted using R 4.3.1 software, and a P value <.05 was taken as the threshold for statistical significance.

Ethics Statement

Because this epidemiology study used retrospective deidentified data, determination of review-not-required status and waiver of informed consent was sought and obtained from our institution's central institutional review board (CIRB reference 2024/2163).

RESULTS

A total of 1823 adult hematology/oncology patients were included. About one-quarter of them (24.0% [n = 437]) were hospitalized with influenza, 12.9% (n = 235) were hospitalized with RSV, and the remainder were hospitalized with other RVIs (208 with HCoV, 204 with HPIV, 128 with HMPV, 100 with adenovirus, and 511 with HRV). About one-third (29.4% [536 of 1823]) required supplemental oxygen or ICU/HD admission or died within 1 month (Table 1). Overall, about one-quarter (26.9% [491 of 1823]) required oxygen, of whom 20.1% (99 of 491) required mechanical or noninvasive ventilation (Supplementary Table 1); 6.9% (126 of 1823) required ICU admission; and 14.8% (269 of 1823) died within 1 month (Table 1). Of patients who died, 76.2% (205 of 269) were receiving supplementary oxygen at the time of death. The median length of stay for RVI hospitalizations (interquartile range) was 7 days (3–20) days, and the median age was 62 (53–70) years.

Almost two-thirds of patients had hematological cancer (64.0% [n = 1166]); of those, 35.9% (n = 419) had leukemia, 37.3% (n = 435) had lymphoma, and 26.8% (n = 312) had other hematological cancers (eg, multiple myeloma). The remaining one-third had solid organ cancer (36.0% [n = 657]); of those, 29.5% (n = 194) had breast cancer, 20.7% (n = 136) had lung cancer, 8.8% (n = 58) had colorectal cancer, and the rest (n = 269) had other cancers. Of all the patients, 78.2% (1426 of 1823) were on active chemotherapy at the time of infection, and one-quarter (24.9% [454 of 1823]) had received B-cell–depleting therapy within the past year, most commonly anti-CD20 agents (eg, rituximab) (Supplementary Table 2). While the majority of patients (71.0% [1295 of 1823]) had community-onset RVIs (CO-RVIs), a significant proportion (29.0% [528 of 1823]) were classified as having HA-RVIs (potential nosocomial onset).

Factors independently associated with RVI severity (oxygen requirement, ICU/HD admission, and deaths) are reported in Table 2. Other RVIs were more severe compared with influenza; numerically, the highest odds of severity, versus influenza, were observed in hospitalizations for adenovirus (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.19 [95% confidence interval [CI], 1.33–3.63]), followed by RSV (1.94 [1.33–2.83]) and HMPV (1.77 [1.12–2.80]). In contrast, the odds of severity, versus seasonal influenza, were numerically lowest for HRV/enterovirus hospitalizations (aOR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.11–2.11]), though CIs overlapped substantially. Older age (≥60 years) (aOR, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.03–1.64]), higher comorbidity burden (1.50 [1.18–1.90]), presence of coinfection (1.70 [1.21–2.38]), chest infiltrates (2.38 [1.90–2.98]), and HA-RVI versus CO-RVI (1.53 [1.20–1.94]) were independently associated with greater odds of severity on multivariate analysis (Table 2).

In sensitivity analyses, when coinfection cases (n = 198) were excluded (Supplementary Table 3), estimates did not significantly change. Other RVIs were still associated with greater odds of severity compared with influenza (eg, aOR, 2.06 [95% CI, 1.38–3.09] for RSV, 1.82 [1.19–2.77] for HPIV, 1.87 [1.14–3.07] for HMPV) (Supplementary Table 4), and RSV was still associated with increased odds of ICU/HD admission (3.43 [1.46–8.02]) or death (3.36 [1.84–6.12]), compared with influenza (Supplementary Table 5). When HRV/enterovirus cases were excluded from the analysis of factors associated with RVI severity, increased odds of severe RVI were still associated with higher comorbidity burden, presence of coinfection, chest infiltrates on imaging and nosocomial onset (Supplementary Table 6); in addition, neutropenia was also significantly associated with subsequent severity (aOR, 1.73 [95% CI, 1.05–2.87]).

When analysis was restricted to the subset of RVI hospitalizations overlapping with the emergence of COVID-19 (2020–2024), other RVIs, compared with influenza, were still associated with greater odds of severity (Supplementary Table 7), and RSV was still associated with increased odds of death (aOR, 6.70 [95% CI, 2.13–21.07]) (Supplementary Table 8), though CIs were wide due to the relatively smaller number of outcome events. When the study population was restricted to patients with hematological cancer (n = 1166) (Supplementary Table 9), hospitalizations for adenovirus (aOR, 2.34 [95% CI, 1.28–4.30]), hMPV (1.87 [1.03–3.37]) or RSV (1.91 [1.19–3.07]) were still associated with higher odds of overall severity, compared with hospitalization for seasonal influenza (Supplementary Table 10). In patients with hematological cancers, RSV hospitalizations were also associated with higher odds of requiring supplemental oxygen (aOR, 2.19 [95% CI, 1.19–4.04]) and all-cause mortality events at 1 month (2.22 [1.01–4.98]), compared with hospitalizations for seasonal influenza (Supplementary Table 11).

DISCUSSION

In this large single-center study of hematology/oncology patients hospitalized for RVI, patients with hospitalization attributed to seasonal influenza or RSV made up one-third of the cohort; <5% of those hospitalized for influenza had previously received influenza vaccination. Substantial disease and death was associated with RVIs; almost one-third of patients (29.4%) had severe disease, with one-quarter (26.9%) requiring oxygen, 6.9% requiring ICU/HD admission, and 14.8% dying within 1 month. Other RVIs (eg, RSV, HMPV, and adenovirus) were more severe than seasonal influenza, with increased odds for ICU/HD admission and all-cause mortality events in patients hospitalized for RSV versus seasonal influenza. Older age, comorbid conditions, coinfection, and nosocomial transmission were significantly associated with greater severity.

Among hematology/oncology patients in a tropical setting with year-round RSV and influenza circulation, RSV was significantly associated with greater overall severity, compared with seasonal influenza; 33.2% of patients hospitalized for RSV required supplemental oxygen, 11.5% required ICU/HD admission, and 18.3% died. Comparable rates were reported from hematology/oncology cohorts in temperate countries hospitalized for RSV (with ICU admission in 9.3%–22.3%, death in 11.0%–13.3%, and supplemental oxygen in 37.0%) [2, 3]. Immunosuppression is associated with higher odds of death among individuals hospitalized for RSV [29]; severity of RSV in this cohort of hematology/oncology patients was substantially higher compared with the general adult Singaporean population, in which only 3.8% of adults hospitalized for RSV (in 2021–2023) required ICU/HD admission, and 5.4% died [11]. Greater comparative severity of RSV hospitalization, versus influenza, was also observed in the general adult Singaporean population [11]. This disparity may reflect limited options for vaccination/therapeutics to mitigate RSV severity, compared with influenza. The study period predated the availability of RSV vaccines; while almost two-thirds of patients hospitalized for influenza were treated with oseltamivir, only a quarter of those hospitalized for RSV received treatment. While ribavirin is used for RSV infections in hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients and patients with hematological cancer, a systematic review did not find an overall association between ribavirin usage and reduced mortality rate [30].

Vaccination remains an alternative means of providing protection, given limited availability of therapeutic options for RSV. However, while new RSV vaccines confer effective protection against severe RSV disease, the original clinical trials that demonstrated vaccine effectiveness did not include immunocompromised individuals [31]. Real-world data suggests that while RSV vaccine effectiveness was lower in patients with immunocompromise, vaccines still confer effective protection against RSV-associated hospitalization across successive seasons [32]. In a large population-based cohort of cancer survivors in the US, individuals with influenza/pneumonia had higher total healthcare expenditure (mean, $19 387 [USD] vs $12 714; P < .001) and total out-of-pocket expenditure (mean, $1494 vs $1159; P < .001) than uninfected individuals, suggesting that vaccination programs for vaccine-preventable RVIs in patients with cancer may yield potential benefits [10]. However, vaccine hesitancy remains a significant hurdle; <5% of patients hospitalized for influenza in our cohort had previously received an influenza vaccine, despite widespread availability of subsidized influenza vaccination in primary care [11]. Similarly, in a multicenter cohort of patients with hematological cancer with an RVI diagnosed during the 2023–2024 autumn–winter season (n = 1312), only 5.3% had received prior influenza or COVID-19 vaccines [4]. Achieving high vaccination coverage in this at-risk population remains challenging; targeted interventions are required to address vaccine hesitancy among patients with cancer [33].

More than half of the patients in our cohort, however, were hospitalized for non–vaccine-preventable RVIs; higher odds of severity were observed for other RVIs (eg, HMPV and adenovirus) versus seasonal influenza. Comparable severity of non–vaccine-preventable RVIs (eg, HMPV and HPIV) was also observed in other cohorts of patients with hematological cancer [4, 34]. Underlying hematological cancer (vs solid organ cancer) is associated with increased risk of progression to lower respiratory tract infection in HMPV-infected individuals, which was in turn associated with higher 30-day mortality rate [35]. This may explain the higher proportion of severe HMPV infections reported in our study and other cohorts predominated by patients with underlying hematological cancer [4]. RVIs can be associated with more severe extrapulmonary manifestations; disseminated adenoviral infections, for instance, have been reported in patients with cancer [36], and increased risk of cardiac events has been observed in patients hospitalized for RSV, versus seasonal influenza [37]. The lack of vaccination and treatment options for other RVIs (eg, HMPV and HPIV) highlights the urgent need for improved antiviral and therapeutic options against other RVIs, particularly in patients with cancer at risk of severe disease.

Given the lack of therapeutics/vaccinations, preventing RVI transmission is important, particularly in hospital environments where immunocompromised patients may be at risk due to preexisting complications. Nosocomial infections caused by RVIs other than SARS-CoV-2 are common and underappreciated, with one-fifth or more of cases of hospital-acquired pneumonia attributed to viruses rather than bacteria [22]. In our cohort, nosocomial RVI was associated with greater severity of infection, compared with CO-RVI. Cases of HA-RVI are potentially preventable; the COVID-19 pandemic provided impetus for the introduction of universal masking and other enhanced infection prevention and control measures as part of containment measures, with the effect of reducing HA-RVIs among patients with cancer during the pandemic period [16, 17, 38, 39]. Introduction of universal masking policies during the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in an unprecedented decline in HA-RVI among hematology and oncology inpatients at our institution [16, 17], as part of a multilayered infection prevention and control approach including enhanced segregation for patients with symptoms of acute respiratory infection. This decrease in HA-RVI was subsequently sustained across hematology/oncology wards in the postpandemic period, even after rebound of CO-RVI [38].

Similarly, adoption of universal masking at a large cancer center was associated with sustained reduction of non–SARS-CoV-2 nosocomial RVI over ≥2 years [39]. Non–SARS-CoV-2 RVIs can contribute significantly to delays in cancer treatment. In a cohort of patients (n = 128) testing positive for RVIs at a Japanese tertiary cancer center, one-third experienced postponement of cancer treatment, including delays in chemotherapy initiation as well as rescheduling of planned surgery [40]. More can be done to mitigate nosocomial transmission of RVIs in this at-risk immunocompromised population.

Our study had limitations. While its single-center nature allowed for standardized simultaneous testing for various RVIs via multiplex PCR, mitigating bias associated with differential testing, this also limited generalizability. Influenza vaccination status was limited to vaccination records within our institution and may thus be underestimated; however, local uptake of influenza vaccination is low, with <15% vaccination rates among inpatients hospitalized for influenza [11]. Data on the overall costs of RVI hospitalization or the impact of RVI on subsequent cancer treatment (eg, delay in chemotherapy) were also not collected. Mortality events were defined as deaths due to any cause within 1 month of an RVI diagnosis; given the retrospective nature of this study, it was not always possible to conclusively attribute a death to a specific cause. Finally, this retrospective cohort was limited to hospitalized patients only; outpatients with potentially milder infection were not included due to the paucity of testing in the clinic setting, thus restricting comparisons across the full spectrum of severity.

In conclusion, our single-center experience demonstrated substantial disease and deaths associated with hospitalisation for RVIs in hematology/oncology patients. RSV was significantly more severe than seasonal influenza, suggesting that this at-risk group should be prioritized for RSV vaccination. However, higher odds of severity, versus seasonal influenza, were also observed for other RVIs including HMPV and adenoviral infections. Given limited treatment options for RVIs and the significant risk of disease and death associated with nosocomial RVI, infection prevention and control measures are crucial for preventing onward transmission in this immunocompromised population.
