Clinical red flags and referral strategies for odontogenic deep neck space infections: The primary care dentist’s role as the first line of defense
Authors: Sih-Yi Wu
Published: 2026

As primary care providers, dental professionals serve as the first line of defense in intercepting life-threatening odontogenic infections. Over 90% of head and neck infections originate from dental pathology, with mandibular molars being the most frequent site due to their proximity to submandibular, parapharyngeal, and masticator spaces.1,2 Notably, odontogenic infections account for approximately 10.38 % of all emergency department visits, highlighting a significant public health burden that could be mitigated through timely outpatient recognition.3

In a primary care setting, dentists must prioritize rigorous screening for high-risk patients before performing extractions or prescribing antibiotics. Patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) are 1.409 times more likely to develop odontogenic facial cellulitis and often require longer hospitalizations. 1,4 This immunological dysfunction becomes evident approximately two years after the initial DM diagnosis, making a thorough medical history assessment essential.1 Additionally, patients with systemic comorbidities, such as psychiatric disorders, dementia, or immunosuppressive conditions, face significantly higher risks of deep neck space infections (DNIs), often exacerbated by poor oral hygiene compliance.4

Prompt identification of clinical “red flags” is critical, as these infections can rapidly progress to fatal conditions, such as Ludwig’s angina or necrotizing mediastinitis, within hours.5 Dentists must initiate immediate emergency referral if patients present with airway or swallowing compromise, including dyspnea, stridor, dysphagia, odynophagia, or excessive drooling.3,4 Severe anatomical distortions, such as an elevated or protruding tongue, trismus, or extensive submandibular swelling, are also critical warning signs of imminent airway obstruction, which is the most common cause of death in these patients.5 Furthermore, systemic toxicity or tissue crepitus, which indicates gas-producing bacteria, predicts a highly severe clinical course associated with increased surgical complications, extended Intensive Care Unit (ICU) stays, and higher mortality rates (Table 1).4,5

When red flags are identified, maintaining airway security is the priority. Dentists must refrain from outpatient extractions or incisions when severe airway compromise is suspected, as surgical trauma can exacerbate edema and trigger respiratory failure.4,5 Instead, patients must be immediately transferred to a hospital equipped with oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMS) and ICU capabilities. During the clinical handover, it is essential to document patient’s comorbidities, observed red flags, and the suspected offending tooth to facilitate expedited contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) imaging.4,5

Upon hospital admission, the clinical team will secure the airway and perform surgical drainage.4,5 It is vital to educate their patients that alongside broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, early extractionS of the offending tooth is not merely an option but a cornerstone of definitive treatment. Performing extraction simultaneously with surgical drainage, or within three days of admission, significantly reduces the length of hospital stay.4 Conversely, clinical data demonstrate that delaying tooth extraction for more than three days is associated with two-fold increase in the rate of fatal mediastinitis, increases the need for repeated neck surgeries, and drastically prolongs ICU admissions.2
