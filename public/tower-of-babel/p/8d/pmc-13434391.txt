Exploring multi-feedstock biodiesel–hydrogen synergies for enhanced diesel engine performance using hybrid AI techniques
Authors: Shiv Lal, Mohd Parvez, Osama Khan, Khaled Alnamasi, Saad Arif, Ashok Kumar Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years of crude oil shortage and price rise, biodiesel has emerged as a new-age sustainable fuel, owing to its positives mainly providing lower amount of gas emissions and compatibility with prevailing petro-diesel engines. The contemporary study explores multiple feasible biodiesels (Mustard, Binola, Caster, Rapeseed, Canola, Peanut, Jatropha, and Thumba) based on composition and testing feasibility, so as to be inter-mixed with hydrogen gas, for improved engine performance and minimal effluents. Primary engine characteristics brake thermal efficiency (BTE), brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC), and multiple effluents of diesel engine were experimentally explored and compared for specific fuel sets. A hybrid methodology comprising of machine learning (k-means clustering) and prediction models is employed to find the optimal biodiesel among the variants, validated against ASTM standards. BTE and BSFC show a strong negative correlation, CO and BSFC a positive correlation, CO₂ and BTE a positive one. With minimal prediction uncertainty, Canola-based oil emerged as the superior biodiesel among others with a RMSE = 1.0, MAPE = 2.95%, R2 = 0.95, owing to favorable fatty acid composition. Canola and Rapeseed oils are placed in the best performing group, validating the improved performance efficiency characteristics and reduced pollutant output. Canola biodiesel achieved the best overall performance with 31.2% BTE, 0.26 kg/kWh BSFC, and low emissions of 0.03% CO, 24 ppm HC, 32 mg/m3 PM, 4.5 ppm SO₂, and 770 ppm NOx, due to improved atomization, oxygenated combustion. This study’s novelty lies in integrating experimental data with hybrid machine learning models for robust of biodiesel blends.

Introduction

Over the years, the fluctuating prices of crude oil resources have been a tremendous concern for growing economies dependent on other countries for regular supply1. This has put the current lot of researchers under pressure to search for alternative forms of fuels which could power the energy intensive systems in a sustainable, renewable and pollutant free manner2. Scientist in recent times have explored multiple forms of renewable energies which have an edge over the conventional forms of energy in terms of emission free power generation3. One such popular form of energy widely used in automotive industry is the Biodiesel fuel. It is often derived from non-edible vegetable oils and is seen as a feasible alternative fuel for diesel engine vehicles4. The ever-rising population across the world has triggered the exploration of alternative fuels which could be potent substitutes to crude oil, simultaneously capable of emitting lower effluents to the environment5. With countries having lower share of raw crude oil, feasible options like biodiesel are rapidly developing into a feasible replacement6. This requires extensive research in exploring the viable materials which can be blended in the biodiesels, enabling it to be the fuel of future.

With similar operation conditions and lower emission rate in diesel engine, Biodiesel has emerged as a primary candidate for replacing petro-diesel based resources without compromising on performance of the system7. Prior studies have reported an appreciable drop of 75% in emission rate with application of biodiesels in comparison to diesel fuel in engines, owing to its cleaner combustion process8. Application of biodiesels further improves the engine life since these fuels possess superior lubrication capabilities eventually reducing the excessive wear and tear within the engine. Moreover, additional capabilities such as being non-toxic and sustainable in nature offers superior storage safety9. Prior studies exploring non-edible oils have produced appreciable performance with reduced exhaust emissions in diesel engine10,11. With variations in blending ratio, biodiesels can be further procured without any modifications in existing diesel engine setup12. With this blending aspect, various biodiesel needs to be compared based on its mixing capacity with other suitable fuels so as to comprehend its enrichment potential in diesel engine13.

Blending hydrogen gas in biodiesels have gained appreciable attention in recent times owing to its benefits such as better combustion efficiency, reduced harmful emissions and enhanced engine performance. This is accomplished since hydrogen has a high flame speed and a wide flammability range which aids in a wholesome combustion process14. Nonetheless, combustion and emission behavior vary greatly with the various kinds of biodiesels comprising variable physicochemical characteristics like viscosity, cetane number, and oxygen content. These properties in turn affect the behavior of combustion process within the engine cylinder15. These properties broadly influence the engine burning characteristics such as ignition delay and heat release rate16. It is thus important to identify the best biodiesel type enriched with hydrogen to enhance the performance of the hybrid fuel, thereby ensuring that the emission is minimized while simultaneously maximizing the overall performance of the engine. Conventional methods of biodiesel comparison often fail to accurately differentiate biodiesel blends due to the significant variability in their physicochemical properties17. These methods typically rely on static or limited parameters and overlook the complex interactions and uncertainties inherent to biodiesel fuels, especially when blended with hydrogen gas18. Therefore, a hybrid modeling technique is crucial to achieve reliable, performance- and emission-optimized fuel blends for sustainable diesel engine operation.

Earlier work on biodiesels have considered the uncertainty analysis of the experimental diesel engine system19. Nevertheless, with application of artificial intelligence, the uncertainty in prepared biodiesel can be individually checked for multiple errors and then compared by providing priority to each performance and emission as outcomes20. The weighted outcomes are then fed into machine learning clustering model which is ready for prioritized comparison based on vehicle requirement free from fuel-based uncertainties. Application of this model will result in a complete comparison of different sets of biodiesels prepared from diverse classes of vegetable oils which earlier seldomly compared with one another. Comparing diverse vegetable-oil biodiesels is essential because their fatty-acid profile, viscosity, cetane quality, and oxygen content differently affect combustion, efficiency, and emissions. A unified comparison helps identify the best biodiesel–hydrogen blend instead of relying on isolated feedstock-specific results. Although several works have been conducted in past to compare biodiesels in previous studies, however few studies have considered comparing the experimental results with prediction models and comparing them based on a priority-based clustering models which effectively differentiates between the produced biodiesels. This study uniquely compares biodiesel blends using both experimental results and advanced prediction models combined with priority-based k-means clustering. The approach quantifies uncertainty in each measurement and prediction, allowing the selection of biodiesel blends with the least variability and most consistent cost-effectiveness.

The comparison process is facilitated by employing a prioritization-prediction machine learning hybrid model, which combines Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) and k-means, to rank biodiesels. The basis for the optimization model is established on based on performance efficiency and emission characteristics of diesel engine, which is more reliable and objective in decision-making. The study is novel in the sense that it constructed a superior model to compare biodiesel that is not normally possible because of the variation in the property of the biodiesel that influence the dynamics of the diesel engine. By addressing property variations in biodiesels, the model enables precise comparison and ranking of the best-performing blends for practical applications. Material and method

This study utilizes biodiesels derived from multiple feedstocks, namely Mustard, Binola, Castor, Rapeseed, Canola, Peanut, Jatropha, and Thumba oils. The extracted oils were subjected to a transesterification process to produce biodiesel suitable for diesel engine applications. Hydrogen gas was employed as a supplementary fuel to form biodiesel–hydrogen blends aimed at improving engine performance and reducing exhaust emissions.

Biodiesel preparation

The conversion of raw oils into biodiesel involves a transesterification reaction as depicted in Fig. 1. In this method, triglycerides of raw fuel react with an alcohol mainly methanol in the presence of a catalyst to produce methyl esters and glycerol as a byproduct. The reaction requires precise conditions, including maintaining a temperature between 50 and 60 °C and ensuring the molar ratio of alcohol to oil is typically 6:1 to drive the reaction to completion. An alkali catalyst, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), is used to speed up the reaction. The catalyst is dissolved in methanol to form methoxide, which reacts with the triglycerides in the oil. This breaks the oil into smaller molecules, resulting in biodiesel and glycerol. After the reaction, the biodiesel is separated from the glycerol layer and purified. The biodiesel is washed with distilled water in order to remove any heavy compounds left in the reaction. Later the biodiesel separates and is taken out and stored. In the subsequent section the transformed biodiesel is first tested for different physicochemical properties and recorded. The biodiesels are later used in diesel engine for performance and emission analysis.

The Redwood viscometer is used to measures the viscosity of the diesel and various biodiesels in the laboratory. The Redwood viscometer is designed for viscosity test of petroleum product, biodiesel, vegetable oils and it confirms IP70 (former) standard. This comprises of a bath with electrical heating suitable for 220 V AC supply, tap, oil cup with precision jet of 1.82 mm diameter and 10 mm length, cup cover, ball valve, stirrer, Thermometer/thermocouple with clip, and Kohlrausch Flask. The flash point apparatus applied electrical heating with energy regulator is used for flash and fire point testing of diesel and biodiesels in the laboratory. This apparatus conforms to the IP34, ASTMD-93 and IS1448 (part 1), 1270 (P-21) and IS 1209–1953 method B.

Engine specifications and experimentation on the test setup

Eight biodiesel feedstocks were selected for the present investigation, namely Mustard oil, Binola raw oil, Castor oil, Rapeseed oil, Canola oil, Peanut oil, Jatropha oil, and Thumba oil. These feedstocks were converted into biodiesel through alkali-catalyzed transesterification and were subsequently used as the liquid pilot fuel for diesel engine operation. For comparative engine testing, each biodiesel was blended with diesel at a fixed biodiesel blend ratio of B20, consisting of 20% biodiesel and 80% diesel by volume. Hydrogen gas was used as a supplementary gaseous fuel and was supplied at a constant flow rate of 4 LPM for all biodiesel–diesel blend cases to maintain uniform comparison among the tested feedstocks. Hydrogen was supplied from a compressed hydrogen cylinder and introduced into the intake manifold of the CI engine using a controlled external supply line. The hydrogen supply system consisted of a pressure regulator, hydrogen flow meter, non-return valve, and flame arrestor to ensure safe and uniform gas induction. The pressure regulator was used to reduce the cylinder pressure to the required operating level, while the flow meter maintained the hydrogen flow rate. The non-return valve prevented reverse flow from the intake manifold.

The experimental setup comprised a single-cylinder, four-stroke, direct-injection diesel engine coupled with an eddy-current dynamometer as shown in Table 3. The engine was operated at constant speed under different loading conditions operation. Biodiesel was supplied through the conventional injection system, while hydrogen was admitted into the intake manifold using a regulated hydrogen supply line equipped with a pressure regulator, gas flow meter, flame arrestor, and non-return valve. Exhaust emissions were measured using a calibrated exhaust gas analyzer and particulate measuring device. Fuel consumption was measured using a burette flow meter, whereas brake power was obtained from the dynamometer. All tests were repeated under steady-state conditions to reduce experimental uncertainty.

The engine was operated at a constant speed of 1500 rpm under steady-state conditions. Tests were conducted at different brake load levels to evaluate the influence of biodiesel–hydrogen operation on performance and emission characteristics. For each biodiesel, the engine was first stabilized under the selected operating condition, after which hydrogen was supplied through the intake manifold at a controlled flow rate. Hydrogen was used as a supplementary gaseous fuel and inducted through the intake manifold, while biodiesel acted as the pilot liquid fuel injected into the cylinder to initiate compression ignition. During combustion, hydrogen’s high flame speed and wide flammability range accelerated flame propagation, improved oxidation of the biodiesel spray. For each test condition, outcomes were recorded after steady engine operation was achieved.

The experiment used a single-cylinder, four-stroke, water-cooled, direct-injection compression ignition diesel engine rated at 5.2 kW and operated at a constant speed of 1500 rpm. The engine had a compression ratio of 17.5:1, with a bore and stroke of approximately 87.5 mm × 110 mm, enabling strong air compression and sufficient temperature rise for auto-ignition of the injected biodiesel fuel. During operation, air entered the cylinder and was compressed to high pressure and temperature. The biodiesel blend was then injected directly into the combustion chamber at an injection pressure of 200 bar and an injection timing of around 23° bTDC, ensuring proper atomization, mixing, and ignition before top dead centre.

Initially, the engine was operated with diesel fuel to obtain baseline performance and emission readings. After baseline stabilization, each biodiesel sample was tested separately under identical operating conditions. Before recording data, the engine was allowed to run for a sufficient stabilization period so that exhaust temperature, speed, and load became steady. Fuel consumption was measured using the fuel flow meter, while brake power was obtained from the dynamometer. Brake thermal efficiency was calculated from brake power and fuel energy input, whereas BSFC was determined from the fuel mass flow rate per unit brake power. Exhaust gases were analyzed using calibrated emission instruments.

Uncertainty analysis

Uncertainty analysis is a statistical representation for ranges of outcomes39. The uncertainty estimation for each outcome was based on the accuracy and precision of the experimental instruments, along with the propagation of measurement errors through derived parameters40. The primary sources of uncertainty include the dynamometer used for brake power (BP) measurement, the fuel flow meter for fuel mass flow rate, and the gas analyzers employed for determining emissions of carbon mono-oxide (CO), carbon di-oxide (CO₂), particulate matter (PM), NOx and sulphur di-oxide (SO₂). All values are expressed as relative standard uncertainties (fractional form) as shown in Table 4. The cumulative standard uncertainty for the entire experiment would be calculated by squaring the uncertainties, adding them all up, and then calculating the square root of the result, if the standard uncertainty of each equipment measurement is provided by a, b, c, etc.

Methodology

The methodology applied is to tackle the complexity in biodiesels properties which require a combined procedure which initially begins with establishing the experimental outcomes for performance and emission parameters of diesel engine. Further the corelation among the outcomes is established, setting a priority for outcomes with higher weightage in diesel engine operation. These values are further simulated for different membership functions establishing the oil with lowest uncertainty. The prediction model is further justified by machine learning clustering model which clusters oil into best, worst and average clusters. The same is explained in Fig. 2 as shown below:

The ANFIS model is chosen for its strength in mapping complex nonlinear relationships between biodiesel properties and engine outcomes using adaptive neuro-fuzzy logic, which reduces prediction errors. The AHP is used to assign priorities to critical engine performance and emissions parameters based on their impact, creating a robust multi-criteria decision framework. k-means clustering is employed to objectively group and rank biodiesels by their performance and reliability, enhancing fuel selection through data-driven, unsupervised classification.

Outcome estimation and analysis

In order to compare multiple biodiesels blended with hydrogen gas, suitable diesel engine characteristics needs to be chosen which complies with ASTM standards. Primarily performance indicators such as brake thermal efficiency (BTE) which interprets the conversion efficiency while, Brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) indicates the energy efficiency and economy of diesel engine. In terms of emission profile, effluents such as CO, CO2, PM, NOx and SO2 are identified as primary outcomes owing to their impact on environmental hazards. Effluents such as CO and PM indicate incomplete combustion and particle matter related pollution. ANFIS prediction modelling

The experimental dataset derived for the eight biodiesel–hydrogen fuel cases are further modelled using a combined framework involving Pearson correlation, prediction, AHP priority weighting, and k-means clustering. In the ANFIS (Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System) stage, the dataset was divided into 70% training and 30% testing subsets, and different membership functions were assessed to identify the most accurate nonlinear prediction structure. The model was validated using RMSE, MAPE, and R2, while the final decision-making reliability was further confirmed by AHP-based weighting and k-means clustering of biodiesel blends into best, average, and worst performance categories.

The ANFIS prediction model is incorporated into the study to analyze and establish complex non-linear relationships between the input parameters and engine outcomes41. AHP weightage method

The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) is an organized administrative technique that allocates priority to standard outcomes based on their relevance in a hierarchical context43. Pairwise comparisons are accomplished for every inter-dependent parameter, where algorithm assign the relative importance of one outcome over other44. A consistency ratio guarantees steadfast verdicts. The consequential evaluation matrix is normalized, and eigenvectors are premeditated to originate priority values for each outcome. These weightages rank substitutes, enabling a better standing which aids in a multi-criteria comparison problem, frequently applied in engine-based studies.

k-means clustering method

K-means is a centroid-based clustering algorithm that groups data into k distinct clusters by minimizing the variance within each cluster45. It iteratively assigns data points to clusters based on proximity to centroids, which are updated until convergence. k-means clustering is pivotal in this analysis as it effectively groups biodiesel in respective clusters46. This describes subsequent steps of k-means clustering:

Step 1: Initialize centroids

The k cluster centers are initialized which correspond to actual data points within the dataset.

Step 2: Assign data points to clusters

Compute the distance of each data point from all cluster and assign each data point to the cluster based on Euclidean distance.

Result and discussion

Biodiesel often prepared by different vegetable oils are not compared together due to complex physio-chemical properties among the variants which result in irregular comparison. The current study applies a hybrid weighted machine learning prediction model which takes in consideration the complexities in properties of such oils with a simulated predicted model using a corelation analysis for outcomes, which is further justified by priority-based machine learning approach. In subsequent sections the comparison between these variants is explained in detail.

Corelation analysis

The Pearson correlation matrix in Fig. 3 explains the interdependence among performance and emission outcomes for the tested biodiesel–hydrogen blends, where the strength and direction of each relationship are governed by fuel oxygen content, viscosity, atomization quality, combustion completeness, and hydrogen-assisted flame propagation. A very strong negative correlation is observed between BTE and BSFC with r = − 0.98953 and adjusted R2 = 0.9757, confirming that blends producing higher useful brake power consume less fuel per unit power output. This trend is expected because biodiesels with better atomization, suitable cetane quality, and hydrogen-assisted fast combustion promote more complete energy release, thereby increasing BTE while reducing BSFC48. The BSFC–CO relationship shows a strong positive correlation with r = 0.98349 and adjusted R2 = 0.96179, indicating that fuels requiring higher consumption also tend to produce more CO due to incomplete oxidation. This occurs when higher viscosity or poorer volatility delays air–fuel mixing, creating locally rich zones where carbon is not fully converted into CO₂. Similarly, CO₂ shows a strong positive correlation with BTE with r = 0.98832 and adjusted R2 = 0.9729, suggesting that efficient combustion promotes more complete carbon oxidation, converting CO into CO₂ and improving thermal efficiency49.

Fig. 3Pearson-r corelation analysis for diesel engine outcomes.

Pearson-r corelation analysis for diesel engine outcomes.

The added HC and NOx parameters further strengthen the combustion interpretation. HC is strongly and positively correlated with BSFC, CO, and PM because unburned hydrocarbons increase when fuel droplets are poorly atomized or combustion is locally incomplete. PM also increases with BSFC and CO, showing that soot-forming conditions are linked with poor fuel utilization and incomplete combustion50. In contrast, NOx shows a negative correlation with BTE with r = − 0.91892 and adjusted R2 = 0.81849, and a positive relation, indicating that in this dataset NOx is not governed only by peak temperature but also by local combustion heterogeneity and high-temperature residence zones51. Therefore, biodiesels such as canola can achieve higher BTE with comparatively controlled NOx because their favorable physicochemical properties support smoother atomization, shorter combustion duration, and reduced localized hot spots52. SO₂ shows comparatively weaker practical influence because biodiesels inherently contain very low sulfur; therefore, its variation mainly reflects minor feedstock-level sulfur differences rather than dominant combustion chemistry52.

Prediction simulation based analysis

The recognized corelations for experimental results where further fed into the ANFIS model to prepare a prediction simulation model which would identify the biodiesels prepared for least error, thereby establishing an agreement between the experimental and predicted results. Amongst the tested biodiesels, Canola based biodiesels exhibit lowest uncertainty in the predicted model (RMSE = 1.0, MAPE = 2.95%, and R2 = 0.95) as shown in Fig. 4. This is primarily due to exceptional fatty acid composition, enabling superior cetane number with diminished viscosity levels. With such exceptional properties a favourable combustion process is developed within the diesel engine enabling optimal outcomes. Following closely, Rapeseed biodiesel also shows lower error rate (RMSE = 1.1, MAPE = 3.1%, and R2 = 0.95), reflecting a better combustion process with better properties. In contrast other identified biodiesels, predicted higher levels of uncertainty in particularly for oils such as Mustard (RMSE = 1.2, MAPE = 3.25%) and Thumba (RMSE = 1.5, MAPE = 3.8%). This is eventually due to their unwanted variation in physio-chemical properties where heightened viscosity and volatility effects the combustion process.

Prediction model performance for various biodiesel oils.

Gaussian and generalized bell-shaped membership functions often deliver higher accuracy due to their smooth mapping of nonlinear relationships, but they also demand greater computational effort in terms of CPU time and memory storage. In contrast, simpler membership functions like triangular or trapezoidal types are typically less computationally intensive, offering faster training and reduced memory usage, although with marginally lower predictive accuracy for highly complex datasets. The AI-model outcomes are validated by comparison with previous engine-AI studies, where ANFIS and ML models were successfully validated against experimental engine data using RMSE, MAPE, and R2; for example, Roy et al. (2015)29 used ANFIS to reproduce experimental diesel-engine performance/emission observations, while Sanjeevannavar et al. (2023)23 applied ML models to predict diesel-engine performance and emissions, confirming that experimental–predicted agreement is an accepted validation route for such studies.

AHP weightage analysis

The AHP method, supported by correlation analysis, is used to assign priority weights to the diesel engine performance and emission outcomes for comparing biodiesels, as shown in Fig. 5. BSFC acquired the highest priority weight of 0.32, because fuel economy is highly sensitive to biodiesel viscosity, density, calorific value, and atomization quality, which directly affect fuel consumption and combustion stability. CO emissions received the second-highest weight of 0.26, as CO strongly reflects incomplete combustion caused by poor air–fuel mixing, delayed oxidation, and variation in oxygen availability among biodiesel feedstocks. NOx is assigned a significant weight of 0.15, because biodiesel–hydrogen combustion can increase local flame temperature and oxygen availability, making NOx a major limiting pollutant. CO₂ received a moderate weight of 0.12, since it represents the degree of complete carbon oxidation and combustion efficiency, but its variation among biodiesel–hydrogen cases was less dominant than BSFC, CO, and NOx. PM and SO₂ were assigned lower weights of 0.08 and 0.05, respectively, because biodiesel’s inherent oxygen content generally suppresses soot formation, while its naturally low sulfur content limits SO₂ formation. BTE received the lowest weight of 0.02, not because it is unimportant, but because the BTE variation among the tested biodiesels was comparatively narrow; therefore, it had lower discriminating power.

Discussion

Emission analysis plays a crucial role in evaluating the environmental performance of fuels, as it reflects the combustion efficiency and the extent of pollutant formation in diesel engine operations. CO₂ emissions are comparatively higher for fuels like Canola and Jatropha, indicating more complete combustion due to their higher oxygen content as shown in Fig. 8. CO emissions decrease for Rapeseed and Thumba biodiesels because the inherent oxygen in biodiesel promotes better oxidation of CO to CO₂, while higher viscosity fuels such as Castor oil show slightly elevated CO due to poor atomization. PM emissions are lowest for Mustard and Thumba biodiesels since biodiesel combustion generates fewer soot precursors and lacks aromatic hydrocarbons, though high-viscosity fuels like Jatropha and Peanut may form larger droplets and incomplete combustion31,51. NOx levels for Canola blends were also reported to be on the lower side because its comparatively balanced unsaturated fatty-acid profile, moderate cetane quality, lower effective, and better volatility than heavier oils such as castor, jatropha, and thumba promoted finer atomization and uniform premixed combustion56–58. This further lead to suppressing of local over-temperature zones and reducing high-temperature in NOx residence time. SO₂ emissions remain minimal for all biodiesels because vegetable-based fuels contain negligible sulfur compared to petroleum diesel, with minor differences attributed to residual sulfur in the feedstock35. Overall, the emission trends are governed by the biodiesel’s oxygen content, viscosity, and molecular structure, which enhance oxidation reactions, leading to reduced CO, PM, NOx and SO₂ but slightly higher CO₂ emissions demonstrating biodiesel’s cleaner and more efficient combustion characteristics in diesel engines59.

Fig. 8Comparison of emission outcomes for all biodiesels.

Comparison of emission outcomes for all biodiesels.

The canola biodiesel as compared to other have established their dominance as the superior fuel in diesel engine operation. The priority machine learning approach also clustered canola oil into the best cluster with optimal outcomes in accordance with diesel engine operation. The biodiesel has favourable properties as compared to other oils such as superior cetane number and higher oxygen content which results in better combustion scenario, eventually lowering emissions such as CO60,61. Furthermore, lower content of carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, limits the transformation of major portion of CO into CO262,63. Furthermore, the surplus oxygen in canola biodiesel lowers the PM predominantly limiting the sulphur content64.

In terms of performance parameters (BTE and BSFC), Canola oil registered optimal values such as higher BTE and lower BSFC value amongst other biodiesels as shown in Fig. 9. This is primarily due to better fatty acid concentration in the initial oil conversion process65. This eventually transmits into wholesome combustion characteristics, yielding a better energy utilisation and efficiency66. Furthermore, diminished viscosity levels of the above biodiesel results in better atomisation with superior air–fuel mixing. This in turn reduces ignition delay leading to superior energy conversion with better BTE values in diesel engine. Subsequently, the lowered viscosity levels lead to lower fuel consumption leading to a lower BSFC value as compared to other biodiesels. These combined assets of Canola biodiesel establish it as a superior choice for improving engine performance.

Conclusions

Biodiesel is currently being explored as a potential alternative source of energy generation renewable fuels with environmental and economic benefits. However, its extensive application in diesel engines is often hindered due to its high viscosity and combustion inefficiency in diverse climates. These issues can be efficiently addressed through addition of hydrogen gas in diesel engines to supplement biodiesel which can boost the efficiency of combustion, simultaneously decreasing the emission rate. Diverse physicochemical characteristics of various types of biodiesels implies that several blends should be evaluated systematically to determine the optimal oil when blended with hydrogen gas, regarding engine operation and emissions.

The research applies a hybrid research methodology that combines prediction and priority-based machine learning (k-means clustering) and artificial intelligence (ANFIS) models that can accurately analyze diesel engine operation outcomes. The hybrid method helps in the process of determining the best biodiesel-hydrogen blend to achieve efficient and low-emissions operation in diesel engines. Although the above study displayed appreciable advantages, however possible limitations of the study comprise of scalability issues where produced fuel needs to be transported from laboratory to industries thereby overcoming Raw material accessibility, distribution network effectiveness, and expense limitations. Moreover, extended engine durability issues still remain an issue unexplored which is a direct consequence of diesel engine fueled with biodiesel and hydrogen blends. Future studies may be based on combining the developed optimization algorithms such as harmony search or genetic algorithms in order to get a better predictive accuracy with the least computational effort. The inclusion of the real-world transient conditions would generally improve model robustness and facilitate its application in commercial biodiesel engine management systems. Moreover, a detailed techno-economic assessment, feedstock cost, transesterification cost, hydrogen supply cost, and scale-up infrastructure should be performed in future to assess whether the selected biodiesel–hydrogen blends are economically viable beyond laboratory engine testing.
