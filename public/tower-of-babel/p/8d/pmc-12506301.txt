Association of longitudinal blood urea nitrogen trajectory with in-hospital and 28-day mortality in acute heart failure with cardiorenal syndrome
Authors: Peng Liu, Fenying Qu, Chunyong Ma, Yongkui Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivePatients with acute heart failure (AHF) complicated by acute cardiorenal syndrome (CRS) demonstrate substantial mortality risk. Conventional blood urea nitrogen (BUN) assessments using single-point measurements inadequately capture the temporal dynamics of renal dysfunction and disease progression. This study investigated the prognostic utility of BUN trajectory patterns for predicting in-hospital and 28-day mortality in AHF patients with CRS.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database as the derivation cohort and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) as the validation cohort. BUN measurements were obtained at baseline (< 24 h), 72 h, and 144 h across both databases. Longitudinal BUN trajectory patterns were identified using latent class growth modeling (LCGM). Multivariable logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses evaluated the association between trajectory groups and in-hospital and 28-day all-cause mortality, respectively. Subgroup and interaction analyses assessed the robustness of trajectory-based predictions across patient characteristics.ResultsAmong 2931 patients across both cohorts, LCGM identified three distinct BUN trajectory patterns: persistent-low, persistent-moderate, and persistent-high trajectory groups. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a linear relationship between baseline BUN levels and both mortality outcomes (P > 0.05 for non-linearity), though with inconsistent predictive performance. Compared with the persistent-high trajectory group, the persistent-low trajectory group demonstrated significantly reduced in-hospital mortality (derivation cohort: OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.27–0.85; validation cohort: OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.93) and 28-day mortality (derivation cohort: HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.94; validation cohort: HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.85). Subgroup analyses indicated enhanced predictive performance of the persistent-low trajectory group in non-diabetic patients across both cohorts. No significant interactions were observed across subgroups, including different acute kidney injury stages.ConclusionBUN trajectory patterns provide independent prognostic information for short-term outcomes in AHF patients with CRS. Persistent-low BUN trajectory are associated with significantly improved survival compared to persistent-high trajectory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03232-9.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) complicated by acute cardiorenal syndrome (CRS) presents substantial clinical challenges due to the intricate bidirectional interactions between cardiac and renal dysfunction [1, 2]. In cardiac intensive care units (ICUs), the prevalence of AHF with concurrent CRS ranges from 25 to 50% [3, 4]. The pathophysiologic cascade involves rapid deterioration of cardiac function resulting in decreased cardiac output, impaired renal perfusion, elevated venous congestion, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), and inflammatory cascade activation, collectively compromising renal function [5, 6]. CRS subsequently perpetuates persistent volume overload, diuretic resistance, and severe acid–base and electrolyte disturbances, thereby complicating AHF management and frequently necessitating renal replacement therapy, ultimately contributing to adverse patient outcomes [7–9]. These complex pathophysiologic interactions underscore the critical importance of timely and accurate risk stratification in this vulnerable patient population.

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) serves as a pivotal biomarker for both diagnostic evaluation and prognostic assessment in AHF complicated by CRS [10–13]. In patients with CRS, excessive neurohormonal activation through the RAAS and sympathetic nervous systems elevates BUN concentrations, establishing it as a robust predictor of progressive renal dysfunction [11, 12]. Beyond reflecting renal metabolic capacity, BUN demonstrates strong associations with adverse clinical outcomes in AHF patients [4, 6, 8, 10]. However, contemporary research predominantly employs single-point BUN measurements for risk assessment in AHF or CRS, serving primarily as static prognostic indicators [14–16]. This conventional approach presents inherent limitations for ICU risk stratification, as the absence of dynamic monitoring prevents accurate capture of the rapid physiologic fluctuations and disease progression characteristic of critically ill patients, potentially resulting in suboptimal risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making.

Trajectory-based analytical approaches, originally developed by Nagin et al. [17], employ latent class growth modeling (LCGM) to identify distinct developmental patterns within heterogeneous populations over time. This methodology has gained substantial traction in contemporary medical research, demonstrating superior performance in capturing temporal disease evolution compared to traditional single-point or simple change-based metrics [18–20]. By identifying latent subgroups with similar longitudinal patterns, trajectory modeling provides enhanced prognostic granularity and clinical utility. Therefore, this study investigated the association between BUN trajectory patterns and in-hospital and 28-day mortality in AHF patients with CRS, hypothesizing that distinct BUN trajectory groups would demonstrate differential prognostic significance beyond conventional single-point measurements.

Methods

Data sources

This retrospective cohort study utilized two large-scale critical care databases: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) as the derivation cohort and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) as the validation cohort. The MIMIC-IV database encompasses clinical data from over 50,000 ICU patients treated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. The eICU-CRD contains medical records for more than 200,000 patients across 335 ICUs in 208 hospitals throughout the United States between 2014 and 2015. Both databases provide comprehensive clinical information including demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory results, therapeutic interventions, illness severity measures, and diagnostic codes based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) [21]. All patient data were de-identified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) standards. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and received institutional review board approval with waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature and use of de-identified data.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using Navicat Premium (version 16.1.12) with structured query language (SQL) from MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) and eICU-CRD (version 2.0). Database access was obtained by a certified investigator (Liu) following completion of the National Institutes of Health online training program (certificate number 43105842) and institutional approval.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged ≥ 18 years with complete medical records and concurrent diagnoses of AHF and CRS within 24 h of ICU admission.

Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years, repeat ICU admissions for the same patient, and incomplete clinical data preventing trajectory analysis. Disease identification utilized validated ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes for AHF and CRS as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Variable extraction included demographic characteristics (age, sex), vital signs (heart rate, oxygen saturation, mean arterial pressure, body temperature), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, severe liver disease, chronic pulmonary disease, acute kidney injury staging), mechanical ventilation status, and laboratory parameters (white blood cell count, red blood cell count, platelet count, serum creatinine, glucose, BUN). BUN measurements were systematically collected at three time points: baseline (< 24 h), 72 h, and 144 h following ICU admission.

Data processing protocols employed the worst recorded values within the first 24 h for vital signs and the most abnormal laboratory results within the corresponding time windows. Demographic and comorbidity data were recorded at ICU admission. Temporal data extraction utilized database timestamp functionality with custom SQL queries to ensure precise time-window adherence and comprehensive variable capture.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint was 28-day all-cause mortality from ICU admission. The secondary endpoint was in-hospital all-cause mortality. Mortality data were extracted from hospital discharge records and verified through database cross-referencing to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. Between-group comparisons employed the Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate based on data distribution and sample size.

Dose–response relationship analysis

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles examined non-linear associations between baseline BUN levels and mortality outcomes.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves evaluated time-to-event outcomes from ICU admission to death or censoring at 28 days, with between-group comparisons using the log-rank test.

Trajectory modeling

LCGM identified distinct longitudinal BUN trajectory patterns assuming population heterogeneity with individual membership in discrete latent classes. Model specifications included linear, quadratic, and cubic polynomial functions to capture trajectory complexity. Model selection systematically compared 1–6 trajectory classes using parameter estimates from simpler models as initial values for complex models to prevent convergence to local optima.

Model selection criteria included the following: (1) minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC); (2) mean posterior class membership probability > 0.8; (3) mean posterior probability > 0.7; and (4) minimum class size > 5% of total sample to ensure statistical stability.

Regression analyses

Multivariable logistic regression assessed associations between BUN trajectory groups and in-hospital mortality, while Cox proportional hazards models evaluated 28-day mortality risk. Covariates included age, sex, diabetes mellitus, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), baseline serum creatinine, and acute kidney injury stage. Proportional hazards assumptions were verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Stratified analyses examined trajectory-mortality associations across clinically relevant subgroups (age categories, sex, diabetes status) with formal interaction testing using likelihood ratio tests.

Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with packages including lcmm for trajectory modeling and survival for time-to-event analyses.

Results

Identification of three distinct BUN trajectory patterns in 2931 patients

This analysis included 2931 patients across both cohorts: 1056 patients in the derivation cohort (MIMIC-IV) and 1875 patients in the validation cohort (eICU-CRD) (Fig. 1). LCGM identified three distinct longitudinal BUN trajectory patterns. The persistent-low trajectory group comprised 717 patients (67.9%) in the derivation cohort and 1172 patients (62.5%) in the validation cohort, characterized by consistently low BUN concentrations throughout the study period. The persistent-moderate trajectory group included 268 patients (25.4%) in the derivation cohort and 579 patients (30.9%) in the validation cohort, demonstrating stable intermediate BUN levels with minimal temporal variation during follow-up. The persistent-high trajectory group encompassed 71 patients (6.7%) in the derivation cohort and 124 patients (6.6%) in the validation cohort, exhibiting persistently elevated BUN concentrations throughout the observation period (Fig. 2).

Validation cohort analysis demonstrated consistent patterns with the derivation cohort. The persistent-high trajectory group again showed significantly higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus and peripheral vascular disease, as well as increased CRRT utilization (all P < 0.05). This group also exhibited higher proportions of male patients and serum creatinine, BUN, and glucose concentrations. Restricted cubic spline analysis of baseline BUN and mortality outcomes

Restricted cubic spline analysis examined the dose–response relationship between baseline BUN levels and mortality outcomes in both cohorts (Fig. 3). The analysis revealed predominantly linear associations between baseline BUN concentrations and both in-hospital and 28-day mortality (P for non-linearity > 0.05 in both cohorts), suggesting consistent prognostic utility across the BUN range. However, baseline BUN demonstrated inconsistent predictive performance for 28-day mortality in the validation cohort, indicating potential limitations of single-point BUN measurements for risk stratification in this patient population (Fig. 3).

28-day mortality analysis

Cox proportional hazards regression revealed consistent patterns for 28-day mortality outcomes. In the derivation cohort, the persistent-low trajectory group maintained significant mortality risk reduction (adjusted HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.94), while the persistent-moderate trajectory group showed no significant difference compared to the persistent-high trajectory group (adjusted HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.56–1.46). The validation cohort confirmed these findings, with the persistent-low trajectory group demonstrating significant protection against 28-day mortality (adjusted HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.85). Notably, the persistent-moderate trajectory group also showed significant mortality reduction in the validation cohort (adjusted HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.48–0.89) (Table 2). The consistency of associations across all three adjustment models supports the robustness of the relationship between BUN trajectory patterns and mortality outcomes in AHF patients with CRS.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of BUN trajectory groups

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for 28-day mortality demonstrated distinct survival patterns across BUN trajectory groups in both cohorts (Fig. 4A, B). The persistent-low trajectory group exhibited the highest survival probability with stable survival curves throughout the 28-day observation period. Conversely, both persistent-moderate and persistent-high trajectory groups showed progressive survival deterioration over time, with the persistent-high trajectory group demonstrating the poorest survival outcomes. Log-rank testing confirmed highly significant differences in survival distributions among trajectory groups (P < 0.001 in both cohorts).

Subgroup analysis of 28-day mortality by BUN trajectory groups

Derivation cohort subgroup analysis

Stratified analyses revealed differential trajectory effects across patient subgroups (Fig. 5). Age-stratified analysis showed significant mortality reduction in the persistent-low group among patients > 50 years (adjusted HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.29–0.81, P = 0.006), with non-significant effects in younger patients. Sex-stratified analysis demonstrated significant risk reduction in the persistent-low group among female patients (adjusted HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.14–0.75, P = 0.009) but not males. Diabetes stratification revealed significant mortality reduction in the persistent-low trajectory group among non-diabetic patients (adjusted HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.19–0.77, P = 0.007). Similar protective trends were observed in patients not receiving CRRT and those with advanced acute kidney injury stages, though statistical significance was not consistently achieved. Formal interaction testing revealed no significant effect modification across any examined subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Validation cohort subgroup analysis

Validation cohort analyses largely confirmed derivation cohort findings with some variations (Fig. 6). Age-stratified analysis showed trends toward mortality reduction in the persistent-low trajectory group among patients > 50 years, though statistical significance was not achieved (adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.47–0.82, P = 0.066). Sex-stratified analysis revealed significant risk reduction in males (adjusted HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.36–0.80, P = 0.02) but not females, contrasting with derivation cohort findings. Consistent with the derivation cohort, non-diabetic patients in the persistent-low trajectory group demonstrated significantly reduced mortality risk (adjusted HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.29–0.91, P = 0.004). CRRT stratification showed trends toward improved survival in the persistent-low trajectory group among patients not receiving CRRT (adjusted HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.43–0.89, P = 0.099). Acute kidney injury stage analysis revealed no significant associations between persistent-low trajectory group membership and AKI severity (all P > 0.05). Importantly, interaction tests across all examined subgroups were non-significant (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating consistent trajectory effects across patient characteristics.

Discussion

Novel insights from BUN trajectory analysis in AHF with CRS

This investigation identified three distinct BUN trajectory patterns in 2931 patients with AHF complicated by CRS using LCGM: persistent-low, persistent-moderate, and persistent-high trajectory groups. The persistent-low trajectory group, characterized by consistently lower BUN concentrations, demonstrated superior survival outcomes with significantly reduced in-hospital mortality and enhanced 28-day survival rates compared to the persistent-high trajectory group. This represents the first application of trajectory-based analysis to BUN dynamics in the AHF-CRS population, revealing previously unrecognized phenotypic heterogeneity that traditional single-point measurements fail to capture [22–25].

The pathophysiologic significance of these trajectory patterns extends beyond simple glomerular filtration rate fluctuations. In AHF complicated by acute CRS, the cascade of reduced cardiac output and decreased effective circulating volume triggers compensatory neurohormonal activation involving both the RAAS system and sympathetic nervous system [2, 7, 26]. This activation promotes enhanced sodium and water reabsorption in proximal tubules, with urea transport intrinsically linked to this process through shared transport mechanisms. Concurrent vasopressin elevation further augments urea reabsorption via collecting duct transporters (UT-A1), resulting in increased circulating urea levels [26]. Our trajectory patterns therefore reflect distinct neurohormonal states: persistent-high BUN trajectories likely represent sustained maladaptive responses with maximal renal urea salvage despite therapeutic intervention, while persistent-low trajectories suggest preserved renal functional reserve and appropriate neurohormonal modulation. This mechanistic framework explains why BUN trajectories provided independent prognostic information beyond baseline creatinine and AKI staging in our fully adjusted models, supporting their clinical utility as dynamic markers of cardiorenal interaction severity.

Advancing from static markers to dynamic trajectories: the innovation of LCGM in CRS risk stratification

While the prognostic significance of BUN in AHF and CRS is well established through studies utilizing single-point measurements [10, 13, 16, 27–29], our study represents a methodological advancement by applying LCGM to delineate heterogeneous temporal patterns of renal dysfunction. Unlike static “snapshots” or simple dynamic metrics (delta-BUN, BUN/creatinine ratio changes) that assume uniform patterns across populations, our LCGM approach employs a data-driven, unsupervised methodology to identify latent subgroups with distinct BUN trajectory patterns without a priori assumptions[13, 30]. This innovation is particularly relevant in CRS, where temporal evolution reflects complex interplay between cardiac output, neurohormonal activation, and therapeutic responses [6, 26]. The identification of three distinct trajectory groups reveals that patients with similar baseline characteristics can follow fundamentally different clinical courses—our persistent-high trajectory group, comprising only 6.7% of patients but accounting for 35% of deaths, represents a previously unrecognized high-risk phenotype that would be missed by traditional approaches. The clinical superiority of trajectory-based modeling is evidenced by robust associations with outcomes in both cohorts, with the persistent-low trajectory conferring a 53% reduction in in-hospital mortality (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.27–0.85) and 44% reduction in 28-day mortality (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.94) compared to the persistent-high group, even after adjusting for baseline renal markers and AKI stage. From a mechanistic perspective, distinct trajectory patterns likely reflect different pathophysiologic processes: persistent-high trajectories may indicate sustained neurohormonal overdrive with maximal urea reabsorption despite therapy, while persistent-low trajectories suggest preserved renal functional reserve [7, 26]. This pathophysiologic heterogeneity, invisible to static measurements, underscores why trajectory-based approaches provide superior risk stratification and aligns with recent trajectory studies in critical care contexts [24, 31].

Building upon this mechanistic foundation and clinical evidence, our trajectory-based approach aligns with current clinical practice guidelines. The 2023 ESC Heart Failure Guidelines explicitly recommend “serial measurements of eGFR and potassium, especially in patients with CKD or at high risk for AKI such as those with cardiorenal syndrome” [32], supporting the rationale for dynamic monitoring over static assessments. Our LCGM methodology operationalizes this guideline recommendation by systematically capturing the cardiac-renal interplay throughout hospitalization, offering superior prognostic value compared to single-point measurements that can be influenced by non-renal factors [10]. The broad clinical applicability of this approach is demonstrated through subgroup analyses, which revealed consistent protective effects of the persistent-low trajectory across diverse patient populations—including those older than 50 years, non-diabetics, and patients not receiving CRRT—with no significant interactions (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating robust performance across different clinical contexts.

Clinical management strategies based on BUN trajectory patterns

While our findings suggest potential clinical applications, translation to clinical practice requires careful consideration of current evidence limitations and established treatment paradigms. Patients exhibiting persistent-low BUN trajectories may benefit from standard guideline-directed medical therapy optimization, including appropriate RAAS inhibition, beta-blockade, and SGLT2 inhibitor therapy where indicated, with careful monitoring to maintain favorable trajectory patterns [27, 29]. For patients developing persistent-moderate BUN trajectories, intensified surveillance and potential therapy escalation warrant consideration. This might include enhanced diuretic strategies, earlier consideration of combination therapies, and more frequent monitoring intervals, though specific interventions require validation in prospective studies before routine implementation [4, 6]. The persistent-high trajectory group presents the greatest clinical challenge, potentially benefiting from early multidisciplinary evaluation including nephrology and advanced heart failure consultations. While our data suggest poorer outcomes in this group, the optimal therapeutic approach—whether involving early renal replacement therapy consideration, mechanical circulatory support evaluation, or other interventions—remains to be determined through prospective investigation [1, 3, 9]. Importantly, these trajectory-based risk stratification insights should complement rather than replace established clinical assessment and guideline-directed care. The broad consistency of trajectory effects across patient subgroups (age, sex, diabetes status) without significant interactions suggests potential generalizability, though external validation remains essential before widespread clinical adoption.

Limitations of this study

Several important limitations merit consideration. First, this retrospective analysis of MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD data carries inherent risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding despite comprehensive covariate adjustment. Second, certain patient populations—including those with advanced chronic kidney disease or post-cardiac surgery patients—were underrepresented, potentially limiting generalizability to these important clinical contexts. Third, while LCGM successfully identified distinct trajectory patterns, this observational design cannot establish causal relationships or definitively guide therapeutic decision-making without prospective validation. Finally, the clinical management strategies proposed in Sect. 4.3 represent expert consensus interpretations of our findings rather than evidence-based recommendations, requiring prospective validation before clinical implementation. Despite these limitations, our findings provide novel insights into cardiorenal syndrome pathophysiology and establish a foundation for future prospective studies investigating trajectory-guided therapeutic approaches.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BUN trajectory patterns, identified through LCGM in 2,931 patients across two independent cohorts, independently predict mortality outcomes in AHF complicated by CRS. The persistent-low trajectory group exhibited approximately 50% reduction in both in-hospital and 28-day mortality compared to the persistent-high trajectory group after comprehensive adjustment. Our trajectory-based approach reveals superior prognostic value of dynamic biomarker monitoring over traditional single-point measurements, offering a promising tool for enhanced risk stratification in cardiorenal syndrome management. Prospective validation remains necessary before clinical implementation.
