The "Eagle Ray Sign" of the lumbar epidural space: A case of vascular-dominant spinal angiolipoma mimicking Aetobatus spp. morphology with favorable outcome and literature review

Introduction

Spinal epidural angiolipomas (SEALs) are rare, benign mesenchymal tumors composed of mature adipocytes and anomalous vascular channels. They account for only 0.04–1.2% of all spinal axis tumors, with a strong predilection for the posterior thoracic region (Kuroda et al., 1990; Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Umana et al., 2022). While thoracic cases are well-documented, lumbar localizations remain uncommon, often involving two to three vertebral segments (Akhaddar et al., 2000; Kang et al., 2017; Louis et al., 2016; Pagni and Canavero; Thellakanthiah Ekambaram et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023).

Histologically, SEALs are classified by their degree of vascularity, typically featuring peripheral capillary proliferation and occasional fibrin thrombi (Louis et al., 2016; Thellakanthiah Ekambaram et al., 2025). Despite their benign nature, SEALs present diagnostic challenges due to their polymorphic radiological features, which can mimic more aggressive epidural lesions. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), they typically appear iso-to hyperintense on T1 and hyperintense on T2 sequences, showing marked enhancement with gadolinium (Leu et al., 2003; Provenzale, 1996). Total surgical excision remains the standard of care for symptomatic lesions, yielding an excellent prognosis (Lin, 1974).

In this manuscript, the authors describe a rare case of a lumbar SEAL in a 32-year-old woman, which manifested as progressive, chronic, and mechanically-induced low back pain. A distinctive macroscopic feature of the resected specimen was its resemblance to an Aetobatus spp., leading the authors to propose the term "Eagle Ray Sign" as a morphological descriptor for this finding. The authors provide a detailed analysis of the clinical presentation, diagnostic imaging, and histology. This case, supplemented by a review of the existing literature, seeks to expand clinician knowledge and stimulate further research into this uncommon pathology.

Case report

The patient is a 32-year-old woman with a significant occupational history involving work as a factory sewing machine operator, a job that necessitates maintaining a static sitting posture for over 8 h per day. She is G2P2 (Gravida 2, Para 2), with an unremarkable past medical and surgical history. She denies current or past use of tobacco or alcohol. The woman presented for neurosurgical evaluation following referral for the progressive management of chronic, debilitating low back pain. This pain, which was of insidious onset and had increased in severity, is explicitly described as non-radicular, lacking the typical dermatomal distribution consistent with classic sciatica or lower extremity radiculopathy. Critically, the pain was mechanically induced, becoming markedly exacerbated by even minimal axial spinal loading, and had demonstrated therapeutic resistance to conventional pharmacological interventions, specifically non-opioid analgesics and non-steroidal anti-iflammatory drugs. Further historical inquiry reveals an absence of crucial "red flag" symptoms, including a denial of sphincter or sexual dysfunction, neurogenic claudication, antecedent spinal trauma, or recent febrile episodes.

Upon physical examination, the woman demonstrated mild to moderate antalgic posturing and was mildly distressed. Active and passive range of motion of the thoracolumbar spine was significantly restricted, manifesting as a severe limitation in spinal mobility. This restriction was compounded by pronounced hypertonia and guarding of the paravertebral musculature upon palpation. Quantitative assessment revealed severely impaired forward flexion, specifically noted by a finger-to-floor distance exceeding 1 m and a pathological result on the Schober Test. Importantly, a detailed neurological assessment of the lower extremities yielded no evidence of focal motor deficit, with osteotendinous reflexes symmetrically intact and bilaterally preserved. Furthermore, the patient exhibited no signs of sensory deficit in the lower limbs, nor was there any indication of saddle anesthesia, further mitigating suspicion of a significant central or cauda equina compressive lesion.

The lumbar computed tomography (CT) scan (Fig. 1) exhibited a significant intracanal lesion in the lumbar spine, which is described as isodense and dimensioned at 14 × 53 mm (anteroposterior × craniocaudal). This lesion is strategically positioned to occupy and expand the epidural space between the L2 and L3 vertebral levels. Crucially, the absence of associated osseous erosion or bony involvement at the adjacent vertebral bodies or posterior elements suggests that the lesion is primarily soft-tissue in origin rather than an aggressive osseous tumor. The presence of this large, space-occupying lesion provides a compelling anatomical explanation for the patient's severe, mechanically-exacerbated low back pain and its refractory nature.

Importantly, the bone window (c, white arrows) and the three-dimensional volume rendering (d, white arrows) definitively exclude any osseous erosion or pathological bony involvement of the adjacent vertebral bodies or posterior elements.

The subsequent lumbar MRI (Fig. 2) was instrumental in the definitive characterization of the lesion, confirming an expansive mass situated within the posterior epidural canal at the L2-L3 intervertebral level, now dimensioned at 17 mm in thickness by 56 mm in height. This mass exhibited a characteristic T1-weighted hyposignal and a T2-weighted hypersignal semiology, with a heterogeneous signal on the Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) sequence. Crucially for diagnosis, the lesion demonstrated marked and homogeneous enhancement following the intravenous administration of gadolinium chelate, Furthermore, this pathological process exhibited bilateral foraminal extension at L2-L3 and exerted significant mass effect, resulting in the anterior displacement and severe compression of the cauda equina nerve roots. All these feature suggested a highly vascular or cellular tumor such as an epidural neurogenic tumor (e.g., schwannoma) or a highly cellular metastatic process. The strong suspicion of a highly vascular lesion, derived from the marked and homogeneous enhancement observed on the post-gadolinium T1-weighted MRI sequence, naturally necessitates the inclusion of hemangioma in the differential diagnosis. This consideration is academically robust, as epidural hemangiomas are benign, slow-growing vascular tumors that often present with non-specific, chronic, and mechanically exacerbated low back pain, matching the patient's initial presentation.

The spinal cord angiography (Fig. 3), performed via right femoral puncture with selective catheterization of the T10-L3 and internal iliac arteries. While it provided critical preoperative vascular information, it also introduced diagnostic complexity due to the absence of tumor blush. or abnormal vascularity within the L2-L3 region corresponding to the mass. This finding contradicts the hypothesis of a highly vascular lesion, such as an aggressive epidural hemangioma or a hypervascular metastasis, despite the intense, homogeneous gadolinium enhancement seen on MRI. The absence of a tumor blush suggests the mass may be hypovascular or that the enhancement on MRI is due to a highly cellular structure with an intact blood-brain barrier (e.g., certain schwannomas or lymphomas) rather than extensive vascular channels. Furthermore, the angiogram confirmed the normal vascular anatomy and the origin of the crucial Artery of Adamkiewicz from the left D10 level, a vital piece of information for surgical planning to minimize the risk of iatrogenic spinal cord ischemia.

Given the lesion's substantial size and the associated severe mass effect leading to significant anterior displacement and compression of the cauda equina roots, the immediate clinical imperative remains rapid surgical decompression and subsequent histopathological analysis to definitively rule out more aggressive, though less common, epidural tumors enhancement.

The preoperative electroneuromyography study yielded normal physiological data throughout, effectively ruling out a concurrent peripheral neuropathy or established chronic radiculopathy. Specifically, motor and sensory conduction velocities and distal latencies were preserved in the bilateral lower limb nerves. Crucially, the needle showed a complete absence of pathological activity at rest (e.g., fibrillation potentials), thereby excluding acute or subacute denervation in the tested L2-S1 musculature. During maximal contraction, motor unit potentials exhibited normal morphology and recruitment.

All laboratory investigations were within normal limits. The complete blood count showed a leukocyte count of 6500 cells/mcL, RBC of 4 million cells/mcL, hemoglobin of 13 g/dL, hematocrit of 39%, and platelets of 240,000 cells/mcL. The electrolyte panel was unremarkable: sodium 139 mEq/L, potassium 4.5 mEq/L, chloride 101 mEq/L, calcium 8.6 mg/dL, and magnesium 2.3 mg/dL. The hemostasis panel revealed a prothrombin time of 13 seconds, an international normalized ratio of 0.94, and an activated partial thromboplastin time of 25 seconds. Her chest X-ray and her electrocardiogram demonstrated no acute cardiopulmonary abnormalities.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient during pre-assessment clinics and surgery was scheduled. Following the induction of general anesthesia and orotracheal intubation, the patient was carefully positioned in the prone position on the operating table, utilizing plain dyed medical cotton rolls beneath the chest and iliac crests; this maneuver was crucial for minimizing intra-abdominal pressure to optimize venous return and consequently reduce peroperative bleeding. The surgical access started with a standard posterior midline incision, precisely delineated from L1 to L4, followed by a meticulous subperiosteal dissection and bilateral detachment of the paraspinal musculature to expose the posterior spinal elements.

Subsequently, a bilateral L2-L3 laminectomy was executed, performed in conjunction with a meticulous flavectomy, achieving comprehensive and safe decompression and exposure of the underlying dural sac. Intraoperatively, the ligamentum flavum exhibited pathological calcification at the L2-L3 segment. Upon dural exposure (Fig. 4), a distinct epidural mass was visualized, manifesting as a compact, intensely dark red to purplish-red mass. Macroscopically, the tissue was characterized by a gelatinous/globular consistency, possessing a smooth, glistening, and irregularly lobulated or scalloped surface, consistent with a highly vascular or potentially mucoid/hemorrhagic pathological entity such as hematoma or angiolipoma. The lesion was noted to occupy the entire posterior aspect of the dural sac. Crucially, a clear surgical cleavage plane existed between the lesion and the underlying dura mater, facilitating its easy mobilization and blunt dissection. No significant compressive mass effect on the dural sac was observed, nor was there evidence of direct invasion into adjacent structures. The entire specimen was ultimately resected en bloc without complication and without the use of the ultrasonic aspiration. The excised specimen (Fig. 5), characterized by its irregular, amorphous shape and lobulated margins, was visually likened to the morphology of a duckbill eagle ray (Aetobatus spp.). The surgical bed was smoothly and meticulously irrigated, and hemostasis was performed prior to closure. The incision was subsequently closed layer-by-layer utilizing standard surgical technique over a Redon suction drain placed within the surgical cavity.

The drain was electively removed on postoperative day 2. The immediate postoperative course was clinically uneventful. Specifically, the woman reported a significant and marked improvement in her lower back pain, a benefit that was sustained even during periods of orthostasis and when ambulating with the aid of an adjustable lumbar support orthosis.

The histopathological analysis and microscopic examination (Fig. 6) revealed a well-circumscribed mass surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule. The tumor is composed of a benign proliferation featuring two distinct intermingled components, often arranged in lobules separated by delicate fibrovascular or collagenous septa. The prominent vascular component consists of numerous thin-walled capillary structures lined by regular endothelial cells, with lumina dilated and engorged with erythrocytes. Interspersed throughout is the adipocytic component, composed of regular, mature adipocytes. Crucially, there is a complete absence of histological features of malignancy, including necrosis, cytological atypia, nuclear pleomorphism, or atypical mitotic activity. These collective and unequivocal features confirm the final diagnosis of a benign angiolipoma.

Histomicrograph (hematoxylin and eosin stain) of the surgical specimen showing a non-malignant proliferation of two key elements: (a) Low magnification (x 25) illustrating the overall architecture. (b) High magnification (x 100) detailing the definitive features of angiolipoma: a prominent vascular component composed of dilated capillary structures filled with red blood cells (white arrow), and a mature adipocytic component consisting of benign fat cells (black arrow).

The patient was deemed medically stable and subsequently discharged on day 4 postoperatively. The prescribed discharge regimen included non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and analgesics for optimized pain management. A structured follow-up appointment at our outpatient neurosurgical clinic was scheduled to monitor long-term recovery, assess wound healing, and evaluate the sustained neurological and symptomatic improvement.

During the scheduled 30-day postoperative outpatient appointment, our patient reported the complete and sustained resolution of her pain. Her symptomatic improvement was sufficiently robust to permit the discontinuation of the lumbar support belt as early as postoperative day 10. Clinical assessment, including a detailed neurological examination, yielded entirely unremarkable findings. Local inspection of the surgical site confirmed the incision was clean, fully consolidated, and in excellent cosmetic condition.

At the three-month outpatient appointment, the woman reported being completely asymptomatic. She confirmed that she had successfully and gradually discontinued the use of the lumbar immobilization and support belt and had fully resumed her professional activities without any restrictions or pain.

Discussion

The historical recognition of spinal angiolipomas (SEALs) dates back to Berembruch's 1890 report and Liebscher's 1901 description, with the first lumbar involvement documented in 1929 (Berenbruch, 1890; Liebscher, 1901; Kasper, 1929; Pagni and Canavero, 1992). The modern pathological definition was established by Howard and Helwig in 1960, who characterized the lesion as a distinct entity of mature adipocytes and proliferating vascular components, a distinction from conventional lipomas that was further solidified by Haddad in 1986 (Howard and Helwig, 1960; Haddad et al., 1986). Lumbar SEALs remain rare; following the early reports by Kasper and Cowan (1929) and Gonzalez-Crussi et al. (1966), the literature has expanded slowly. As of 2024, approximately 54 lumbar cases have been documented, according to recent comprehensive reviews (Gonzalez-Crussi and Enneking, 1966; Benvenutti-Regato and De la Garza-Ramos R COE; Lu et al., 2024).

While the precise etiology of spinal angiolipomas is not fully elucidated, two main theories exist. They are primarily characterized as congenital malformations, as per the classification by Bucy and Ehni (Lin, 1974; Petrella et al., 2005). However, an alternative perspective suggests that although congenital, these lesions may undergo significant expansion in response to specific stimuli like injury or inflammatory processes (Lin, 1974; Petrella et al., 2005; Matsushima et al., 1987). Beyond congenital theories, other predisposing factors for angiolipomas have been proposed. Some authors suggest that SEALs originate from the differentiation of pluripotent mesenchymal cells following specific stimuli. Another prominent theory points to a hormonal influence, given their increased incidence around menopause and the tendency for their size to fluctuate with hormonal changes, such as those occurring during pregnancy (Ehni, 1945).

The seminal pathological description of SEALs, detailing their anatomopathological composition, was provided by Howard et al. (Howard and Helwig, 1960) as mentioned above. Grossly, these lesions are typically grey-reddish, soft, and possess little to no encapsulation, which facilitates dissection. Their growth is characterized by a longitudinal, spindle-shaped expansion within the epidural space, with potential extension through the intervertebral foramen resulting in a dumbbell-shaped lesion. SEALs are traditionally classified into two types: a common, well-encapsulated non-infiltrating type, as seen in our patient, and a rare, ill-defined infiltrating type with a propensity for bony invasion (Lin, 1974). In the present case, the excised specimen deviated from the classic grey-reddish appearance; it was compact, intensely dark red to purplish-red, and exhibited a distinctive irregular, lobulated morphology with a gelatinous consistency and a smooth, glistening surface. This unique appearance was visually likened to a duckbill eagle ray (Aetobatus spp.). We propose the "Eagle Ray Sign" as a memorable macroscopic descriptor to characterize the appearance of such vascular-dominant lesions upon surgical excision. While this observation is anecdotal and specific to our patient, it provides a vivid intraoperative descriptor that may assist in the visual recognition of these rare lesions during total resection.

According to the World Health Organization, angiolipomas are defined as a benign lipoma subgroup composed of mature adipocytes and capillary-sized vascular elements in varying proportions. This definition was confirmed in our patient, where histopathological analysis revealed a well-circumscribed, encapsulated tumor with a dual proliferation of mature adipocytes and numerous thin-walled, dilated vascular channels engorged with erythrocytes, all supported by a fibrovascular stroma. Critically, both cellular components were devoid of any histological signs of malignancy, such as cytological atypia, nuclear pleomorphism, or atypical mitotic activity. Long-standing lesions may also feature interstitial fibrosis, and the characteristic immunohistochemical profile—typically positive for CD31, CD34, and S-100 with a low Ki-67 proliferation rate—is essential for differentiating this benign entity from histological mimics such as spindle cell angiosarcoma and Kaposi's sarcoma (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Si et al., 2014; Carrasco et al., 2017). However, given the classic and unequivocal histological features observed in our patient, the need for supplementary immunohistochemical profiling was deemed unnecessary for a definitive diagnosis. These collective features are therefore unequivocally diagnostic of a benign angiolipoma.

Current understanding indicates that SEALs most commonly present in middle-aged adults, particularly during the fifth decade of life, and show a slight female predilection (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009). A few cases in children have been described (RA, 1948). Their clinical presentation mirrors that of other benign, space-occupying spinal lesions, resulting from compression or infiltration of neural structures. The most frequent initial symptoms are lower limb numbness or paresthesia, leg weakness, and back pain. While the symptomatic course typically evolves gradually over months to years, a sudden onset or acute deterioration can occur. This is often attributable to a rapid increase in tumor size secondary to intratumoral hemorrhage or thrombosis (Tsutsumi et al., 2011). Illustrating this common presentation, our patient was referred for neurosurgical evaluation with a history of progressive, chronic, and debilitating low back pain. The pain was of insidious onset, had increased in severity, and was explicitly described as non-radicular, lacking the dermatomal distribution characteristic of sciatica or classic radiculopathy. A relapsing-remitting clinical course has also been documented in SALs. This fluctuating pattern has been linked to dynamic changes in the tumor's vascular component, which can be influenced by several factors. These include increased epidural venous pressure, blood redistribution during Valsalva maneuvers, blood flow steal phenomena, impaired spinal venous return, thrombosis, hormonal influences such as steroids, and obesity (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Turgut, 1999).

SEALs are typically benign tumors whose imaging peculiarities are a direct reflection of their pathological composition of adipose and vascular tissue. Simple radiography may reveal signs of bony involvement including pedicle erosion or foraminal expansion (Guzey et al., 2007). CT imaging, known for its superior sensitivity in characterizing osseous changes, did not reveal any such changes in our patient. The reported literature shows the tumor's typical density relative to the spinal cord on CT is highly variable: it may be hypodense (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009), isodense and consistent with our case findings (Kuroda et al., 1990; Michilli and Tzonos, 1993), or present with mild hyperdensity (Rabin et al., 2004). A heterogeneous appearance has been documented in a solitary case (Akhaddar et al., 2000). Regarding associated bony changes, calcification has been reported as a rare finding in only two patients (Provenzale, 1996; Akhaddar et al., 2000), and trabeculation of the vertebral body was similarly noted in two distinct cases (Turgut, 1999; Taylor and Harries, 1951).

"MRI remains the gold standard for characterizing SEALs, typically revealing a posterior, spindle-shaped epidural mass. The lesion's signal intensity reflects its dual composition: the fatty component appears hyperintense on T1 and T2 sequences with signal loss on fat-suppressed imaging (STIR/SPIR), while the vascular component is generally T1-hypointense and T2-hyperintense with intense gadolinium enhancement (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Leu et al., 2003; Provenzale, 1996; Fourney et al., 2001). In our patient, the predominantly T1-hypointense and STIR-heterogeneous signal identified the lesion as a vascular-dominant subtype. While radiological classifications have been proposed to categorize these tumors—such as Hu et al.’s differentiation between fatty (Type 1) and centrally vascular (Type 2) clusters (Hu et al., 2013), or Si et al.’s anatomical subtyping (Si et al., 2014)—their clinical utility in predicting outcomes remains uncertain. These systems often oversimplify the pathological spectrum and, as seen in this case, serve primarily as descriptive guides rather than prognostic indicators. Ultimately, the diagnosis depends on recognizing the characteristic mixture of fat and vessels rather than strict adherence to a specific radiological category."

However, a key diagnostic feature of SEALs is the general absence of vascular flow voids, attributed to a lack of well-developed intratumoral arterioles (Leu et al., 2003; Provenzale, 1996). The integrity of the dura is optimally assessed on sagittal T2-weighted imaging, where it appears as a slit-like low signal between the tumor and the spinal cord (Carrasco et al., 2017). While SEALs are generally distinct from other entities—for instance, their intrinsic fat differentiates them from hemangiomas—some variants can be diagnostically challenging.

This diagnostic challenge is occasionally highlighted by a discrepancy between MRI and angiography. Although preoperative angiography can identify feeders from intercostal or lumbar arteries in some cases, it frequently fails to demonstrate a significant tumor blush in SEALs. This phenomenon, which has been previously documented, likely reflects the capillary-sized nature of the anomalous vessels or a relatively slow flow within the lesion's vascular component (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009). Consistent with these reports, the preoperative angiography in our patient did not demonstrate abnormal vascularity or blush at the L2-L3 level despite the intense MRI enhancement. This underscores the established understanding that negative angiographic findings do not rule out a highly vascular-dominant SEAL. Advanced MRI techniques like dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE)-MRI may reveal variable perfusion kinetics, while Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) typically shows no restricted diffusion (Wang et al., 2017). On the other hand, the advanced nuclear medicine technique of Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET/CT) shows that SEALs often exhibit a slightly increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake (Shen and Su, 2019). In our patient, we did not perform a PET/CT scan because we determined that MRI remains the gold standard for characterizing the benign, fatty-vascular composition of a SEAL for surgical planning, thus rendering the additional, non-specific metabolic data from PET/CT unnecessary, cost-prohibitive, and a source of avoidable radiation exposure.

The main radiological differential for a spinal angiolipoma with predominant fat includes lipoma and spinal lipomatosis. Crucial distinguishing features are the presence of contrast enhancement and signal changes on fat-suppressed sequences, which indicate a vascular component (Provenzale, 1996; Carrasco et al., 2017). Conversely, angiolipomas with a prominent or centrally clustered vascular component can mimic angiomas or neurogenic tumors, as the peripheral fat can be mistaken for compressed epidural tissue. Given the variable proportion of tissue components, morphology, potential complications like hemorrhage, and bone involvement, these tumors can present with a wide spectrum of appearances. This variability, especially in an oncological or traumatic context, poses a significant diagnostic challenge (Carrasco et al., 2017).

The treatment of choice for SEALs is complete surgical resection (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Turgut, 1999; Wang et al., 2023). Given their typical location, a laminectomy usually provides adequate access. These tumors are generally easily dissected from adjacent tissues, despite multiple attachments to their supplying epidural vessels. This typically allows for an en bloc resection, though more extensive lesions may require piecemeal removal. The need for spinal stabilization depends on the degree of osteoligamentous resection and is particularly recommended after corpectomy, combined approaches, or procedures involving joints and highly mobile segments (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009). Additionally, some authors oppose adjuvant radiation, even in cases of partial resection (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009; Somrani et al., 2022). However, this therapeutic modality is widely considered ineffective for this condition (Turgut, 1999).

In our patient, the tumor occupied the entire posterior aspect of the dural sac. Crucially, a clear cleavage plane existed between the lesion and the dura, facilitating easy mobilization, blunt dissection, and ultimately, an en bloc resection. We did not perform any adjuvant therapy.

The surgical prognosis for spinal angiolipomas is excellent, with nearly all patients achieving satisfactory functional recovery irrespective of initial lesion size or symptom duration (Gelabert-González M GAA, 2009). Long-term follow-up data confirm this positive outlook. For instance, one study of 21 patients reported no recurrences over a ten-year period (Si et al., 2014), while another series found that even infiltrating tumors managed with subtotal resection remained recurrence-free after a mean of 40 months (Wang et al., 2014). Indeed, recurrence is exceptionally rare, with only two documented cases: one after gross-total removal that manifested after 12 years (Bender and Van Landingham, 1974), and another in an infiltrating tumor following subtotal resection (Kujas et al., 1999). The most critical prognostic factor remains the rapidity of neurological deficit onset. An abrupt, severe deficit from intratumoral hemorrhage warrants urgent surgical intervention (Pinto-Rafael et al., 2002).

Strengths and limitations

This manuscript's principal strengths lie in the detailed documentation of a SEAL in a rare lumbar location and the proposal of the 'Eagle Ray Sign' as a memorable morphological descriptor for the en bloc excised tumor. The case is further strengthened by a comprehensive diagnostic workup, specifically highlighting the illustrative discrepancy between intense MRI enhancement and the absence of an angiographic tumor blush. Recognizing this vascular-dominant subtype is clinically significant, as its radiological appearance can mimic more aggressive or hypervascular lesions, such as schwannomas, paragangliomas, or even solitary metastases, potentially complicating the preoperative differential diagnosis.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As a single-case study, the generalizability of the findings is inherently restricted. Furthermore, while the 3-month postoperative outcome was excellent, the follow-up duration is relatively short. Given that SEALs are benign but possess a rare potential for late recurrence, long-term clinical and radiological surveillance remains necessary to ensure sustained remission. Finally, while the surgical approach was effective in this instance, the optimal timing for intervention and the necessity of preoperative angiography in cases without a clear vascular blush remain areas for further discussion due to the scarcity of comparative studies. These limitations are offset by the work's contribution to understanding the polymorphic nature of this uncommon spinal pathology.

Conclusion

Spinal angiolipomas (SALs) are rare, benign, mesenchymal tumors composed of mature adipose tissue and anomalous vascular channels. Although they are an uncommon cause of spinal cord or cauda equina compression, they should be considered in the differential diagnosis of epidural masses, even in the lumbar spine. MRI remains the gold standard for preoperative evaluation, though histopathological examination is required for definitive diagnosis.

In this report, we described the "Eagle Ray Sign," an anecdotal macroscopic observation of the tumor's morphology noted during en bloc resection. We acknowledge that this descriptor is specific to the current case; its reproducibility and potential utility as a characteristic morphological feature of vascular-dominant SEALs require further validation in larger clinical series.

The primary treatment remains complete surgical excision, which typically leads to an exceptionally favorable prognosis. Even in cases involving significant vascularity or delayed presentation, patients often achieve excellent functional recovery and long-term remission. While preoperative angiography may be considered for highly vascular lesions, its routine necessity remains a subject of clinical judgment, particularly when a discrepancy exists between MRI enhancement and actual arterial supply.
