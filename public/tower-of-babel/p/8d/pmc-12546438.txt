Pseudotumoral pancreatic tuberculosis revealed by obstructive jaundice
Authors: Amine Bentayeb, Rokia Faris, Mehdi Zouaoui, Yassine Chait, Mbarek Azouaoui, Youssef Hnach, Nourdin Aqodad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated pancreatic tuberculosis (TB) is extremely uncommon. The combination of its low incidence and nonspecific clinical presentation presents a real diagnostic challenge and complicates management. The diagnosis of pancreatic TB is based on a combination of clinical, biological, and radiological findings, and most importantly, on histological evidence. We report a case of pseudotumoral pancreatic TB revealed by obstructive jaundice in a 48-year-old immunocompetent woman. The diagnosis was confirmed by the presence of small epithelioid and multinucleated giant cell granulomas with central caseous necrosis on biopsies of peripancreatic adenopathy obtained via endoscopic ultrasound. The patient was successfully treated with quadruple anti-tuberculous therapy.Keywords: Pancreatic tuberculosis, Obstructive Jaundice, Pseudotumoral lesion, Caseous necrosis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health issue, with millions of new cases reported annually [1]. While pulmonary TB is the most common manifestation, extrapulmonary TB accounts for approximately 15%-20% of all cases [2]. Among the various forms of extrapulmonary TB, abdominal TB is relatively uncommon [3,4], and within abdominal TB, pancreatic involvement is considered rare. Diagnosing pancreatic TB is particularly challenging due to its nonspecific symptoms and its ability to mimic other abdominal diseases, including malignancies, potentially leading to unnecessary surgical intervention [3]. Imaging and histopathological analysis are crucial for establishing an accurate diagnosis [5]. Pancreatic TB should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a peripancreatic mass, especially in younger patients, those from TB-endemic regions, or individuals with a prior history of TB. We report a rare case of pancreatic TB. This case highlights the importance of considering TB in atypical abdominal presentations.

Case report

A 48-year-old woman with no significant medical history was admitted for cholestatic jaundice. She reported generalized itching, dark urine, pale stools, intermittent postprandial vomiting, night sweats, unquantified weight loss, evolving over 3 weeks. On examination, she also had a fever of 38°C. There were no signs of gastrointestinal bleeding or changes in bowel habits, and lymph node palpation was normal.

The biochemical profile revealed cholestasis with elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin levels, alongside mild cytolysis and normal coagulation status. Hematology showed mild anemia without leukocytosis or thrombocytopenia (Table 1).

Abdominal ultrasound revealed a hypoechoic, heterogeneous lobulated lesion measuring 42 × 35 mm at the pancreatic head, suggestive of a pancreatic cystic tumor, with mild bile duct dilation and a distended gallbladder with thin walls and anechoic content. The liver was normal in size and structure. The portal vein and hepatic veins were patent.

An abdominal computed tomography (CT) was performed as a complementary investigation, revealing a tissue mass in the head of the pancreas with mixed components, suggestive of a serous cystadenoma, associated with mild dilatation of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts (Fig. 1).

Heterogeneous pancreatic head mass associated with the dilation of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts.

For better characterization of the lesions, an magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography was conducted showing a dilation of the common bile duct to 14.7 mm (Fig. 2) and of the intrahepatic bile ducts was noted, secondary to a poorly defined, tissue-expansive lesion in the upper part of the pancreatic head, suggestive of a pseudotumoral or tuberculous process, measuring 36 × 34 mm (Fig. 3). This lesion is associated with three confluent lymph nodes at the hepatic pedicle (retro- and preportal) and in the celiac region, encasing the hepatic artery. The largest of these nodes, measuring 54 mm in its longest axis, is extensively necrotic and encases the proximal segment of the hepatic artery (Fig. 4A and B). Two additional small lymph nodes were identified: one located postero-inferior to the pancreatic head and the other along the gastric coronary chain. No suspicious hepatic lesion was observed.

Given the suspicion of pancreatic TB, a tuberculin skin test was performed and returned positive with an induration of 13 mm. To evaluate for pulmonary involvement, a chest radiograph (Fig. 5), sputum smear microscopy, and Xpert MTB/RIF assay were conducted, all of which were negative. In light of a possible differential diagnosis of malignancy, serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen and carbohydrate antigen 19-9, were assessed and found to be within normal limits. HIV serology was also negative.

Normal chest x-ray.

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with biopsy was performed for histological assessment of deep peripancreatic adenopathy (Fig. 6). Endoscopic findings were normal for the stomach, duodenum, and papilla. Echo-endoscopic examination revealed a normal pancreas except for suspected infiltration of the upper pancreatic head, nondilated main pancreatic duct, normal retro-pancreatic common bile duct, and dilated intrahepatic bile ducts. Necrotic lymph nodes encasing the bile duct and hepatic artery were noted. A transbulbar biopsy of a lymph node was performed.

Endoscopic ultrasound image showing biopsy of a deep peripancreatic lymph nodes.

Histopathological analysis of the lymph node infiltrating the bile duct revealed extensive caseous necrosis and multiple epithelioid and giant cell granulomas (Figs. 7 and 8). The final diagnosis was pseudotumoral pancreatic and peripancreatic lymph node TB.

Granulomatous inflammation composed of a few epithelioid granulomas with the presence of a giant cell (HE ×200).

Caseous necrosis punctuated by some lymphocytic inflammatory elements (HE ×200).

The patient underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with biliary stenting, with an uncomplicated postoperative course.

Antitubercular treatment with streptomycin at 15 mg/kg/d, rifampin at 10 mg/kg/d, isoniazid at 5 mg/kg/d, and pyrazinamide at 25 mg/kg/d was administered for 2 months, followed by rifampin at 10 mg/kg/d and isoniazid at 5 mg/kg/d for 4 months. The patient experienced weight gain, progressive resolution of clinical and biochemical cholestasis, and a disappearance of peripancreatic adenopathies with atrophic appearance in relation to the site of the initial process and absence of dilatation of the inter and extra hepatic bile ducts on abdominal CT control after anti TB treatment for 06 months (Fig. 9). She has been followed up for 10 months without recurrence.

Discussion

TB remains a major public health challenge in developing countries, exerting a significant social and economic burden. Over the past decades, its clinical presentation has evolved. Approximately 12% of TB patients present with abdominal involvement, most commonly affecting the ileocecal region, peritoneum, mesenteric lymph nodes, spleen, gallbladder, or liver [6,7]. The incidence of pancreatic TB is estimated to be 2.1%-4.7% in autopsy series of miliary TB cases [8,9]. Isolated pancreatic TB is even rarer [10].

The pathogenesis of isolated pancreatic TB remains unclear. It is believed to result from hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination from an occult or latent pulmonary focus, often reactivated due to immunosuppression [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

Pancreatic TB tends to occur more frequently in younger individuals. More than half of the cases reported in the global literature involve patients under 30 years of age. While several authors have described an equal distribution between sexes [12], a systematic review by Panic et al. [13], including 166 patients, found a mean age of approximately 41 years and a male predominance of 62.1%.

Diagnosing pancreatic TB is particularly challenging, not only due to its rarity but also because of its nonspecific clinical presentation. Clinically, it is considered a great mimicker, often presenting with symptoms such as abdominal pain, weight loss, fever, and jaundice [13]. Other manifestations may include fatigue, night sweats, nausea, and vomiting [12]. In our patient, the presentation with cholestatic jaundice and weight loss initially suggested a pancreatic malignancy.

TB should be considered when evaluating a pancreatic mass, particularly in young patients or immunocompromised individuals from endemic areas [11]. Most lesions are located in the pancreatic head (59%), and peripancreatic lymph nodes are involved in nearly half of the cases (47.3%) [13].

A wide spectrum of imaging findings has been reported. On ultrasonography, the pancreas typically appears enlarged and heterogeneous. Hypoechoic intrapancreatic collections are commonly observed, sometimes containing internal fine echoes [12]. CT and magnetic resonance imaging often reveal a pseudotumoral mass, frequently hypodense on CT with central necrosis, and associated necrotic peripancreatic and portal lymphadenopathies. Less frequently, nonspecific diffuse enlargement of the pancreas, resembling acute pancreatitis, is seen [14]. Biliary dilatation resulting from obstruction by a pancreatic head mass or periportal lymphadenopathy has also been reported [15]. In the series by Ray et al. [10], the typical CT finding was extensive necrosis of peripancreatic lymph nodes with rim enhancement, a useful clue suggesting TB rather than malignancy.

Definitive diagnosis requires histological confirmation, typically revealing caseating granulomas with epithelioid and multinucleated giant cells. Histology remains the most frequent method of diagnosis (59.6%) in reported cases, followed by culture, staining, and, in a smaller number, polymerase chain reaction or cytology [10]. Fine-needle aspiration under radiologic or EUS guidance can provide a definitive diagnosis [16,17]. In our case, the diagnosis was confirmed via EUS-guided biopsy of peripancreatic lymph nodes. This approach has become the method of choice, offering a high diagnostic yield while avoiding unnecessary surgery.

Standard antituberculous therapy, combining isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and streptomycin or ethambutol, leads to clinical and radiologic resolution in the vast majority of cases. In the review by Panic et al. [13], over 91% of patients achieved complete remission, with surgery required only in selected cases. Similarly, all patients in the study by Ray et al. [10] responded favorably to medical therapy alone, except those initially misdiagnosed and surgically treated. In our patient, standard antituberculous therapy led to rapid clinical improvement, resolution of cholestasis, and complete radiological disappearance of the peripancreatic lymphadenopathies. Follow-up at 10 months showed no recurrence.

Conclusion

Pancreatic and peripancreatic lymph node TB is a major differential diagnosis for pancreatic cancer, cystic tumors, and pseudocysts. It should be considered in patients with pancreatic masses, particularly in young or immunocompromised individuals in endemic regions. Although fine-needle aspiration biopsy is the preferred diagnostic method, surgery may be necessary to manage complications such as compressions, fistulas, or hemorrhages.
