Refractory verrucous psoriasis achieving clearance with guselkumab and subsequent response to excimer therapy
Authors: Luke C. Moore, Clay Cockerell, Angela Yen Moore
Published: 2026

Introduction

Verrucous psoriasis (VP) is a rare type of psoriasis characterized clinically by hyperkeratotic, warty lesions and histopathologically by acanthosis, papillomatosis, and hyperkeratosis.1 Fewer than 40 cases of VP have been reported in the literature.2

Here, we present a case of a woman with VP and her response to treatments, including topical therapy, excimer laser, systemic treatments, and biologic therapy.

Case report

A 69-year-old woman presented with hyperkeratotic, verrucous plaques on her hand (Figs 1 and 2), elbows, buttocks, and lower back for 1 year. She had a medical history of hypertension, arthritis, hidradenitis suppurativa, stroke without sequelae, congestive heart failure, and an implanted defibrillator. Her clinical differential diagnosis included atypical mycobacterial infection, deep fungal infection, verrucous vulgaris, hypertrophic lichen planus, and squamous cell carcinoma. Biopsy of a plaque on her third finger revealed histologic findings of VP, with irregular psoriasiform hyperplasia, with marked hyperkeratosis and abundant neutrophils (Figs 3 and 4). A periodic acid–Schiff stain was negative for hyphae. Initial treatments with topical triamcinolone 0.1% cream, clobetasol propionate 0.05% cream, roflumilast 0.3% cream, and cryotherapy were unsuccessful. Oral isotretinoin, halobetasol propionate cream, and tazarotene 0.1% cream were then initiated, with minimal improvement. Isotretinoin, topical therapy, and excimer treatments were discontinued. Monotherapy with guselkumab injections for 1 year was initiated, which consisted of a loading dose of 100 mg every 4 weeks, followed by maintenance doses every 8 weeks for 52 weeks. Flattening of the marked hyperkeratosis was observed after 8 weeks, with clearance after 12 weeks that persisted for another 5 months after guselkumab was discontinued.

Because guselkumab was no longer available for the patient, topical calcipotriene, betamethasone 0.05% cream, and tacrolimus 0.1% ointment were restarted without response when VP lesions recurred 5 months after guselkumab was discontinued. Nine weekly 308-nm excimer laser sessions were added with minimal response, despite increasing 30% per session for a cumulative dose of 1.75 J/cm2.

Topical clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment and weekly methotrexate injections of 15 mg, after a 7.5 mg initiation dose, were added with only minimal additional response. Methotrexate was discontinued, and oral deucravacitinib was initiated but discontinued after 4 months due to nausea and gastrointestinal upset, leading to vomiting but only a minimal clinical improvement.

Three weekly 308-nm excimer laser treatments were restarted, with an increase of 30% per session, for a cumulative dose of 1.56 J/cm2, in combination with topical clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment and triamcinolone 0.1% cream, which resulted in marked flattening of the lesions and subsequent maintenance only with topical steroids.

Discussion

Given the rarity of VP, no consensus treatment has been reported. Although a recent systematic review in 2025 on treatments for VP showed a frequent need for combination therapy, no reports of treatments with guselkumab, deucravacitinib, or excimer laser were included.3 Treatments included mechanical removal, topical therapies, cryotherapy, methotrexate, acitretin, cyclosporine, apremilast, narrowband UV-B therapy, and a few biologic agents.3 These biologic agents included tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α inhibitor adalimumab, with response in 1 of 3 patients; TNF-α inhibitor etanercept in 2 patients, with partial improvement in 1 of these 2 patients; TNF-α inhibitor infliximab in 1 patient, with no improvement; interleukin (IL) 17A inhibitor ixekizumab with adjuvant topical steroids in 1 patient, with response; and IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab in 1 patient, with response.3 Because our literature search on treatments for VP had identified isotretinoin and methotrexate as possibly effective treatments, they were attempted in our patient, but without notable improvement.

Because many patients diagnosed with VP have previously or concurrently had plaque-type–psoriasis vulgaris,1 and IL-23 inhibitors have shown a 57% Psoriasis Area and Severity Index reduction of 90% within 16 weeks for plaque-type psoriasis,4 guselkumab was initiated in our patient, with remarkable improvement by 8 weeks and clearance of VP in 12 weeks. IL-23 and TNF-α are produced by dermal dendritic cells and activate T helper 17 (Th17), which then release IL-17 in the pathogenesis of psoriasis.5 Subsequent to the treatment of our patient with VP with guselkumab, another case report was published in 2025 with a similar clinical response.6

Excimer laser treatment was attempted in our patient because the procedure was covered by her insurance and the VP lesions were localized. Although the excimer laser initially failed, her VP responded to later attempts with the excimer laser after 1 year of successful treatment with guselkumab, an IL-23 inhibitor, and 4 months of unsuccessful treatment with deucravacitinib, a TYK2 inhibitor. It is unclear if the later response to excimer laser treatment resulted from a decrease in the thickness of recurrent VP lesions or from local modulation in inflammatory profiles after treatment with the IL-23 inhibitor and TYK2 inhibitor. It is also unclear if the nausea and gastrointestinal upset of deucravacitinib in this patient may have impaired absorption due to vomiting, which would decrease clinical efficacy with deucravacitinib but still have some local modulation in inflammatory profiles.

Despite the clinical differential initially being concerned for an infectious agent, the histopathologic findings ruled out infectious etiologies and confirmed a diagnosis of VP. Instead, the histologic findings in this case consisted of a psoriasiform dermatitis with no granulomatous or lichenoid inflammation, excluding the diagnosis of lichen planus or any granulomatous disorder. There were no koliocytes, and no atypia was noted in the lymphoid cells or keratinocytes. A negative periodic acid–Schiff stain, as well as the clinical course, virtually excludes the diagnosis of a fungal infection. Based on the clinical and histologic features, the diagnosis of psoriasis with verrucous features remains the best diagnosis.

Notably, this patient with a rare case of VP had a marked, complete clearance of VP during 1 year of treatment with the IL-23 inhibitor guselkumab, but recurrence was noted 5 months after discontinuation of guselkumab, as well as responsiveness to combination treatment with topical steroids and weekly excimer laser treatment subsequent to IL-23 inhibitor response but TYK2 inhibitor failure.
