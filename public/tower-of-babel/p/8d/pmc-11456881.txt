Introduction

Hepatoblastoma (HB) is the most common liver tumor in childhood, with 90 % of cases occurring within the first five years of life [1]. Congenital hepatoblastoma is extremely rare, most congenital hepatoblastomas develop in the neonatal period, with some cases identified in utero. There is currently no standardized protocol for managing congenital hepatoblastoma. Managing large tumors located in challenging anatomical sites poses significant difficulties.

Due to the unique physiological characteristics of neonates, diagnosing congenital hepatoblastoma is extremely challenging. Identification based on conventional abdominal ultrasound is often challenging; therefore, enhanced computed tomography (CT) or biopsy is necessary for accurate diagnosis. Performing surgical resection on complex tumors presents significant challenge.

We report a case of hepatoblastoma diagnosed following a puncture biopsy. The tumor was large, occupying the first and second hepatic hilum, and was deemed inoperable after the initial assessment. Following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the tumor significantly reduced in size, and AFP levels decreased. A staged surgical resection was subsequently performed, and postoperative recovery was uneventful. No recurrence was observed during follow-up. This case provides insights and lessons learned.

Our work has been reported in line with the SCARE Guidelines 2023 criteria [2].

Case presentation

A 20-day-old male infant was admitted to our hospital with an abdominal mass detected during his mother's ultrasound examination at 39 weeks of gestation. Postnatally, an abdominal ultrasound revealed a large liver mass, initially considered a hepatic hemangioma, and regular follow-up was recommended. Twenty days later, a follow-up ultrasound revealed significant growth of the liver mass, with the AFP level exceeding 1000 ng/ml. Consequently, the patient was admitted to our hospital. The mother's pregnancy history, as well as the child's birth and family history, were unremarkable.

Upon transfer, an abdominal ultrasound indicated a liver mass measuring 8.7 × 6.4 × 8.0 cm, involving the first hepatic hilum, with the portal vein and hepatic artery penetrating the tumor. Enhanced abdominal CT confirmed a 7.8 × 7.0 × 8.1 cm mass occupying the first and second hepatic portals (Fig. 1a). Chest CT showed no pulmonary metastases. The child's AFP level was 205,902 ng/ml, highly suspicious for hepatoblastoma. A puncture biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of hepatoblastoma (fetal type), classified as PRETEXT stage III, intermediate risk.

Given the tumor's size and location, a one-stage resection was not feasible. The child underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy with the C5VD (vincristine, fluorouracil, cisplatin, and doxorubicin) regimen for 4 cycles, reducing AFP to 1205 ng/ml. The initial chemotherapy dose is one-third of the conventional dosage. Subsequent imaging showed a tumor size of 6.2 × 4.1 × 5.9 cm (Fig. 1b). Enhanced CT and 3D visual analysis aided in surgical planning. At four months old, the child underwent surgery, where the tumor was found in the middle liver and caudate lobe, involving the first and second hepatic portals. Intraoperative navigation using the 3D model enabled successful block resection without hepatic hilum obstruction.

Postoperative pathology confirmed hepatoblastoma (70 % fetal epithelium + 30 % mesenchymal and bone-like stroma). Six cycles of postoperative intensified chemotherapy were administered, comprising 4 cycles of C5VD and 2 cycles of VI (vincristine, irinotecan). Levels of AFP, AFP-L3, and the percentage of AFP-L3 (AFP-L3%) were monitored before each chemotherapy session. Although AFP levels did not return to normal by the end of the treatment, AFP-L3 was reduced to less than 10 ng/ml for two consecutive sessions. Additionally, AFP-L3% was below 10 % for four consecutive sessions. Children were regularly monitored for two years following the completion of treatment, with monthly assessments of AFP levels, abdominal ultrasounds, and chest CT every three months. Additionally, abdominal ultrasounds, chest CT, and abdominal enhanced CT or nuclear magnetic resonance imaging were performed every six months. During the two-year follow-up, no recurrence or metastasis was detected, and AFP levels remained normal.

Discussion

Tumors detected in the fetal period and within the first three months of life are generally considered congenital and account for 1.5–2 % of all childhood tumors. Liver tumors represent 5 % of congenital tumors, with hepatoblastoma being the most common malignancy [3]. Due to its rarity, experience in managing congenital hepatoblastoma is limited. Our case highlights the importance of differentiating between hepatoblastoma and hepatic hemangioma, as treatment strategies differ significantly.

Congenital liver lesions are rare, with hepatic hemangiomas being the most common, accounting for 60 % of all liver tumors. Mesenchymal malignancies and hepatoblastomas follow in prevalence [4]. Hepatoblastoma and hepatic hemangioma can sometimes present similarly, yet their treatment strategies differ significantly [5]. In our case, hepatic hemangioma was initially considered based on ultrasound findings. Since AFP levels are elevated in all newborns [6], regular ultrasound monitoring during pregnancy, combined with postnatal monitoring of AFP levels in liver tumors detected at birth, can aid in the differential diagnosis, enabling earlier detection of malignant tumors and timely treatment. Pathology remains the gold standard for confirming malignancy. Promptly following a biopsy with chemotherapy is considered safe. In this case, to ascertain the tumor composition, we obtained pathological results through puncture biopsy and combined them with imaging findings, leading to a definitive diagnosis of hepatoblastoma, PRETEXT stage III, intermediate-risk group. The child experienced no complications following the puncture procedure.

For tumors that are initially difficult to surgically resect, neoadjuvant chemotherapy can induce tumor shrinkage and reduce AFP levels. It plays an important role in improving the complete resection rate and reducing recurrence [7]. The use of chemotherapeutic drugs in neonates is significantly limited [8]. There is limited experience with chemotherapy for congenital hepatoblastoma. According to COG guidelines, the C5VD regimen is recommended for children in the intermediate-risk group [9]. However, due to the patient's young age and low body weight, the dosage was reduced to ensure tolerability. In our case, we initiated regular chemotherapy at 42 days old and observed a significant therapeutic effect with good tolerance. This outcome demonstrates that chemotherapy is safe and effective for treating congenital hepatoblastoma in the perinatal period.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for hepatoblastoma. Segmental resection or lobectomy is recommended for stage I, II, and stage III hepatoblastoma without vascular involvement. For stage IV and stage III hepatoblastoma with vascular involvement, liver transplantation or extended hepatectomy is advised [10]. In this case, after four preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy sessions, the child's tumor significantly shrank, with AFP levels decreasing by more than 95 % and a marked reduction in tumor activity. However, the tumor remained large, occupying the first and second hepatic portals, making traditional segmental hepatectomy challenging and necessitating either liver transplantation or segmental resection of the liver mass. To preserve the child's quality of life, liver transplantation was avoided. Based on enhanced CT data, 3D visual analysis and modeling of the tumor were performed to fully understand the tumor's anatomical structure and surrounding vascular routes before surgery (Fig. 2c). Following multidisciplinary consultation, it was decided to perform a block resection of the tumor, considering its location in the caudate lobe and its close relationship with surrounding blood vessels. Intraoperatively, the tumor was successfully resected in segments using the 3D model for navigation without obstructing the hepatic hilum (Fig. 2a/b/d). Minimal intraoperative bleeding was observed. This case demonstrates that with the aid of 3D visual analysis technology and a comprehensive understanding of the tumor and surrounding vasculature, it is feasible to achieve complete segmental resection of stage III tumors occupying the first and second hepatic hilum. However, the inadequate visualization of the biliary system through 3D analysis modeling based on CT data resulted in ineffective intraoperative protection of the biliary tract and a transient postoperative bile-derived encapsulated effusion. This condition was resolved with symptomatic treatment and did not impair liver function.

As a specific tumor marker for hepatoblastoma, the normalization of AFP levels is a crucial criterion for determining the completion of treatment. Despite undergoing ten chemotherapy sessions, the patient's AFP levels remained elevated. The role of AFP-L3 in assessing the efficacy of treatment for adult primary hepatocellular carcinoma is well-established [11]. Therefore, we considered the use of AFP-L3 as an end-of-treatment indicator. Following two consecutive normal readings of both the percentage of AFP-L3 and the AFP-L3%, the child was successfully concluded the treatment successfully. The child was then monitored for two years post-treatment, during which no recurrence or metastasis was observed, indicating a favorable therapeutic outcome. AFP-L3 and AFP-L3% may serve as indicators for evaluating the efficacy of hepatoblastoma treatment.

Conclusion

Puncture biopsy is a definitive and safe diagnostic method for congenital hepatoblastoma, while neoadjuvant chemotherapy is effective and facilitates subsequent complete tumor resection. Additionally, 3D visual analysis shows significant potential in the surgical treatment of pediatric liver masses. This case report demonstrates good therapeutic outcomes following segmental resection surgery; however, further investigation in a larger cohort is necessary to validate these findings.
