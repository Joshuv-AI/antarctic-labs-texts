TRIM28 drives immune evasion via PARP1 SUMOylation and NAD+ depletion in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Xiangpeng Zhan, Hongji Hu, Yang Liu, Hao Wan, Fuchun Zheng, Luyao Chen, Xiaoqiang Liu, Jing Xiong, Xingang Cui, Songhui Xu, Bin Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has demonstrated significant clinical potential in a variety of cancers; however, its efficacy in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) remains suboptimal. In ccRCC, an increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells does not necessarily correlate with improved prognosis, indicating the presence of unique immune evasion mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME).MethodsTripartite motif-containing 28 (TRIM28) was identified as a potential therapeutic target through single-cell transcriptomics (GSE159115) and Geneformer-based perturbation screening. Functional validation was performed by constructing shTRIM28 and overexpression cell models to assess tumor proliferation, CD8+ T cell co-cultures, flow cytometry, and patient-derived xenograft models. Co-immunoprecipitation and GST pull-down assays were used to analyze the TRIM28-poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) interaction. SUMOylation/ubiquitination studies elucidated the mechanism regulating PARP1 stability, and chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR identified the transcriptional regulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). High-throughput screening was conducted with RNA-seq, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, and metabolomics. Virtual screening identified the TRIM28 inhibitor Eltrombopag, which was tested in combination with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) therapy for in vivo efficacy and metabolic reprogramming.ResultsWe identified TRIM28 as a central regulator of immune evasion in ccRCC. Using high-throughput gene knockout screening, we demonstrated that TRIM28 depletion reprograms malignant epithelial cells toward a less aggressive phenotype and significantly enhances tumor cell susceptibility to cytotoxic T lymphocyte killing. Mechanistically, TRIM28 promotes immune resistance through dual immunometabolic mechanisms: first, by stabilizing PARP1 and promoting its SUMOylation, which in turn amplifies PD-L1 expression via NAD+-SIRT1-p65 signaling; second, by depleting NAD+ in the TME, limiting NAD+ availability for CD8+ T cells and impairing their respiration and effector function. These findings provide a novel mechanistic framework for TRIM28-driven immune suppression, integrating tumor-intrinsic metabolic reprogramming with CD8+ T cell dysfunction. Notably, we identified Eltrombopag as a candidate TRIM28 inhibitor, which synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy to enhance antitumor immunity and overcome ICB resistance in murine models.ConclusionsThis study reveals that TRIM28 is a key regulator of PD-L1 expression and T cell dysfunction in ccRCC through PARP1 stabilization and NAD+ metabolic reprogramming. Targeting TRIM28/PARP1/PDL1 with Eltrombopag reshapes the immunosuppressive TME and enhances checkpoint blockade efficacy, providing a novel combinatorial strategy for ccRCC immunotherapy.

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) represents the predominant histological subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for over 70% of all RCC cases and contributing significantly to kidney cancer-related morbidity and mortality on a global scale.1 The asymptomatic nature of early-stage disease, combined with a complex and heterogeneous molecular landscape, often results in diagnosis at advanced stages, necessitating systemic therapeutic interventions.2 In recent years, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, particularly those targeting the programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis, have revolutionized the first-line treatment paradigm for advanced ccRCC. These therapies are frequently administered in combination with tyrosine kinase inhibitors, yielding improved outcomes in subsets of patients.35 However, despite these advances, substantial interpatient heterogeneity in treatment response persists, and both intrinsic and acquired resistance to ICB remains formidable challenges limiting durable clinical efficacy.

To address these limitations, a deeper understanding of the molecular determinants of immune evasion in ccRCC is urgently needed. Among the diverse mechanisms involved, post-translational modifications (PTMs) of key regulatory proteins are emerging as pivotal modulators of tumor–immune interactions.6 SUMOylation, a highly dynamic and reversible PTM process, orchestrates numerous cellular processes, including protein stability, subcellular localization, and transcriptional regulation.79 This cascade is initiated by the SUMO-activating enzyme, followed by conjugation via Ubc9 and substrate-specific SUMOylation mediated by E3 ligases.10 One such E3 ligase, tripartite motif-containing 28 (TRIM28), is a multifunctional protein that serves as both a transcriptional co-repressor and a SUMO E3 ligase. TRIM28 has been implicated in chromatin remodeling, epigenetic silencing, and oncogenic transformation across several tumor entities,11 yet its role in ccRCC immune modulation remains poorly characterized.

In this study, we identified TRIM28 as a potential therapeutic candidate in ccRCC using a combination of single-cell RNA sequencing and deep learning–based gene prioritization via Geneformer. TRIM28 expression was enriched in malignant epithelial cells and correlated strongly with T cell exhaustion signatures and poor patient prognosis. Mechanistic investigations revealed that TRIM28 interacts with and SUMOylates poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), a nuclear NAD+-consuming enzyme, thereby stabilizing PARP1 and promoting its nuclear retention. Notably, TRIM28-mediated SUMOylation selectively depletes nuclear NAD+ without affecting cytoplasmic NAD+ pools, establishing a compartment-specific metabolic reprogramming. The ensuing nuclear NAD+ depletion facilitates acetylation and activation of NF-κB p65, driving PD-L1 transcriptional upregulation and enabling tumor immune evasion. In addition to its tumor-intrinsic effects, TRIM28 also impairs the antitumor immune response by reducing NAD+ availability in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, thereby suppressing their cytotoxic function. We validated these findings through functional assays and metabolic profiling of T cells in the ccRCC microenvironment. Finally, using a structure-based virtual screening strategy, we identified and validated small-molecule inhibitors targeting TRIM28, which synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical models.

Together, these findings delineate a novel nuclear metabolism-linked immunoregulatory axis in ccRCC, positioning TRIM28 as a key modulator of PD-L1 expression and immune evasion through spatially confined NAD+ depletion. Targeting TRIM28 may thus offer a promising strategy to potentiate immunotherapy responses and overcome resistance in renal cancer.

Results

TRIM28 is a potential therapeutic candidate for ccRCC

To explore novel therapeutic vulnerabilities in ccRCC, we leveraged single-cell transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE15911512 and employed Geneformer,13 a recently developed foundation model capable of simulating gene perturbations in a biologically informed manner. We conducted in silico gene knockout experiments specifically on malignant epithelial cells and assessed the resulting transcriptional states to determine whether gene inactivation shifted the cells toward a normal epithelial phenotype or drove them further away from it. We first focused on tumor tissues (GSM4819725, GSM4819726, GSM4819728, GSM4819733, GSM4819735, GSM4819737, and GSM4819738), where individual cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes (online supplemental figure S1A), allowing for the precise identification of malignant epithelial cells. As a reference for the target state, we identified normal epithelial cells from adjacent non-tumor tissues (GSM4819727, GSM4819729, GSM4819730, GSM4819731, GSM4819734, and GSM4819736). Subsequently, we performed in silico gene knockout simulations on 500 randomly selected malignant cells using Geneformer. A total of 11,403 genes were individually perturbed, identifying 105 genes whose knockout shifted the transcriptional state of malignant cells either toward or away from that of normal epithelial cells (online supplemental figure S1B). Notably, several well-established ccRCC-associated genes, including VHL, MET, and PBRM1, were among the top hits, underscoring the biological relevance and predictive power of the model1416 (figure 1B). Of particular interest, a systematic evaluation of all gene knockout effects unexpectedly revealed that loss of TRIM28 markedly shifted malignant cells toward a normal epithelial-like transcriptional state (figure 1B,C), suggesting that TRIM28 may play a critical role in the development of ccRCC and represent a potential therapeutic target.

To further investigate the clinical relevance of TRIM28 in ccRCC, we first analyzed its transcriptional levels in tumor versus adjacent normal tissues using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and GSE36895.17 In both cohorts, TRIM28 was significantly upregulated in tumor samples (figure 1D,E). Consistently, Western blot analysis of 12 paired ccRCC and adjacent normal tissues confirmed higher TRIM28 protein expression in tumors (figure 1F). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of an independent cohort comprising 80 paired tumors and adjacent normal tissues further validated this finding, revealing markedly elevated TRIM28 expression in tumor tissues compared with their normal counterparts (figure 1G). Moreover, TRIM28 expression levels were positively correlated with tumor stage, with higher expression observed in high-stage tumors relative to low-stage ones (figure 1H). Quantitative analysis of TRIM28 expression via IHC H-score demonstrated that elevated TRIM28 levels were significantly associated with more advanced clinical features, including higher T stage, N1 status, and presence of distant metastasis (M1) (figure 1I). Finally, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses in both the ICGC (the International Cancer Genome Consortium; KIRC) and TCGA cohorts revealed that high TRIM28 expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) outcomes (figure 1J,K). In summary, these findings highlight the critical role of TRIM28 in ccRCC, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for improving clinical outcomes in this challenging cancer.

TRIM28 modulates CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity in ccRCC

To investigate the functional role of TRIM28 in ccRCC, we generated stable cell lines with either short-hairpin RNAs (shRNA) TRIM28 or overexpression (OE) TRIM28 in two commonly used ccRCC cell lines, 786-O and CAKI-1. We first evaluated cell proliferation using colony formation assays. In both cell lines, no significant difference in colony-forming ability was observed between the shControl and shTRIM28 groups (online supplemental figure S2A,B), nor between the Plenti-vector and OE-TRIM28 groups (online supplemental figure S2C,D). To further validate these findings, we performed 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine incorporation assays to assess DNA synthesis as an indicator of proliferative activity. Consistently, neither knockdown nor OE of TRIM28 significantly affected the proliferation of 786-O or CAKI-1 cells (online supplemental figure S2E–H). These findings indicate that the functional role of TRIM28 in ccRCC may depend on interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME), which are not fully recapitulated in standard in vitro settings. It demonstrates the successful knockdown and OE of TRIM28, confirming the experimental modifications (online supplemental figure S2E–H).

To investigate the potential role of TRIM28 in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment in ccRCC, we first analyzed its expression and immune infiltration status using the TCGA-KIRC (Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma) cohort. We observed that high TRIM28 expression was significantly associated with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and lower overall T cell abundance scores (figure 2A,B). Furthermore, high TRIM28 expression correlated with a higher stromal score and a lower immune score, indicating a shift toward a more immune-excluded tumor phenotype (figure 2C,D). To functionally validate these findings, we performed co-culture assays by incubating 786-O and CAKI-1 RCC cells with CD8+ T cells activated via CD3/CD28 stimulation. Cytotoxicity was assessed by Calcein/PI (Propidium Iodide) staining. In the absence of T cells, there was no significant difference in tumor cell death between the sh-Control and sh-TRIM28 groups across both cell lines. However, on co-culture with CD8+ T cells, the sh-TRIM28 group exhibited a markedly higher proportion of dead tumor cells compared with sh-Control, suggesting enhanced sensitivity to T cell-mediated killing (figure 2E,F, online supplemental figure S2I,J). To further evaluate the functional activation of CD8+ T cells, we measured the concentrations of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), GAMB, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and Perforin in the culture supernatants by ELISA. Consistently, sh-TRIM28 tumor cells induced significantly higher levels of both cytokines compared with sh-Control cells (figure 2G and online supplemental figure S2K). We next assessed whether the immune context was essential for TRIM28-mediated tumor growth regulation in vivo. Sh-Control and sh-TRIM28 the Renca murine RCC cell line were subcutaneously implanted into both immunodeficient nude mice and immunocompetent BALB/c mice. In nude mice, no significant difference in tumor volume or weight was observed between the two groups (figure 2H,I), suggesting that TRIM28 does not intrinsically affect tumor cell proliferation in the absence of adaptive immunity. In contrast, in immunocompetent mice, sh-TRIM28 tumors displayed significantly reduced growth compared with controls, both in terms of volume and final tumor weight. To determine whether TRIM28 knockdown augments the cytotoxicity of T cells in vivo, we isolated CD8+ T cells from the aforementioned renca tumors in BALB/c mice, and the results indicated that the effector function of CD8+ T cells was enhanced in the shTRIM28 group (figure 2J,K). Then, we observed a significant increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration in the sh-TRIM28 group, while no significant difference in CD4+ T cell infiltration was found between the two groups (figure 2L). Meanwhile, we also assessed the proportions of other immune cells in the TME, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and macrophages. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups (online supplemental figure S3A–C). Overall, these findings suggest that TRIM28 may regulate the immune microenvironment in ccRCC by directly affecting tumor cells, thereby influencing CD8+ T cell infiltration and effector function, rather than by modulating other immune cells. To test this hypothesis, we subcutaneously co-injected Renca cells and murine CD8+ T cells into NRG mice (Rag2KO) to investigate the direct interactions between tumor cells and CD8+ T cells. We found that Renca cells with high TRIM28 expression exhibited greater resistance to CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S3D).

TRIM28 interacts with PARP1

To elucidate the mechanistic role of TRIM28 in ccRCC, we sought to identify its potential protein interaction partners. We ectopically expressed Flag-tagged TRIM28 in 786-O cells and performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) using anti-Flag magnetic beads, followed by silver staining. Distinct protein bands were observed in the immunoprecipitated complex, indicative of specific interacting proteins (figure 3A). Mass spectrometric analysis via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) identified PARP1 as the most significantly enriched interactor (figure 3B, online supplemental figure 4A,B), suggesting a potential functional association between TRIM28 and PARP1.

To validate this interaction, we established HEK293T cell lines stably expressing Flag-tagged TRIM28 and Myc-tagged PARP1. Reciprocal co-IP experiments using anti-Flag and anti-Myc antibodies confirmed a robust physical interaction between TRIM28 and PARP1 (figure 3C,D). Importantly, endogenous co-IP assays using anti-TRIM28 and anti-PARP1 antibodies in HEK293T, 786-O, and CAKI-1 cells corroborated the interaction under physiological expression conditions (figure 3E–H, online supplemental figure S4C,D). To determine whether TRIM28 directly binds PARP1, we conducted in vitro GST pull-down assays using recombinant GST-tagged TRIM28 protein. These assays demonstrated direct physical binding between TRIM28 and PARP1 in a cell-free system (figure 3I). Consistently, molecular docking simulations predicted a stable binding interface between TRIM28 and PARP1, providing structural support for a direct interaction (online supplemental figure S3E). Immunofluorescence microscopy further revealed colocalization of TRIM28 and PARP1 within the nucleus of 786-O and CAKI-1 cells, implying potential cooperation in nuclear processes (figure 3J). To map the interaction domains, we generated a series of truncation mutants for both TRIM28 and PARP1 based on their known domain structures (figure 3K–N). Co-IP assays performed in HEK293T cells expressing the respective mutants demonstrated that the RING domain of TRIM28 is responsible for interacting with the catalytic domain of PARP1 (figure 3L,M). This domain-specific interaction was independently confirmed using GST pull-down assays with truncated PARP1 proteins and GST-tagged TRIM28 (figure 3O), firmly establishing a direct and domain-dependent interaction between TRIM28 and the PARP1 catalytic core.

TRIM28 acts as an E3 SUMO ligase for PARP1 and regulates its SUMOylation in ccRCC

Post-translational SUMOylation plays a critical role in regulating protein function in cancer.1820 To investigate whether TRIM28 facilitates SUMOylation of PARP1 in ccRCC, we initially performed Ni²+-NTA pull-down assays in HEK293T cells cotransfected with His-tagged SUMO isoforms (SUMO1–4), Flag-tagged TRIM28, Myc-tagged PARP1, and HA-tagged UBC9. Our results revealed that TRIM28 specifically enhanced SUMO2 modification of PARP1 (figure 4A,B).

To identify the potential SUMOylation sites on PARP1, we used the GPS-SUMO prediction tool (online supplemental figure S5A) and selected four candidate lysine residues to be mutated to arginine (K→R). Mutation at K203 or K486 individually reduced PARP1 SUMOylation, while simultaneous mutation of both sites completely abrogated SUMO2 modification (figure 4C,D), indicating that K203 and K486 are the major SUMOylation sites on PARP1. Interestingly, co-IP experiments showed that mutation at these residues markedly diminished the interaction between PARP1 and TRIM28 (figure 4E), suggesting a SUMO-dependent mechanism for complex formation. Furthermore, OE of TRIM28 carrying a C651A mutation21 failed to promote PARP1 SUMOylation to the same extent as wild-type (WT) TRIM28 (figure 4F), confirming that TRIM28 functions as an E3 SUMO ligase for PARP1.

To determine whether this SUMOylation is specific to TRIM28, we compared the effects of overexpressing several well-characterized SUMO E3 ligases, including PIAS1–4 and TRIM28, in 786-O cells.22 IP of endogenous PARP1 revealed that only TRIM28 robustly enhanced PARP1 SUMOylation (figure 4G). Consistent with this, TRIM28 knockdown in both 786-O and CAKI-1 ccRCC cell lines significantly reduced SUMOylation of PARP1, whereas OE of TRIM28, but not its E3-deficient mutant, led to increased SUMO2 modification of PARP1 in a dose-dependent manner (figure 4H,I; online supplemental figure S5B,C). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated substantial nuclear colocalization of PARP1 and SUMO2/3 in 786-O and CAKI-1 cells. This colocalization was markedly reduced on stable knockdown of TRIM28 (figure 4J,K), supporting a role for TRIM28 in mediating nuclear SUMOylation of PARP1. Given the dynamic nature of SUMOylation, we next sought to identify the deSUMOylating enzyme responsible for PARP1 regulation. Cotransfection of Myc-PARP1 with various HA-tagged SENP family members revealed a specific interaction between PARP1 and SENP3 (online supplemental figure S5D). This interaction was further validated by both exogenous and endogenous co-IP assays in HEK293T and 786-O cells (online supplemental figure S5E–H). Notably, OE of SENP3 partially reversed TRIM28-mediated SUMOylation of PARP1 (online supplemental figure S5I), implicating SENP3 as a functional PARP1 deSUMOylase in this context.

TRIM28-mediated SUMOylation stabilizes PARP1

Since numerous studies have demonstrated that SUMOylation often contributes to protein stabilization,23 24 we speculated that TRIM28-mediated SUMOylation could play a similar role in regulating PARP1. Treatment with the SUMOylation inhibitor 2-D08 led to a substantial decrease in PARP1 protein levels in both 786-O and CAKI-1 cells (figure 5A, online supplemental figure S6C), supporting this hypothesis. Interestingly, quantitative real-time PCR revealed that PARP1 messenger RNA (mRNA) levels were also significantly decreased on TRIM28 silencing (online supplemental figure S6A). Analysis of TCGA datasets further supported a positive correlation between TRIM28 and PARP1 mRNA expression (online supplemental figure S6B), suggesting that TRIM28 may regulate PARP1 at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. To further explore the effect of TRIM28 on PARP1, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays. Knockdown of TRIM28 accelerated the degradation of PARP1, while OE of WT TRIM28 significantly prolonged its half-life. In contrast, the SUMOylation-deficient mutant TRIM28C651A exhibited a diminished capacity to stabilize PARP1 (figure 5B,C). We next examined whether SUMOylation of PARP1 itself affects its stability. OE of a non-SUMOylatable PARP1 mutant (PARP12KR) in 293 T cells led to an accelerated degradation rate compared with wild-type PARP1 in CHX assays (figure 5D), indicating that SUMOylation contributes to PARP1 stabilization. To determine whether TRIM28 affects PARP1 ubiquitination, we assessed the ubiquitination levels of endogenous PARP1 following TRIM28 knockdown. Notably, loss of TRIM28 led to a pronounced increase in PARP1 ubiquitination (figure 5E, online supplemental figure S6D). Conversely, OE of TRIM28WT significantly reduced PARP1 ubiquitination, while TRIM28C651A had no such effect (figure 5F, online supplemental figure S6E). Similarly, ubiquitination assays in 293 T cells cotransfected with HA-Ub and either PARP1WT or PARP12KR revealed higher ubiquitination levels in the PARP12KR mutant (figure 5G). To dissect the type of ubiquitin linkage affected by TRIM28, we overexpressed various lysine-specific ubiquitin mutants (HA-Ub-K6, HA-Ub-K11, HA-Ub-K27, HA-Ub-K33, HA-Ub-K48, and HA-Ub-K63) along with TRIM28 in 293 T cells. TRIM28 specifically suppressed K63-linked ubiquitination of PARP1 (figure 5H). Furthermore, OE of a K63R mutant abolished the ability of TRIM28 to reduce PARP1 ubiquitination, confirming the specificity for K63-linked chains (figure 5I). To probe the underlying mechanism, we screened for E3 ubiquitin ligases previously implicated in PARP1 regulation (online supplemental figure S6F–H).2528 Among them, RNF114 was identified as a potential mediator of PARP1 ubiquitination. TRIM28 OE abrogated RNF114-induced PARP1 degradation (figure 5J), and co-IP assays demonstrated that TRIM28 reduced the interaction between RNF114 and PARP1 (figure 5K), suggesting that TRIM28-mediated SUMOylation stabilizes PARP1 by interfering with RNF114-dependent K63-linked ubiquitination. Finally, we performed exogenous validation in 293 T cells and found that RNF114 can increase K63-linked ubiquitination of Myc-PARP1, and OE of TRIM28 can reverse RNF114-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination of PARP1 (figure 5L).

TRIM28–PARP1 axis drives NF-κB-dependent PD-L1 transcription in ccRCC

To elucidate the functional impact of TRIM28 and PARP1 in ccRCC, we performed transcriptomic profiling in 786-O cells following knockdown of either TRIM28 or PARP1 (online supplemental figure S8A,B). Given our previous observations that TRIM28 promotes immune evasion in ccRCC, we specifically interrogated transcriptomic changes in pathways associated with immune regulation. Differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC|>1) were subjected to Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses, which revealed a significant enrichment of the “PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer” (figure 6A,B). To explore whether TRIM28 reduces CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity through PARP1, we co-cultured stable TRIM28-overexpressing 786-O cells with CD8+ T cells. We found that OE of TRIM28 significantly impaired the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells. However, silencing PARP1 restored the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S7A). This finding aligns with our initial hypothesis and suggests that TRIM28 and PARP1 may contribute to immune suppression through modulation of PD-L1 expression.

Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, RNA-seq peak profiles revealed that knockdown of TRIM28 or PARP1 led to a notable reduction in PD-L1 transcription (figure 6C). Immunoblotting further confirmed that PD-L1 protein expression was significantly reduced on knockdown of either gene (figure 6D). Notably, ectopic expression of PARP1 in TRIM28-depleted cells partially restored PD-L1 protein levels, indicating a functional interplay between TRIM28 and PARP1 in regulating PD-L1. Similarly, OE of TRIM28 alone resulted in increased PD-L1 expression (figure 6E). To further explore the regulatory effect under inflammatory conditions, we treated 786-O cells with IFN-γ. In both IFN-γ–stimulated and unstimulated settings, TRIM28 knockdown significantly diminished PD-L1 protein levels, as validated by both western blotting and flow cytometry (figure 6F,G). Immunofluorescence staining of 786-O and CAKI-1 cells showed decreased PD-L1 expression in TRIM28-deficient cells, which was rescued by PARP1 OE (online supplemental figure S8C,D). In clinical samples, immunohistochemistry revealed a positive correlation between TRIM28 expression and PD-L1 positivity in ccRCC tissues (figure 6H,I), suggesting clinical relevance of this regulatory axis. To investigate the underlying mechanism, GSEA enrichment of differentially expressed genes identified multiple signaling pathways previously implicated in PD-L1 regulation (figure 6J). Using the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-Atlas enrichment platform, we found significant occupancy of PD-L1 promoter regions by key transcription factors, including NF-κB (p65), HIF1A, STAT1, and STAT3 (figure 6K, online supplemental figure S8E). ChIP-quantitative PCR (qPCR) using primers targeting enriched promoter regions (Primer 1 and Primer 2) demonstrated that TRIM28 knockdown significantly reduced p65 occupancy at the PD-L1 promoter. Strikingly, this reduction was reversed by wild-type PARP1 OE (figure 6L). For the other transcription factors—HIF1A, STAT1, and STAT3—TRIM28 did not alter the level of their binding to the PD-L1 promoter (online supplemental figure S8F–H). Conversely, TRIM28 OE enhanced p65 recruitment to the PD-L1 promoter, while a SUMOylation-deficient TRIM28 mutant failed to do so (figure 6M), indicating that TRIM28-mediated PTM of PARP1 is essential for p65 activity at this locus.

In a subcutaneous xenograft model using the Renca murine RCC cell line implanted in BALB/c mice, pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB signaling with bortezomib significantly impaired tumor growth (figure 6N). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating cells revealed that bortezomib treatment reduced both PD-L1 expression and the percentage of PD-1 in CD8+ T cells (figure 6O), supporting a role for NF-κB-driven PD-L1 expression in mediating immune evasion in vivo.

TRIM28-mediated NAD+ metabolic reprogramming promotes immune escape

To investigate the mechanism by which TRIM28 influences PDL1 transcription, we first examined potential interactions between TRIM28, P65, and PARP1 in 786-O cells using co-IP. No direct interaction was detected between TRIM28 and P65 or between PARP1 and P65 (online supplemental figure S9A–D). These findings suggest that TRIM28 may facilitate P65 recruitment to the PDL1 promoter via indirect or multicomponent regulatory mechanisms.

To further explore the functional role of TRIM28, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling on 786-O cells stably overexpressing TRIM28. The analysis revealed a significant increase in the abundance of nicotinic acid and butyrylcarnitine (figure 7A), both of which are key intermediates in NAD+ biosynthesis. Considering PARP1’s role as a major NAD+-consuming ADP-ribosyltransferase, we hypothesized that TRIM28 may modulate NAD+ metabolism in renal carcinoma cells. Consistent with this hypothesis, OE of TRIM28 in both 786-O and CAKI-1 cell lines resulted in a marked reduction in intracellular NAD+ and NADH levels. Notably, this decrease could be partially rescued by silencing PARP1 expression (figure 7B,C). In contrast, OE of a TRIM28 mutant failed to reproduce this effect (figure 7D,E). To determine whether TRIM28’s influence on NAD+ metabolism is linked to global cellular energy metabolism, we assessed glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in TRIM28-overexpressing cells. Neither extracellular acidification rate nor oxygen consumption rate was significantly altered by TRIM28 OE in either 786-O or CAKI-1 cells (online supplemental figure SE–H), suggesting that TRIM28 preferentially affects nuclear rather than cytoplasmic NAD+ pools. Given that SIRT1 is a well-characterized NAD+-dependent deacetylase predominantly located in the nucleus,29 and is known to regulate P65 activity via deacetylation at lysine 310 (K310),30 31 we next examined whether TRIM28 modulates PDL1 expression through SIRT1-mediated P65 acetylation. Knockdown of TRIM28 in 786-O cells led to a significant decrease in P65(K310) acetylation, whereas co-depletion of SIRT1 restored acetylation levels and elevated PDL1 expression (figure 7F). Conversely, TRIM28 OE increased P65(K310) acetylation, which could be reversed by supplementation with the NAD+ precursor nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). NMN supplementation also attenuated the TRIM28-induced upregulation of PDL1 (figure 7G). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed that OE of TRIM28 increased the enrichment of acetylated P65(K310) at the PDL1 promoter, and this effect was abolished on NMN treatment (online supplemental figure S10A,B). These results collectively suggest that TRIM28 promotes PDL1 expression by limiting NAD+ availability, thereby attenuating SIRT1 activity and enhancing P65 acetylation. To investigate whether this metabolic regulation affects immune surveillance, we co-cultured TRIM28-overexpressing tumor cells with primary human CD8+ T cells. Notably, NAD+ and NADH levels in CD8+ T cells were significantly reduced when co-cultured with TRIM28-overexpressing tumor cells (figure 7H,I). Furthermore, the glycolytic activity and mitochondrial respiration of CD8+ T cells were markedly suppressed under these conditions (figure 7J,K). These findings suggest that TRIM28 OE may impair CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity through metabolic competition for NAD+. Functionally, TRIM28 OE significantly reduced tumor cell death in co-culture with CD8+ T cells, an effect that was reversed by NMN supplementation (online supplemental figure S10C,E). Cytokine analysis of the co-culture supernatant revealed that levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, GZMB, and perforin were all decreased in the TRIM28-overexpressing group but could be restored by NMN treatment (online supplemental figure S10D,F). Flow cytometry confirmed that TRIM28 OE had no effect on spontaneous tumor cell apoptosis in the absence of T cells (online supplemental figure S10G,H); however, in the presence of CD8+ T cells, TRIM28 significantly reduced apoptosis, an effect once again reversed by NMN (figure 7L). To validate these observations in vivo, we conducted subcutaneous xenograft experiments in BALB/c mice. Tumors derived from TRIM28-overexpressing cells exhibited accelerated growth compared with control tumors, whereas NMN supplementation mitigated this effect (figure 7M). Consistent with the in vitro findings, NAD+ and NADH levels were significantly reduced in TRIM28-overexpressing tumor tissues (figure 7N). IHC staining further revealed that TRIM28 OE increased PDL1 expression while reducing CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration into the TME, both of which were partially reversed by NMN treatment (figure 7O).

Collectively, these findings suggest that TRIM28 regulates PDL1 expression and tumor immune evasion by modulating NAD+ metabolism and SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of P65 and ultimately reshapes the NAD+ metabolism within the TME through metabolic competition with CD8+ T cells, thereby shaping the antitumor immune response.

Structure-based discovery and functional validation of TRIM28 inhibitors

We performed a structure-based virtual screening to identify potential small-molecule binders of TRIM28. Using Food and Drug Administration-approved and de novo compound libraries, high-affinity candidates were selected via AutoDock Vina docking. To identify functional inhibitors of TRIM28, we selected the top three small-molecule candidates based on docking affinity scores—ZINC000011679756, ZINC000052955754 (Ergotamine), and ZINC000064033452 (VX-809)—for further evaluation (figure 8A–C). on treating 786-O cells with these compounds for 24 hours, we observed that Eltrombopag markedly reduced PARP1 SUMOylation levels, as assessed by immunoblotting (figure 8D–F). Molecular dynamics simulations revealed a relatively stable interaction between eltrombopag and TRIM28 over a 100 ns trajectory, supporting the potential binding compatibility of the compound (figure 8G). In the co-culture system of CD8+ T cells and 786-O cells, Eltrombopag can reverse the CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity resistance caused by TRIM28 OE (online supplemental figure S11A). Meanwhile, we tested the IC50 of Eltrombopag in both 786-O and CAKI-1 cells (online supplemental figure S11B,C). To evaluate its in vivo efficacy, we established subcutaneous tumor xenografts in BALB/c mice and administered Eltrombopag therapeutically. Eltrombopag significantly inhibited tumor growth compared with the control group (figure 8H). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) revealed elevated levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, GZMB, and perforin in the eltrombopag-treated group (figure 8I,J), accompanied by a notable increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration (figure 8K). Meanwhile, no antitumor effect of Eltrombopag was observed in immunodeficient nude mice, suggesting that its tumor-suppressive activity depends on intact immune function (online supplemental figure S11D). To investigate whether Eltrombopag influences tumor growth through the modulation of TRIM28, we treated BALB/c mice implanted with TRIM28 knockdown Renca cells with Eltrombopag. We found no significant difference in tumor growth rates between the Eltrombopag-treated group and the group with low TRIM28 expression but without Eltrombopag treatment, suggesting that Eltrombopag and low TRIM28 expression have similar effects on tumor growth. Meanwhile, Eltrombopag showed no significant difference in tumor growth inhibition between tumors with normal TRIM28 expression and those with reduced TRIM28 expression. These results indicate that Eltrombopag likely affects tumor growth by modulating TRIM28 (online supplemental figure S11E). Building on the aforementioned findings, we explored the potential of Eltrombopag as a therapeutic agent to enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy in human tumors. To more accurately replicate the TME of RCC, we used a preclinical model in which immunocompromised NCG mice were engrafted with RCC patient-derived xenografts (PDXs)32 33 and subsequently received tumor-specific CD8+ T cell transfer (figure 8L). The impact of Eltrombopag on the therapeutic response to anti-PD-1 therapy was assessed in a humanized immune system model. Our findings revealed that both Eltrombopag and anti-PD-1 monotherapies effectively suppressed tumor growth and extended OS. Notably, the combination of Eltrombopag and PD-1 blockade resulted in a significantly greater reduction in tumor burden and a more substantial improvement in OS compared with either Eltrombopag or anti-PD-1 therapy alone (figure 8M–P). Finally, H&E staining was performed to assess the potential toxicity of combined eltrombopag and α-PD1 antibody treatment in mouse heart and liver tissues. Histological examination revealed no significant structural abnormalities in these major organs, suggesting that the combination therapy does not induce apparent organ toxicity (online supplemental figure S11F).

Discussion

TILs have been associated with favorable clinical outcomes in a range of solid tumors, including non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer.3436 CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, in particular, play a pivotal role in cancer immunosurveillance, and their presence within the TME is commonly regarded as a positive prognostic indicator, as well as a predictor of response to (ICB) therapy.37 38 However, this paradigm does not universally apply. In RCC, especially the ccRCC, a paradoxical relationship has been observed whereby elevated CD8+ T cell infiltration does not correlate with improved survival.39 Multiomics analyses have further identified an RCC subgroup (GP1) characterized by a hyperinflamed phenotype yet poor clinical prognosis.26 These findings suggest a non-canonical mechanism of immune escape in ccRCC. Rather than excluding T cells, ccRCC appears to exploit T cell dysfunction to evade immune destruction.40 It is now understood that only early dysfunctional T cells can be reinvigorated by PD-1 blockade, while terminally dysfunctional T cells exhibit resistance to ICB.41 42 Nevertheless, the molecular drivers underlying immune suppression and escape in the RCC microenvironment remain incompletely defined.

In this study, using a high-throughput gene knockout simulation approach based on Geneformer, we identified TRIM28 as a critical regulator of immune evasion in ccRCC. TRIM28 depletion in malignant epithelial cells resulted in a marked shift toward a less malignant transcriptional phenotype. Clinically, high TRIM28 expression was consistently correlated with advanced tumor stage, increased metastatic potential, and inferior patient survival across multiple independent datasets, underscoring its potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target.

Prior research has shown that NAD+ metabolism exerts epigenetic regulation over immune cell functions. For instance, NAMPT, a rate-limiting enzyme in NAD+ biosynthesis, promotes PD-L1 expression in various tumors via the IFN-γ signaling pathway, driving immune evasion mechanisms.43 In this process, NAD+ maintains the activity of Tet1, which promotes the demethylation of Irf1 and upregulates PD-L1 expression. Our findings mirror this mechanism in ccRCC, where TRIM28 stabilizes PARP1, alters NAD+ metabolism, and suppresses T cell function. Furthermore, NAMPT depletion results in reduced NAD+ levels, which causes T cell dysfunction and impaired immune responses within the TME, as supported by multiple studies.4446 Specifically, the loss of NAD+ homeostasis correlates with dysfunction in glycolysis and mitochondrial activity in TILs. The direct consequence of NAD+ metabolic disruption is a reduction in ATP generation within T cells, which limits their cytotoxic function and immune surveillance capacity. These findings reinforce our observation that TRIM28 depletion enhances T cell-mediated tumor cell toxicity and improves T cell effector function through NAD+ metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC.

In ccRCC, we further elucidate how TRIM28, through the stabilization of PARP1, creates a metabolic competition between tumor cells and immune cells, limiting T cell oxidative phosphorylation and effector function. This metabolic competition significantly impairs T cell immune function, ultimately facilitating immune evasion. The accumulation of lactate in the TME exacerbates this competition, as lactate inhibits the expression of key metabolic enzymes such as NAMPT, further diminishing NAD+ levels in immune cells and intensifying immune suppression.47 48 Our study demonstrates that ccRCC tumors actively deplete essential metabolic resources from immune cells, effectively evading immune detection and destruction. Moreover, we reveal a previously unrecognized aspect of TRIM28-driven immune suppression: metabolic competition. By promoting tumor-intrinsic NAD+ consumption via PARP1, TRIM28 restricts NAD+ availability for neighboring CD8+ T cells. This metabolic hijacking impairs T cell oxidative metabolism and effector function, further contributing to immunosuppression. These findings provide a comprehensive explanation of TRIM28’s role in orchestrating immune escape in ccRCC.

Previous efforts in ccRCC therapy have primarily targeted angiogenesis and immune checkpoints.5 49 Yet, a substantial proportion of patients fail to benefit from ICB monotherapy, highlighting an urgent need to decipher alternative resistance mechanisms. While prior studies have investigated either tumor-intrinsic metabolic rewiring or T cell dysfunction in isolation,5052 our findings integrate both perspectives. We uniquely demonstrate that TRIM28 simultaneously facilitates PD-L1 transcription via NAD+-SIRT1-p65 signaling and suppresses CD8+ T cell function through intercellular NAD+ competition. This dual role situates TRIM28 at the nexus of immunometabolic regulation, offering a mechanistic explanation for the immunologically “hot” yet unresponsive phenotype in ccRCC.

In this study, we found that eltrombopag binds to TRIM28 and inhibits the SUMOylation of PARP1, thereby altering the metabolic state within the TME to enhance immune cell function and ultimately increase the sensitivity of RCC to immunotherapy. When combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, eltrombopag demonstrated the potential to prolong survival in mouse models. These results suggest that eltrombopag may serve as an adjuvant to immunotherapy, synergizing with ICB to overcome primary or acquired resistance to immunotherapy. Originally developed as a thrombopoietin receptor agonist for the treatment of thrombocytopenia,53 previous studies have indicated that eltrombopag can also play a role in cancer therapy. For instance, one study demonstrated that eltrombopag interferes with the binding of TFEB to DNA, thereby inhibiting autophagy, which could reduce the ability of tumor cells to survive through autophagy during immune evasion.54 Given that autophagy plays a critical role in tumor cell growth, resistance, and immune evasion, eltrombopag may enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to immunotherapy by overcoming autophagy-related therapeutic resistance. Additionally, studies have shown that eltrombopag effectively manages post-treatment cytopenias, aiding high-risk patients in recovering blood cell counts, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of CAR T-cell therapy.55 The above results suggest that eltrombopag has significant potential in cancer immunotherapy.

Despite these advances, our study has limitations. The majority of our data is derived from in vitro models and murine systems. Validation in larger, multicenter human cohorts is necessary to confirm the clinical relevance of TRIM28 in immune resistance. Additionally, while our work focused on PARP1 SUMOylation and NAD+ metabolism, TRIM28 is a multifaceted protein with broader epigenetic roles, including potential effects on DNA methylation and histone modifications that remain unexplored. The long-term safety and pharmacokinetic properties of Erlotinib also warrant rigorous evaluation in future preclinical and clinical studies. Moreover, our current model of NAD+ competition between tumor and T cells represents a conceptual advance but requires deeper investigation across diverse immune cell subsets. Whether TRIM28 exerts similar metabolic control in other TME components, such as Tregs or MDSCs, remains an open question with potential clinical implications.

In summary, our study identifies TRIM28 as a central regulator of immune escape in ccRCC, functioning through dual mechanisms: stabilization of PARP1 to drive PD-L1 expression, and metabolic competition to suppress CD8+ T cell fitness. These findings reveal a critical immunometabolic vulnerability and establish TRIM28 as a compelling therapeutic target. Combined inhibition of TRIM28 and PD-1 signaling offers a rational and potentially effective strategy for overcoming immune resistance in ccRCC.

Experimental section

Patients and specimens

All human RCC tissue specimens were obtained from the Department of Urology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Nanchang, China). RCC tissues were stored at −80 °C and used exclusively for western blotting analyses. Patient information is presented in online supplemental table S1.

Cell lines

786-O cells (#ATCC; CRL-1932) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). CAKI-1 cells (#CL-0052) and Renca cell (#CL-0568) line were purchased from Pricella Biotechnology (Wuhan, China) and HEK293T cells (#SCSP-502) were acquired from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Beijing, China). All cell lines were confirmed to be free from contamination. All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin. Routine testing for mycoplasma contamination was performed using PCR, and cell line authentication was conducted by short tandem repeat profiling. Authentication and quality control were carried out by the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resources of China.

Antibodies and reagents

A complete list of antibodies and assay kits used in this study is provided in online supplemental tables S5 and S6.

RNA interference and lentivirus construction

Small interfering RNAs targeting SIRT1 were synthesized by RiboBio (China) for in vitro experiments. shRNA lentiviral particles and OE lentiviral vectors were constructed, validated, and provided by Bioyard Biotechnology (China). The target sequences are listed in online supplemental table S2.

Western blotting

To extract proteins, triplicate samples were lysed in prechilled RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat. no. 20115ES60, Yeason, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat. no. 20201ES76, Yeason, China). Protein samples were resuspended in Laemmli sample buffer, subjected to ultrasonic disruption (30 s×15 cycles with intermittent cooling), and denatured by boiling with β-mercaptoethanol and bromophenol blue prior to SDS-PAGE (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis). Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF (Polyvinylidene Fluoride) membranes and blocked with EpiZyme fast blocking buffer. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer containing 0.2% Tween-20. Prior to secondary antibody incubation, membranes were either reblocked at room temperature for 10–15 min using fast blocking buffer or directly incubated in a diluent buffer containing 0.2% Tween-20 and 0.01% SDS for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence and exposed to X-ray film for detection. Films were developed using a darkroom processor and scanned for analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR and Chip-qPCR

Details of the primers and ChIP-qPCR kits employed in this study are provided in online supplemental tables S3, S4 and S6.

Primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cell and CD8+ T cell isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood obtained from healthy donors using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare, Cat# 17-5442-02), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated PBMCs were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual platelets and contaminants. CD8+ T cells were subsequently purified from PBMCs using the CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-096-495) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of isolated CD8+ T cells was confirmed to be greater than 95% by flow cytometric analysis.

Co-IP and mass spectrometry analysis

Cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were lysed in a cell lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (CoWin Biosciences). 10% of the lysate was retained as input control and stored at –20 °C for subsequent analyses. The remaining lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific antibodies and protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific) under gentle rotation. After five washes with cold wash buffer (5 min each), the beads were resuspended in 1×SDS sample buffer and heated to denature bound proteins. Immunoprecipitated proteins were either stored at –20 °C or directly subjected to SDS-PAGE, silver staining, or LC–MS/MS. Mass spectrometry was performed by Shenzhen Wininnovate Bio-Technology (Shenzhen, China). Protein identification and quantification were conducted using Thermo Scientific Proteome Discoverer software (V.1.4).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded onto confocal dishes and cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for another 15 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour at room temperature, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. The following day, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies—Alexa Fluor 488 or Cy3-conjugated antibodies—for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) if needed. Images were acquired using a Zeiss confocal laser scanning microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Kidney tissue microarrays were obtained from Spector Biotech (Shandong, China). For IHC staining, paraffin-embedded kidney tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (ZLI-9079, ZSGB-BIO, China). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with PV-6001 (ZSGB-BIO), followed by non-specific binding blockade using ZLI-9056 (ZSGB-BIO). Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies and processed using the HRP detection kit (ZLI-9017, ZSGB-BIO), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Staining was independently assessed by two experienced pathologists. The staining index was calculated based on both staining intensity and the proportion of positively stained cells. Intensity was scored as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong).

Isolation of primary renal carcinoma cells

Tumor specimens pathologically confirmed as ccRCC were collected and rinsed thoroughly with cold PBS supplemented with 2% penicillin–streptomycin. The tissues were finely minced and digested in DMEM containing 0.002% DNase I, 0.01% hyaluronidase, 0.2% collagenase IV, and 3×10⁻³ M CaCl₂ at 200 rpm for 60 min at 37 °C. The digested mixture was centrifuged at 300 × g, and the supernatant was filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer. The resulting cell suspension was seeded into 6-well plates and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Flow cytometry

Tumor cell digestion was performed as previously described to isolate primary RCC cells. After filtration, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 5×10⁻³ M EDTA and 1% FBS. To assess PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, immune cells were removed by density gradient centrifugation, and the remaining cells were stained with anti-PD-L1 flow cytometry antibodies to evaluate PD-L1 expression within the tumor tissue.

To assess cytokine production in the TME, tumor-infiltrating cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate, 1×10⁻⁶ M ionomycin, and protein transport inhibitor (BD Biosciences) for 6 hour at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the Fixation and Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD, 554714) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by intracellular staining with antibodies against IFN-γ, TNF-α, GZMB, and perforin. Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (40302ES60, Yeasen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. TILs were stained with anti-mouse CD8 and PD-1 antibodies, and the proportion of CD8+ T cells as well as their PD-1 expression levels was analyzed. Isotype-matched control antibodies were used to validate staining specificity. All samples were analyzed on a NovoCyte D3000 flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (V.10).

ELISA

The supernatant from kidney tumor cells co-cultured with tumor-specific CD8+ T cells was collected and stored at –80 °C. The levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, GZMB, and perforin in the supernatant were measured using human ELISA kits. Detailed information about the ELISA kits is provided in online supplemental table 6.

Animals

Male BALB/c, NCG and Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (approval number: CDYFY-IACUC-202306QR020). To establish subcutaneous tumor models, 1.0×10⁶ Renca cells were injected into the right flank of BALB/c or Balb/c male mice. Tumor volumes were measured every 7 days using calipers and calculated using the formula: length×width² × 0.5. Tumor immune phenotypes were analyzed by flow cytometry. Tumor tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and subjected to H&E staining. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests.

PDX model

For the PDX models, fragments of fresh human RCC tumors were subcutaneously implanted into NCG mice. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm³, the NCG mice were sacrificed. The tumors were then separated and cut into 1 mm³ pieces, which were transplanted into the next-generation NCG mice. After three passages, a stable RCC PDX model was successfully established. Once the tumor volume reached 100 mm³, NCG mice were randomly divided into the following five groups: control group, immune reconstitution group, immune reconstitution+eltrombopag treatment group, immune reconstitution+anti-PD-1 treatment group, and immune reconstitution+eltrombopag + anti-PD-1 treatment group. For adoptive T-cell transfer, tumor-specific CD8+T cells, Tregs, and dendritic cells (DCs) were injected via the tail vein to rebuild the human immune system. Mice were then treated with eltrombopag (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally every 4 days for a total of six doses). For anti-PD-1 treatment, mice were intraperitoneally injected with anti-PD-1 antibody (BioXcell; 100 mg per mouse every 4 days for a total of seven doses). The palpable tumor weight was measured weekly. Tumor volume (mm³) was calculated using the following formula: tumor volume = (length×width²)/2. Mice were sacrificed when the tumor size reached 1,500 mm³ or when ulceration occurred.

To establish the PDX model, mononuclear cells were first isolated from the peripheral blood of HLA-A2+ healthy donors. These cells were cultured in VIVO medium (04-418Q, Lonza), supplemented with 100 ng/mL GM-CSF and 30 ng/mL IL-4 (PeproTech) to induce differentiation into DCs. The medium and cytokines were replenished every 3 days. On day 6, the DCs reached maturation and were stimulated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech) for 24 hours. Following this, the DCs were pulsed with tumor lysates from HLA-A2+ patients using a freeze-thaw method in liquid nitrogen for 24 hours, enhancing their ability to present tumor antigens and thereby providing activated DCs for subsequent immune therapy. To generate tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, CD8+ T cells were isolated from the peripheral blood of the same healthy donors as used for the DCs. These CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with mature DCs at a 5:1 ratio in VIVO medium (Lonza), supplemented with 25 IU/mL IL-2 (PeproTech) for 6 days to induce tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. In this manner, the CD8+ T cells were activated and primed to recognize tumor antigens, making them suitable for reconstitution of the human immune system or for adoptive immunotherapy.

Statistics and reproducibility

All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times with consistent results; representative data are shown unless stated otherwise. In vivo experiments were performed with a minimum of three biological replicates per group, and similar trends were observed across independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups with a single experimental factor, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Two-way ANOVA was used for analyses involving two independent variables. Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.8.0.1). Data are presented as mean±SEM unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during the experiments to minimize bias.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using the Seurat R package (V.4.2). Low-quality cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 20% mitochondrial gene content, or extreme total UMI (Unique Molecular Identifier) counts were filtered out. Gene expression counts were normalized to 10,000 transcripts per cell and log-transformed. Highly variable genes were selected, and principal component analysis was performed for dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was conducted using the Leiden algorithm, and two-dimensional visualization was achieved via UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). Cell type annotation was based on canonical marker genes and supported by reference-based classification. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony prior to clustering. Differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses were performed on selected clusters for downstream interpretation.

Bioinformatics analysis

SUMOylation sites were predicted using the GPS-SUMO tool available at https://sumo.biocuckoo.cn/. ChIP enrichment analysis was performed using the ChIP-Atlas platform at https://chip-atlas.org/enrichment_analysis. Publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE159115) and bulk RNA-seq data (GSE36895) were retrieved from the GEO database. Geneformer-based analyses were conducted using a combination of R (V.4.2) and Python (V.3.9).
