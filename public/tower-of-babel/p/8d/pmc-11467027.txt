The benefits of transformational leadership for addressing workplace emotions after COVID-19 at a large multi-campus university
Authors: Nicholas O Rule, Cheryl Regehr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic presented a series of challenges to organizations. Among those that successfully continued operating, the subsequent recovery period catalyzed pressure to redefine work structure after social distancing restrictions lifted. Here, we observed the benefits of transformational leadership in this historically unique context of organizational distress by applying an adjusted version of Ashkanasy and Dorris’s (Ashkanasy and Dorris in Annu Rev Organ Psych Organ Behav 4:67–90, 2017) framework for workplace emotions to a large, multi-campus university. We quantitatively content-analyzed semi-structured interviews of more than 300 divisional leaders and their staff from across the organization. Interviews occurred in the months following the first semester of continuous in-person service delivery, when most employees returned to working in employer-operated space. Despite disproportionate emphasis on negative and self-focused emotions, negative emotions clustered in individuals’ empathic recognition of others’ emotions; though efforts to regulate those emotions proved scant. Positive emotions primarily emerged in response to local leadership efforts to mitigate the negative emotions of students, staff, and faculty. This data pattern suggests that individuals experienced negative emotions, recognized others’ negative emotions, and appreciated leaders’ interventions to ameliorate those negative emotions. Strategies reminiscent of transformational leadership therefore productively addressed the negative impact of workplace stress imposed by the pandemic, helping to facilitate compliance and enthusiasm with return-to-work efforts. The findings thus illustrate how a transformational style of leadership can address individuals’ negative experiences during a period of pronounced existential stress.Keywords: Transformational leadership, Emotion, COVID-19, Resilience, Higher education

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic arrested operations across all industrial sectors on a global scale. Although modern society had faced other pandemics prior to COVID-19, the higher level of interconnection promoted by technology led to rapid spread of the virus after it first emerged in Wuhan, China in December of 2019 [2]. By March of 2020, the World Health Organization had declared COVID-19 a pandemic and, indeed, cases of COVID-19 infection had appeared in nearly every nation, with scores of deaths already mounting even in places with access to the world’s most advanced medical technology [3]. This abrupt introduction of a highly contagious, potentially fatal disease provoked an urgent response to halt interpersonal interaction wherever possible. Countries across the globe resorted to using public health measures such as travel restrictions and social distancing as the key pandemic control mechanisms for the first time in a century [4]. Accordingly, many organizations ceased their operations (particularly those that provided in-person client services; e.g., hair salons, amusement parks) whereas others endeavoured to find ways to continue to operate using alternative business models. Institutions of higher education typically exemplified the latter.

In their most common form, universities represent large nonprofit client-service organizations. Universities vary in size, with some posting as much as billions of dollars in annual revenues from sources that can include government funding, private donors, and client-service fees (e.g., student tuition). Many universities also provide commercial services, such as hosting private laboratories, largescale food-service provisions, and residential leasing structures. As with all other aspects of society, institutions of higher learning responded quickly to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, leaving campuses largely deserted: vastly reducing density or closing student residences, cafeterias, and libraries; instructing staff to work from home; and moving to alternative delivery modes for programs and services, such as through online classes and virtual mental health counselling [5, 6].

At the onset of COVID-19, people generally expected to return to in-person activities following a brief hiatus [7]. However, as various forms of lockdown persisted for days, weeks, and months, many individuals had grown comfortable working from their homes [8]. Many people struggled as they worked to discover a new balance between life and work demands: working flexibly around child schedules; eliminating commuting time, which freed mental and physical energy for other personal and professional tasks; and saving money on child care, commuting, clothes, and work lunches [9]. Others purchased homes further from work in communities that offered more space and value for their cost. Moreover, concerns about lingering health-related threats (fueled by conflicting opinions from health experts regarding safety) initially challenged acceptance of the messages issued by official public health authorities about the reduced risks of in-person activities [10]. Consequently, most industries have been slow to recover their in-person activities, causing concerns that some cities continue to experience slow economic recovery [11, 12].

Rejection of the “return to work” by many employees has presented challenges to a diversity of organizations, ranging from large corporations (e.g., JPMorgan [13]) to small businesses (e.g., family-owned restaurants [14]). Apart from stifling organizations’ operations, a shortage of workers interested in returning to their employers’ physical plants also promoted a renaissance for workers’ rights, with demands for workplace flexibility often prioritised over traditional requests for wage increases [15]. Grappling with these disparate pressures has required employers to adapt their business practices yet again, producing notable interpersonal and intrapersonal stress for employees and employers [16].

Although some industries have reconsidered workforce expectations and space requirements, others have mandated returns and consequently faced considerable resistance [17]. Yet, many higher education institutions could not freely choose how to operate because students returned to campus in numbers that paralleled or exceeded pre-COVID states, and universities required staff and faculty onsite to provide services—and a learning community. As in other sectors, however, staff nevertheless expressed reluctance to return. This need for staff presence triggered a wide array of emotional responses, some of which were positive but many of which challenged successful reintegration. This paper presents the results of a quantitative content analysis of employees’ emotional responses regarding the experience of COVID-19 and the return to work at a large, urban research university. In doing so, it provides a positivist test case for the value of transformational leadership in addressing employee and client distress in the wake of the global pandemic and subsequent transition back to regular organizational operations.

Transformational leadership

Effective regulation of followers’ emotions features as one of the basic tenets of the original theory of transformational leadership [18]. Although recent scrutiny has identified some weaknesses in the conceptual and methodological foundations of transformational leadership [19, 20], it continues as an effective model for describing a broad set of behaviours generally considered admirable in leaders’ behaviour, such as charisma and authenticity [21].

Premised on the idea of one who “transforms” their followers by motivating them on a highly intrinsic level [18, 22], the transformational leader appeals to followers’ needs and fulfills them in a way that respects cultures’ and individuals’ values and notions of morality [23]. Rather than limiting their purview to productivity alone, the transformational leader encourages creativity, new insights, and intellectual contributions from followers with the intention of meaningfully involving them in the process of leadership. Transformational leadership theory posits that this inclusive style motivates followers, enables them to grow and transcend their limits, and stimulates them to work cooperatively towards the group’s goals. Transformational leaders thus attempt to communicate their goals and vision to their followers by linking individuals’ values and personal identities to the goals of the group. As such, transformational leaders are characterised as highly liked and trusted while remaining highly competent and efficient [23]. This combination of ability and likeability often constitutes the prototype of the benevolent leader: one who is strong and active in pursuing the best interests of their followers while maintaining an effective rapport and positive interpersonal relationships. Transformational leaders typically achieve this by modeling prosocial behaviours themselves, serving as a moral and ethical example to their followers, and by taking a genuine interest in the followers’ individual and personal well-being [24].

Transformational leadership classically consists of four pillars: individualised consideration, intellectual stimulation, inspirational motivation, and idealised influence [25]. First, individualised consideration refers to how much the leader attends to followers’ individual needs and then responds with mentorship, demonstrating a high level of empathy and taking care to celebrate followers’ contributions and achievements. Second, intellectual stimulation pertains to the leader’s maintaining an environment that invites dissent, rewards creative thinking, and encourages independent thought among followers, in part by modeling a willingness to take risks and challenge assumptions. Third, inspirational motivation characterises whether the leader inspires followers with a vision that provides them with a clear sense of purpose and motivates them to work towards an articulated goal. Finally, idealised influence describes the degree to which the leader behaves admirably and causes followers to identify with the leader. Consequently, transformational leaders’ efficacy often derives from addressing followers’ emotions [26]. Noting this within the context of the emotionally charged environment generated by the COVID-19 pandemic, we employed a robust model for dissecting emotional experiences within organizations in the current work.

A model for understanding emotions in the workplace

Interest in emotions in the workplace arose in the 1920s and 1930s with the development of new fields of study, industrial and organizational psychology, which focussed (among other things) on individual differences among workers with respect to job satisfaction and motivation; and subsequently how these affected performance and productivity. Researchers in the 1960s and 1970s, by contrast, focussed on characteristics of the organization and the work, and the manner in which factors such as personal agency and organizational justice affected both workers and work [27]. Integrating these various perspectives, Ashkanasy [28, 29] proposed a model for understanding emotions in the workplace, ranging from those that reside within a particular employee; to those that represent interactions between individuals and within workplace groups; to those that are organization-wide, arising from the policies and culture of the organization [1, 28, 29].

Ashkanasy and Dorris [1] describe five levels for identifying the types of emotions that typically occur in work-based organizations based on the original model. In brief summary, the first level refers to within-person emotions felt by an individual—one’s routine affective feeling states. The second level advances to emotions occurring between persons, perhaps principally referring to emotional intelligence (e.g., the ability to recognize, understand, and act according to the emotions of another person; see Mayer et al. for review [30]). The third level builds on the recognition and understanding established in the second level by shifting to the perception, communication, and exchange of emotions within interactions with the person whose emotions had been recognized; in other words, the emotions occurring within a dyad. The fourth level considers the accumulation of emotions in a group or team beyond dyads, with phenomena such as emotional contagion among group members actively shaping the collective mood of a group. Finally, the fifth level addresses the emotional climate of the organization by considering how individuals and groups contribute to that climate while the climate reciprocally structures the emotions felt by those organization members. The model thus notably combines affect, mood, feelings, and discrete emotions, operationalizing the last term to refer to the broad set of these experiences in different ways at different levels. We acknowledge the complexity of these distinctions, particularly as they differentiate in terms of valence and arousal [31], which constitute an active area of inquiry among researchers seeking to develop new means of conceptualizing “emotion” as scholars have traditionally understood it [32]. We therefore follow Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] lead in the present work for economy and consistency, recognizing that each level of workplace “emotion” pertains to a particular facet of affective or emotional experience or exchange.

The behaviour of leaders at both the fourth and fifth levels of Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] model therefore plays an important role in directing the emotional tone of the organization and its subunits. Thus, senior leaders may set policies that facilitate or constrain emotion-laden phenomena in an organization (Level 5) whereas mid-level managers shape the emotions felt by employees within the groups that they lead by (a) regulating those emotions, (b) intervening to resolve conflicts, and (c) modeling appropriate expressions of emotion through their own displays (Level 4). By example, Shen et al. [33] documented how gender differences in leaders’ emotional self-regulation during COVID-19 (Level 1) precipitated stereotype-incongruent management styles (Level 4). This requirement of leaders to influence the emotions of their followers resembles some of the attributes of transformational leadership.

Indeed, one might expect a transformational leader to show individualised consideration at Level 2 by recognizing followers’ emotions; to make space for followers’ dissonant feelings at Level 3 when providing intellectual stimulation; to unite followers’ interests across their emotional experiences by providing inspirational motivation at Levels 4 and 5; and to appropriately regulate their own behaviours as a mechanism for idealised influence such that they supply an example to followers for how to emotionally respond in the face of both challenges and celebrations. Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] model therefore provides a useful tool for understanding how transformational leadership relates to emotional experiences in an organization.

The current study

This paper describes a quantitative content analysis of a set of 300 interviews conducted at a university by a group entitled “The Resilience Project Team” (RPT) that was commissioned to examine the organization’s activities during the years in which the university had paused its usual operations. We embarked on an exploration of the emotions recorded in the interview notes of the RPT to catalogue the emotional experiences expressed by the mid-level leaders within the organization’s faculty and staff employee groups. We used Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] updated model of Ashkanasy’s [29] original classification of workplace emotions, slightly modifying the titles of the categories and their descriptions to better fit this particular corpus of data (see Table 1). Results of this analysis provided a positive test of the value of transformational leadership amid this historically notable challenge to organizational leadership.

As background, the university’s Provost commissioned its University Resilience Project in April 2022 to focus on the organization’s recovery following the relaxation of public health restrictions on in-person gathering in the latter half of 2021. The Project’s mandate was for the RPT to work with academic divisions, campuses, and institutional portfolios to identify and adopt promising practices created during the pandemic and to retire or archive less useful policies, procedures, and activities that could be revised to make the university more resilient. Accordingly, the Provost recruited senior administrative staff from diverse operational portfolios to form a team led by a senior faculty member. RPT members represented the offices responsible for academic programs, campus operations, communications, human resources, research services, and student affairs. In addition, a diverse group of senior academic leaders (Vice-Presidents and Vice-Provosts) oversaw the RPT’s work, and a separate group (with full overlap of the RPT’s membership) considered matters related to operational safety (e.g., mask requirements, symptom screening protocols, air filtration in classrooms) in report to the RPT’s Chair.

Although the ultimate goal of the Project consisted of identifying opportunities to reshape the university’s operational practices, the RPT began by indexing the innovations and lessons learned throughout the organization’s adaptations in response to cyclical shifts in public health guidelines. They thus completed a series of consultation meetings with stakeholders across all three campuses, which they documented in an archive of private notes made available for this purpose. Although the interview questions (see Appendix) focussed on operational issues, what quickly became apparent was respondents’ overwhelming desire to share their emotional responses to the pandemic, and to their return to work. In this paper, we capitalise on this set of interviews to examine the organization’s activities during the years in which the university had paused its usual operations. Whereas such secondary data analysis has limits in terms of methodological control, these data boast benefits in terms of ecological validity, reduction in potential researcher bias, and generalizability.

Indeed, the “work-from-home” period bears many of the characteristics of the experience of individuals working in organizations outside of this particular organization. Although changes in public health restrictions allowed occasional opportunities for this university to resume on-campus operations, variable micro-cultures of risk tolerance within the organization generated different timelines for units and divisions to resume in-person activity even after stay-at-home orders no longer restricted it. In other words—similar to the changes observed in the labour market across industrial sectors—many students and employees had developed a preference for working and receiving services (both curricular and extracurricular) in their homes.

Moreover, concerns about persisting health-related threats and post-traumatic stress inhibited many individuals from desiring interpersonal contact. Differing opinions about the safety of social gathering among health experts provided fodder to proponents of opposite views, promoting a tendency for confirmation bias among individuals eager to return to prepandemic levels of social interaction as well as for those wishing to perpetuate their adaption to working from home.

The unrelenting stress of the pandemic additionally precipitated widespread feelings of burnout and a persistent state of general and focal anxiety. Specifically, many people had endured significant illness, deaths of loved ones, financial uncertainty, major increases in childcare responsibilities, and the need to absorb colleagues’ workloads (consequent to these and other, unmentioned, challenges [34]). Individuals thus appeared to require an opportunity to share feelings about these experiences before they could focus on operational issues related to COVID-19 and its aftermath.

With the RPT’s cooperation, we thus secured ethics approval to access their anonymized interview notes to ascertain the emotions expressed by the participants, both (a) retrospectively regarding their work-from-home experiences and (b) contemporaneously during the return-to-work period in which the interviews occurred.

Method

We conducted secondary data analysis of a set of semi-structured consultation interviews undertaken as part of the University Resilience Project at the University of Toronto. These interviews had the purpose of engaging the university community in consultations regarding their experiences since the onset of public health restrictions related to COVID-19. All eight members of the RPT participated as interviewers, working individually, in pairs, or in triads to meet with individuals or small groups in person or via computer-mediated communication (i.e., Zoom or Microsoft Teams).

The University of Toronto paused online instruction in most of its divisions in mid-March 2020. Although instruction in some essential areas continued in person, the vast majority of instructional experiences moved to a remote delivery format. Likewise, most faculty, staff, and students discontinued coming to campus, with the majority legally barred from traveling outside of their homes at several periods of time. Some faculty, staff, and students working in essential areas did continue to support the university’s operations by working on the campuses, however. Individuals representing both types of experiences participated in the interviews.

The general timeframe during which individuals did not study or work on the university’s campuses ran from approximately March 2020 until January 2022. This broad span aligned with public health restrictions imposed in the public health districts in which the university possesses campuses, punctuated by brief periods in which public health restrictions eased to allow activities on the campuses. Some of the university’s divisions did not reinitiate on-campus activities until later, however (e.g., September 2022), creating an uneven return to the workplace. The RPT’s interviews principally occurred from April 2022 through June 2022, with some interviews still continuing until October 2022.

Participants

All of the leaders of the university’s academic and operational divisions received personal invitations for a consultation meeting to hear about their experiences since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The invitation email indicated that the RPT would be meeting with divisional stakeholders to reflect on the institution’s collective experience to identify the positive adaptations and innovations that emerged, as well as the gaps, lessons learned, and strategies to consider for the organization’s future resilience. Notably, all individuals contacted agreed to meet, sometimes accompanied by members of their support staff; thus, the interviews included every divisional leader across the university’s three campuses (see the Current Study section above for additional detail). More than 300 persons participated in the interviews within a total of 96 separate meetings, roughly 0.3% of the university’s employees and the full complement of academic and operational divisional leaders from across the three campuses. The participants were either peers or in higher-ranking positions relative to the interviewers; thus, participants should not have felt unccomfortable declining the meeting if they did not wish to participate and should have felt free to speak candidly when they did participate.

Instrument

The interviews followed a semi-structured format consisting of nine items (see Appendix), though the interviewers yielded to the participants’ direction. In many cases, participants desired to speak on topics outside of the expected interview structure (e.g., hybrid work structures), resulting in low adherence. The interviewers contemporaneously recorded notes of the participants’ responses, including direct quotes, paraphrasing, and summaries. Each interview lasted approximately one hour.

Analysis

The first author undertook initial quantitative coding of the interview notes according to an adjusted version of Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] framework for workplace emotions (see Table 1). Specifically, each RPT member’s notes were read, taking care not to duplicate entries in cases when multiple RPT members attended the same interview but recorded separate accounts. Every expression of emotion was tagged, and later classified whether it described an intrapersonal emotion (one that the person felt), an interpersonally empathic emotion (one that the person recognized someone else feeling), an interpersonal emotional intervention (acting to modify another person’s emotion), an intragroup emotional intervention (a leader’s modification of their group’s emotion), or emotions related to the organization’s influence (emotions related to the university’s culture or the actions of its senior leaders), extracting directly quoted phrases from the notes for further analysis. The first author then coded each emotional expression’s valence (i.e., positive vs. negative) and subjected the data to descriptive and frequency analyses to understand the distribution of feelings expressed. The initial coding of the first author was then reviewed and verified by the second author; where questions arose, the authors discussed and arrived at a consensus. None of the comments appeared to include substantive references to entities outside of the university, its partners (e.g., affiliated hospitals), or workplace-irrelevant matters.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the University of Toronto, but restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under licence for the current study and so are not publicly available. The data are, however, available from the authors upon reasonable request and with the permission of the University of Toronto].

Results

In total, 1,010 expressions of emotion were recorded in the interviews. Classification showed an uneven distribution, χ2(4, N = 1010) = 74.93, p < .001, V = 0.19, that principally focused on self-focused emotions, Zcontrast = 9.45, p < .001, rcontrast = 0.30 (see Table 2). Appraisals of others’ emotions followed, with substantial commentary about the university as an organization and its culture also featuring prominently in the feelings expressed in the interviews.

Coding the valence of the emotions showed that the preponderance expressed negative feelings, Zcontrast = 20.77, p < .001, rcontrast = 0.65. The greatest share of expressed emotions (intrapersonal negative, Level 1; 33%) consisted of individuals’ expressions of their own anxiety (“we’re not ready for the next thing”), anger (“virtual retirement parties sucked”), and exhaustion (“hybrid is difficult”). The second-greatest share of expressed emotions (negative interpersonal empathy, Level 2; 23%) referred to perceptions of others’ anxiety (“grad students were concerned”), anger (“X feels that coming in is punishment”), and exhaustion (“faculty and administrators overwhelmed”).

Among the positive expressions, participants’ comments contained compassion (“it’s okay to be kind to yourself”), appreciation (“I’ve felt exceptionally supported”), and optimism (“celebrate what can be done and what has been done”). The greatest ratio of positive to negative comments (intragroup intervention, Level 4; 6:1) related to local leaders’ efforts to address their followers’ emotions (e.g., actions by Deans or managers of operational divisions). These expressions largely praised the effectiveness of local leaders’ communication (“Deans in [specific divisions] were good at communicating to their communities”), community-building activities (“X held [an] event in summer and it went well. That helped”), and positive outcomes stemming from divisional responses (“we are now looking at students’ holistic experience more than before”). The lowest ratio of positive emotions occurred in individuals’ recognition of others’ emotions (interpersonal empathy, Level 2; 1:24), suggesting that they primarily observed or attended to more negative than positive expressions from others.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic represented an historic challenge to the survival of humanity as a species. This broadscale existential threat to civilization logically disrupted all sectors of society, including higher education. It therefore remains unsurprising that even individuals with stable jobs in an economically privileged nation would experience negative emotions over a sustained period. The present data reflected that pronounced negativity and show connections to the principles of transformational leadership, which leaders appear to have employed to effectively manage that negativity.

Indeed, sampling the full complement of academic and operational divisional leaders from across the three [organization blinded for review] campuses about their experiences since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic returned a high proportion of negative expressions of emotion in the comments received. Coding the feedback according to a modified version of Ashkanasy and Dorris’s [1] five-tier framework of workplace emotions indicated that the greatest share of expressions referenced individuals’ own feelings about their experiences (Level 1). Although individuals also demonstrated a sizable amount of recognition of others’ emotions (Level 2), responses to those emotions (Level 3) comprised one of the least frequent levels of emotional activity. Responses by local leaders to their followers’ emotions (Level 4) occurred with similar frequency as the individual responses but, interestingly, the valence of emotions showed the highest contrast between these two levels: whereas the highest relative proportion of negative expressions occurred at Level 3, this ratio reversed at Level 4, which accumulated the highest relative proportion of positive emotions. This pattern suggests that individuals recognized a high frequency of negative emotions in other individuals and did not often intervene to regulate them but, when divisional leaders intervened to regulate those emotions, positive reactions typically ensued; illustrating that the level at which positive emotion did emerge (i.e., in leaders’ response to followers’ and clients’ negative emotional expressions) evidenced the benefits of a transformational leadership approach.

These positive responses appear to have contrasted against the backdrop of otherwise overwhelming negativity expressed during the interviews. First, consider that the interviews might have reflected a baseline negativity bias common in social behaviour [35]. Second, the common cultural practice of sharing negative experiences (“troubles talk” [36]) might have compounded participants’ general tendency to focus on their negative rather than positive experiences. Participants’ concentration on negative emotions may therefore have merely illustrated a normal tendency to notice and communicate negative versus positive feelings.

However, the context of the interviews did not particularly lend itself to eliciting negative responses. Of the nine intended questions, only three invited potentially negative thoughts and did so only mildly by orienting attention to limitations or opportunities for improvement, rather than mere complaints. Moreover, the interview structure began with soliciting positive feedback, which one might expect to set the expectation for a positive tone to the interview. The initial communication requesting participation in the interviews likewise began by referring to an interest in participants’ positive experiences.

The balance of probabilities therefore suggests that participants’ feedback represented an authentic desire to express negative feelings that extends beyond base-rate negativity. Indeed, participants expressed more negative than positive emotions at every level of workplace emotions. Thus, even accounting for an uneven starting point that favors expression of negative over positive emotions, participants appear to have heavily emphasised negative feelings. Although this negativity broadly reflects the disposition of staff at universities operating in similar contexts during the COVID-19 pandemic and the period since [37, 38], as well as the low levels of employee engagement more generally over the last decade (https://www.gallup.com/394373/indicator-employee-engagement.aspx), considering that the structure of the interviews prioritised elicitation of positive over negative feedback, the level of negativity in the participants’ responses seems notable.

The concentration of positive responses to local leaders’ efforts to address their followers’ negative emotions thus illuminates the potential value of ameliorative actions. Although the interview structure may have possibly promoted positive responses specifically regarding leaders’ efforts (as noted above), this emotional exchange between leaders and followers resembles the strategies and benefits of transformational leadership [18]. Transformational leadership generally involves leaders modeling behaviours that inspire their followers. Often, this inspiration revolves around aligning followers’ personal identity to the goals of their unit or broader organization but, critically, requires leaders to understand their followers’ professional resources and deficits, which includes recognizing their personal circumstances—such as their emotional states [24]. Effective transformational leaders would therefore identify and empathise with followers experiencing distress, and respond to address that distress in ways that remove it as an obstacle in striving towards the organization’s mission. That appears to have happened here: Successful divisional leaders appear to have heard, validated, and responded empathically with action. This pattern aligns with the principles of individualised consideration, intellectual stimulation, inspirational motivation, and idealised influence characteristic of transformational leadership [21]. Thus, the current data appear to offer an illustration of how the application of transformational leadership tactics may remedy the longstanding negative orientation imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, facilitating reorientation towards the organization’s service mission.

Indeed, because the interviews allowed the RPT to catalogue the many innovations, developments, experiments, and adaptations that individuals and units had engaged under the pressure of COVID-19, it precipitated opportunities to reflect participants’ comments back to the organization’s members. Specifically, the faculty member serving as Chair of the RPT gave a series of 43 presentations about the University Resilience Project to various stakeholders within the university’s community during the 15-month course of the Project. These presentations served as opportunities to recognize and validate the emotions reported in the interviews, and to share the positive developments, innovations, and adaptations described by their colleagues. Because these presentations followed an inspirational design that incorporated the tenets of transformational leadership described above, they anecdotally appeared to palliate the negativity observed in the interviews. More important, they also shifted attention to the organization’s mission by reminding employees of the centrality of their clients’ (i.e., students’) experience as the organization’s guiding principle.

Limitations

As noted above, however, this analysis also presents limits in its use of a positivist approach based on secondary analysis of data from a single organization. Analysis of data from other sectors outside of higher education could prove particularly fruitful for supporting the conclusions drawn from these data. Naturally, the pandemic’s passing presents an insurmountable challenge to constructing a planned empirical test that largely limits investigation to retrospective observation. That said, even in the context of such limitations and the absence of strict researcher control, the present data boast many counterbalancing benefits. Namely, (a) the organization studied is one of the largest institutes of higher education in the world, (b) its physical operations allowed for data from three sites, (c) these sites spanned two municipalities and two public health regions, and (d) its leadership happened to commission a team to conduct a resource-intensive, comprehensive, interview-based investigation (cf. a simple survey distribution) with a 100% response rate from its third-tier leaders. These unique assets provide an opportune snapshot of a historical event that lends insight for future organizational stresses that likely generalize beyond the specific attributes of this organization (e.g., nonprofit, higher education). We thus acknowledge the data’s limitations as descriptive and responsive, while also recognizing their special value.

Conclusion

This case study may provide insight to leaders struggling to recover their workplace culture after the pandemic’s collective trauma by leveraging the principles of transformational leadership to inspire and engage workers in the wake of COVID-19’s collective trauma. Specifically, demonstrating the value of acknowledging followers’ grief, expressing gratitude to them for their sacrifices on behalf of the organization, and clearly rearticulating the organization’s goals for moving forward to support their clients.
