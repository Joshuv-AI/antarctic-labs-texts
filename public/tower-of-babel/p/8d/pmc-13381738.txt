Climate change exacerbates disparities of energy resilience in New York City
Authors: Luo Xu, Ning Lin, A T D Perera, Aaron C Spaulding, Zhecheng Wang, Michael Oppenheimer, Christine Y Blackshaw, H Vincent Poor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate extremes increasingly threaten energy infrastructure, yet whether disparities in energy resilience persist within cities under comparable hazard exposure and how distributed energy resources may reshape them remain largely unquantified. By integrating climate and energy projections, socio-demographic data, and an optimization-based power outage metric that captures initial outages, recovery, and distributed energy resource support, this study reveals evident energy resilience disparities shaped by intersectionality across income, race, and ethnicity in New York City. These disparities are projected to be exacerbated under future climates. Middle-income households exhibit the lowest levels of energy resilience, with their outage risk increasing by 1.5-2 times compared to the wealthiest households under severe events. Low- and middle-income Asian and high-income Black households experience up to twice the average outage risk increase compared to others within the same income groups. While distributed energy resources can partially mitigate disparities, their impact remains limited under business-as-usual growth. Our findings identify climate-vulnerable communities and inform efforts to promote energy justice in a changing climate.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Energy justice, Climate-change impacts

Introduction

Climate extremes such as tropical cyclones are threatening reliable energy access to nearly 60 million people living along the U.S. Gulf and East coasts1. Meanwhile, climate change is intensifying such climate extremes, exposing energy infrastructure to escalating risk. The U.S. experienced about twice as many weather-related power outages during 2014–2023 as during 2000–20092. Ensuring a resilient electricity supply for all is crucial to a sustainable and inclusive energy transition. With about 675 million people still living without reliable electricity supply globally, the United Nations (UN) has identified affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all as one of its 17 Sustainable Development Goals, adopted by all UN members to build a peaceful and prosperous future3.

While climate extremes affect broad geographic regions, their consequences are often disproportionately shouldered by climate-vulnerable communities, even within the same city exposed to similar levels of hazards, which could potentially amplify pre-existing inequities. The inherent spatial heterogeneity of power grid infrastructure naturally leads to unequal exposure to climate hazards across communities4. During Hurricane Isaias in 2020, the local power utility, Consolidated Edison (Con Edison), in New York City (NYC) maintained resilient electricity supply to well-protected areas of Manhattan with minimal outages5, while about 257,000 electricity customers across surrounding counties in NYC experienced outages due to failure in the overhead distribution system6. Beyond unequal disaster impacts, access to opportunities under a clean energy transition is also unevenly distributed7. Distributed energy resources, such as rooftop solar photovoltaic (PV) systems and energy storage systems deployed at the distribution or community level, are increasingly recognized as critical assets for post-disaster recovery and forming microgrids8,9. However, large-scale rooftop solar datasets show that solar adoption has been concentrated in wealthier American communities10. Disadvantaged census blocks identified by the cumulative environmental and socioeconomic burdens in California are also found to have less hosting capacity of their grid infrastructure to accommodate distributed energy resources11.

Emerging efforts are investigating the equity issue of energy resilience under extreme events, particularly for communities with varying levels of hazard exposure12,13. For example, empirical analysis of historical weather-related outage data in Louisiana has shown that coastal communities, which were more directly exposed to hurricane winds and storm surge, experienced substantially longer recovery durations than non-coastal communities12. In particular, coastal-non-coastal contrasts in outage duration are larger for higher-income zip codes, with non-coastal higher-income areas showing the shortest median outage durations12. Multiple cost allocation strategies with income-based prioritization have been proposed to enhance resilience in California communities susceptible to wildfire risks, considering current vegetation conditions and wildfire probabilities13. In NYC, one of the world’s largest urban economies14, recent progress has been made in revealing socio-economic disparities in present-day electricity reliability and accessibility15,16. Evidence from citywide analyses shows that disadvantaged and historically redlined neighborhoods experience higher outage rates and electricity inaccessibility15. Spatial analyses further indicate that more outage calls are associated with neighborhoods with lower income, higher energy burden, and larger shares of people of color16. The New York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA) has also identified historically marginalized and disadvantaged communities to promote a more inclusive clean energy transition17.

However, critical challenges remain in identifying and assessing inequities of energy resilience under climate change. Existing studies primarily focus on addressing regional disparities from differences in disaster exposure12,13, while less attention has been given to intra-city equity issues under comparable hazard exposures but different grid conditions. Evidence from NYC during Hurricane Isaias in 2020 highlights that, even under comparable wind hazard exposure, disparities in peak outages existed across communities in NYC6. Capturing such disparities requires highly granular intra-city analyses of energy infrastructure, electricity demand, and socio-demographic vulnerability. Also, as the climate, particularly extremes, evolves and communities increasingly adopt distributed energy resources such as rooftop solar PV and community-level energy storage systems, energy resilience solutions based solely on current or static near-term climate conditions may be inadequate to meet future needs12,13,15–17. Moreover, the role of distributed energy resources in addressing energy resilience disparities remains insufficiently understood. Therefore, a coupled climate-energy equity assessment is urgently needed to anticipate and address potentially amplified challenges and opportunities. Quantifying these disparities is crucial for guiding utility companies and policymakers toward equitable and sustainable development within individual cities such as NYC.

In this study, we focus on NYC, a diverse metropolis with over 8 million residents, where communities are exposed to comparable levels of tropical cyclone wind hazards, to examine energy resilience disparities in a changing climate. The city’s complex infrastructure, socio-economic diversity, and exposure to climate extremes make it a representative case for studying energy resilience in global metropolitan settings. Households are categorized into income groups based on annual household income (USD), including high-income (> $150,000), middle-income ($50,000–$150,000), and low-income (< $50,000). Racial and ethnic groups (including White alone, Black, Asian, Hispanic, Multiracial, and Others) are defined based on the U.S. Census and American Community Survey (ACS)18.

By integrating an optimized loss of load probability (LOLP) metric that accounts for initial outages, recovery, and the support of distributed energy resources in the utility’s large-scale distribution networks with demographic analysis, we observe clear energy disparities across income groups, races, and ethnicities, even under the historical climate. Our analysis shows that middle-income households in NYC are most at risk from power outages that result from climate hazards, particularly severe storms. Within the same income groups, low- and middle-income Asian households and high-income Black households exhibit the highest LOLP values. Using downscaled climate projections for the mid- and late-21st century, we examine future energy resilience in NYC and find that projected intensifying climate hazards are expected to substantially exacerbate these disparities. Our analysis also explores the potential of integrating additional distributed energy storage systems to mitigate such disparities, though their effectiveness is found to be limited under a business-as-usual capacity trajectory that reflects the projected deployment pathway extrapolated from historical growth trends. Our research highlights the need to identify energy resilience-disadvantaged communities in NYC and other cities, in order to guide effective grid infrastructure hardening and distributed energy resource deployment, ultimately supporting a more sustainable and equitable future in a changing climate.

Results

Energy resilience disparities under the historical climate

The existing distribution networks in NYC exhibit spatial heterogeneity in grid infrastructure configuration across different communities (Fig. 1a, also see Fig. S2 for grid infrastructure with street-view images). NYC’s electricity delivery is managed by the utility Con Edison, which serves approximately 3.6 million electricity customers across all five boroughs of NYC: Manhattan, the Bronx, Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island19. Being responsible for one of the most complex distribution systems in the U.S., Con Edison has divided its distribution network into 70 independent subnetworks (see Fig. S1) across distinct communities. While Manhattan’s distribution system has been fully underground since the early 20th century as part of the city’s urban development, many areas in suburban boroughs with relatively lower building density continue to rely heavily on overhead distribution lines. For example, about 20% of distribution lines in the Bronx remain above ground19,20. In response to these disparities, the New York City Council passed Local Law 13 of 2013, mandating the Office of Long-Term Planning and Sustainability to assess the feasibility of undergrounding power lines citywide21. Although the assessment recognized the resilience benefits of undergrounding electrical infrastructure, it also highlighted a range of countervailing considerations, most notably the substantial cost associated with undergrounding existing overhead lines in fully developed areas. The estimated cost for undergrounding power lines was approximately 8.29 million dollars per mile in the Bronx, with a citywide total projected at 18.5 billion dollars, an extremely high expense that has left NYC’s network topology largely unchanged20.

The disparity in grid infrastructure resilience naturally leads to unequal energy resilience among different communities in NYC during extreme events. During Hurricane Isaias in 2020, the spatiotemporal outage data recorded by the local power utility revealed clear differences in peak power outages across the 70 subnetworks in NYC (Fig. 1b). Staten Island, where a large share of distribution lines are overhead, experienced peak outages approaching 50% in several communities. In contrast, Manhattan and the inner urban areas (high-density neighborhoods) of Brooklyn and the Bronx reported minimal interruptions due to their well-protected distribution networks.

Beyond this event, we systematically investigate the energy resilience of communities in NYC to hurricane wind extremes. To represent historical tropical cyclone hazards, we use machine learning-based tropical cyclone simulations22 downscaled from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) dataset23 between the years 1979 and 2014 (see Methods). This period corresponds to the end of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6)24 historical simulations, which transition to future scenario simulations beginning in 2015.

Given that energy resilience includes not only the initial outage but also the recovery process following extreme events, we measure resilience using the optimized LOLP that captures the likelihood of unmet electricity demand, accounting for the initial disruption, recovery, and optimized supply from distributed energy resources (see Methods). In this study, LOLP is evaluated within one day after the peak outage, capturing the most severe disruption period, as major electricity service is typically restored within this timeframe25,26. Under tropical cyclone simulations in the historical climate, spatial disparities in energy resilience are evident across distribution networks (Fig. 2a), magnifying the differences observed in individual large-scale outage events such as Hurricane Isaias. Central urban districts such as Manhattan and Brooklyn exhibit consistently low LOLP values (generally below 2%), whereas networks in Queens, Staten Island, and parts of the southeast and northeast Bronx experience substantially higher risks, with mean LOLP levels more than three to four times larger. Moreover, by integrating the obtained LOLP with demographic analysis, we reveal that although the energy resilience risk under the historical climate appears moderate (most events, including the 100-year event, exhibit LOLPs below 5%), clear disparities already exist across income groups (Fig. 2b). High-income households (particularly those with annual incomes over $200,000 USD) exhibit the most resilient energy supply. Middle-income households (approximately $50,000 to $150,000 USD27) emerge as the most vulnerable group to extreme climate hazards, experiencing higher LOLP than both low- and high-income households. In contrast, the lowest-income households (less than $15,000 USD) demonstrate relatively higher resilience. The LOLP across income groups under 100-year and 1000-year hurricane events reveals that disparities in energy resilience become more pronounced under more severe storm events, with middle-income groups experiencing nearly twice the LOLP of high-income households.

Since the maximum generation potential of existing distributed energy resources remains low (less than 5% of the total demand), the observed energy resilience disparities can be primarily explained by examining the geographic distribution of income groups (Fig. S3) and disparities in grid infrastructure (Figs. 1a and S2). High-income households are concentrated in inner Manhattan, where electricity distribution systems are fully underground and well protected. Many low-income households reside in densely urbanized areas such as the Central and West Bronx, which also benefit from underground distribution networks. However, middle-income households, while able to afford homes in less densely populated areas, reside where lower population density and limited municipal investment have constrained the transition to underground systems20.

Our analysis further reveals that even within the same income groups, disparities in energy resilience exist across different races and ethnicities (see absolute LOLP values in Fig. 2c and relative deviations in LOLP with respect to White households in Fig. S7). Overall, Asian and Black households face higher risk under extreme events, as reflected by higher LOLP compared to other racial and ethnic groups. Among low- and middle-income households, Asian residents experience particularly elevated risk. In contrast to the overall LOLP pattern for the NYC population, the LOLP of Black residents generally increases with household income, suggesting climate vulnerabilities that are not dictated by economic status alone. White residents with household incomes over $100,000 demonstrate the highest levels of resilience, with average LOLP values less than half of those observed in Black residents within the same income group. These disparities in energy resilience can be attributed to the residential concentration of racial and ethnic groups (Fig. S4). For example, Flushing in Queens, one of the major residential hubs for Asian communities in NYC, is mostly served by overhead distribution lines. Also, similar infrastructure vulnerabilities are observed in Jamaica in Queens and the Northeast and Southeast Bronx, areas with higher proportions of Black households, contributing to the high LOLP of these communities. Notably, among the highest income groups, community segregation28 still exists, with most wealthy Black households concentrated in overhead-line areas of Brooklyn, Queens, and the Bronx (Fig. S5), while wealthy white households are common in large parts of Manhattan and some surrounding neighborhoods with underground infrastructure (Fig. S6). Despite comparable income levels, the enduring effects of historical segregation28 within the city lead to the most pronounced disparities in energy resilience between Black and White communities.

Projected climate change exacerbates energy resilience disparities

Based on bias-corrected tropical cyclone simulations driven by an ensemble of 10 downscaled CMIP6 general circulation models (GCMs) for the mid-21st century (2031-2065) and late-21st century (2066-2100), we show that climate change is expected to intensify wind hazards in NYC under two representative Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) — SSP2-4.5 (moderate emissions scenario) and SSP5-8.5 (very high emissions scenario), relative to the historical climate (see Fig. 3). SSP2-4.5 reflects a moderate mitigation pathway that aligns reasonably well with emissions projections under current policy trajectories29. Although SSP5-8.5 is increasingly viewed as an unlikely pathway to represent ‘business-as-usual’30, it remains widely used as a high-end stress-test scenario31. Given uncertainties in future warming trajectories, climate sensitivity, and the evolving global policy landscape, SSP5-8.5 is included here to capture tail risks and assess system resilience under extreme but physically plausible climate outcomes32.

During the mid-21st century, tropical cyclone wind hazards in NYC under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 exhibit broadly similar characteristics, both exceeding those of the historical climate (see Fig. 3a). Notably, events with return periods of 100 years in the historical climate are expected to occur on average every 30 years by mid-century. A 100-year event, which has wind speeds of approximately 30 m/s (tropical storm) under the historical climate, is projected to intensify to nearly 40 m/s (strong Category 1 hurricane) by mid-century. By the late-21st century, wind hazards under SSP2-4.5 remain comparable to mid-century projections, while those under SSP5-8.5 are expected to increase substantially (see Fig. 3b). Under SSP5-8.5, wind events with a return period of 100 years in the historical climate may occur on average every 10 years by the late 21st century. A 400-year event under the near-present climate, classified as a Category 1 hurricane, is projected to strengthen to a Category 3 hurricane under SSP5-8.5. In addition to tropical cyclone hazards, rising temperatures under future climate scenarios can further increase electricity demand. Research further suggests that hurricane-heatwave compound events are projected to increase33.

Therefore, in our analysis for future climate scenarios, we calculated the future LOLPs across subnetworks in NYC, incorporating tropical cyclone and heat hazards downscaled from GCM projections, baseline demand growth, and the business-as-usual expansion of distributed solar PV systems (see Methods). As climate hazards are projected to intensify, we observe increasingly pronounced energy resilience disparities across income groups in future scenarios (Fig. 4) compared to the historical climate (Fig. 2b). While all income groups experience notable increases in LOLP, middle-income households are disproportionately affected by the projected climate hazards.

During a 100-year hurricane event in the late-21st century under SSP5-8.5, the LOLP for the wealthiest households increases by only about 4.5%, while middle-income households experience nearly twice that increase, rising from about 4% to over 11%. Even under SSP2-4.5 in the mid-21st century, the 100-year event results in over a 1.5-fold higher LOLP for middle-income households compared to the wealthiest households. The LOLP for middle-income households under a 1000-year event under this moderate emissions scenario is 1.7 times that of the wealthiest households and 1.4 times that of low-income households, highlighting their heightened vulnerability in the changing climate.

We also observed that the intensifying climate risks further exacerbate energy resilience disparities across races and ethnicities within the same income groups (see absolute LOLP values in Fig. 5 and value differences compared to White households in Fig. S8). By the mid-21st century, the average LOLP gap between Asian and other racial groups within low- and middle-income households is projected to double compared to the historical climate. Among high-income households, the increase in average LOLP for Black residents is over three times that of White households, with the most pronounced disparities under SSP5-8.5 in the late-21st century. The average LOLP gap between Asian and other racial groups in low- and middle-income households increases by over threefold relative to the historical climate. The average LOLP for high-income Black households increases from 2.8% to 8.6%. Such an increase in LOLP is nearly four times higher than that for the wealthiest White households. As communities are exposed to similar levels of climate hazards, these results highlight large differences in their resilience to climate change impacts due to the unequal resilience of their grid infrastructure. These enlarged disparities further indicate that the business-as-usual expansion of distributed solar PV systems, with a doubling of capacity (see Figs. S9–S13), is insufficient to offset the combined impacts of intensifying wind hazards and increasing demand under rising temperatures.

To assess the robustness of these findings, we conduct additional sensitivity analyses by extending the simulation horizon to two days after the peak outage (Figs. S14–S16) and by excluding demand growth effects (Figs. S17–S18). Extending the evaluation window reduces the relative weight of the peak-outage period, thereby slightly lowering the observed disparities. However, the resulting LOLP values under the two-day horizon decrease by no more than 20% compared to the one-day evaluation (Fig. 2b, c; Figs. 4 and 5), and the overall disparity patterns between racial and income groups remain largely unchanged. Under SSP2-4.5, the LOLP associated with a 100-year hurricane event remains more than 1.5 times higher for middle-income households than for the wealthiest income group, increasing to nearly a twofold difference under SSP5-8.5 in the late-21st century (Fig. S15). Across all future scenarios, the average LOLP for middle-income Asian households remains 1.3−1.7 times higher than that for White households, while among high-income households, Black households continue to experience average LOLP values 2.2–3.8 times higher than those observed for White households within the same income group. These results confirm that energy resilience disparities across communities are structural in nature rather than artifacts of the chosen evaluation window. When demand growth is excluded, clear energy resilience disparities across income groups and racial and ethnic communities persist (Figs. S17–S18). Demand growth contributes less than 1% to absolute LOLP increases in aggregate (Figs. 4 and 5). However, under higher-temperature scenarios, it amplifies disparities across racial and ethnic groups within income categories. For example, under late-21st-century SSP5-8.5 conditions, incorporating demand growth increases LOLP during a 100-year event by a factor of 1.5–2.3 across income groups relative to simulations without demand growth, corresponding to absolute increases of approximately 0.1–0.3%. Together with our consideration of parameter uncertainty in fragility curves for power outage modeling (see Methods and Supplementary Note 1), these sensitivity analyses reinforce the conclusion that projected disparities in energy resilience are robust across modeling assumptions and are driven by persistent structural inequities in grid resilience against comparable hazards in NYC.

The effect of distributed energy resources

To further investigate the effect of distributed energy resources on community-level energy resilience, we consider the scenario that all distributed solar PV systems are paired with energy storage systems (ESSs) of equivalent power capacity, each configured with a common energy-to-power (E/P) ratio of 4 hours34. Although behind-the-meter solar-storage configurations are currently limited, they present an emerging trend, as distributed energy storage systems are receiving increasing attention with the rise of the concepts of virtual power plants and active distribution networks35. Despite the relatively limited capacity of paired distributed energy resources under the business-as-usual pathway, ESSs demonstrate the ability to mitigate projected climate change impacts and reduce disparities across income groups (Fig. 6).

Blue bars indicate the average LOLP when distributed solar PV systems are paired with energy storage systems of equivalent power capacity. By the mid-21st century, middle-income households receive greater resilience benefits from paired ESSs, achieving approximately 1.5 times greater reductions in average LOLP compared to high- and low-income households (about 0.3% compared to 0.2%). Although wind hazards under SSP2-4.5 are comparable between the mid- and late-21st century (Fig. 3a), higher electricity demand projected for the late-21st century, driven by increased temperature, electrification, and economic growth, reduces the marginal effectiveness of energy storage (Fig. 6a, c). By the late-21st century under SSP5-8.5, substantially intensified climate risks, including rising temperatures and stronger wind hazards, combined with the plateau in distributed solar PV capacity growth under the business-as-usual pathway (Figs. S9–S13), increase average LOLP by more than 1.5-fold relative to SSP2-4.5. Although the absolute reduction in LOLP attributable to energy storage is larger under SSP5-8.5 than under SSP2-4.5 (up to 0.36% compared with 0.27%), this mitigation effect remains substantially smaller than the increase driven by more severe extreme events. These results highlight both the effectiveness of energy storage under extreme scenarios and the inadequacy of distributed energy resources under the business-as-usual capacity expansion pathway to enhance energy resilience in NYC.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed energy resilience across large-scale distribution networks in NYC under tropical cyclone hazards in a changing climate. Energy resilience is measured by the LOLP that accounts for initial outages, recovery, and support from distributed energy resources. Our study reveals that even within a single city exposed to comparable climate hazards, evident energy resilience disparities exist across income groups, races, and ethnicities. In NYC, middle-income households consistently emerge as the most vulnerable among all income groups. Low- and middle-income Asian households and high-income Black households exhibit the highest risk of prolonged outages under tropical cyclone hazards. These disparities exist in the historical climate and are projected to be exacerbated under future climate scenarios, particularly under very high emissions scenarios. While all income groups experience increases in LOLP under future climate scenarios, middle-income households face disproportionately higher relative increases, with their LOLP rising at 1.5 to 2 times the rate of the wealthiest households under 100-year hurricane events. In a changing climate, low- and middle-income Asian households face about a 1.5-2-fold greater increase in average LOLP compared to White, Hispanic, and Multiracial households with comparable incomes. High-income Black households experience a nearly 4 times higher increase in average LOLP than White households in the same income group. Our analysis further shows that distributed ESSs paired with distributed solar PV systems can partially alleviate these disparities. However, under a business-as-usual capacity expansion trajectory, their risk mitigation potential is largely constrained, especially under extreme climate change scenarios.

The insights and methodology presented in this study have implications for utilities, policymakers, and governments, not only in NYC but also in other cities and regions facing climate risks. While our findings are based on NYC’s energy infrastructure, demographic composition, and projected climate hazards, energy resilience disparities may exist in other areas with social and energy infrastructure heterogeneity. Applying our climate-energy framework to other climate-vulnerable cities and regions can help assess energy resilience and inform energy justice-oriented planning. As the climate changes, current adaptation policy may not be sufficient for future conditions. By identifying communities that are more sensitive to climate change, our analysis can inform grid infrastructure hardening and distributed energy resource deployment strategies to build a more equitable and resilient energy future in a changing climate. We also find that warmer climate scenarios, especially those in the late-21st century, are associated with substantially greater disparities in energy resilience, underscoring the urgency and importance of accelerating the clean energy transition to combat climate change.

As we examine the energy justice implications of climate hazards in communities exposed to comparable hazard levels, our analysis focuses primarily on the impacts of tropical cyclone wind hazards and temperature, which have been the dominant driver of recent large-scale power outages in NYC through wind-related infrastructure damage and are expected to increasingly threaten energy resilience by elevating electricity demand under future warming36. Flooding hazards are excluded from this analysis as recent hurricanes impacting NYC have caused only limited and spatially localized storm-surge-related inundation. Among recent events, Hurricane Henri produced only localized and relatively low levels of coastal flooding, while Hurricanes Ida (2021), Elsa (2021), and Isaias (2020) were associated with minimal storm-surge impacts in the region according to Coastal Emergency Risk Assessment (CERA) Historical Storm Surge Archive37. Although Hurricane Ida (2021) generated extreme rainfall and caused notable inland flooding, its impacts were concentrated in transportation systems, underground spaces, and low-lying residential areas. Power outages in NYC remained relatively limited compared to wind-driven widespread outage events such as Hurricane Isaias (2020)38. While flooding was not a dominant driver of large-scale outages in NYC in recent years6, it remains an important area for further investigation. For example, during Hurricane Sandy (2012), storm surge led to severe coastal flooding and caused widespread infrastructure disruptions across the city. Since then, major coastal flood barrier systems have been proposed to reduce storm-surge risk39. Under climate change, intensifying extreme rainfall40 and sea-level rise41,42 may increasingly contribute to infrastructure damage and impede recovery processes. Energy resilience disparities under flood events may be different from those under extreme winds, which should be investigated in future research. Also, in flood-prone areas, the risk of water intrusion into underground infrastructure may challenge the conventional preference for undergrounding distribution lines as a resilience strategy. In such contexts, hardening existing overhead lines may offer a more adaptive alternative, particularly when combined with decentralized energy solutions.

Our research can be further improved by overcoming several limitations. First, the hazard-outage relationship is calibrated using four major storm events in NYC since 2019, among which Hurricane Isaias produced the most extensive wind-driven outages. The remaining events resulted in comparatively moderate impacts, with county-level outages generally not exceeding 5%. Although bootstrap resampling is used to account for parameter uncertainty, the limited number of extreme wind-driven outage events may not fully capture the full range of wind characteristics and infrastructure responses. As additional high-resolution outage datasets become available, future work could integrate multiple historical events and combine them with infrastructure-informed fragility analyses to further improve the accuracy of identifying community-level resilience disparities. Second, our future projections focus primarily on SSP2-4.5 as a scenario representing current policy trends and SSP5-8.5 as a high-end stress-test scenario. Additional climate scenarios, combined with uncertainties in demand growth and electrification, are worth further investigation. Third, although NYC is exposed to broadly comparable regional wind hazards, localized variations in storm exposure may exist across communities within the city. In this study, we use a physics-based near-surface wind field model that generates spatiotemporal wind fields for both historical and future storms based on storms’ characteristics such as track data, maximum wind speed, and radius of maximum wind (see spatial distribution of wind hazards across NYC distribution networks with overhead lines under future climate scenarios in Fig. S19). However, this wind hazard model does not explicitly resolve the complex building-scale flow interactions within the urban canopy. Capturing such complex wind dynamics driven by the built environment, such as channeling between buildings and turbulence-induced eddies, would require city-scale computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations at meter-level resolution. Future work could incorporate CFD-informed parameterizations or machine learning surrogates to better represent fine-scale urban wind hazard features43, improving the understanding of community-level energy disparities within cities.

Lastly, we also note that the LOLP in our study is assessed under optimized management of distributed energy resources. However, in practice, a standardized optimal energy management strategy for these distributed energy resources is not yet widely established44. Previous studies have shown that while individually optimized battery systems benefit their own households, they can compromise the resilience of the microgrid45. NYC’s power utility, Con Edison, has pioneered a microgrid project aimed at powering critical communities during emergency events, which demonstrates a scalable and promising approach to enhancing community-level resilience while supporting a more equitable and sustainable future in a changing climate. The existing distribution systems have yet to undergo a large-scale transition towards active distribution networks that possess energy autonomy and the capability to coordinate and optimize distributed energy resources across communities. Realizing the full potential of distributed energy resources requires infrastructure upgrading, distribution-level energy management systems, and effective policy incentives to support microgrid development. Our analysis informs these efforts to promote energy justice under intensifying climate extremes in a changing climate.

Methods

Climate hazards for historical and future climate scenarios

Since wind hazards are a primary driver of large-scale power outages in NYC (the most recent severe blackout caused by Hurricane Isaias in 20206) and this study focuses on energy resilience under high-impact, low-frequency events, we consider wind hazards from tropical cyclones as the primary drivers of initial outages under both historical and future climate scenarios. To assess the changes in tropical cyclone hazard characteristics and their implications for energy resilience, we use synthetic tropical cyclone datasets generated with the Princeton Environment-dependent Probabilistic Cyclone Model (PepC)22. PepC generates large datasets of synthetic tropical cyclones under given climate conditions characterized by key environmental factors, including atmospheric temperature, humidity, wind shear, and ocean features. It has been applied to assess long-term climate trends in the North Atlantic Basin46. To represent the historical climate (1979-2014), tropical cyclones are generated/downscaled from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) dataset23. For future climate hazard projections, we use tropical cyclones downscaled from 10 GCMs (ACCESS-ESM1-5, BCC-CSM2-MR, CanESM5, CNRM-CM6-1, EC-Earth3, FGOALS-g3, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, and MRI-ESM2-0) under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) from CMIP6: SSP2-4.5 (moderate-emissions scenario) and SSP5-8.5 (high-emissions scenario)24. For each climate scenario, storm occurrence in NYC is considered a Poisson process, and the distribution of storm wind intensities is fitted using a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) for values above a threshold and a non-parametric kernel density estimate (KDE) for values below a threshold. To reduce the potential bias in GCM projections, we apply a bias correction procedure to the downscaled GCM outputs47. Storm frequency for each projected climate scenario is bias corrected by applying a scaling factor equal to the ratio between the ERA5-derived historical storm frequency and the corresponding GCM-derived historical storm frequency. Future GCM wind intensity distributions are bias-corrected using quantile delta mapping calibrated between ERA5 and GCM historical intensity distributions. Bias-corrected tropical cyclones are generated by using a nearest-neighbor sampling process to draw corresponding PepC storms in order to obtain the bias-corrected intensity distribution (see Fig. S20–S22 for the detailed storm characteristics across GCM). For the bias-corrected tropical cyclone realizations, to translate the storm characteristics into local wind hazards for specific locations (all 70 subnetworks), we apply a physics-based near-surface wind field model48 to simulate hourly spatiotemporal wind speeds for each tropical cyclone event. In addition to the initial outage caused by tropical cyclones, we also account for the impact of temperature increase on electricity demand by using the projections from the consistent GCMs that generate the tropical cyclone hazards (see subsequent Methods sections).

NYC power grid infrastructure, outage and recovery

We collected detailed infrastructure data from the local power utility, Con Edison19, focusing especially on the distribution network, which comprises 70 subnetworks (Fig. S1) across the five boroughs of NYC (Bronx, Brooklyn, Manhattan, Queens, and Staten Island). Given NYC’s relatively robust and interconnected transmission network compared to its distribution networks, and to remain consistent with the scope of this analysis focusing on energy disparity within local communities, we limit our analysis to the distribution network infrastructure that plays a critical role in connecting end-users to electricity and its heightened vulnerability to localized disruptions.

High-resolution (10-minute) spatiotemporal power outage data for each subnetwork during Hurricane Isaias in 2020, were recorded by the local power utility19. To broaden the empirical basis of the outage model beyond a single event, we further incorporated outage observations from Tropical Storms Henri, Ida, and Elsa in 2021 obtained from the Environment for Analysis of Geo-Located Energy Information (EAGLE-I) dataset38. Tropical cyclone track data, maximum wind speed, and radius of maximum wind were sourced from the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS)49 developed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and used as input to the same physics-based near-surface wind field model as applied to synthetic tropical cyclone analysis. By combining historical outage records from multiple storm events with simulated wind hazard fields, we estimate the relationship between wind intensity and the outage probability of overhead-line distribution networks within each county. We employ a lognormal cumulative distribution function (CDF) as a fragility curve to characterize the probability of outage as a function of wind exposure50. Fragility curves based on the lognormal CDF have been widely applied in regional power outage analyses, particularly for distribution networks exposed to wind hazards50–52. The peak outage level Opeak for each distribution network is determined by evaluating the corresponding fragility function at the maximum wind speed experienced at its location during the storm period. To evaluate the robustness and predictive performance of the fitted wind-outage relationship, we conducted stratified cross-validation using the combined multi-storm dataset. In addition, to quantify parameter uncertainty and the variability of the fragility curves, we applied bootstrap resampling to construct uncertainty bounds for the estimated model parameters and outage probabilities. Details of the validation procedures and uncertainty analysis are provided in Supplementary Information Note 1.

Long-term outage recovery tracking during various events across three US states, including New York, indicates that the outage recovery durations can be well fitted by an inverse Weibull distribution53, independent of disruption causes, post-disaster network structures, and the detailed repair strategy. According to the empirical findings of recovery patterns, we define the recovery ratio R(t) as the percentage of affected customers restored by time t after peak outage, represented by a fitted inverse Weibull function using the high-resolution outage data during Hurricane Isaias. The remaining outage percentage at time t is given by O(t) = Opeak(1 − R(t)), where Opeak denotes the percentage of customers affected at the peak outage (see detailed description of restoration in Supplementary Information Note 2).

Distributed solar energy

Existing installed solar energy projects (including rooftop solar PV systems) in NYC, with detailed location information, generation capacity, and installion year from 2000 to 2023, were obtained from the New York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA)54. The maximum potential solar PV power generation at a given time t is obtained by multiplying the installed generation capacity by the time-specific capacity factor. The capacity factor is determined by averaging the hourly capacity factors of distributed solar PV systems with capacities over 1 MW within the NYC region from the NYSERDA DER Integrated Data System55.

To ensure consistency in our analysis of future scenarios, we employ the Bass model to project the future expansion of distributed rooftop solar installations in communities/subnetworks of NYC. The Bass model describes the adoption and diffusion of technology within a community, capturing the incremental growth of technology adoption over time, and has been used to predict the adoption of distributed solar systems in California10. The projections of annual distributed solar expansion are first conducted at the county level and extend to the year 2070, based on the NYSERDA solar datasets between 2000 and 2023. Then, we consider the solar adoption potential of each subnetwork within the county to be represented by its total rooftop area. Here, we use the high-resolution building geographic information system (GIS) datasets obtained from the Microsoft Building Footprints56 to calculate the rooftop area of all subnetworks (Fig. S24). The projected county-level solar capacity is then allocated to each subnetwork according to its potential. Our results indicate that the saturation period will occur around 2040, aligning with NYC’s net-zero emission goal by 2040. The projections also show an expected increase of 52.8 MW in rooftop solar capacity in 2024, consistent with the latest NYC Clean Energy Program57, which revealed 50 MW of rooftop solar in progress for 2024. We note that our projections for distributed solar expansion are conducted under a business-as-usual pathway and future policy adjustments after 2024 are not considered in this analysis. However, given explicit future expansion plans, our projections can be scaled and adjusted accordingly.

Electricity demand

In this study, we model current and future electricity demand for each subnetwork in NYC, considering the impacts of temperature variations, electrification, and economic growth on demand profiles. Present-day electricity demand for subnetworks was constructed using the 2023 electricity demand profiles obtained from Con Edison19 (NYC power utility) at an hourly resolution. To account for the impact of electrified heating and cooling on future electricity demand, we used the existing heating and cooling demand data from the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS)58 and Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS)59, with the spatial resolution at the county level. As the temporal resolution of RECS and CBECS provides annual values, we applied a degree-hour method60 based on Cooling Degree Hour (CDH) and Heating Degree Hour (HDH) to disaggregate annual heating and cooling demand to hourly resolution. This approach has been widely adopted and previously applied to New York City61.

This method was also used here to project hourly heating and cooling demand under future climate conditions, informed by daily temperature projections from CMIP6 GCMs. We obtained projected daily maximum and minimum temperature data from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6) dataset62. This dataset provides bias-corrected downscaled climate projections from 2015 to 2100 based on CMIP6 GCMs under future scenarios, including SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. To ensure temporal consistency with the environmental background used for downscaled tropical cyclones under future scenarios, we selected the same 10 GCMs used for tropical cyclone projections. Demographic analysis

We employ comprehensive datasets from the 2020 Census Tracts of the U.S. Census Bureau (the most recent geographic units defined every ten years) and the corresponding 2020 American Community Survey (ACS) to conduct the socio-economic and demographic analysis of NYC communities18. These datasets provide detailed and granular information about household counts, household income, race and ethnicity across all NYC census tracts. Census tracts are small subdivisions of a county with a population size generally between 1200 and 8000 people.
