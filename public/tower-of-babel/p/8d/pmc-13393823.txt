Prevalence and determinants of undernutrition among lactating mothers in Jimma Town, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study using Bayesian logistic regressionmodel
Authors: Buzuneh Tasfa Marine, Yematawork Alemu Haile, Yohannes Zewdu Tesfaye, Eyerusalem Gurmessa Geleta, Nigat Ashenafi, Mihiret Genene Zewde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUndernutrition among lactating mothers remains a major public health concern, particularly in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia. Adequate maternal nutrition during lactation is essential for the health and wellbeing of both mothers and their children. Bayesian logistic regression was employed in this study because it provides robust parameter estimation under uncertainty and allows the incorporation of prior information. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of undernutrition and identify its associated factors among lactating mothers in Jimma Town, Oromia Region, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 20 to April 25, 2024, among lactating mothers residing in Jimma Town. A total of 423 participants were selected using systematic random sampling. Data were collected using a pretested interviewer-administered questionnaire. Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and analyzed using R software. Bayesian logistic regression was implemented using Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulation, and posterior adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were estimated to identify factors associated with undernutrition.ResultsThe prevalence of undernutrition among lactating mothers was 21.5% (95% CrI: 17.6%–25.4%). Factors associated with higher odds of undernutrition included low dietary diversity (OR = 2.62, 95% CrI: 1.37–4.60), maternal age 15–25 years (OR = 4.33, 95% CrI: 2.85–6.38), meal frequency fewer than three times per day (OR = 5.44, 95% CrI: 3.67–7.96), and practicing food taboos (OR = 6.39, 95% CrI: 4.32–9.39). Protective factors included maternal employment (OR = 0.42, 95% CrI: 0.21–0.76), monthly income ≥ 6000 ETB (OR = 0.12, 95% CrI: 0.05–0.24), family size fewer than five members (OR = 0.47, 95% CrI: 0.29–0.70), receiving nutritional counseling (OR = 0.59, 95% CrI: 0.30–0.99), attending antenatal care visits (OR = 0.27, 95% CrI: 0.12–0.53), birth interval of at least two years (OR = 0.02, 95% CrI: 0.003–0.07), and breastfeeding frequency fewer than six times per day (OR = 0.03, 95% CrI: 0.008–0.055). These extreme protective estimates should be interpreted cautiously due to the cross-sectional study design and the potential influence of sparse data or residual confounding.ConclusionNearly one in every five lactating mothers in Jimma Town is undernourished. Undernutrition was significantly associated with multiple factors, including the mother’s age, family size, employment status, monthly income, dietary diversity, nutritional counseling, breastfeeding frequency, birth interval, antenatal care visits, frequency of meals, and the practice of food taboos. These findings highlight the urgent need for comprehensive, targeted nutrition interventions and educational programs that address not only dietary habits but also broader determinants such as family planning, maternal education, economic empowerment, and access to health services.

Introduction

Undernutrition, characterized by inadequate intake of energy and essential nutrients, remains a significant global public health concern [1]. Although it affects both developed and developing countries, it disproportionately impacts low- and middle-income regions. Maternal undernutrition continues to be a major challenge in these settings and is associated with adverse health outcomes for both mothers and children, including poor pregnancy outcomes, reduced breastfeeding performance, and increased risk of morbidity [2]. Lactation is a nutritionally demanding period during which mothers are vulnerable to multiple health challenges, including undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, fatigue, anemia, and mental health disorders such as postpartum depression. Recent evidence suggests that postpartum depression is a common maternal health concern globally and may negatively affect maternal dietary intake, breastfeeding practices, and infant care [3].

Women and adolescent girls are particularly vulnerable to undernutrition. Over one billion suffer from various forms of malnutrition, including underweight, micronutrient deficiencies, and anemia [4].Women of reproductive age, particularly lactating mothers, are among the most nutritionally vulnerable groups due to increased physiological demands during breastfeeding [5]. Inadequate maternal nutrition during lactation not only compromises maternal health but also affects the quality and quantity of breast milk, leading to poor infant growth and development [6]. Deficiencies in essential nutrients such as protein, iron, vitamin A, and omega-3 fatty acids during lactation have been linked to impaired immunity, maternal fatigue, and suboptimal cognitive and physical development in infants [4].

In sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, undernutrition remains a serious concern, accounting for two-thirds of the world’s underweight women and adolescent girls, and nearly 60% of global anemia cases [5]. In these regions, common indicators such as low body mass index (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) and short maternal stature (< 145 cm) reflect chronic undernutrition. In countries like Ethiopia, India, and Madagascar, the national prevalence of undernutrition among women often exceeds 20% [7]. Addressing undernutrition is also critical for achieving global development targets, particularly Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2, which aims to end all forms of malnutrition by 2030 [8].

In Ethiopia, undernutrition among lactating mothers remains a critical issue. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled national prevalence of undernutrition among lactating mothers at 23.86% (95% CI: 19.01–28.72) [9]. Regional studies report even higher rates in certain areas for example, a study in rural Dedo and Seqa-Chekorsa districts revealed that 40.6% of lactating mothers had a BMI below 18.5 kg/m² [10]. Identified factors contributing to undernutrition among lactating mothers in Ethiopia include low dietary diversity, poor meal frequency, food insecurity, low household income, limited access to antenatal and postnatal care, and harmful cultural practices such as food taboos [11, 12]. Although Ethiopia has implemented national programs such as the National Nutrition Strategy and National Food and Nutrition Policy, gaps in intervention coverage remain especially in urban and semi-urban areas, where localized data on maternal nutrition are still limited [13, 14]. In urbanizing contexts, women may face a double burden of malnutrition, balancing both undernutrition and rising risks of overweight and non-communicable diseases, which are often masked in national averages [15].

Jimma Town, located in the Oromia Region of southwestern Ethiopia, is one such area where the nutritional status of lactating mothers has not been well documented. Despite the presence of health extension programs and community-based nutrition initiatives, maternal undernutrition remains a challenge due to persistent socioeconomic inequalities, poor dietary practices, and limited health service utilization. Understanding the local context and associated factors is essential for designing effective interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of undernutrition and identify factors associated with it among lactating mothers in Jimma Town using Bayesian logistic regression analysis. The findings are intended to inform local policy and health programs, guide targeted nutrition interventions, and contribute to the broader goal of improving maternal and child health outcomes in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area

The study was conducted in Jimma Town, located in the Oromia Region of southwestern Ethiopia, approximately 352 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city. Jimma Town is one of the rapidly growing urban centers in Ethiopia and serves as an important administrative and health service hub for surrounding districts. The town is administratively divided into into several kebeles with an estimated population of 195,228 and about 40,450 households. Jimma Town has several public health facilities, including health centers that provide maternal and child health services such as antenatal care, postnatal care, immunization, and nutrition counseling, as well as referral linkage to Jimma Medical Center. Geographically, Jimma Town is located at 7°40′24.47″ north latitude and 36°5′4.95″ east longitude. The town has a subtropical highland climate (“Weyna Dega”) with moderate temperature conditions suitable for human settlement, with an average annual temperature ranging approximately from 20 °C to 25 °C [16].

Study design and study period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 20 to April 25, 2024 among lactating mothers residing in Jimma Town, Oromia Region, Ethiopia. A cross-sectional design was chosen because it allows the estimation of the prevalence of undernutrition and the assessment of associations between undernutrition and multiple sociodemographic, dietary, and health-related factors.

Population

Source population

All mothers with children less than two years living in Jimma Town Southwest Ethiopia.

Study population

All randomly selected mothers with children aged less than two years who were living in the selected kebeles in Jimma Town.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Lactating mothers who had lived in Jimma Town for at least six months were included in the study. Mothers who were severely ill at the time of data collection or had known chronic medical conditions that could affect nutritional status (such as chronic renal disease, cancer, or severe infectious diseases) were excluded. These conditions were identified based on self-report during interview.

Sampling techniques and procedures

Jimma Town comprises 17 kebeles, which are the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia. For this study, six kebeles (Jiren, Hirmata, B/Bore, B/Addis, Ginjo, and A/Mendera) were selected using a simple random sampling technique (lottery method). Selecting approximately 30% of the kebeles ensured operational feasibility while maintaining adequate geographic and socioeconomic representation. After selection of kebeles, a sampling frame was prepared for each kebele using updated household and maternal health registries maintained by health extension workers. These registries contain regularly updated lists of households with lactating mothers (defined as mothers with children under two years of age), which enabled accurate identification of eligible participants.

The total sample size (n = 423) was proportionally allocated to each selected kebele based on the number of eligible lactating mothers in each kebele (Nj). Within each kebele, a systematic random sampling technique was applied to select study participants. The total number of eligible lactating mothers across the selected kebeles (N = 7,392) was divided by the sample size (n = 423), yielding a sampling interval (k ≈ 17). A random starting number between 1 and 17 was selected using the lottery method. Subsequently, every 17th eligible household with a lactating mother was selected until the required sample size for each kebele was reached. If a selected household was unavailable after repeated visits, it was revisited later on the same day or the following day; persistent non-response led to selection of the next immediate household to maintain the sampling sequence. This multistage probability sampling approach including random selection of kebeles, development of complete sampling frames, proportional allocation, and systematic random sampling with a random start ensured that all eligible lactating mothers had a known and non-zero probability of selection, thereby improving representativeness across Jimma Town.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study is undernutrition status, which is measured using the Body Mass Index (BMI). It is classified as a binary variable where lactating mothers with a BMI less than 18.5 kg/m² are considered undernourished (coded as 1), while those with a BMI equal to or greater than 18.5 kg/m² are classified as not undernourished (coded as 0). This binary classification allows for the assessment of factors associated with undernutrition using logistic regression analysis.

Independent variables

The independent variables included socio-demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such as maternal age, maternal education status, maternal occupation, husband’s occupation, husband’s educational status, marital status, religion, monthly income, family size, and the sex of the index child. Maternal health, obstetric, pregnancy, and delivery-related factors were also considered, including gravidity, preceding birth interval, place of delivery, type of pregnancy, mode of delivery, antenatal care (ANC) visits, medical problems, tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccination, family planning use, and anemia during pregnancy. In addition, dietary intake and behavioral-related factors during pregnancy and lactation were assessed, including minimum dietary diversity of women, nutritional counseling, frequency of meals per day, family encouragement to consume more meals, consumption of additional meals, increased portion size of meals, use of prenatal vitamins or supplements, dietary restrictions (food taboos), and food cravings or aversions.

Operational definitions

Undernutrition: Undernutrition was defined as a Body Mass Index (BMI) of less than 18.5 kg/m² among lactating mothers. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Mothers with BMI < 18.5 kg/m² were classified as undernourished according to the criteria of the World Health Organization [17].

Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W): was assessed using the standard guideline developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Mothers were asked to recall all foods consumed in the previous 24 h. The reported foods were classified into the following ten standard food groups: [1] grains, white roots and tubers, and plantains; [2] pulses (beans, peas, and lentils); [4] nuts and seeds; [5] dairy products; [6] meat, poultry, and fish; [7] eggs; [8] dark green leafy vegetables; [9] other vitamin A–rich fruits and vegetables; [10] other vegetables; and [11] other fruits. Based on this classification, dietary diversity was computed by summing the number of food groups consumed. Mothers who consumed foods from five or more food groups were classified as having adequate dietary diversity (high MDD-W), while those who consumed foods from fewer than five food groups were classified as having low dietary diversity (low MDD-W) [18].

Household Food Security: Household food security status was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) developed by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance.

Food Taboo: Food taboo refers to culturally or traditionally restricted foods that lactating mothers avoid due to beliefs about their potential effects on maternal or child health [19]. In this study, food taboos were identified based on preliminary qualitative interviews and existing local literature. Commonly avoided foods included meat, eggs, milk, yogurt, certain vegetables (such as cabbage and pumpkin), legumes, and animal-source foods restricted during religious fasting periods (e.g., fish, meat, and dairy products).

Data collection process and instruments

Data were collected through home-to-home visits using a structured, pretested, interviewer-administered paper-based questionnaire and anthropometric measurements. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published literature and standardized instruments, including the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) guideline for measuring household and individual dietary diversity and the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) questionnaire [18]. The questionnaire consisted of sections on socio-demographic characteristics, maternal and child health service utilization, reproductive history, dietary practices, household food security, and feeding behaviors. Maternal dietary diversity was assessed using a 24-hour dietary recall method, in which mothers were asked to recall all foods consumed during the previous 24 h in accordance with FAO dietary diversity guidelines [18].

Anthropometric measurements were obtained following standard procedures. Maternal weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a battery-powered digital weighing scale, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Maternal height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wooden stadiometer with a sliding head bar while participants stood upright without shoes, with heels together and head positioned in the Frankfurt plane [20]. Data collection materials included a digital weighing scale, stadiometer, paper-based questionnaires, pens, pencils, and other field materials required for data recording and measurement.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was initially developed in English and then translated into the local languages, Amharic and Afaan Oromo, by language experts. To ensure accuracy, the questionnaire was back-translated into English by independent translators. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the total sample size in a kebele not included in the main study population to identify and resolve any ambiguities. Additionally, data collectors received one day of training on the study objectives, data collection tools, and ethical considerations. Throughout the data collection process, the principal investigators regularly supervised and checked the collected data for completeness and consistency in the field.

Data processing

Data collection was carried out by four trained BSc nurses under the supervision of an MSc holder. After collection, the data were thoroughly checked for completeness and consistency. The verified data were entered into EpiData software version 3.1, followed by meticulous cleaning and cross-checking to ensure accuracy before proceeding to data analysis.

Method of data analysis

The cleaned data were analyzed using R software version 4.2.2. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were computed to summarize participant characteristics. A Bayesian logistic regression model was fitted to identify factors independently associated with undernutrition among lactating mothers. Candidate variables were selected based on prior literature and their relevance to maternal nutrition. To reduce the risk of overfitting, a parsimonious model-building approach was used, and only relevant predictors were retained in the final model. Let Yi denote the nutritional status of the ith mother, where Yi=1 if undernourished and Yi=0 otherwise. The Bayesian logistic regression model was specified as: Yi∼Bernoulli(pi) logit(pi)=β0 + β1 × 1 + β2 × 2+⋯+βkXk.

Weakly informative Normal priors were assigned to all regression coefficients, including the intercept: βj∼Normal(0,10000), j = 0,1,2,…,k where the prior mean was set to 0 and variance to 10,000, allowing minimal prior influence on posterior estimates in the absence of strong prior information [21]. Model estimation was performed using four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains with 10,000 iterations per chain, including a burn-in period of 2,000 iterations and a thinning interval of 2. Posterior convergence was assessed using the Gelman–Rubin convergence diagnostic, with values of R̂ < 1.01 indicating satisfactory convergence. Visual diagnostic methods, including trace plots, autocorrelation plots, and posterior density plots, were also examined. Effective sample sizes (ESS) were evaluated for all model parameters to ensure sufficient posterior sampling precision. Model adequacy and fit were assessed using posterior predictive checks and comparison of deviance-based model diagnostics. Results were reported as posterior odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Variables with 95% CrIs excluding 1 were considered to have meaningful posterior associations with undernutrition.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 423 lactating mothers participated in the study. The largest proportion of participants were aged 26–35 years (41.1%), followed by 36–49 years (39.5%). Nearly half of the respondents were housewives (48.2%), and most participants were married (73.5%). More than half of households had fewer than five family members (53.7%), and 43.3% reported a monthly household income of 3,000–6,000 ETB. Regarding educational status, 31.4% of mothers were unable to read and write, while 18.7% had college-level education or above. The dominant religious affiliations were Orthodox Christianity (40.2%) and Islam (39.5%). Detailed socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1.

Prevalence of under-nutrition among lactating mothers

Among the 423 lactating mothers who participated in the study, a total of 91 individuals were found to be undernourished. This corresponds to a prevalence rate of 21.5%, indicating that more than one in five mothers were affected by under-nutrition. The 95% Credible intervals (CrIs) for this estimate ranges from 17.6 to 25.4, suggesting a statistically reliable measure of the true prevalence within the broader population of similar mothers. This finding highlights a concerning level of nutritional inadequacy among lactating women, a group with increased nutritional requirements due to the demands of breastfeeding. The high prevalence of under-nutrition may reflect inadequate dietary intake, poor dietary diversity, or other underlying socio-economic and health-related factors affecting maternal nutrition as presented in Fig. 2.

Assessment of MCMC Convergence

Several standard diagnostic methods were used to assess convergence of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations. In this study, convergence was primarily evaluated using time series (trace) plots, kernel density plots, and autocorrelation plots. The trace plots showed good mixing of three independently generated chains, with parameters fluctuating around a stable mean without evidence of trends or drifts, indicating satisfactory convergence (Fig. 3). In addition, kernel density and autocorrelation plots supported the stability and independence of the posterior samples, confirming adequate model convergence.

Factors associated with undernutrition among lactating women

The analysis applied weakly informative Normal priors for all regression parmeters and used Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. Model convergence was confirmed through examination of trace plots, autocorrelation plots, posterior density plots, and Gelman–Rubin diagnostic values (R̂ < 1.01 for all parameters). Effective sample sizes for all coefficients were adequate, indicating stable and reliable posterior estimates. After confirming satisfactory convergence and precision of the posterior samples, eleven variables remained significantly associated with undernutrition in the final Bayesian model, as indicated by 95% credible intervals that did not cross the null value. These factors included maternal age, family size, dietary diversity score, nutritional counseling, maternal employment status, monthly income, birth interval, frequency of breastfeeding, ANC visits, frequency of meals, and food taboos.

The results presented in Table 4 provide the posterior estimates adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% credible intervals (CrI) for factors associated with undernutrition among lactating mothers in Jimma Town, based on Bayesian logistic regression analysis. Mothers with low dietary diversity scores were 2.62 times more likely to be undernourished compared to those with high dietary diversity (OR = 2.62; 95% CrI: 1.37–4.60). Similarly, lactating mothers aged 15–25 years had a 4.3 times higher likelihood of undernutrition than those aged 36–49 years (OR = 4.3; 95% CrI: 2.85–6.37). Mothers in households with fewer than five members have 53% lower odds of being undernourished (OR = 0.47; 95% CrI: 0.29–0.70), suggesting that smaller households may experience reduced nutritional strain. Higher monthly income (≥ 6000 ETB) showed a strong protective effect, reducing the odds of undernutrition by 88% (OR = 0.12; 95% CrI: 0.05–0.24), highlighting the positive influence of economic stability on maternal nutrition. Mothers who received nutritional counseling had 41% lower odds of undernutrition than those who did not receive counseling (OR = 0.59; 95% CrI: 0.30–0.99). Similarly, employed mothers had 58% lower odds of undernutrition compared with unemployed mothers (OR = 0.42; 95% CrI: 0.21–0.76). Meal frequency was also significantly associated with maternal nutritional status. Mothers who consumed fewer than three meals per day had 5.44 times higher odds of undernutrition compared with those who consumed three or more meals per day (OR = 5.44; 95% CrI: 3.67–7.96), while practicing food taboos increased the odds by 6.39 times (OR = 6.39; 95% CrI: 4.32–9.39). Mothers who breastfed fewer than six times per day were less likely to be undernourished compared with those who breastfed six or more times per day (OR = 0.03; 95% CrI: 0.008–0.055). Similarly, mothers with a birth interval of more than two years had lower odds of undernutrition than those with a birth interval of less than two years (OR = 0.02; 95% CrI: 0.003–0.07) (Table 4). However, these strong protective associations should be interpreted with caution because of the cross-sectional nature of the study and the potential influence of residual confounding or sparse data.

Discussion

This study applied a Bayesian logistic regression model to identify factors associated with undernutrition among lactating mothers in Jimma Town, Southwestern Ethiopia. The analysis revealed that undernutrition remains a significant public health concern, affecting 21.5% of lactating mothers. Key risk factors included younger maternal age (15–25 years), low dietary diversity, larger family size, adherence to food taboos, lower meal frequency, and lack of antenatal care (ANC) visits. Protective factors were higher household income (≥ 6000 ETB), maternal employment, receipt of nutritional counseling, frequent breastfeeding (≥ 6 times per day), and longer birth intervals (≥ 2 years). The finding that younger mothers are more vulnerable aligns with prior studies [22, 23] indicating that adolescents and young adults often have lower nutritional knowledge, limited caregiving experience, and higher physiological nutrient requirements during lactation. Low dietary diversity and food taboos were among the strongest predictors of undernutrition, reflecting the importance of nutrient variety and highlighting how culturally driven restrictions can exacerbate maternal nutrient deficiencies. These results are consistent with previous research in Ethiopia [24–26] and other low-resource settings [27], which shows that cultural practices, food beliefs, and restricted diets contribute substantially to maternal undernutrition.

This study identified that 21.5% of lactating mothers in Jimma Town were undernourished, indicating a substantial public health burden with important implications for maternal and child health. The observed associations suggest that undernutrition is driven by a combination of behavioral, socioeconomic, and health service-related factors rather than a single determinant. Low dietary diversity was a significant predictor of undernutrition, which may reflect limited household food access, poor nutrition knowledge, and reliance on monotonous staple-based diets that fail to meet increased nutrient requirements during lactation. Younger maternal age was also associated with higher odds of undernutrition, possibly due to reduced nutritional knowledge, lower economic independence, and competing physiological demands from early childbearing.In addition, the practice of food taboos was strongly associated with undernutrition, suggesting that culturally rooted dietary restrictions may contribute to reduced intake of nutrient-rich foods such as animal-source products and micronutrient-dense foods. Infrequent meal consumption further indicates inadequate energy intake, which may be linked to household food insecurity or poor feeding practices during the postpartum period. On the other hand, antenatal care utilization appeared to play a protective role, likely because it provides opportunities for nutrition counseling, health education, and early identification of nutritional risks.

The prevalence of undernutrition found in this study (21.5%) is consistent with rates reported in several other Ethiopian districts, including Ambo district [25], DireDawa district [28], Yilmana Densa district [29], Nekemte district [30], Adama District [26], Dega-Damot district [31], and Mizan-Aman district [32] which showed the prevalence of under-nutrition among lactating women of 21.5%, 22%, 22.6%, 20.5%, 19.5%, 21.8%, and 23.7%, respectively. This similarity likely reflects comparable socio-demographic characteristics and living conditions across these regions, suggesting that undernutrition among lactating mothers remains a widespread challenge in similar settings. Higher than the findings of previous studies done in Indonesia (9%) [24], Dehradun, India (7.33%) [24], and Ibadan, Nigeria (5%) [27]. Similarly, another cross sectional study conducted in Nigeria on the nutritional status and dietary intake of lactating women in Umuahia showed that only 1.25% of the women were underweight [27]. As well as evidence from the “Uganda Demographic Health Survey 2016” on underweight and associated factors among lactating women in Uganda revealed that the prevalence of under-nutrition among lactating women was 8.2% [33]. However, the findings of this study were lower than the findings of previous studies done in the Haramaya district (47.9%) [34], Abala district in the Afar region (32.8%) [35], and Miesso district in Hararge region (30.3%) [36]. The reason behind this discrepancy might be the difference in socioeconomic status and sample size.

This study found that lactating mothers aged 15–25 years had higher odds of undernutrition compared to those aged 36–49 years. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia, including Kembata Tembaro [37], Dire Dawa [28], and Ambo [25] where younger maternal age was similarly identified as a significant risk factor for undernutrition during lactation. The consistency across studies suggests that younger mothers are a particularly vulnerable group in terms of nutritional status [38, 39]. The observed association may be explained by a combination of biological, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors. Biologically, younger mothers may still be in a phase of physical growth, which increases their nutritional requirements in addition to the demands of pregnancy and lactation, thereby placing greater strain on limited nutrient reserves. From a socioeconomic perspective, younger women are more likely to have lower educational attainment, limited decision-making power, and reduced economic independence, all of which may constrain their ability to access diverse and adequate diets. In addition, limited experience in childbearing and nutrition management may further contribute to inadequate dietary practices during lactation.

This study found that lactating mothers with low dietary diversity were significantly more likely to be undernourished compared to those with more diversified diets. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [31, 39, 40], which also identified dietary diversity as a key determinant of maternal nutritional status. The observed association may be explained by the fact that low dietary diversity reflects limited access to a range of food groups, leading to inadequate intake of essential macro- and micronutrients required during lactation. Dependence on monotonous staple-based diets may therefore fail to meet the increased nutritional demands of breastfeeding mothers, increasing their vulnerability to undernutrition. These findings highlight the critical role of dietary quality in maternal nutrition and are consistent with evidence from different settings in the country. They also suggest that improving maternal nutritional status requires not only increasing food quantity but also ensuring diversity in food intake. Therefore, targeted nutrition education and counseling for lactating mothers are essential, particularly in the early postpartum period. Such interventions should promote the use of locally available and affordable food groups while also addressing structural barriers such as household food insecurity and culturally influenced dietary restrictions.

This study found that lactating mothers who attended antenatal care (ANC) visits were less likely to be undernourished compared to those who did not attend ANC. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [37, 41], which also reported a positive relationship between ANC utilization and improved maternal nutritional status. The protective effect of ANC may be explained by multiple mechanisms, including early screening and management of nutritional problems, provision of iron–folic acid and other supplements, and repeated opportunities for health and nutrition education during pregnancy. These services likely contribute to improved dietary practices and better awareness of maternal nutritional needs during the postpartum period. Similarly, mothers who received nutrition counseling were less likely to be undernourished, highlighting the importance of behavioral change communication in improving dietary practices during lactation. Nutrition counseling may enhance mothers’ knowledge of balanced diets, appropriate meal frequency, and the use of locally available food sources, thereby improving dietary adequacy [39, 42, 43]. Economic factors also showed a strong influence on maternal nutritional status. Maternal employment and higher household income were associated with lower odds of undernutrition, likely due to improved access to diverse foods, greater purchasing power, and increased ability to utilize health services. These findings suggest that maternal nutrition is shaped not only by health service utilization but also by underlying socioeconomic conditions that determine food security and dietary quality.

This study also found that lactating mothers from smaller households (fewer than five members) had significantly lower odds of undernutrition, supporting previous findings that larger family size can strain household resources and limit access to adequate food and healthcare. Likewise, higher monthly income (≥ 6000 ETB) was associated with better maternal nutritional status, reflecting the role of economic stability in enhancing food security, dietary diversity, and access to health services [39, 44]. These results emphasize the importance of addressing underlying socio-economic factors such as poverty and household size in efforts to reduce maternal undernutrition. Policies aimed at improving family planning services, increasing household income through livelihood support programs, and strengthening social safety nets could contribute meaningfully to better nutrition outcomes for mothers.

In this study, a birth interval of two years or more was associated with lower odds of undernutrition among lactating mothers. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting higher levels of undernutrition among women with short interpregnancy intervals (less than 24 months) [3, 40, 44].The observed protective effect of longer birth spacing may be explained by the physiological recovery period it provides, allowing mothers to replenish depleted nutrient stores following pregnancy and lactation. Adequate spacing between births may also reduce cumulative nutritional stress, thereby improving maternal energy balance during the postpartum period.In addition to biological mechanisms, birth spacing may also reflect broader contextual factors. Women with longer birth intervals are more likely to have better access to family planning services, higher health awareness, and improved socioeconomic conditions, all of which may indirectly contribute to better nutritional status. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted within the context of these potential underlying factors. Overall, the consistency of this finding with previous studies highlights the importance of optimal birth spacing as a component of maternal nutrition. This supports the integration of family planning services with maternal nutrition interventions to improve maternal health outcomes.

This study found that lactating mothers consuming fewer than three meals per day had significantly higher odds of undernutrition compared to those who consumed three or more meals daily. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in India [23] and Ethiopia [29], which similarly reported that low meal frequency is associated with increased risk of undernutrition among lactating women. The observed relationship may be explained by insufficient daily energy and nutrient intake among mothers with infrequent meals, which is unlikely to meet the increased physiological demands of lactation. Inadequate meal frequency may also reflect household food insecurity or poor feeding practices, further contributing to nutritional deficits. In contrast, more frequent meals can help ensure a more regular intake of energy and essential nutrients, support metabolic stability, and reduce periods of energy deficit during lactation. These findings underscore the importance of promoting adequate meal frequency as part of maternal nutrition interventions, particularly in settings where food insecurity and dietary constraints are common.

The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for a comprehensive, multisectoral approach to address maternal undernutrition. Strengthening maternal health services is essential, particularly by integrating routine nutrition assessments, counseling, and supplementation into both antenatal and postnatal care. Promoting dietary diversity through community-based nutrition education using locally available foods and respecting cultural practices can play a critical role in improving maternal diets. Targeted interventions are needed for young and first-time mothers, including adolescent-friendly health services, peer support groups, and tailored counseling. Enhancing women’s economic resilience through income-generating activities, vocational training, and access to microfinance can improve household food security and reduce vulnerability to undernutrition [3]. In addition, integrating family planning services is key to encouraging optimal birth spacing and maintaining manageable household sizes. Communities should be actively engaged in addressing harmful practices such as food taboos through culturally appropriate behavior change communication strategies. Ultimately, tackling maternal undernutrition requires not only improvements in healthcare delivery but also broader investments in education, economic development, and women’s empowerment.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Self-reported dietary and behavioral data may be affected by recall bias, and anthropometric measurements may involve minor measurement errors despite standard procedures. In addition, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Although multistage sampling was used, some selection bias and limited generalizability beyond Jimma Town may still exist due to contextual differences in socioeconomic and cultural factors.

Conclusion

This study revealed a high prevalence of undernutrition (21.5%) among lactating mothers in Jimma Town. This study identified several factors associated with maternal undernutrition including, younger maternal age, low dietary diversity, food taboos, infrequent meal consumption, and lack of antenatal care follow-up. Protective factors included smaller family size, higher monthly income, maternal employment, nutritional counseling, breastfed fewer than six times, birth interval greater than two years and regular ANC visits. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of maternal undernutrition, driven by socioeconomic, cultural, dietary, and healthcare-related factors. Addressing undernutrition in this population requires integrated interventions that promote diversified diets, improve maternal healthcare utilization, challenge harmful cultural practices, support women’s economic empowerment, and encourage appropriate feeding practices during lactation. Strengthening community awareness and tailoring nutrition programs to local contexts will be essential to improve maternal nutritional status and break the cycle of malnutrition affecting mothers and their children.
