Antifungal activity and defense response activation by Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles against tomato root rot disease
Authors: Aly Derbalah, Ahmed Mohamed, Nehad El-Gammal, Warda Hussain, Ayman Omar, Saleh Alhewairini, Mahmoud Abdelfatah, Abdelhamed Elshaer, Hanaa Omar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fusarium root rot, caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici, is a severe soil-borne fungal disease that affects tomato production worldwide. This work evaluated Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) against F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycoperisici, which causes root rot disease in tomatoes, either as resistance inducers or by providing direct antifungal action. The direct antifungal activity of these nanoparticles was evaluated by assessing their ability to inhibit F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycoperisici growth in vitro and reduce disease severity under greenhouse conditions. The induction of resistance was examined by assessing the expression of two pathogenesis- and defense-related genes as well as phenolic compound content in treated tomato plants in response to F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici relative to untreated plants. The effect of Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs on the growth characters of tomato plants was also investigated. The growth of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici was significantly inhibited by the examined nanoparticles and fungicide compared to untreated control. The highest suppression of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici growth was 98.4%, 98%, and 97% for Co-, Ni-doped ZnO NPs, and fungicide, respectively. Root rot disease severity was significantly reduced in tomato plants treated with Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide as compared to untreated plants under greenhouse conditions. The results indicated that tomato plants treated with Co-doped ZnO NPs, fungicide and Ni-doped ZnO NPs exhibited higher expression levels of pathogenesis-related protein 1 (PR1) (5, 4.5, and 2.9-fold) and linoleate 9 S-lipoxygenase D (LOXD) (3.5, 3, and 2.7-fold) during F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici infection, compared to untreated infected plants, respectively. Tomato plants treated with Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs alongside fungicide showed significant phenolic content compared to untreated plants. Growth parameters of tomato plants significantly increased by treatment with Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide compared to untreated plants under pathogen stress. Based on our findings, the use of these nanoparticles may present an effective approach for the management of this disease. However, more research is needed to clarify their production costs, efficacy under real field conditions, and ecological effects.

Introduction

Tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum L.) are a major plant crop globally. Tomatoes are popular all around the world. Ripe tomato fruit is rich in nutrients, including vitamins and minerals. Furthermore, it is the top-ranked manufactured crop among vegetables1. It is consumed fresh or used to make pastries, mash, ketchup, and fruit cocktails2. Tomatoes are grown on various seasonal farms throughout the year in Egypt as one of the most significant plant crops, providing a larger income to small and large farmers than other plant crops3,4.

Fusarium root rot, caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici (FORL), is an economically important disease that affects tomatoes worldwide5. In conventional agriculture, chemical fungicides are the primary method for controlling phytopathogenic fungi. However, fungicide-based management strategies present several limitations, including environmental contamination, health risks associated with chemical residues, toxicity to non-target organisms, and the emergence of fungicide-resistant Fusarium strains6.

The reduced efficacy of already available disease control compounds/formulations and the emergence of novel pesticide-resistant pathogen strains have increased the need for alternative measures such as the application of NPs to control crop diseases. The main aim of NPs’ application in plant disease management is to act as an agricultural amendment with greater ability to control the spread of crop diseases in a sustainable way7. The use of nanotechnology to increase plant growth and yield as well as manage plant diseases is one example of how the search for control alternatives has increased recently8–14. Their small size, large surface area, and high reactivity have made them useful as nanofertilizers and in the control of various diseases9–14. Among used NPs in this field, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), which are reported to have properties such as growth promoters, bactericides, and fungicides15–19, resulted in a reduction in the severity of diseases. Moreover, ZnO nanostructures can also be doped with additional elements such as Ni and Co, which can help to improve their antimicrobial activity20–22. While both are effective antimicrobial agents, doping, the intentional introduction of small amounts of a different metal into the lattice of a host metal oxide, creates new electronic states, reduces the energy band gap, and significantly boosts the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), making the material more potent against plant pathogens23. Control of root rot of tomato by several nanoparticles such as silver, zinc oxide, copper oxide, alumina, and silica nanoparticles has been reported24–29. However, the use of metal-doped NPs for controlling the root rot pathogen of tomato has not been used before.

For effective plant pathogen management, control agents should not only suppress the pathogen directly but also activate the plant’s own defense mechanisms against disease10–14. Resistance induction in plants, which can account for plant disease control, includes induction of plant defense genes and induction of secondary metabolites such as phenolic content14,30–33. Expression analysis was carried out for several genes mediated by plant defense mechanisms in tomato plants34, such as LOX (lipoxygenases) in the jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthetic pathway and pathogen-related protein (PR1) in the salicylic acid (SA) pathway35. Phenolic compounds are among the most important and widely distributed secondary metabolites in plants, playing a crucial role in disease resistance across various crop species. These compounds are involved in essential physiological processes such as growth, reproduction, pigmentation, rhizogenesis, and pathogen resistance14,36–38.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the direct antifungal activity of Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs against Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici in vitro; to determine the extent of their ability to control root rot disease in tomato plants under greenhouse conditions; to identify their resistance induction mechanism against root rot pathogen by measuring the expression of pathogenesis-related protein 1 (PR1) and linoleate 9 S-lipoxygenase D (LOXD) as well as the produced free, conjugated, and total phenols in treated tomato plants compared to untreated ones; and finally, to examine the effects of the tested nanoparticles on some growth characteristics of tomato plants.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Thiophanate-methyl, with the trade name of Topsin-M 70% WP, produced by Sumitomo Corporation, Egypt, was used as a recommended fungicide for root rot control in tomato.

Fabrication of Co-doped ZnO and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles

Co-doped ZnO nanorod arrays were grown using a low-temperature and low-cost hydrothermal technique39,40. Typically, 0.25 M of cobalt nitrate nonahydrate [(Co (NO3)26H2O, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich)] was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water (DI) as a precursor for zinc ions. 4% by weight of cobalt nitrate nonahydrate [Co(NO3)2 6H2O, 99.997%, Oxford] was added to the zinc ion precursor as a precursor of Co ions. Then, 20 mL of 2.1 M potassium hydroxide, [KOH), 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich] was prepared as a catalyst and precursor of the oxygen source. Both solutions were stirred frequently for 10 min to achieve identical mixtures. The resulting mixture was transferred into a Teflon autoclave, where the temperature was maintained at 140 °C in an oven for 18 h. Finally, the obtained powder was collected after cooling down, after which Co-doped ZnO nanorod arrays were grown, employing a low-temperature and low-cost hydrothermal technique39,40. The formed powder was collected after cooling down and washed five times with DI and ethanol, respectively, to remove any excess of precursors. For Ni-doped ZnO nanorods, nickel nitrate nonahydrate was used instead of cobalt nitrate nonahydrate, following the above steps.

Screening of the ZnO and Ni-doped ZnO NPs against F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici under laboratory conditions

The antifungal activity of the three tested NPs (Co and Ni-doped) was against F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici under laboratory conditions. The efficacy of the tested treatments was determined as the percentage of inhibition in the growth of selected fungus relative to the control treatment. Five concentrations for each treatment were used. The required concentrations for the tested nanoparticles (0, 5, 10, 15, 25, and 50 ppm) and fungicide (1, 4, 6, 8, and 10 ppm) were obtained by adding the appropriate amount of stock solution used to 60 mL portions of autoclaved PDA cooled to about 45 °C. Four Petri dishes, 9 cm in diameter, were used as replicates for each concentration of each treatment, including the control. The control treatment was conducted without adding the tested materials. Each dish was inoculated in the center with a disk (5 mm in diameter) bearing the mycelium growth from the F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici culture (5-day-old culture). The dishes were sealed with parafilm to avoid the evaporation of volatile compounds. The dishes were incubated at 28 °C until the full growth (mycelium reaching the edge of the plate) of the control treatment was observed. The percentage of inhibition of radial growth of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici was calculated using the formula suggested by Vincent41. Greenhouse experiments

This experiment was carried out at the Gemmeiza Agricultural Research Station, Research Institute of Plant Pathology, Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt. This experiment used a completely randomized design with four replicates to ensure accurate data. Sandy-clay soil was sterilized using 5% formalin solution and then placed in 25-cm-wide pots containing 4 kg of soil per pot. Artificial inoculation was performed in pots one week before transplanting at the rate of 3% utilizing rice hull culture (w/w) and kept wet until transplanting. On the day of transplanting, tomato (var. Alisa F1) seedlings (obtained from BASF Agricultural Solutions Company, Cairo, Egypt) were dipped in fungicide and NPs solutions for 20 min and then transplanted into pots with seedlings in each pot. The fungicide (2 g a.i. per liter) and Co-doped ZnO, as well as Ni-doped ZnO NPs (25 and 50 mg a.i. per liter), were added to the pots as a soil drench ten days after transplanting and repeated three times. Each treatment was represented by four replicates, and each replicate consisted of five plants. For the control treatment, pots were infested only with the pathogenic fungus. Pots were kept in a greenhouse and watered as needed. The disease incidence of post-emergence damping-off and plant survival were recorded 45 days after seedling, as shown in the following equations42. Fresh and dry weights of shoots and roots of tomato plants were measured in both growing seasons.

Differential gene expression analyses using quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR was used to analyze the transcripts of two stress-responsive and pathogenesis-related genes (LOXD and PR1) in treated tomato plants compared to untreated ones. Tomato plants have been shown to respond to the F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici disease by demonstrating these genes. The stimulation of two defense genes (LOXD and PR1) in tomato plants treated with Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs as well as the recommended fungicide was assessed compared to the untreated infected and healthy plants in order to better understand how tomato plants respond to infection of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici. To determine the optimal sampling time, a preliminary time-course experiment was conducted for treated plants at 6, 12, and 24 h post-treatment. Based on this preliminary analysis, 24 h post-treatment was selected for subsequent gene expression analysis as it showed the highest induction of PR1 and LOXD. RNA was extracted from treated plants 24 h after the last treatment. Samples were directly frozen in liquid nitrogen after harvesting and stored at -80 °C until use. A total of 100 mg of tomato plants were extracted using the RNeasy® Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A no-template control (NTC) was included in all RT-qPCR assays to detect potential contamination and non-specific amplification; no amplification was observed in these controls, confirming the specificity of the reactions. RNA purity was assessed using spectrophotometric measurements, and only samples with acceptable A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios were included. In addition, RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, where intact ribosomal RNA bands confirmed the absence of degradation. A total of 1 to 2 µg of DNase I-treated RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Kit). cDNA was assessed by quantitative real-time PCR using the SYBR kit (Takara) in the Thermal Cycler Bio-Rad Real-Time System II (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Primer amplification efficiencies were determined from standard curves constructed using 5-point, 10-fold serial dilutions of cDNA. Only primer pairs with efficiencies between 90 and 110% and correlation coefficients (R²) > 0.99 were used for subsequent gene expression analysis. Amplification involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

Primers specifically for PR1 and LOXD genes were designed using primer-blast (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) and primer3 (https://primer3.ut.ee/) software as shown in Table 1. Primers were used in RT-qPCR analysis to detect fragments of ~ 80–200 bp in length using the Takara SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ in 25 µL reactions containing 60 ng template cDNA, 5 µL of 1 µmol/L of each oligonucleotide, and 12.5 µL SYBR premix Ex Tag. Amplifications were conducted with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 120 s followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and at 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curves were generated for each data point to verify the specificity of the PCR products. To analyze gene expression results, the delta-delta Ct method was employed. Average CT values were determined from triplicate experiments for each gene, calculated by subtracting the average CT value of the genes from the CT value of the Alaska alpha-tubulin gene. Tubulin served as a housekeeping gene to normalize cDNA concentrations.

Biochemical analysis

Phenolic content in tomato leaves was determined using the colorimetric method as reported by Snell and Snell43.

Sample preparation

Crude extracts were produced using 7 g of oven-dried tomato leaves with an ASE-200 (Dionex) extractor. Extractions were performed with 80% (v/v) aqueous methanol with shaking for 2 h at room temperature. Extracts were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min, and 1 mL of each supernatant was taken to develop the colorimetric reaction44.

Determination of total phenols

The total phenols were measured by boiling 0.03 ml of the sample in a water bath for 10 min after adding 0.5 mL of concentrated HCL. Following cooling, 2.0 mL of NaCO3 (20%) and 0.5 mL of Folin-Denis reagent were added. Distilled water was added to the mixture to bring the total volume to 10 mL. Using a spectronic colorimeter, the color density was measured at 520 nm after 20 min. A standard curve was created for catechol, and the total phenol content was estimated. Information is shown as µg catechol/ml of plant extract44.

Determination of free phenols

Free phenols were determined by mixing 2.0 mL of NaCO3 (20%) with 0.5 mL of Folin-Denis reagent and 0.03 mL of the sample. After adding distilled water to bring the total volume to 10 mL, the mixture was allowed to stand for 20 min. The color density was measured at 520 nm. The amounts of free phenols were calculated from the catechol standard curve and reported as µg catechol/ mL of plant extract45.

Determination of conjugated phenols

Conjugated phenols were calculated by subtracting free phenols from total phenols45.

Statistical analysis

Laboratory, greenhouse and PCR data were analyzed statistically using SPSS software version 15 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. The effects of season and the season × treatment interaction were initially examined and found to be non-significant (P > 0.05). Therefore, data from both seasons were pooled for subsequent analyses. Treatment means were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test at P ≤ 0.05. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).

Results

Characterization of fabricated nanoparticles

Figure 1 displays an XRD chart of ZnO doped with Co and Ni ions, revealing the orientation planes of (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201). Such peaks indicate the hexagonal structure for Co-doped ZnO nanorods, which is consistent with the card (JCPDS) # 36.1451)39,40. There were no additional peaks associated with metallic or impurities, indicating that the doped metal ions were mostly present in the ZnO lattice46. Figure 2 shows SEM micrographs of Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanostructures, respectively. It is evident that metal-doped ZnO is produced in hexagonal nanorods. The nanorods are aggregated into grains or shapes resembling coral bars. These outcomes support and validate the XRD findings.

Figure 3 displays EDX spectra of Co-doped ZnO and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles, demonstrating the presence of ZnO as the host material via distinct Zn and O characteristic peaks. Co and Ni dopants were observed at minimal concentrations, indicating favorable integration into the ZnO lattice without the development of secondary impurity phases. The effective substitution of Co²⁺ and Ni²⁺ ions within Zn²⁺ lattice positions is due to the similarity in their ionic sizes. These doped samples may exhibit favorable characteristics for enhanced uses like antimicrobial effectiveness.

Figure 4 illustrates DLS results for Co-doped ZnO (A) and Ni-doped ZnO (B) nanoparticles, where particle size distributed with comparatively good consistency. The average particle size of Co-doped ZnO nanoparticles was determined to be about 64–381 nm, while Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated a smaller average particle size of about 58.8–310 nm. Such results are coherent with the improved crystallinity shown in the XRD pattern. Zeta potential measurements indicate that Co-doped ZnO and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles have good colloidal stability as presented in Fig. 4C,D, respectively. The zeta potential value of Co-doped ZnO nanoparticles is found to be about − 22.16 mV, whereas Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles exhibited a higher negative value of about − 24.1 mV, representing good dispersion stability and lower aggregation tendency for samples. Such results demonstrate that Co and Ni doping advances nanoparticle stability and dispersion behavior, making them appropriate for biological applications.

Expression of genes linked to defense and pathogenicity in tomato plants treated with Co-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide under infection of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici

The expression of two biotic stress-responsive genes (PR1 and LOXD) in tomato plants treated with Co-, Ni-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide compared to untreated infected plants was illustrated in Fig. 6A,B. The results revealed significant (p ≤ 0.05) fold differences in the expression of targeted genes in tomato plants treated with Co-, Ni-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide under F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici infection compared to untreated infected plants. Specifically, PR1 expression increased with 5.0, 4.5, and 2.9-fold in Co-doped ZnO NPs, fungicide, and Ni-doped ZnO NPs-treated plants relative to the untreated control infected with the pathogen, respectively (Fig. 6A). Similarly, LOXD expression was higher in tomato plants treated with Co-doped ZnO NPs, fungicide, and Ni-doped ZnO NPs and with 3.5,3, and 2.7-fold, respectively (Fig. 6B). In tomato plants exposed to Co, Ni-doped ZnO NPs and fungicide, the relative expression of the PR1 gene was greater than that of the LOXD gene when compared to untreated plants. The highest expression of PR1 and LOXD genes was recorded for tomato plants treated with Co-doped ZnO NPs followed by the recommended fungicide and Co-doped ZnO NPs, respectively.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that the growth and the disease severity of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici were both greatly suppressed by Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs. This agrees with the findings of Verma et al.47, who reported that the growth of Fusarium spp. was also inhibited by doped nanostructures, such as Ag-doped ZnO47. Additionally, ZnO nanocrystals doped with Au, Co, and Fe demonstrated strong antifungal activity against other fungi such as Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus flavus, Rhizopus, and Penicillium48. Moreover, metal-doped ZnO showed antibacterial potential against Pantoea ananatis49, Xanthomonas gardneri50, and Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris51.

The mechanism by which Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles inhibit the growth of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici. is not well understood. Some believe that these nanoparticles have the potential to inhibit the growth of microorganisms. They may result in stress, which damages cells by acting as an imbalance. Additionally, they have the ability to damage cell membranes in a manner similar to shattering a house’s wall. They can also disrupt the cells’ ability to produce energy and other vital functions. They are even more able to adhere to the cells52–54. Additionally, doping Co and Ni to ZnO nanoparticles can alter the physicochemical properties of ZnO nanoparticles and potentially increase their biological activity21,55. However, this study did not directly check for reactive oxygen species generation, cell membrane damage, or how the nanoparticles are taken up by cells or other mechanistic endpoints, which may explain how these nanoparticles work. To determine how these nanoparticles attack F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici, more research is needed employing microscopy, oxidative stress biomarkers, and molecular analyses.

The higher efficacy of Co-doped ZnO NPs over Ni-doped ZnO NPs could be attributed to changes in electrical structure and defect structure caused by doping ions. According to studies, adding cobalt into a zinc oxide network boosts oxygen vacuole concentration and improves charge carrier separation, resulting in increased reactive oxygen species production and antimicrobial activity56–58. Reactive oxygen species can damage proteins and nucleic acids, disrupt fungal cell membranes, and ultimately prevent fungal growth. In this study, Co-doped ZnO NPs showed higher antifungal activity and increased expression of PR1 and LOXD genes and accumulation of phenolic compounds compared to Ni-doped ZnO NPs, which indicates more activation of the host’s immune system defense responses. These results suggest that enhanced disease control obtained from cobalt doped and nickel zinc oxide nanoparticles are likely due to enhanced antifungal activity of reactive oxygen species and enhanced stimulation of plant immune responses.

Our results revealed distinct expression patterns of PR1 and LOXD genes in tomato treated with Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs under F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici infection compared to untreated plants. In this respect, 24 h post-treatment exhibited peak expressions of PR1 and LOXD in our preliminary time-course analysis. Although defense gene expression is dynamic over time, previous studies in tomato have similarly reported maximal PR1 and LOX transcript accumulation at 24 h post-treatment59. This mechanism is in agreement with what has been stated by Stangarlin60, who clarifies that the resistance induction within the plant involved the enhancement of the activity of self-defense genes as a result of plant treatment with inducers to protect against pathogens. Systemic acquired resistance achieves long-term systemic resistance to various pathogens in tomato plants61,62 and can be triggered by several chemicals and nanoparticles63,64. These results are in accordance with several previous research studies that demonstrate that numerous chemicals, either in their normal usage or at the nanoscale, encourage resistance in tomatoes against P. infestans65 as well as against additional plant pathogens53,64,66. PR1 and LOXD are widely recognized as key molecular markers involved in salicylic acid (SA)-mediated systemic acquired resistance67, and these findings provide molecular evidence supporting the role of Co-doped ZnO NPs as promising resistance inducers for sustainable management of tomato root rot disease.

Our findings demonstrated that treating tomato plants with Co- or Ni-doped ZnO increased phenolic compound content compared to untreated plants. This agrees with the findings of Tryfon et al.68, who reported that total phenols in treated plants had significantly higher levels than the untreated control group. Accumulation of phenolic compounds at infection sites showed a correlation with the restriction of pathogen development, since such compounds are toxic substances to pathogens69. Also, the resistance may be increased by a change in pH of plant cell cytoplasm, due to the increase in phenolic acid content, resulting in the inhibition of pathogen development70. In addition, phenolic compounds may impede pathogen infection by increasing the mechanical strength of the host cell wall71.

Thus, plants protect themselves by using phytochemicals such as phenolic compounds, which function as antioxidants, to scavenge free radicals72. It was discovered that there is a correlation between the total phenolic levels and the 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging activity73. Also, Elgali45 explained that resistance to bean root rot caused by Macrophomina phaseolina and Rhizoctonia solani seemed more closely correlated with the high production of phenolic compounds in the resistant type compared to the susceptible one. Therefore, it may be suggested that phenol contents in the plant are major chemical constituents that could be used as indicators for resistance to tomato root rot disease and several plant pathogens. Finally, the integration of P4 (PR-1 family) and LOX genes in conjunction with a high level of phenolic compounds creates a potent, multilayered defense mechanism in tomatoes against F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici. This approach combines direct structural defense (phenols), systemic signaling (LOXD), and hydrolytic degradation of pathogens (PR1) to achieve significant disease suppression74,75.

Although high PR1 and LOXD levels show that defense pathways are activated, our experiment does not clearly separate the effects of resistance and direct antifungal action. This finding is important because Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles have been shown to inhibit F. oxysporum f. Sp. radicis-lycopersici in lab tests. So, the reduced disease severity we see in greenhouse tests could be from direct pathogen suppression or the plants’ defense system activation. Nanomaterials and other plant protectants have issues in separating direct antimicrobial effects from defense induction. The distinction is significant since Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles have shown inhibitory effects against F. oxysporum f. Sp. radicis-lycopersici in vitro, and thus the observed reduction in disease severity under greenhouse conditions could be attributed to both direct pathogen suppression and host defensive system activation, which uses PR1 and LOXD. Similar difficulties in distinguishing antimicrobial effects from defense induction have been seen in research using nanomaterials and other plant protection agents76–78. Thus, a combination of the two factors is probably responsible for the disease control seen in this study. To fully comprehend each’s efficacy, more research is required, using nanoparticles and pathogen-free nanoparticle treatment or by examining split-root systems or temporal analyses of defense gene expression before contracting an infection. These investigations will assist us in determining the relative importance of plant defense versus a direct antifungal activity on the fungus in the fight against this disease.

Our results showed that the application of Co- or Ni-doped ZnO NPs increased the root and shoot fresh and dry weight of treated tomato plants compared to the untreated control under pathogen stress. This is in agreement with the findings of El-Zohri et al.79, who reported that foliar application of ZnO-NPs at specific concentrations (25 and 50 mg/L) showed increased shoot and root biomass in tomato compared to ZnO-NPs-untreated controls. Also, in accordance with our results, the foliar spray of ZnO-NPs boosted up all growth parameters in wheat and peanut plants80,81. Furthermore, numerous findings show that, despite their extremely strong antimicrobial action, ZnO and AZO NPs are not significantly harmful to human cell lines82–84. Consequently, these nanoparticles’ minimal harm to human cells makes them very attractive for use in biomedical devices and food packaging, among other applications.

The results in this study revealed that the use of Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles significantly improved the growth of treated tomato plants compared to those that were infected and received no treatment. Although Co- and Ni-doped ZnO NPs significantly enhanced the plant growth when compared to infected untreated control in our study, the lack of healthy non-inoculated control limits the ability to differentiate between direct growth-promoting effects and growth recovery as a result of disease reduction. Therefore, the improved growth of treated plants in our study could be interpreted as higher plant performance under reduced pathogen pressure rather than as conclusive evidence of intrinsic biostimulant effect. Similar limitations have been reported in studies where disease suppression and plant growth responses occur simultaneously, making it difficult to separate direct physiological effects from indirect benefits associated with improved plant health78,85. However, plants that are healthy and free of pathogens should be used in these tests, and This will enable us to determine whether ZnO nanoparticles doped with Co and Ni have any characteristics that may help in plant growth other than their capacity to control disease.

Nanoparticle-based fungicides are becoming effective, eco-friendly options compared to traditional chemical fungicides. Despite providing better efficacy at reduced doses, their elevated production costs and difficulties in large-scale synthesis presently restrict their extensive commercial use compared to conventional fungicide options86. Conventional chemical fungicides typically possess reduced production expenses, established manufacturing techniques, and economies of scale. Nonetheless, nanoparticles typically necessitate high-volume use and regular spraying for effectiveness, resulting in higher overall operational expenses. Although nanoparticles (e.g., silver) may be costly, the total expense of nanopesticides can be lowered by employing smaller, more potent doses86. Green synthesis techniques (employing plant extracts or fungi) are recognized for their cost-effectiveness, environmental friendliness, and use of waste materials, presenting a more economical alternative to conventional chemical synthesis87. They are extensively scalable and currently manufactured on a large scale. The shift from laboratory-scale to industrial-scale production encounters major challenges in reproducibility, formulation stability, and standardization, which restricts scalability. Studies are progressively centered on creating economical, large-scale manufacturing techniques, including biosynthetic methods utilizing microorganisms87–89.

The high cost of production and a lack of large-scale, cost-effective manufacturing limit the broad use of nanoproducts for plant disease control, with only a few commercialized nanoproducts currently available. The use of Co- and Ni-doped ZnO nanoparticles for fungi control appears very promising. But before we start employing these nanoparticles on a large scale in traditional farming, we need to take into account concerning environmental impacts. We still have a lot to learn about what happens to metal-based nanoparticles in soil. This is especially true in terms of their longevity, their change, their movement, and their interaction with other living beings in the soil. ZnO nanoparticles have been shown in previous studies to influence soil microbial diversity, enzymatic activity, nutrient cycling, and non-target species90,91. Furthermore, zinc, cobalt, and nickel are recognized as important micronutrients; yet, excessive accumulation may negatively impact plant growth and soil biota85. Because this study focused on how successfully nanoparticles fight fungi, we did not investigate what happens to nanoparticles in the environment, how long they persist, or their long-term consequences on the ecosystem. More research is needed in real-life situations to determine how long nanoparticles last when they accumulate in living things, how they affect target organisms, and overall environmental safety77,90,91. This type of data is essential to developing risk assessments and ensuring that employing nanoparticles to manage disease in agriculture is both safe and sustainable. Additionally, the ecological nanotoxicity concerns of these materials need to be identified before approving the use of zinc nanomaterial-based agriproducts92–94.

Conclusions

As compared to untreated control, Co and Ni-doped ZnO NPs significantly inhibited the growth of F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici in a laboratory setting. Moreover, the disease severity of root rot in tomato plants treated with the tested nanoparticles was significantly reduced compared to untreated control plants grown in greenhouses. The expression of PR1 and LOXD genes confirmed up-regulation of these genes in tomato plants treated with Co-doped ZnO NPs under F. oxysporum f. sp. radicis-lycopersici infection compared to untreated plants. Treating tomato plants with Ni- and Co-doped ZnO NPs as well as fungicide significantly improved tomato growth characteristics. Considering the first outcome, using these nanoparticles could be a novel way to manage this disease. However, still there are some challenges such as evaluating their efficacy under real field conditions and ecological effects like bioaccumulation, phototoxicity, and large-scale industrial manufacturing of these nanoparticles.
