Nitrofurantoin-induced Toxicity. A Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Patricia P. Huelamo-López, Raquel García-Sevila, Ignacio Gayá-García-Manso, Alejandro Díez-DeLeón, María J. Soler Sempere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nitrofurantoin is an antimicrobial widely used in the treatment and prophylaxis of recurrent urinary tract infections. It carries a potential risk of pulmonary complications, that, while low (<1%), should be suspected in patients who develop respiratory symptoms during its administration, especially in subacute and chronic presentations. Given the non-specific clinical and radiological presentation, nitrofurantoin-induced pulmonary toxicity should be included in the differential diagnosis. We report the case of an 83-year-old female patient who, after several months of treatment with nitrofurantoin, developed progressive cough and dyspnea until she was finally admitted to our department for respiratory failure. Imaging studies revealed bilateral interstitial lung involvement. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion to avoid, as far as possible, progression to more severe pulmonary forms.

Nitrofurantoin is an antimicrobial agent traditionally used in the treatment and prophylaxis of recurrent urinary tract infections.1 It is associated with a potential, although rare (<1%), risk of pulmonary complications.2 Symptoms are non-specific in subacute and chronic forms, so a high index of suspicion is required for early diagnosis and treatment to avoid potential complications and progression to fibrosing forms. We report the case of an 83-year-old woman, a non-smoker diagnosed with asthma in her youth, who was admitted to our department for respiratory failure. She had a history of over 6 months of cough and progressive dyspnea associated with exertion, to the point that her basic activities were limited. For the past year, she had been receiving treatment with nitrofurantoin for recurrent urinary tract infections. At the time of admission, bilateral Velcro crackles were noted in both sides of the chest, and her baseline oxygen saturation in room air was 84%. Chest X-ray showed bilateral interstitial infiltrates predominantly in the upper lobes (Fig. 1A), which were not present in previous studies. High-resolution computed tomography of the chest showed bilateral, diffuse pulmonary involvement consisting of foci of consolidation associated with bronchiectasis, predominantly in the upper lobes along the bronchovascular structures, forming polygonal structures with perilobular infiltrates (Fig. 1B). The autoimmunity study was negative. No findings were obtained in several microbiological studies, including samples obtained by fiberoptic bronchoscopy, with the exception of Escherichia coli (100,000 CFU/ml) in urine culture. However, immunophenotyping of bronchoalveolar lavage showed lymphocytic alveolitis with a predominance of CD8+ cells (31%), a CD4+/CD8+ ratio of 0.55, and expansion of natural killer (NK) cells (16.7%). Lung function tests showed FVC 1.40 L (68%), FEV1 1.33 L (84%), and FEV1/FVC 95. Despite long-standing knowledge of the potential risk of pulmonary complications, nitrofurantoin continues to be frequently used in the treatment and prophylaxis of urinary tract infections.1 Nitrofurantoin pulmonary toxicity is uncommon (1%),1 but acute and chronic forms have been reported2, 3 and can be fatal if not diagnosed in time.4 Acute toxicity, characterized by fever, cough and rapid onset of dyspnea, is more common and typically presents within hours to 3 weeks of initiation of treatment. This form is associated with a hypersensitivity reaction. Chest X-rays may be normal or show bilateral interstitial infiltrates and pleural effusions, while CT scans often reveal bilateral ground-glass opacities.5 Chronic pulmonary nitrofurantoin toxicity may develop after 1 month to 6 years of nitrofurantoin treatment. It is thought to result from direct drug toxicity and may cause severe pulmonary disease with irreversible lung fibrosis, respiratory failure and death.4, 5 Clinical presentation is non-specific and making a diagnosis is more challenging.

The diagnosis of nitrofurantoin-induced pulmonary toxicity is based on a combination of clinical and radiological factors, duration of exposure to nitrofurantoin, and the exclusion of other causes, such as infectious, interstitial lung disease or autoimmune diseases.

Clinicians should maintain a high degree of suspicion in patients who receive this treatment and develop respiratory symptoms. It is essential to avoid associating the patient's symptoms exclusively with possible decompensation of underlying conditions, as may have occurred in our patient who had bronchial asthma. Nitrofurantoin must be immediately discontinued.

Although some cases of spontaneous resolution after withdrawal have been described,6 the response to drug withdrawal in chronic forms is more limited, and the role of corticosteroids remains controversial.7 Clinical observations suggest that corticosteroids may benefit patients with severe toxicity, but this approach should be decided on an individual case basis according to severity until strong evidence becomes available from controlled clinical trials.5, 8 Our patient required systemic corticosteroids due to the severity of the presentation and showed clinical and radiological improvement.

We would like to emphasize the importance of performing a differential diagnosis, as nitrofurantoin-induced-toxicity is a diagnosis of exclusion. In this context, cytotoxic lymphocytic alveolitis with a low CD4/CD8 ratio and an increase in NK cells in bronchoalveolar lavage samples have been associated with persistent cell damage in chronic forms,5 supporting the diagnosis.

In conclusion, the non-specific nature of respiratory symptoms in patients receiving nitrofurantoin calls for high clinical suspicion. Discontinuation of the drug should be considered while systematically excluding other potential causes, in order to prevent the progression to more severe pulmonary forms.
