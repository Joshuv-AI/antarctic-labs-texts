Effects of rice bran milling fractions on the eating quality, starch digestion properties, and flavor characteristics of steamed bread
Authors: Bingjie Chen, Xiao Wang, Shuqi Wu, Songheng Wu, Jinglin Zhang, Longshen Wang, Yongjin Qiao, Hongru Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three rice bran fractions (BF1, BF2, and BF3) were obtained through sequential milling. Increasing the degree of milling was found to significantly reduce the content of total dietary fiber, total phenolics, and total flavonoids in rice bran. Steamed bread (SB) was prepared by replacing wheat flour with rice bran at substitution levels of 5%, 10%, and 15%. Notably, substitution levels above 10% increased the hardness of SB, and imparted a distinct bran off-flavor. In contrast, 5% substitution enhanced the antioxidant activity and reduced the starch hydrolysis rate. Among all samples, the BF1–5% group exhibited the most pronounced effects, with the estimated glycemic index reducing from 97.53 to 81.97. Furthermore, the BF1–5% and BF2–5% groups showed significantly higher concentrations of characteristic volatile compounds (e.g., 3-methyl-1-butanol, 2-methylpropanoic acid, ethyl acetate). These findings indicated that substituting wheat flour with BF1–5% can significantly enhance the nutritional value, functional properties, and sensory quality of SB.

Introduction

Steamed bread (SB), a traditional Chinese staple with a history spanning over 1700 years, is primarily prepared using wheat flour, leavening agents, and water (Zhu, Xiong, Wang, & Ju, 2020). SB is rich in protein and carbohydrates and shows improved nutrient bioavailability due to the degradation of phytic acid during fermentation. Owing to its strong cultural acceptance, favorable nutritional profile, and convenience, SB is widely consumed across China. Compared with baked bread, SB has a lower processing temperature, which reduces the formation of harmful Maillard reaction products (e.g., acrylamide) and makes it a relatively healthier option. However, SB is typically produced using refined wheat flour. Thus, it exhibits a high glycemic index (GI) due to the loss of dietary fiber and micronutrients during processing, leading to rapid postprandial blood glucose responses. Extensive epidemiological evidence has linked the long-term consumption of high-GI refined grains to an increased risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases (Zhao et al., 2024). Consequently, incorporating whole grains, dietary fiber, and other functional ingredients to enhance the nutritional quality of SB and reduce its GI has become a key focus of food and nutrition research.

Several studies indicate that the incorporation of functional ingredients can effectively modulate the glycemic response of SB. For instance, Cao, Zhang, Guo, Dong, and Li (2019) reported that the addition of potato pulp reduces the rapidly digestible starch (RDS) content of SB from 46.98% to 31.40% and lowers the estimated glycemic index (eGI) from 87.81 to 72.79. Similarly, the addition of Chinese yam (Dioscorea opposita) decreases the eGI of SB from 75.4 to 68.1 (Zhu, Yuan, Zhu, Li, & Zhao, 2023). Rice bran, which accounts for 5–8% of all rice grains and consists of the germ, pericarp, aleurone, and subaleurone layers, is a major by-product obtained during rice milling. Rice bran is rich in dietary fiber, protein, minerals, vitamins, and bioactive compounds such as γ-aminobutyric acid, tocopherols, and polyphenols (Xie et al., 2025). The dietary fiber obtained from rice bran supports gastrointestinal function, reduces GI, and mitigates the risk of cardiovascular diseases and certain cancers (Tlais, Fiorino, Polo, Filannino, & Di Cagno, 2020). Meanwhile, its phenolic compounds exhibit protective effects against chronic conditions, including diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disorders (Tang et al., 2024a). Notably, rice bran also contains a relatively high content of lipids, which further contribute to its nutritional value. However, these lipids are highly susceptible to oxidation, which causes quality deterioration and flavor instability during storage and thermal processing, thereby limiting the application of rice bran in staple foods.

Although rice bran has been incorporated into pasta and baked products, its application in SB remains underexplored. The targeted utilization of differentially milled rice bran fractions in SB, guided by a systematic analysis of their functional components, could enhance the nutritional density and functional value of SB to meet the growing demand for healthier staple foods. Additionally, it could also promote the high-value utilization of this by-product. However, systematic investigations into how different milling fractions and flour substitution levels influence the physicochemical properties of rice bran and its performance in SB remain limited. Accordingly, this study aimed to characterize the nutritional and physicochemical differences among sequentially milled rice bran fractions. Additionally, this study also sought to evaluate their effects on the quality, starch digestibility, and the flavor of SB at 5–15% wheat flour substitution levels. The findings are expected to support the development of nutritionally enhanced SB and to promote the high-value application of rice bran in staple foods.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Rice bran (variety: Shangshi No. 19) was obtained from Shanghai Shengzhi Agricultural Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequential milling was performed using a grain flexible layered grinding device (Maidao Intelligent Grain Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to obtain different rice bran fractions. The milling procedure was as follows (Fig. S1): Fresh brown rice (20 kg) was milled for 1 min using the rice milling machine to obtain bran fraction 1 (BF1, 3.50 ± 0.12%) and milled rice 1. Subsequently, the milled rice 1 was processed for 1 min to obtain BF2 (3.38 ± 0.10%) and milled rice 2. Similarly, the milled rice 2 was processed for 1 min to obtain BF3 (2.40 ± 0.09%). The milling experiments and sample preparation were performed in triplicate. Each sample was sealed and stored at −20 °C prior to assessment. All physicochemical analyses were completed within 2 weeks to mitigate the interference caused by lipid oxidation and prevent the development of rancid off-flavors. Wheat flour was purchased from Yihaijiali Food Marketing Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and had protein, fat, and carbohydrate contents of 11 g/100 g, 1.6 g/100 g, and 73.5 g/100 g, respectively. Low-sugar, high-activity dry yeast was obtained from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China). 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was purchased from Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Meanwhile, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), and a starch content assay kit were procured from Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Physicochemical indicators of rice bran

The color parameters (L* [lightness], a* [red–green chromaticity], and b* [yellow–blue chromaticity]) of rice bran were measured using a CM-5 spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). The starch content was quantified using a commercial assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The protein content was determined via the Kjeldahl method, while the fat content was analyzed through Soxhlet extraction in accordance with the Chinese national standard GB 5009.6–2016. Finally, the contents of dietary fiber, including total dietary fiber (TDF), soluble dietary fiber (SDF), and insoluble dietary fiber (IDF), were determined using the enzymatic–gravimetric method in accordance with GB 5009.88–2023.

Analysis of nutritional components in rice bran

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The TPC was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method according to the procedure described by Pham et al. (2024). Briefly, 0.1 mL of the extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, followed by the addition of a 7.5% sodium carbonate solution (0.4 mL). The mixture was incubated in the dark at 40 °C for 40 min, and the absorbance was then measured at 765 nm. The TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per 100 mL of sample (mg GAE/100 mL).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

The TFC was quantified using a colorimetric method based on the protocol described by Wu et al. (2025). Briefly, 1 mL of the extract was mixed with 0.3 mL of a 5% sodium nitrite solution and incubated at room temperature for 6 min. Then, 0.3 mL of a 10% aluminum chloride solution was added, and the mixture was allowed to react for an additional 6 min after thorough mixing. Subsequently, 2 mL of a 1 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min. Absorbance values were measured at 510 nm. The TFC was expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents per 100 mL of sample (mg RE/100 mL).

Total sterol content

The total sterol content was determined using a phosphosulfuric acid–iron colorimetric method in strict accordance with the protocol provided in the commercial assay kit.

Preparation of SB

SB was prepared as described by Zhang, Zhang, Wang, Jia and Wu (2024), with slight modifications. Briefly, wheat flour was replaced with three rice bran fractions (BF1, BF2, and BF3) at mass ratios of 0% (control, CK), 5%, 10%, and 15%. These substitution levels were selected based on preliminary experiments and previous studies. Substitution levels below 5% generally result in limited improvement in nutritional and functional properties, whereas levels above 15% tend to cause marked deterioration in texture and overall acceptability. Accordingly, 5%, 10%, and 15% were selected as the representative low, medium, and high substitution levels to systematically evaluate the effects of rice bran incorporation on SB quality. The resulting formulations were designated as follows: CK (0%), BF1–5%, BF1–10%, BF1–15%, BF2–5%, BF2–10%, BF2–15%, BF3–5%, BF3–10%, and BF3–15%.

For each 200-g batch of mixed flour, 2.8 g of high-activity dry yeast and 106 g of warm water (38–40 °C) were added. The mixture was kneaded mechanically for 10 min to form a homogeneous dough, followed by primary fermentation at 37 °C for 1 h. The dough was then re-kneaded for 10 min for degassing, divided into 60-g portions, and shaped. After secondary fermentation at 37 °C for 15 min, the dough pieces were steamed in a boiling water bath for 20 min (Fig. S2). The steamed SB samples were cooled immediately to ambient temperature (23 ± 2 °C) for subsequent analyses of appearance, microstructure, and quality attributes.

Dough volume

The volume of dough after secondary fermentation was determined using the volume displacement method (Zhao et al., 2024).

Quality characteristics of SB

Color

The color parameters of SB were determined as described in Section 2.2. Using the L0*, a0*, and b0* values of the CK group as references, the total color difference (ΔE) was calculated according to Eq. (1):

ΔE = [(L*–L0*)2 + (a*–a0*)2 + (b*–b0*)2]1/2 (1)

Specific volume

The specific volume of SB samples was determined according to GB/T 35991–2018. The volume (V) of each sample was measured using a volume determinator, and the mass (m) was measured using an analytical balance. Specific volume (P) was calculated according to Eq. (2):

P = V/m (2)

Textural properties

SB samples were cut into uniform cubes (2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm). Texture analysis was conducted using a texture analyzer (TA-XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Surrey, UK) following the method described by Zhu et al. (2020). The operating parameters were as follows: P36R probe; pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 1.5 mm/s; trigger force of 5 g; and compression distance of 10 mm. To ensure reliability and reproducibility, six parallel replicates (n = 6) were analyzed for each sample. The mean values of hardness, springiness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience were reported.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Freeze-dried SB powder was mounted on a sample stage, sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold, and observed using SEM (TM400, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 1000× magnification to examine the microstructure of SB.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation of SB was conducted using a method adapted from Yang et al. (2025). A panel of 20 trained assessors (10 women and 10 men, aged 22–45 years) participated in the analysis. All panelists had received systematic training, including basic sensory theory, the standardized use of the 9-point hedonic scale, unified attribute descriptors for SB, and evaluation consistency testing, and had prior experience in the sensory evaluation of cereal products. The evaluations were carried out in a dedicated sensory laboratory under controlled conditions (25 ± 1 °C, uniform lighting, and odor-free environment). SB samples were cooled to 35–40 °C and presented to panelists within 1 h after steaming. All samples were coded with random three-digit numbers and served in a randomized monadic order. To minimize carry-over effects, panelists rinsed their mouths with deionized water before each assessment. A 9-point hedonic scale was employed to assess color, flavor, taste, external appearance, internal structure, and overall acceptability, where 9 indicated “like extremely” and 1 indicated “dislike extremely” (detailed scoring criteria are provided in Table S1). Samples with an overall acceptability score below 7 were considered sensorily unacceptable. Ethics approval was not required for this study, as it only involved a noninvasive sensory evaluation conducted by adult participants. All assessors volunteered for this study and provided signed informed consent. We ensured that the rights and privacy of all participants were safeguarded during the research.

Functional components and antioxidant properties of SB

TPC and TFC

Freeze-dried SB powder (100 mg) was subjected to ultrasonic-assisted extraction with 1 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol at 40 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 3000 ×g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected for analysis. The TPC and TFC were determined as described in Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2, respectively.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays

DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities were measured as described by Jiang, Li, Ma, Gao, and Yu (2025), with minor modifications. For the DPPH assay, 100 μL of the sample solution was combined with 100 μL of a 0.1 mmol/L DPPH/ethanol solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark at ambient temperature (23 ± 2 °C) for 30 min, and its absorbance was measured at 517 nm. For the ABTS assay, 100 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 400 μL of the ABTS radical working solution and incubated in the dark at ambient temperature for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm. Radical scavenging activity was calculated according to Eq. (3):

DPPH/ABTS radical scavenging activity (%) = [1–(As–Ab)/Ac] × 100% (3)

where As is the absorbance of the sample mixed with the DPPH/ABTS solution, Ab is the absorbance of the sample mixed with deionized water, and Ac is the absorbance of deionized water mixed with the DPPH/ABTS solution.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The FRAP working solution was prepared according to a method previously described by Ziarno, Cichońska, Kowalska, and Zaręba (2024). A 50 μL aliquot of the sample solution was mixed with a freshly prepared FRAP working solution (1.5 mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 539 nm.

In vitro digestion properties

The in vitro digestion properties of SB were evaluated according to the model established by Fan, Qin, Zeng, Li, and Liu (2024). The pH was adjusted using 1 mol/L HCl or 1 mol/L NaOH as required.

Oral phase (pH = 7): Ground SB (5 g) was mixed with 5 mL of simulated salivary fluid (containing 750 U α-amylase and 7.5 μmol CaCl2), followed by incubation in the dark at 37 °C, with shaking at 220 r/min for 2 min.

Gastric phase (pH = 3): The orally digested sample was combined with 10 mL of simulated gastric fluid (containing 4000 U pepsin and 1.5 μmol CaCl2) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C, with shaking at 220 r/min for 2 h.

Intestinal phase (pH = 7): The gastric digesta was mixed with 20 mL of simulated intestinal fluid (containing 4000 U pancreatic enzyme and 12 μmol CaCl2), followed by incubation in the dark at 37 °C, with shaking at 220 r/min for 2 h.

Aliquots were collected at 0, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min during the intestinal digestion phases. Each sample was immediately centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the glucose concentration in the supernatant was determined using the DNS method. The starch hydrolysis rate and contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) were calculated according to Eqs. (4)–(7):

Starch hydrolysis rate = (0.9 × Gt)/TS × 100% (4)

RDS (%) = (G20–G0) × 0.9/TS × 100% (5)

SDS (%) = (G120–G20) × 0.9/TS × 100% (6)

RS (%) =100–RDS–SDS (7)

Here, Gt is the glucose content (mg) released at time t; G0 is the free glucose content (mg) prior to enzymatic hydrolysis; G20 and G120 are the glucose contents (mg) released after 20 min and 120 min of intestinal digestion, respectively; and TS refers to the total starch content (mg) of the sample, determined by analyzing freeze-dried SB powder using the commercial total starch assay kit described in Section 2.2. Thereafter, the eGI was calculated according to Eq. (8):

eGI = 0.862 × HI + 8.198 (8)

Here, HI (%) is the hydrolysis index, defined as the area under the in vitro starch hydrolysis curve of the sample relative to that of an equivalent amount of white bread (reference standard), expressed as a percentage.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD analysis was performed using a SmartLab SE diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The instrument was operated at 30 kV and 10 mA, scanning from 5° to 50° (2θ) with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 2°/min.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra were recorded using a Nicolet iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Spectra were acquired over the range of 400–4000 cm−1, with 32 scans collected to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Flavor characteristics of SB

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were analyzed using a gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) system (FlavorSpec, G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). Briefly, 2 g of each SB sample (excluding the crust) was accurately weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial and spiked with 10 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone (8.0 mg/L in water), which served as an internal standard for quantification. After equilibration at 80 °C for 15 min, the headspace was automatically sampled and injected. GC-IMS conditions were adapted from Yang et al. (2025). The GC column temperature was maintained at 60 °C. High-purity nitrogen (≥ 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas with a programmed flow rate: 2.0 mL/min for 2 min, increased to 10.0 mL/min over 8 min, then to 100.0 mL/min over 10 min, and finally held for 20 min. The IMS drift tube was maintained at 45 °C with a constant drift gas (N2, ≥ 99.999%) flow rate of 150 mL/min. Data processing, including differential spectra, fingerprint plots, and principal component analysis (PCA), was performed using VOCal software by employing the Reporter, Gallery Plot, and Dynamic PCA plugins.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of triplicate measurements, unless otherwise specified. Statistical analysis was conducted based on one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp., USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Figures were generated using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., USA). Color images were converted to 8-bit grayscale and subsequently binarized using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Nutritional components of rice bran

The nutritional components (starch, protein, fat, dietary fiber, etc.) present in different rice bran milling fractions are presented in Table 1. As milling progressed layer-by-layer, the starch content of rice bran fractions increased significantly (p < 0.05). Among all the fractions, BF3 exhibited the highest starch content (40.45 ± 1.21 g/100 g), likely due to the gradual exposure of the starch-rich inner layers during milling. In contrast, the degree of milling had no obvious effect on rice bran protein. However, the crude fat content of the BF3 group was significantly lower than that of the BF1 and BF2 groups (p < 0.05), indicating that lipids are predominantly located in the outer milling fractions (BF1 and BF2).

The contents of TDF, IDF, and SDF decreased significantly as milling progressed. This trend could be attributed to the structural role of dietary fiber in the outer grain layers, where it maintains stability and stress resistance, shielding internal tissues from adverse environmental conditions. Notably, IDF accounted for 78.29%, 69.23%, and 87.54% of TDF in BF1, BF2, and BF3, respectively, confirming that the dietary fiber in rice bran predominantly exists in the insoluble form. BF1 (outermost layer) is enriched in pericarp and seed coat tissues, where the structural polysaccharides are predominantly insoluble cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. BF2 (middle layer) represents a fraction enriched in the aleurone layer; the cell walls of the aleurone layer contain considerable amounts of soluble dietary fibers, including arabinoxylans and β-glucans, which lower the IDF/TDF ratio (Nordlunda, Katinaa, Auraa, & Poutanen, 2013). It is worth noting that the TDF content of BF3 was far lower than that of BF1 and BF2. Consequently, despite the higher IDF/TDF ratio observed in BF3, the amount of IDF in this fraction was in fact the lowest among the three fractions. The elevation of this ratio can be mainly ascribed to the substantial loss of SDF during milling, rather than to any absolute increase in the insoluble fraction.

Table 1 also presents the distribution of different bioactive components (total phenolics, total flavonoids, and total sterols) across the different rice bran fractions. BF1 contained the highest TPC (0.39 ± 0.01 g/100 g), TFC (0.18 ± 0.01 g/100 g), and total sterol content (0.88 ± 0.06 g/100 g). The contents of these bioactive components decreased significantly (p < 0.05) from the outer to inner layers, consistent with the findings reported by Zhang et al. (2022). These results confirmed that most bioactive components are concentrated in the outer layers of rice bran, and their levels decline markedly as the degree of milling increases.

Dough volume

The dough volume after secondary yeast fermentation is a critical indicator of CO2 production and retention within the dough prior to steaming. Compared with the control group (CK, 132.33 ± 2.52 mL), the dough volume in ice bran-supplemented groups—except for BF2–5%, BF3–5%, and BF3–10%—was significantly reduced (Table S2). Across all rice bran fractions, the dough volume tended to decrease as the level of substitution increased. At the same substitution level, the post-fermentation dough volume was the highest after BF3 addition, followed by BF2 and BF1 addition. Among all samples, BF1–15% displayed the lowest dough volume (117.33 ± 3.06 mL). These differences could be explained by the compositional variations among the different rice bran fractions. As the outermost layer of rice bran, BF1 contained the highest contents of dietary fiber and phenolic compounds. Studies have shown that dietary fiber competes with gluten for water, reducing the water activity in the dough and suppressing both yeast metabolic activity and CO2 production. Meanwhile, phenolic compounds can exert inhibitory effects on yeast, thereby decreasing dough volume (Wang, Yang, Guo, & Zhu, 2021). In addition, an abundance of IDF can severely disrupt the continuity of the gluten network and weaken the gas retention capacity of the dough matrix (Espinales et al., 2022). In this study, BF3 exhibited the lowest dietary fiber content and the highest starch content, corresponding to the weakest water competition and sufficient availability of carbon sources. Owing to these factors, BF3 supplementation was theoretically the most conducive to yeast growth and gas production, resulting in better dough expansion.

Effect of rice bran on the quality of SB

Color

The color of raw materials typically determines the color of SB, thus influencing the sensory qualities of the final product. Compared with the control the SB samples supplemented with rice bran fractions exhibited distinct color differences (Table S2), namely, reduced L* values and increased a* and b* values. These alterations could be attributed to the natural pigments (e.g., carotenoids and phenolic compounds) present in rice bran. A smaller ΔE value indicated lower deviations in color compared with the control. Here, the color of SB samples was closer to that of the CK group when the substitution level was lower. Notably, SB fortified with BF3 exhibited the smallest color difference when compared to CK (ΔE = 1.82 ± 0.81), consistent with the intrinsic color trends across different rice bran fractions.

Specific volume and texture analysis

The specific volume of SB largely depends on the formation and expansion of the gluten protein network. Furthermore, it reflects the volume expansion capacity of the dough, its gas retention ability, and the bulkiness of the final product. During fermentation, yeast cells typically produce CO2 through anaerobic respiration, giving SB a soft, expanded, and honeycomb-like internal structure. As shown in Fig. 1A, incorporating rice bran significantly reduced the specific volume of SB (p < 0.05) from 3.37 ± 0.03 mL/g (CK group) to 1.96 ± 0.07 mL/g (BF1–15% group). Further analysis revealed a negative correlation between the rice bran substitution level and the specific volume of SB, with the specific volume decreasing progressively as the rice bran content increased. This phenomenon was attributed to the following key factors. First, the dietary fiber and other components of rice bran could dilute the gluten protein concentration in wheat flour and disrupt the gluten network, impairing the gas retention and expansion capacities of SB (Espinales et al., 2022). Second, the hydroxyl groups in dietary fiber could compete with gluten for water via the formation of hydrogen bonds, compromising gluten continuity and promoting premature gas cell rupture (Ouyang et al., 2024). Overall, the results of our analysis were consistent with the volume variations observed in dough after secondary fermentation. These variations were driven by multiple synergistic factors. On one hand, the damaged gluten network reduced the gas retention capacity of dough. On the other hand, the water competition caused by dietary fiber, the differences in nutrient availability, and the potential inhibitory components found in rice bran collectively modulated yeast fermentation efficiency and CO2 production. Among texture parameters, springiness and resilience are positively correlated with product acceptability, whereas hardness, gumminess, and chewiness are negatively correlated (Lv et al., 2025). In this study, as the substitution level of rice bran increased, the hardness, gumminess, and chewiness of SB increased significantly, while springiness and resilience showed a marked reduction (Fig. 1B-1F; p < 0.05). This pattern was consistent with previous reports on the effects of brown rice or oats on the textural properties of SB (Hsieh, Weng, Yu, & Wang, 2017). Specifically, compared with the CK group (hardness: 142.92 ± 13.30 g; gumminess: 122.01 ± 11.89; chewiness: 116.19 ± 11.36), the BF1–15% group exhibited the highest hardness (410.37 ± 29.18 g), gumminess (314.96 ± 18.81), and chewiness (274.64 ± 1.92). The higher hardness and chewiness reflected the denser internal structure of rice bran-supplemented SB and primarily resulted from gluten dilution. The gluten dilution hindered network formation, reduced gas retention, and created a compact texture. As a result, the springiness decreased from 0.97 ± 0.01 (CK group) to 0.86 ± 0.01 (BF2–15% group), and the resilience decreased from 0.49 ± 0.01 (CK group) to 0.36 ± 0.01 (BF1–15% group). This change could be attributed to the lower relative concentrations of key gluten components (gliadin and glutenin), impairing dough extensibility and expansion during fermentation while disrupting the structural continuity of gluten (Cui, Fei, & Zhu, 2022). From a sensory perspective, hardness and chewiness act as key texture parameters and affect consumers' preferences for cereal-based products. Here, SB containing 15% rice bran showed relatively high hardness and chewiness, which significantly compromised its sensory quality and consumer acceptance.

Multi-dimensional structural characterization

Fig. 2 presents the top-view, cross-sectional view, and microstructures of SB prepared using different rice bran fractions at different substitution levels. On SEM analysis, the CK group exhibited a relatively large specific volume, good morphological integrity, and a uniform cross-sectional structure. This was because starch granules are disrupted and fully gelatinized after steaming, subsequently interlocking with the gluten network to form a dense and continuous microstructure (Zhu et al., 2020). Notably, as the rice bran substitution increased, the micromorphology showed marked alterations: the overall texture became coarser, local crumb-like structures emerged, numerous irregular pores were formed within the matrix, and the originally continuous network became visibly fragmented. The primary cause of these structural changes was the competition for water between rice bran dietary fiber and gluten proteins, which limited the water available for gluten hydration. In vitro digestion properties

SB is a staple food with a high content of starch. Therefore, the starch digestion directly affects postprandial blood glucose levels following SB consumption. In this study, the addition of rice bran was found to significantly alter the starch hydrolysis kinetics, starch fraction composition, and eGI of SB. As shown in Fig. 5A, all rice bran-supplemented groups exhibited a significantly lower starch hydrolysis rate than the CK group (p < 0.05), with the extent of reduction varying among the different rice bran fractions. The final hydrolysis rates were 77.75% for BF1–5%, 83.58% for BF2–5%, 85.73% for BF3–5%, and 90.88% for the CK group. This trend was consistent with previous reports on the inhibition of starch digestion by ultra-fine purple corn flour, highland barley, and tartary buckwheat bran (Guo et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021).Fig. 5Effects of rice bran fractions on digestion properties, XRD, FT-IR of SB. CK: control group; BF1: outermost bran fraction; BF2: middle bran fraction; BF3: innermost bran fraction.Fig. 5

Effects of rice bran fractions on digestion properties, XRD, FT-IR of SB. CK: control group; BF1: outermost bran fraction; BF2: middle bran fraction; BF3: innermost bran fraction.

Among the different starch fractions, RDS is rapidly hydrolyzed by digestive enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract to produce glucose, which causes a sharp glycemic spike in the blood. Here, compared with the CK group (60% RDS), all rice bran-supplemented groups showed reduced RDS contents, ranging from 51% to 57% (Fig. 5B). This suggested that rice bran supplementation could attenuate the postprandial glycemic response induced by SB. In contrast to RDS, RS is not easily digested in the small intestine and reaches the colon, where it undergoes microbial fermentation to produce short-chain fatty acids and other metabolites beneficial for glycemic control and gut health (Ma et al., 2025). In this study, the RS content increased from 18% in the CK group to 24–27% in the rice bran-supplemented groups, further highlighting the role of rice bran in regulating glucose metabolism. In line with these findings, the in vitro eGI values followed the order: CK (97.53) > BF3–5% (91.43) > BF2–5% (85.84) > BF1–5% (81.97) (Table S3).

These effects could be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. First, the dietary fiber in rice bran, particularly IDF, has a high molecular weight and abundant hydroxyl groups, and it competes with starch for water during dough formation. This can limit starch gelatinization and enzyme accessibility (Wang et al., 2019). Second, dietary fiber can form a physical barrier around starch granules, impeding enzyme–substrate contact and promoting RS formation (Xiong et al., 2024). Third, the phenolic compounds in rice bran inhibit digestive enzyme activity through hydrophilic interactions (Martinez-Gonzalez et al., 2017). Additionally, they bind to starch via hydrophobic forces to form indigestible amylose–polyphenol complexes, thereby increasing the RS content and reducing overall starch hydrolysis (Assi et al., 2015). BF1, which had the highest content of dietary fiber and phenolics, showed the strongest inhibitory effect on starch digestion in this study. BF3, which had the lowest content of these bioactive components, exerted the weakest effect.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns and relative crystallinity of different SB samples are presented in Fig. 5C. The CK group displayed characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 13° and 20°, corresponding to a typical V-type starch structure. This pattern was attributed to the formation of amylose–lipid complexes, consistent with the previously reported crystalline features of starch-based staple foods (Tang et al., 2021). Rice bran substitution did not alter the position or intensity of these diffraction peaks, indicating that the crystal structure of starch remained unchanged after substitution. However, the relative crystallinity increased from 20.30% in the CK group to 31.22%, 28.74%, and 21.07% in the BF1–5%, BF2–5%, and BF3–5% groups, respectively. This increase was mainly due to the IDF in rice bran, which promoted the ordered arrangement of starch molecular chains during retrogradation, thereby generating more stable crystalline regions (Chai et al., 2025). Given that BF3 contained the lowest dietary fiber content (Table 1), the BF3–5% group exhibited significantly lower crystallinity than the BF1–5% and BF2–5% groups. The higher crystallinity in the latter groups caused the starch structure to be more compact, hindering granule swelling, reducing water absorption, and producing a rigid gel network, which increased the hardness of SB (Zhang et al., 2024).

Ft-IR

FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the functional groups present in the SB samples. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, all SB samples exhibited similar spectral profiles, with minor variations in the intensities and wavenumbers of the characteristic absorption bands. In FT-IR spectra, the broad, strong peak centered around 3440 cm−1 corresponds to O—H stretching vibrations, primarily derived from starch (Zhang et al., 2024a). Meanwhile, the absorption peaks at 1657 cm−1 and 1540 cm−1 can be attributed to the amide I and amide II bands, respectively, which are associated with the vibrational modes of amino acid residues in proteins and are commonly used to monitor changes in protein secondary structure (Chen et al., 2026). Additionally, the bands at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 reflect the ordered (crystalline) and amorphous regions of starch, respectively. The intensity ratio of these two bands (R1047/1022) is widely utilized to evaluate the short-range ordered structure of starch, with higher ratios indicating more organized molecular arrangements (Tang et al., 2024b).

As summarized in Table S3, the R1047/1022 values were 0.908 for the CK group, 0.925 for BF1–5%, 0.922 for BF2–5%, and 0.913 for BF3–5%, indicating that rice bran addition enhanced the local ordered structure of starch, with BF1 having the most pronounced effect. This improvement could be attributed to two key components found in rice bran. First, the IDF provided abundant hydroxyl groups, which formed hydrogen bonds with starch chains, limiting molecular mobility and promoting the formation of locally ordered structures during processing and retrogradation. Second, the phenolic compounds could intercalate into the amorphous regions of starch and stabilize the helical conformation of amylose, further increasing the compactness of the starch matrix (Ma et al., 2025).

Appropriate levels of organic acids, particularly acetic acid, can balance sweetness and enrich flavor complexity in fermented flour products (Tang et al., 2024a). In this study, the proportion of volatile acids increased from 9% in the CK group to 10–12% in the rice bran groups, primarily due to elevated levels of propionic acid and 2-methylpropionic acid, which confer cheesy and sour notes (Wang et al., 2022). Specifically, the content of 2-methylpropionic acid increased significantly (p < 0.05) from 6.11 μg/kg in the CK group to 8.30, 7.88, and 7.67 μg/kg in the BF1–5%, BF2–5%, and BF3–5% groups, respectively. This rise could be attributed to the rich protein content of rice bran, which—upon hydrolysis—supplied additional amino acid substrates. Meanwhile, endogenous bran enzymes further accelerated amino acid metabolism. Acetic acid, which provides a mild sour and vinegar-like aroma, also increased in abundance following rice bran substitution.

The ketones in SB were predominantly derived from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids during fermentation. As a ketone that imparts sweet and creamy flavors, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone (acetoin) is mainly produced extracellularly from α-acetolactic acid through a series of biochemical reactions (Song et al., 2024). As shown in Table 2, the concentration of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone did not differ significantly between the CK group (14.49 μg/kg) and the BF1–5% group (14.79 μg/kg) (p > 0.05). However, its levels in the BF2–5% group (12.41 μg/kg) and BF3–5% group (12.08 μg/kg) were significantly decreased (p < 0.05). 2-Propanone, which contributes strong fresh fruit aroma (Sun et al., 2025b), showed a concentration of 24.53 ± 0.74 μg/kg in the CK group. However, its levels were significantly elevated to 27.96 ± 0.51 μg/kg and 28.05 ± 0.01 μg/kg in the BF1–5% and BF2–5% groups, respectively. Aldehydes are characteristic oxidation products derived mainly from the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., linoleic and α-linolenic acids) and the Strecker degradation of amino acids such as L-phenylalanine (Sun et al., 2024). These compounds generally possess low odor thresholds and serve as important carbonyl compounds in SB. Here, the key aldehydes identified in SB were benzaldehyde (bitter almond, cherry, and nutty notes); (E)-2-heptenal-M (spicy, green vegetable, fresh, and fatty aromas); hexanal (fresh, green, and fruity characteristics); and propanal (green grassy scent). Compared with the CK group, only the BF1–5% group exhibited a slight increase in the total aldehyde content (6% vs. 5% in CK), while no significant change was observed in the other two groups (p > 0.05). Overall, the total content of aldehydes and ketones did not differ significantly between the rice bran groups and the CK group, indicating that rice bran substitution did not markedly alter the cumulative levels of lipid oxidation end-products in SB. Given the lipid content of rice bran, these results suggested that its antioxidant components may partially inhibit oxidation, thus maintaining more stable flavor characteristics in SB.

Esters are generally recognized to contribute sweet, fruity, and floral notes to food products, enhancing the overall harmony and pleasantness of aroma. During fermentation, these compounds are primarily formed through enzyme-catalyzed esterification reactions (dehydration condensation between carboxyl and hydroxyl groups) involving metabolically derived acyl-CoA donors and corresponding alcohol substrates (Li et al., 2025). As a representative flavor-producing ester, ethyl acetate plays a crucial role in shaping the characteristic flavor of SB. As indicated in Table 2, when compared with the CK group (2.77 ± 0.53 μg/kg), ethyl acetate levels increased significantly in the BF1–5% and BF2–5% groups, reaching 8.68 ± 1.18 μg/kg and 11.87 ± 0.28 μg/kg, respectively (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant change in these levels was observed in the BF3–5% group (2.56 ± 0.40 μg/kg; p > 0.05).

The production of alcohols and esters is largely driven by fermentative yeast metabolism, whereas aldehydes and ketones mainly arise from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and amino acids present in rice bran and wheat flour. Notably, we found that the partial substitution of wheat flour with 5% BF1 or 5% BF2 effectively increases the contents of specific volatile compounds in SB, including 3-methyl-1-butanol, 2-methylpropionic acid, acetic acid, and ethyl acetate. Given the reported odor properties of these compounds, it appears that rice bran can enhance mellow, sweet, and fermented aroma notes in SB, contributing to the distinctive flavor of rice bran-supplemented SB.

Discussion

The reduced starch digestibility of SB supplemented with rice bran was closely associated with the changes in starch structural ordering induced by compositional differences among the rice bran fractions. These alterations were likely a consequence of the synergistic effects of physical barrier formation, molecular interactions, and enhanced structural ordering. Specifically, the IDF enriched in BF1 competitively restricted water availability, thereby inhibiting starch granule gelatinization and swelling, which physically impeded the enzymatic hydrolysis of starch. Meanwhile, the physical barrier created by IDF around starch granules further limited the interaction between α-amylase and starch, reducing contact between the enzyme and substrate (starch) hydrolysis sites, which ultimately decreased the enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency (Ma et al., 2025). More importantly, the abundant phenolic compounds in BF1 not only interacted with amylose via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, promoting the formation of amylose-phenolic complexes and inducing the rearrangement of starch molecular chains into more ordered structures, but also bound directly to digestive enzymes such as α-amylase, altering their spatial conformation, inhibiting their catalytic activity, and delaying starch hydrolysis (Ma et al., 2025).

The structural changes induced by the components were highly consistent with the spectroscopic and diffraction date. Specifically, XRD analysis showed increased relative crystallinity, and FT-IR revealed enhanced short-range molecular order (increased R1047/1022). This highly ordered and compact starch matrix possessed fewer enzyme-accessible hydrolysis sites and showed lower water permeability, significantly impeding enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency and ultimately decreasing starch digestibility. In contrast, BF3 contained lower levels of IDF and phenolics, resulting in weaker water competition, insufficient structural reinforcement, and limited enzyme inhibition. The starch matrix was looser and contained more exposed hydrolysis sites, resulting in higher digestibility. This mechanistic pathway, from compositional differences to the regulation of structural ordering, provides a reasonable explanation for the observed changes in starch digestibility.

Notably, although BF1 was rich in dietary fiber and phenolic compounds—components often associated with bitterness and astringency—it provided superior flavor quality to SB when compared to BF3. This may be attributed to synergistic effects involving volatile composition, oxidation control, and matrix interactions. First, BF1 had higher levels of key desirable volatile compounds, such as 3-methyl-1-butanol, ethyl acetate, and acetoin, which impart malty, fruity, and creamy notes and may help mask undesirable flavors. Second, at the 5% substitution level, the phenolic compounds in BF1 exerted antioxidant effects via dual mechanisms. On the one hand, they scavenged the free radicals generated during lipid oxidation (e.g., alkoxyl radicals and peroxyl radicals) to terminate oxidative chain reactions. This inhibited the formation of typical off-flavor substances such as aldehydes derived from lipid oxidation. On the other hand, the phenolic compounds could chelate metal ions and prevent them from catalyzing lipid oxidation (Mira et al., 2002). Overall, this protected the SB matrix from oxidative deterioration, preventing the accumulation of bitter and astringent oxidation products. Third, the high content of dietary fiber in BF1, especially insoluble dietary fiber, regulated flavor release through physical matrix interactions. The three-dimensional network structure of the matrix enabled the selective adsorption and retention of certain undesirable volatile substances (Nie, Xue, Xiong, Yin, & Huang, 2024). Additionally, it acted as a slow-release carrier for aroma compounds and modulated their dynamic release profile during oral processing, allowing pleasant aromas to be perceived in a more persistent and balanced manner (Liu et al., 2023). Collectively, owing to the combined effects of the above mechanisms mean that BF1, which was rich in dietary fiber and phenolic compounds, did not produce the expected bitter and astringent taste at a supplementation level of 5%. Instead, it provided better overall flavor quality than BF3 by inhibiting oxidation and regulating aroma.

This study has certain limitations. The odor activity values of the identified volatile compounds were not calculated, and their correlation with sensory evaluation data were not analyzed further. Therefore, the contributions of individual compounds to overall flavor remain to be validated in future studies involving more comprehensive approaches.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the targeted application of different rice bran milling fractions in SB preparation and systematically evaluates the effects of varying rice bran substitution levels on eating quality, in vitro starch digestibility, and flavor profile. Our findings showed that sequential milling markedly reduces the dietary fiber, total phenolics, and flavonoids in inner bran fractions, confirming that bioactive compounds are concentrated in the outer layers (BF1). A critical substitution threshold of 10% was identified, above which SB quality deteriorated significantly, as indicated by reduced specific volume, coarse texture, and undesirable bran-derived off-flavors. In contrast, 5% substitution, particularly with BF1 and BF2, enhanced the antioxidant activity of SB and attenuated postprandial glycemic responses by modifying starch crystallinity and increasing short-range molecular order, without compromising sensory acceptability. Notably, the addition of 5% BF1 or BF2 increased the contents of key volatile compounds, such as 3-methyl-1-butanol and ethyl acetate, enriching mellow, sweet, and fermented flavor notes in SB. Overall, this study provides a strategy for the comprehensive enhancement of nutritional, functional, and sensory properties of SB. It offers a practical roadmap for the high-value utilization of rice bran and supports the development of low-glycemic, antioxidant-rich, nutritionally enhanced staple foods.
