Human health risk assessment of potentially toxic and essential elements in medicinal plants consumed in Zabol, Iran, using the Monte Carlo simulation method
Authors: Gholamreza Ebrahimzadeh, Abdullah Khalid Omer, Maziar Naderi, Kiomars Sharafi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedicinal plants (MPs) have long been used for their therapeutic properties in traditional forms of medicine. However, the presence of potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in MPs raises concerns about their safety, efficacy, and potential adverse effects on human health. The current study aimed to determine the level of potentially toxic and essential elements (PTEEs) in commonly consumed MPs in Zabol, Iran, along with their health risk assessments. To conduct the present study, 10 types of MPs widely used in Zabol, Iran, were selected, and 15 samples of each type (150 samples in total) were taken. Each sample was analyzed for the presence of various PTEEs using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) for Lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), chrome (Cr), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), aluminum (Al) and manganese (Mn); and Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) for arsenic (As) and mercury (Hg). Finally, to better comprehend the scope of exposure and its possible effects, the Monte Carlo simulation method is successfully applied to assess human health risks related to PTEs in MPs. Statistical analysis revealed statistically significant (P < 0.001) variations in PTE averages among MP types. Furthermore, all samples’ overall PTE mean concentration (range: 0.18 to 215.5 µg/kg) was below the World Health Organization’s (WHO) regulatory standards. Probabilistic health risks, including non-carcinogenic-target hazard quotient (THQ) for each element, total target hazard quotient (TTHQ) for all elements, and carcinogenic-incremental lifetime cancer risk (ILCR) for each element, and total carcinogenic risk (TCR) for all elements, were significantly lower than the acceptable limit for children and adults. Accordingly, it can be said that consuming MPs sold in Zabol is safe for children and adults regarding carcinogenic (ILCR/TCR = 10− 4) and non-carcinogenic (THQ/TTHQ = 1) effects. In light of the findings presented here, and to our understanding, the primary factor contributing to lower levels of PTEs in MPs in Zabol City markets is the cultivation of plants in nonindustrialized areas, separate from urban and industrial zones. This practice keeps them from environmental contaminations, including soil quality and water sources. It is recommended that it is essential to regulate the sources that enhance the transfer of PTEs and other harmful pollutants from surroundings to the soil and, consequently, MPs. It is also suggested that, like chemical drugs, MPs should undergo rigorous testing by quality control agencies before being made available to the market.Keywords: Health risk assessment, Medicinal plants, Toxic elements, Zabol, IranSubject terms: Plant sciences, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Due to their therapeutic characteristics, medicinal plants (MPs) have been used in various traditional medical practices for decades. These plants have a wide variety of bioactive substances that can treat and prevent the development of different diseases. However, potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in therapeutic plants have raised concerns regarding their safety and potential negative impact on human health1,2. Moreover, if MPs are not used under the supervision of specialists and physicians, they may worsen the patient’s condition3. From 1983 to 2009, more than two million cases of poisoning due to improper consumption of MPs were reported to the US Poison Control Agency, of which 45 cases resulted in death4.

The production and sale of MPs worldwide is a multi-billion-dollar industry. Unlike drugs, which undergo numerous analyses to determine their effectiveness and safety before entering the market, there is no such control over MPs5. A regulatory agency does not obligate manufacturers of MPs to certify that the products are safe and effective. In addition, the manufacturers of MPs are not obliged to provide warnings and side effects of MPs in their leaflets or instructions for use. Also, the mismatch of the names of MPs in different nomenclature systems causes mistakes in correctly identifying those MPs6.

PTEs are naturally occurring elements with high atomic weights and densities, such as Pb, Cd, Hg, As, Cr, etc. They can enter the environment through natural processes like weathering and volcanic activity; human activities such as industrial processes, using fertilizers, and pesticides can also contribute to their release into the soil, water, and air1,7–10. Exposure to these environmental sources allows MPs to absorb PTEs from the contaminated soil and water through their roots. Once absorbed, these elements can accumulate in various plant parts, including leaves, stems, and roots. The presence of PTEs in MPs can be attributed to several factors, such as the plant’s geographic location, the soil’s quality, and the surrounding environment’s contamination level1,10. The study by Mohammadi et al.11 focuses on the levels of several heavy metals (HMs), such as Cd, As, Cr, Ni, and Pb, in soils in an industrial zone in Neyshabur, Iran. The study underscores the negative impact of these HMs on the health of nearby residents, hence requiring regular monitoring and risk analysis to minimize these health hazards.

On the other hand, HMs pose significant health risks to humans. Their presence in plants, including MPs, is a significant concern due to their potential to accumulate in the human body over time. Furthermore, the adverse health risks associated with HMs can vary depending on the specific metal, the level and duration of exposure, and individual susceptibility12–14. For example, cognitive deficiencies and behavioral abnormalities are caused by Pb exposure, which is especially harmful to the neurological, reproductive, and skeletal systems. It can cause hypertension and kidney problems. Anemia and gastrointestinal problems can also be caused by chronic exposure15. Cd is highly toxic, and prolonged exposure can result in renal impairment, bone demineralization (osteoporosis), and heightened susceptibility to carcinomas, including lung cancer. Furthermore, it can induce cardiovascular and reproductive disorders16. Hg is a potent neurotoxin, particularly in its methylmercury form. It can cause severe neurological damage, cognitive deficits, and developmental delays in children. It can lead to tremors, memory problems, and cardiovascular issues in adults. Hg exposure is also associated with kidney damage17.

Pollution of soil and water environments with PTEs is a serious and expanding problem. The entry of toxic metals through human activities has caused the pollution of many soils18. PTEs exist in the soil in different forms, each with various mobile and absorbable forms. Excess amounts of metals in the soil can lead to more absorption by plant roots. One of the characteristics of these elements is their stability in the environment; PTEs cannot be decomposed through chemical or biological processes in nature like most other organic pollutants9.

Since HMs are non-degradable and cause physiological consequences in living organisms, they are considered significant even at low doses. Elevated levels of metals in living species, such as plants and animals, can lead to their entry into the human food chain, including MPs, and consequent health issues19. One of the essential results of the stability of PTEs is the large biological extent in food chains; due to this biological process, the amount of metals in the food chain can reach several times the amount found in the air20.

Nowadays, in Asian countries, the use of MPs by people to treat various diseases is increasing21,22. However, the safety of using these plants has been questioned due to their possible side effects and contamination with various pollutants, especially PTEs and pesticide residues23. MP pollution may be caused by soil, water, or air pollution. MPs may become contaminated when they grow on contaminated land, such as roadsides, near metal mines, or metal industries24.

Iranian people have a great desire to use MPs. This consumption trend is increasing, so it is necessary to evaluate side effects and measure unwanted pollutants, especially PTEs, in this type of plant. So far, limited research has been done on measuring PTEs in MPs, but in such studies, it is limited to measuring the concentration of metals25–37. In the studies mentioned above, the possible health risk of this type of pollutant through the consumption of MPs has not been evaluated.

The accumulation of PTEs in MPs raises concerns about the safety and efficacy of herbal and medicinal preparations derived from these plants. Prolonged exposure to PTEs can have toxic effects on the human body, causing damage to vital organs and systems. Moreover, PTE contamination in MPs can also lead to adulterating herbal products, compromising their quality and therapeutic value1. Recently, in some studies related to the health risk assessment of pollutants, the Monte Carlo simulation method has been used, which provides results with high confidence for decision-making in health policy38–42. In their study, Kiani et al.43 determined a complex correlation between HMs and colon cancer. Furthermore, the study revealed a positive correlation between elevated levels of Cd and cobalt and colon cancer, indicating that these HMs may contribute to an increased risk of cancer. Therefore, PTE levels in herbal products must be monitored and regulated to protect the safety and quality of MPs.

The WHO has set standards and limits for the amount of PTEs found in MPs and herbal products. MPs’ diverse pharmacological activities and slight side effects make them essential in traditional medicine. Unfortunately, PTEs can be absorbed from polluted MPs. Furthermore, the composition of compounds and the uncertain adverse effects make them not recommended for all categories of users. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first to comprehensively evaluate the levels of PTEs in MPs sourced from Zabol City and to conduct a detailed human health risk assessment associated with their consumption. While previous studies have only explored PTE contamination, our study uniquely focuses on the MPs in Zabol, offering critical insights into the potential health risks for local consumers and filling a significant gap in the existing literature on environmental health in this region. Therefore, this article aims to explore the issue of PTE contamination in MPs, highlighting the potential risks associated with their presence and the importance of quality control measures, as well as determining the probabilistic carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk of the discussed pollutants, including Cd, As, Pb, Ni, Cr, Hg, Fe, Cu, Zn, Al and Mn in 10 types of commonly consumed MPs was designed in Zabol, Iran. By understanding the sources, mechanisms of accumulation, and potential health impacts of PTEs in MPs, we can promote their safe and responsible use, ensuring their efficacy and minimizing the risks to human health.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Zabol is located in the northeast of Iran and is one of the cities of Sistan and Baluchistan province, at a distance of 205 km northeast of Zahedan. Zabol is 480 m above sea level and has a population of about 15,000,000 people (Fig. 1). First, a list of all MPs was prepared by referring to stores and shops selling MPs. Then, based on the sellers’ opinions, ten types of MPs with the highest sales volume and consumption were selected (Table 1). Next, the city was divided into five main parts (North, South, East, West, and Center), and one MPs store was randomly selected from each part of the city. Ten types of MPs and three samples of each type (30 samples from each store) were purchased from each store. According to the selection of five MPs stores in Zabol, 150 samples were taken to the laboratory to analyze their PTEE level.

Preparation and analysis of samples

The chemicals used throughout this investigation were of analytical quality and obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) was used to purify deionized water. After transferring the samples to the laboratory, the samples were washed twice with distilled water and then deionized water before being left to air dry at room temperature (22–25 °C) and in the shade. Next, the dried samples were powdered thoroughly in a Chinese mortar several times until completely crushed and homogenized. Finally, 2 g of each sample were weighed and acid-digested according to the ASTM method44. Accordingly, 2 g of the sample were added to a mixture of three acids, including 30 mL of 70% nitric acid, 10 mL of 70% hydrochloric acid, and 5 mL of sulfuric acid. Then, the mixture was placed on a shaker for 30 min and heated by a heater to obtain a clear extract of 3 mL from the above mixture. After cooling, the resulting extract was filtered through a Whatman No. 41 ashless filter paper to collect it in a volumetric flask (25 mL) and diluted to volume with deionized distilled water. With the use of Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, SPECTRO model, Kleve, Germany), ions of various metals, including Cd, Pb, Cr, Ni, Cu, Mn, Fe, Al, and Zn, were analyzed. Elements of As and Hg were also analyzed using atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS, Varian spectra 220 model, Australia).

The results were validated as part of a complete quality control program by comparing them to reagent blanks, duplicate samples, and certified international reference material of SRM1568b (rice flour, National Institute of Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg, USA)2. Based on the results, the limit of detection (LOD) for Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, Ni, Cu, Hg, Al, Fe, Mn, and As equals 0.04, 0.31, 0.44, 0.57, 0.82, 0.72, 0.08, 0.75, 0.55, 0.82, and 0.15 ppb, respectively. The limit of quantification (LOQ) values for the above elements equals 0.21, 0.32, 4.10, 0.28, 0.51, 0.12, 0.42, 50.0, 0.32, 0.25, and 0.42 ppb, respectively. Additionally, the recovery percentage for the assessed mentioned elements equals 97.2 ± 5.1, 98.5 ± 4.8, 95.9 ± 6.8, 96.1 ± 7.6, 97.4 ± 8.3, 99.2 ± 5.5, 98.1 ± 8.5, 94.3 ± 3.8, 92.5 ± 5.4, 97.6 ± 4.1 and 97.9 ± 8.4%, which indicated the stable measurement process and the accuracy of the results for all PTEEs investigated. It should be mentioned that all the tests were repeated three times as part of a complete quality control program.

Human health risk assessment

To evaluate the risk to human health from exposure to different PTEs through the consumption of MPs, the potential of non-carcinogenic risk (PNCR) and the potential of carcinogenic risk (PCR) was estimated according to the guidelines of the US Environmental Protection Agency45. CSF is the cancer slope factor, and the value of this factor for carcinogenic metals, including Cd, Cr, Pb, Ni, and As equals 6.3, 0.5, 0.0085, 0.91, and 1.5 mg/kg/day, respectively2,50–52. Since the CSF value is not defined for other metals, which are known as non-carcinogenic metals, the ILCR value was not calculated.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis approach

Oracle Crystal Ball software was used to run the Monte-Carlo simulation technique with 100,000 iterations in the EXCEL environment (Version 11.1.2.4) for uncertainty analysis38–40. To perform uncertainty analysis by this technique, the range of changes of independent parameters, including metal concentration (MC), the body weight of consumers (BW), and consumption of medicinal plants (IR), all possible states of the variables mentioned above to calculate the amount of estimated daily intake (EDI) ) was considered. This action was repeated a hundred thousand times. Then, the 5th percentile, 50th percentile, and 95th percentile values were calculated for THQ, TTHQ, ILCR, and TCR parameters. Finally, the obtained values were compared with the acceptable level of PNCR and PCR. In order to perform sensitivity analysis by this technique, the influence of each independent parameter (MC, BW, and IR) on the dependent parameters (THQ, TTHQ, ILCR, and TCR) was estimated in terms of percentage and finally ranked.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed statistically using IBM SPSS Version 16.00 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). At first, the Komologorov-Smirnov test was used at the significance level of α = 0.05 to determine the normality of data distribution. Due to the non-normality of the data distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used at a significance level of α = 0.05 to compare the average of a specific metal among different MPs and the average of different metals in a specific MP.

Results and discussion

Due to industrial activities, PTEs are mainly present in the Earth’s crust and lithosphere surfaces. These PTEs are released into air, water, and soil and accumulate in biota through ingestion, inhalation, and absorption11,13,19,53,54. Over time, PTEs have piqued the attention of researchers because of their association with diseases such as cancer, respiratory diseases, orofacial clefts in infants, autism, hepatitis, anemia, ulcers, and cardiovascular diseases14,43,55–57. Additionally, they are recognized for their toxic implications on the liver, kidney, and nervous system12,58. Numerous reports have suggested that metals are potent mutagenic agents and have been linked to the production of mutations, which eventually lead to the development of cancer in humans and animals54,59. The primary objective of this research was to estimate the level of various PTEs among the most popular Iranian MPs in the city of Zabol. For the convenience of the readers, instead of presenting the full name of each MP, their abbreviations (MP1 to MP10) were used, and the binomial name corresponding to each of these abbreviations was presented in Table 1.

The average of different toxic and essential metals related to each MP was highly variable and had significant differences from each other (P < 0.001). In addition, the average of each element among different types of MPs was significant (P < 0.001), so the average amount of Cu, Al, Zn, Fe, Mn, As, Cd, Pb, Ni, Cr, and Hg were determined to be 285.9, 2558.1, 1140.4, 3330.1, 221.0, 1.06, 0.49, 2.35, 32.67, 187.7 and 0.22 µg/kg, respectively (Tables 2 and 3).

In general, many environmental and non-environmental factors are influential in the pollution of soil and plants with PTEs, which include the increase in industrial activity, especially the chemical industry, metal extraction, increasing the use of pesticides, organic fertilizers, and sewage effluents in agriculture, usage of fossil fuels and air pollution2. The correlation between MPs and soil contamination relates mainly to the influence of polluted soils on the quality and safety of MPs. MPs, for instance, can assimilate HMs and other pollutants from the soil. Pharmacological usage of these plants can result in the transmission of toxic compounds to people, posing possible health hazards. The presence of soil pollution can negatively impact herbal plants’ growth, development, and chemical composition. Contaminated soils can diminish the effectiveness of the therapeutic qualities of these plants or potentially generate harmful substances11,19,60. Moreover, in their study, Kazemi Moghaddam et al.53 found high levels of Pb in both soil and vegetables in Neyshabur, Iran. They concluded that consuming these vegetables over a long period can increase the risk of cancer. Therefore, it is crucial to regularly and comprehensively evaluate the presence of HMs in soil, water, and food.

Soil and water pollution are the most important factors in plant pollution. The degree of soil pollution with PTEs depends on various factors, such as the amount of pesticides, chemical and organic fertilizers, weather conditions, and inherent pollution9,18,20. Therefore, preventing soil pollution through controlling the abovementioned factors effectively reduces soil pollution with PTEs. In addition, underground water sources in Iran are widely used for drinking water, farming, and industrial applications. PTEs can accumulate in living organisms, remain in the natural environment for extended periods, and cause hazardous effects58. Implementing acceptable irrigation methods can be a viable approach to mitigating the health hazards associated with PTEs.

The results of some previous similar studies are consistent with the present study and not consistent with others. Similar to the present study, the results of Habibollahi et al.2 in Zahedan, Iran, revealed that the average of PTEs in fifteen various kinds of MPs was highly variable and had significant differences. In addition, all samples analyzed in the mentioned study were contaminated with PTEs2. Barki Vandi et al.26 reported in Hamedan, Iran, that the average As, Al, Zn, and Cu in Melissa officinalis were 170, 13,900, 340, and 250 µg/kg, respectively. Also, those authors stated in the results of their study that the average of the 4 metals mentioned above in Borago officinalis was 0.16, 9.02, 0.27, and 0.15 µg/kg, respectively26. In another similar study in Hamedan, Iran, the research findings of Karimi et al.25 showed that the average metal arsenic in 4 commonly used MPs, including Viola odorata, Zataria multiflora, Ziziphus jujube, and Mentha pulegium, was equal to 0.033, 0.035, 0.018 and 0.029 µg/kg, respectively. In addition, the average Zn in the 4 MPs mentioned above was 1880, 920, 320, and 1750 µg/kg, respectively25. In the study conducted in Sanandaj, Iran, by Kohzadi et al.28, it was reported that the average metals, including Al, As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Hg, Mn, Ni, Pb, and Zn, are 2070, 0.79, 0.768, 136.66, 68.64, 2966.86, 0.24, 197.56, 70.52, 9.06 and 634.06 µg/kg, respectively28, which were almost similar to the results of the present study for most PTEs. Abdi et al.30 conducted research in Kermanshah, Iran. They found that the average concentrations of Cd in various MPs—including Mentha pulegium, Scrophularia striata, Fumaria officinalis, Stachys lavandulifolia, Matricaria chamomilla, Althaea officinalis, Cichorium intybus, Glycyrrhize glabra, Anethum graveolens, and Mentha spicata were 35.80, 71.86, 57.33, 90.73, 43.30, 44.90, 86.27, 13.70, 73.90, and 18.30 µg/kg, respectively. In addition, in the study mentioned above, the average Pb in the aforementioned MPs was reported as 12.90, 10.20, 11.07, 12.21, 20.73, 11.17, 10.91, 17.17, 9.91, and 10.61 µg/kg, respectively30. The study of Ziarati35 showed that the average metals including Pb, Cd, Cu, Ni and Hg in Ocimum basilicum were equal to 610 ± 7830, 150 ± 1050, 1510 ± 11,730, 50 ± 4260 and 50 ± 210 µg/kg, respectively. In addition, the concentration of the aforementioned elements in Foeniculum vulgare was equal to 70 ± 13,300, 20 ± 2040, 120 ± 9360, 980 ± 3190 and 30 ± 360 µg/kg, respectively35.

In addition to Iran, other similar research has been conducted in other countries, and based on the findings reported in them, the amount of PTEs reported in those studies was higher than the amount reported in the present study. In Serbia, Kostić et al.61 reported that the average Zn and Cu in some MPs were 36,920 and 18,980 µg/kg, respectively61. In Ethiopia, Baye and Hymete62 estimated that the average of Cu and As in some MPs equals 10,100 and 3030 µg/kg, respectively62. Furthermore, Subramanian et al.63 researched India and found that the average levels of Cu and Zn in certain MPs were 49,760 and 2750 µg/kg, respectively63. In another similar study, Meenakshi et al.64 reported that the overall average of As in 8 types of MPs equals 1150 µg/kg64. The research findings conducted by Okem et al.65 in South Africa showed that some MPs’ amount of Al and Fe was equal to 5.6 × 10 + 6 and 4.6 × 10 + 6 µg/kg, respectively65.

Reasons for various outcomes include variations in soil and air quality, the application or non-application of chemical pesticides and fertilizers, the irrigation or non-irrigation of areas associated with MPs cultivation, the quality of the irrigation water source, and the presence or absence of rainfall from land used for mechanized farming at the site where MPs are grown2,27–29,33–37.

The comparison with the standard level

The current investigation contaminated all analyzed samples with at least one toxic element. Furthermore, the concentration of each metal in all samples and the average level of each metal in all MPs were well below the WHO regulatory standards (Tables 2 and 3). All the MPs analyzed in this study grow naturally as wildflowers and, therefore, are unaffected by various environmental pollutants. Therefore, it is expected that the types of MPs that grow mechanized and under special conditions (in terms of irrigation, fertilization, and spraying) will most likely have higher levels of PTEs than those of MPs that grow naturally in plains and mountains2. Similarly, in the studies of Karimi et al.25, Abdi et al.30, Kohzadi et al.28, Keshvari et al.33, Jena and Gupta66, Kostić et al.61, Baye and Hymete62, , Subramanian et al.63 and Meenakshi et al.64, reported that the amount of PTEs estimated from the analysis of MPs samples was lower than the permissible level. While the findings of Ziarati35, Krejpcio et al.67, Okem et al.65 showed that the amount of some PTEs in some MPs were higher than the permissible level. As mentioned earlier, factors such as air quality, the amount of pesticides and chemical fertilizers used at the MPs’ cultivation site, the quality and standard of water for irrigation, the amount of rainfall from the land used for mechanized agriculture at the MPs’ cultivation site, and so on2,27–29,33–37.

All metals and MPs are treated equally regarding parameters such as consumer body weight (BW), intake rate (IR) of MPs, exposure period (ED), and average time (AT) over a lifetime. Because of this, the various EDI of each metal can be attributed to the varying average PTEs found in MPs and the different BW of MPs consumers (Tables 4 and 5).

Based on the results of the present study, it can be said that consuming any of the MPs is safe for consumers’ health in terms of possible carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic effects related to the intake of PTEs. The main reason for this output is the low content of PTEs evaluated; the reasons for the low level of metals were also mentioned in the previous sections. Avoidance of chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and contaminated irrigation sources for the growth of most of the MPs supplied to Iran’s markets reduces the concentration of PTEs in them. The mechanized method of producing MPs requires using pesticides and chemical fertilizers, which initially causes the transfer of PTEs to the soil and then to the plants9,62. This means that MPs produced in plains and mountains away from urban and industrial regions are the safest option when considering environmental contaminants, notably PTEs.

Research into the health risks posed by PTEs in MPs is limited; the results of this study were compared with those of several other studies and found to be consistent with some and not consistent with the results of others. For example, the study by Habibollahi et al.2 in Zahedan, Iran, revealed that the possible non-carcinogenic risk of 11 PTEs in 15 different types of MPs was below the acceptable level2. According to a study conducted by Barki Vandi et al.26 in Hamedan, Iran, the levels of THQ associated with Al, As, Cu, and Zn in 2 varieties of MPs, including Melissa officinalis and Borago officinalis were below permissible levels for both children and adults26. The findings of Karimi et al.25 showed that the value of THQ connected to Zn and As in 4 brands of MPs commonly used in Hamedan, Iran, including Viola odorata, Zataria multiflora, Ziziphus jujube, and Mentha pulegium for both children and adults age groups, is less than the limit was acceptable25. Similarly, Kohzadi et al.28 reported that the amount of THQ calculated for PTEs, such as Cd, As, Fe, Cu, Hg, Ni, Mn, Zn, and Pb in 16 brands of MPs supplied to the marketplace of Sanandaj, Iran was lower than the permissible value, while for Al and Cr, it was estimated to be more than the permissible level28. The main objective of the study of Meseret et al.68 is the evaluation of PTEs, including Cd, Cr, and Cu, in 6 types of commonly used MPs in Ethiopia. The results of the mentioned study showed that the amount of THQ related to Cr for two types of MPs for the adult age group was higher than the acceptable limit, while the amount of TTHQ from all three PTEs mentioned above was higher than the acceptable limit for the three types of MPs analyzed (TTHQ = 1)68. Adusei-Mensah et al.52 evaluated Cr, Mn, Ni, Cu, As, Cd, Pb, and Hg in 6 types of MPs supplied to the market of Kumasi metropolis of Ghana. The findings of the mentioned study showed that the amount of THQ associated with all PTEs (except Cu) in all 6 types of MPs investigated for both adult and children age groups was less than the acceptable level52. In addition to the studies mentioned above, the results of Geronimo et al.51 in Brazil showed that the amount of THQ and TTHQ associated with 8 PTEs in 6 types of MPs was below the permissible level51.

Much more limited studies have been conducted about the possible carcinogenic risk assessment of toxic metals in MPs. Habibollahi et al.2, in their study at Zahedan, Iran, showed that the ILCR and TCR levels of 11 PTEs in 15 brands of MPs were below the acceptable level2. While Adusei-Mensah et al.52 in Ghana reported that ILCR related to As and TCR related to carcinogenic metals in MPs for children and adults age groups were higher than the acceptable level52.

The difference between the findings of the present study and the findings of previous similar studies in different countries in terms of probabilistic carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk estimation factors, including TCR, ILCR, THQ, and TTHQ, may be connected to the different parameters affecting the indicators mentioned above, including the concentration of PTEs, the number and the type of PTEs under investigation, the consumption rate of MPs, the type of MPs, the body weight, the age group of the consumers, and the duration of consumption of MPs2,26,28,30,51,52,68.

Although the results of the Monte Carlo simulation method for uncertainty analysis showed that the 95th percentile (P95) of TTHQ and TCR factors for both children and adult age groups were higher than the estimated point level (Fig. 2), it still was lower than acceptable level (Figs. 3 and 4). Moreover, the results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the greatest contribution of the independent variables on the amount of possible carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk factors was related to the consumption of MPs, the concentration of metals, and the weight of consumers, respectively (Fig. 5). Therefore, it is suggested that in the first step, people should refrain from arbitrarily consuming MPs without a doctor’s permission. In the second step, MPs producers and health and pharmaceutical supervisors should sufficiently monitor the quality of MPS supplied to the consumer market.

Conclusions

The present work focused on determining and evaluating the human health risk index (HHRI) associated with consuming potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in 150 samples of 10 well-known MP brands frequently consumed MPs in Zabol, Iran. Based on the results of HHRI, including non-carcinogenic-target hazard quotient (THQ) for each element, total target hazard quotient (TTHQ) for all elements, and carcinogenic-incremental lifetime cancer risk (ILCR) for each element, and total carcinogenic risk (TCR) for all elements, were significantly lower than the acceptable limit for children and adults. Accordingly, it can be said that consuming MPs sold in Zabol is safe for children and adults regarding carcinogenic (ILCR/TCR = 10− 4) and non-carcinogenic (THQ/TTHQ = 1) effects. In this regard, the possible risk of carcinogenesis and non-carcinogenesis was estimated to be lower than the acceptable level. Therefore, it can be concluded that Iranian consumers’ consumption of MPs supplied to the Zabol market is safe. To our understanding, the primary factor contributing to lower levels of PTEs in MPs in Zabol City markets is the cultivation of plants in nonindustrialized areas, separate from urban and industrial zones. This practice keeps them from environmental contaminations, including soil quality and water sources. Furthermore, preventing from using chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and polluted irrigation supplies for the cultivation of the majority of the MPs brought to Iran’s markets decreases the levels of PTEs in them. However, it is suggested that the organizations responsible for the quality control of drugs, as they control the quality of chemical medicines with high precision and sensitivity, should also do the same for MPs released to the market.
