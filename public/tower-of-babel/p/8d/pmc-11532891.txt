Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor: A rare cause of intestinal obstruction: Case report
Authors: Nargis Maqbool, Khawaja Mohammad Babar Pal, Zeeshan Uddin, Fatima Safdar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importanceInflammatory myofibroblastic tumor IMT1 is a rare neoplasm with diverse clinical presentations, needing histological confirmation for diagnosis. The tumor often mimics malignant conditions, making accurate diagnosis challenging.Case presentationWe report a case of a 29-year-old male presenting with long standing recurrent abdominal pain, with more recent increasing frequency and associated weight loss. Introduction

An Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is an ultra-rare neoplasm composed of myofibroblastic and fibroblastic spindle cells partnered with inflammatory cells [1]. It has been classified as a neoplastic disease of intermediate biological potential given the low risk of recurrence and metastases [2].

A range of terms have been used to describe inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors including inflammatory pseudotumor, pseudosarcomatous myofibroblastic lesion and plasma cell granuloma. However, these names are synonymous, and all share the key pathological distinction: a dominant spindle cell proliferation with a variable inflammatory component [3,4].

IMT1 was first observed in the lungs and described in 1939. It was named IMT1 by Umiker et al [5] because it mimics a malignant neoplasm clinically, radiologically and histopathologically. Various theories of pathogenesis have been proposed; reactive, infectious, autoimmune and neoplastic, but the etiology still remains mostly unknown [6].

IMT1s have been found in number of places, but the majority occur in the abdominal cavity (75 % of cases), particularly in the mesentery, greater omentum and retroperitoneal space [7]. The presenting signs and symptoms vary depending on the anatomical location but are mostly associated with the tumor mass effect, swelling and local inflammation. We present a case of 29-year-old male with recurrent abdominal pain culminating in episodes of mechanical bowel obstruction, needing surgery. The final histological diagnosis of IMT1 was a surprise.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old male with no prior comorbidities presented to our outpatient clinic with history of recurrent episodes of colicky abdominal pain for 2 years. There were no specific precipitating factors. Over the last 6 months, episodes had become more frequent, accompanied by abdominal distension and intermittent constipation. The patient reported no nausea or vomiting. Due to a perceived post prandial association, patient had reduced his diet and lost 10 kg in weight.

His physical examination was essentially normal the patient appeared well, and general examination did not show any pallor, jaundice, edema or palpable lymph nodes. Chest examination had bilateral equal air entry with no added sounds, heart sounds were normal with no murmur. Abdominal examination showed mild symmetric distension of abdomen, with no tenderness. The percussion note was tympanic and bowel sounds were exaggerated.

Given the duration of symptoms, the patient had undergone multiple evaluations at different medical facilities. Both upper and lower GI endoscopies were normal and base line laboratory tests including complete blood counts, creatinine & electrolytes were within normal limits (Table 1). An abdominal ultrasound revealed mild distention of bowel loops but did not provide a conclusive diagnosis. A contrast enhanced CT scan revealed incomplete mechanical small bowel obstruction caused by a complex, multilobulated enhancing soft tissue mass, involving the mid small bowel. Enteroliths, proximal to obstruction and multiple enlarged mesenteric and para-aortic lymph nodes were reported. (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Radiological guided biopsy of the mass was denied, due to the absence of a safe window.

Due to worsening of symptoms and subacute intestinal obstruction, the patient was advised an elective surgical resection. However, he later presented to the emergency room with two episodes of vomiting, absolute constipation and worsening abdominal pain. He was admitted and initially managed with nasogastric decompression, a Foley catheter and was kept nil per oral before undergoing laparotomy with small bowel resection and anastomosis. Intraoperative findings included a 6x5cm multilobulated mass causing luminal narrowing at 130 cm proximal to Ileocecal junction, 100 ml of peritoneal fluid, and multiple enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes. (Fig. 3). Preoperatively, the patient received IV cefazolin and metronidazole for antibiotic prophylaxis.

Intraoperative picture demonstrating multilobulated small bowel mass resulting in intestinal obstruction.

The patient's postoperative course was closely monitored. Table 1 summarizes the preoperative and postoperative laboratory results. He received intravenous antibiotics for 24 h, followed by gradual progression of diet as his bowel function returned. The patient was started on a full liquid diet on postoperative day 4 and had his first bowel movement on postoperative day 5. Heparin 5000 IU was administered subcutaneously three times daily for thromboprophylaxis, and pain was managed with patient controlled analgesia (PCIA). In addition, patient used an incentive spirometer, wore DVT stockings and started ambulating on first postoperative day, according to the standard postoperative protocols. He was discharged tolerating oral intake on 6th post operative day.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor involving the serosal aspect and muscularis propria, (Fig. 4, Fig. 5) characterized by myofibroblast cells in an edematous myxoid background with abundant blood vessels and interspersed inflammatory infiltrate, (Fig. 6a & b) staining weakly for Alk protein2 (Fig. 7) and cytokeratin AE1/AE3. The tumor cells were negative for immunohistochemical stains DOG-1 and CD117 which ruled out the possibility of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). Lymph nodes recovered during surgery were found to be benign and reactive. Ascitic fluid cytology was negative for malignancy. Discussion

IMT1 is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by a proliferation of myofibroblastic spindle cells interspersed with varying degrees of inflammatory cells [4]. Originally described in the lungs, IMT can arise at virtually any anatomical site, although abdominal involvement, particularly within the mesentery and retroperitoneum, accounts for the majority of cases, the involvement of small bowel is extremely rare [9]. The pathogenesis of IMT remains incompletely understood, with the proposed mechanisms including reactive, infectious, autoimmune, or neoplastic processes [10]. Despite its classification as a benign or locally aggressive tumor IMT can exhibit unpredictable behavior necessitating careful diagnosis and management [11].

Our case highlights several noteworthy aspects of IMT. Firstly, the presentation of IMT as acute on chronic obstruction in a young adult is rare and underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with this uncommon tumor. Cianci et al. highlights the challenging nature of abdominal IMTs, which often present with non-specific symptoms such as fever, weight loss, abdominal pain and, as observed in our case, rarely intestinal obstruction [12]. The development of enteroliths, pointing towards chronic obstruction [13], adds to uniqueness of this case, as such findings have not yet been reported in existing literature outside of appendiceal involvement of IMT [14].

Imaging modalities, particularly contrast enhanced CT play a pivotal role in diagnosing IMT by delineating the extent, characteristics and local effects of the tumor [15]. Cantara et al., in their review, emphasized the role of contrast enhanced CT in gastrointestinal IMT, typically revealing well defined, hypodense, heterogeneous masses with variable enhancement [16].

However, the definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation. Our case exhibited multiple enhancing soft tissue density lesions in the pelvis, showing peripheral enhancing walls and central hypodensity causing mass effect on distal ileum, resulting in proximal dilatation of small bowel loops. Multiple enhancing periaortic and mesenteric lymph nodes were noted.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for localized IMT, [17] providing both diagnostic clarification and curative intent. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen is essential for confirming the diagnosis, characterized by spindle cell proliferation with a variable inflammatory infiltrate as reported in our case as well. Immunohistochemical staining, particularly for anaplastic lymphoma kinase ALK protein, [18] is often positive in IMT and can aid in confirming the diagnosis and guiding therapeutic decisions. An important clinical and histological differential of IMT is gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) which is relatively more common than IMT in the abdominal cavity but carries completely different therapeutic and prognostic implications [19]. This further highlights the importance of histopathological evaluation of surgical specimens from a reputable laboratory which is equipped with necessary immunohistochemical markers.

In cases of unresectable or metastatic IMT, therapeutic options beyond surgery include targeted therapies, such as ALK inhibitors, which have shown promising outcomes in select patients [20]. However, due to the rarity of IMT, optimal treatment strategies remain to be defined, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary approaches involving oncologists, surgeons and pathologists.

Prognostic factors in IMT are variable with the majority of cases demonstrating indolent behavior and low rates of recurrence and metastasis following complete surgical resection. However careful long-term follow-up is warranted due to potential for late recurrence or malignant transformation in a subset of cases.

Conclusion

IMT continues to be a diagnostic rarity and is usually a histological surprise for clinicians. Radiological assessment with a contrast enhanced CT may be the earliest clue, though often the diagnostic features are more obvious in retrospect.

Further studies are warranted to elucidate the pathogenesis and optimal management strategies for inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors.
