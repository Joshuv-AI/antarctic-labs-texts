Seasonal controls on groundwater irrigation quality in a semi-arid granitic watershed of Telangana, India: an integrated IWQI-GIS-multivariate approach
Authors: Mamatha Ullengula, Ratnakar Dhakate, N Subba Rao, Pradeep Kumar Badapalli, K V Suryabhagavan, Sakram Gugulothu, Nandita Mahanta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroundwater is a vital resource for irrigation in arid and semi-arid regions. This study evaluates groundwater suitability for irrigation in the granitic watershed of Dilawarpur Mandal, Nirmal District, Telangana, India, using 61 groundwater samples collected during pre- and post-monsoon seasons. Major physicochemical parameters (pH, EC, TDS, Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, HCO3−, Cl−, SO42−, and F−) were analysed. Irrigation suitability was assessed using the Irrigation Water Quality Index (IWQI) in conjunction with EC, %Na⁺, SAR, SSP, KR, MHR, PI, and RSC. Multivariate statistical techniques, including Pearson correlation, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA), and Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), were applied to identify dominant hydrochemical controls and evaluate predictive relationships. Although elevated EC indicates moderate to high salinity at several locations, most groundwater samples are suitable for irrigation. IWQI results show that 64% of pre-monsoon and 66% of post-monsoon samples fall within excellent to good categories, with post-monsoon improvement attributed to monsoonal dilution of dissolved salts and Na+. Spatial IWQI analysis indicates an expansion of excellent-quality zones from 85% in pre-monsoon to 96% in post-monsoon season. Correlation analysis, PCA, and HCA consistently identify Na+-related indices and salinity as the primary controls on irrigation suitability, while carbonate balance and soil permeability act as secondary modifiers. PCA explains 92.21% and 87.57% of the total variance during pre- and post-monsoon seasons, respectively. MLR reveals stronger and more predictable hydrochemical control during the pre-monsoon season (R2 = 0.71) compared to the post-monsoon season (R2 = 0.33), where dilution and hydrochemical mixing reduce model predictability. The study highlights Na⁺-related processes as the principal constraint on irrigation water quality and emphasizes the need for season-specific groundwater management strategies in semi-arid hard-rock terrains.Keywords: Groundwater quality, IWQI, Seasonal variation, Multivariate analysis, GIS, Granite terrainSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Groundwater is often the only reliable source of freshwater in arid and semi-arid regions, where surface water availability is limited, intermittent, or highly seasonal1,2. In these environments, groundwater is critical for supporting domestic needs, livestock, and irrigated agriculture, thereby underpinning food security and rural livelihoods3,4. However, rapid increases in groundwater abstraction, increased fertilizer use, and inefficient irrigation methods have gradually changed aquifer hydrochemistry5,6. These pressures typically accelerate salinization and sodium (Na⁺) enrichment, leading to soil clay dispersion, reduced permeability, impaired infiltration, and long-term declines in agricultural productivity7–9. Consequently, a systematic and seasonally consistent assessment of groundwater suitability for irrigation has become vital for sustainable agricultural planning in semi-arid regions10,11.

Irrigation water suitability is traditionally evaluated using a suite of hydrochemical indices, including Electrical Conductivity (EC), Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), Sodium Percentage (%Na+), Kelly’s Ratio (KR), Soluble Sodium Percentage (SSP), Magnesium Hazard Ratio (MHR), Permeability Index (PI), and Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC). Each index reflects specific soil–water interaction mechanisms, such as osmotic stress, sodicity-induced dispersion, or carbonate-related permeability reduction12,13. However, independent interpretation of these indices often yields fragmented or contradictory assessments, particularly under seasonally variable hydrogeochemical conditions. Recent studies have therefore emphasized the need for integrated evaluation frameworks, such as the Irrigation Water Quality Index (IWQI). The IWQI synthesizes multiple parameters into a single management-oriented metric and improves the spatial and temporal discrimination of irrigation suitability14,15.

Hard-rock aquifers in southern India, particularly granitic terrains, are especially vulnerable to irrigation-related salinity and sodium hazards due to low primary porosity, restricted fracture connectivity, limited buffering capacity, and long groundwater residence times16,17. In these aquifers, monsoonal recharge plays a dominant role in regulating groundwater chemistry through dilution, ion exchange, and mixing processes, often resulting in short-term improvements in irrigation water quality18. However, emerging evidence suggests that dilution alone may not fundamentally alter sodium-dominated hydrochemical facies or carbonate-related hazards. Consequently, soil degradation risks may persist even during post-monsoon periods19.

Despite extensive groundwater quality research in Telangana and adjoining semi-arid regions, several critical knowledge gaps remain insufficiently addressed. Most existing studies emphasize drinking water suitability or rely on single-season datasets, thereby failing to capture seasonal hydrochemical dynamics relevant to irrigation management20–22. As a result: (i) seasonal variability in salinity, sodicity, and carbonate-related irrigation hazards remains inadequately quantified; (ii) the hierarchical interdependence among irrigation indices under contrasting hydrological conditions is poorly constrained; (iii) multivariate statistical groupings are rarely interpreted in terms of underlying hydrogeochemical processes; and (iv) predictive frameworks capable of translating hydrochemical variability into irrigation-relevant decision support are limited.

Addressing these gaps is crucial because irrigation management decisions based on incomplete or seasonally biased assessments may underestimate sodium-induced soil degradation and compromise long-term agricultural sustainability.

Previous studies from the study region have shown limitations in single-season assessments of groundwater quality for drinking and irrigation23–25 and in the temporal dynamics of groundwater quality and associated health risks26To address these limitations, the present study conducts a comprehensive spatiotemporal assessment of irrigation water quality in a semi-arid granitic watershed in Dilawarpur Mandal, Nirmal District, Telangana. This study integrates classical hydrochemical analysis with multivariate and predictive statistical methods, including Pearson’s correlation (r), PCA, HCA, and MLR. PCA and HCA identify dominant hydrogeochemical controls and parameter associations, while MLR quantifies the influence of key variables on IWQI and evaluates seasonal variations in predictive stability and explanatory power27–29. This descriptive–predictive framework enables robust interpretation of controlling processes and their seasonal modulation.

Furthermore, the study examines whether monsoonal recharge fundamentally restructures sodium-dominated hydrochemical regimes in hard-rock aquifers or merely induces transient dilution effects. By explicitly linking recharge-driven chemical transformations to multivariate structure and predictive modeling, this work advances a process-based understanding of the evolution of irrigation water quality in semi-arid granitic terrains.

Based on the available literature, such a seasonally resolved, predictive IWQI–multivariate framework has not been previously applied to semi-arid granitic watersheds in Telangana. Accordingly, the specific objectives of this study are to:

Evaluate pre- and post-monsoon variations in major irrigation water quality parameters (EC, %Na+, SAR, KR, MHR, SSP, PI, RSC, and IWQI);

By explicitly addressing key methodological and conceptual gaps in seasonal irrigation water quality assessment, this study provides a decision-oriented framework for sustainable groundwater management in hard-rock aquifers. The findings provide new insights into the persistence of sodium–salinity dominance under monsoon-driven hydrological variability. These results support the development of sustainable irrigation strategies to protect soil health, maintain crop productivity, and preserve long-term groundwater resources, thereby contributing to Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 2 and SDG 6).

Study area

Location and climate

The study was conducted in the Dilawarpur Mandal watershed of Nirmal District, northern Telangana, India, covering an area of approximately 156 km2 (Fig. 1). The watershed lies between latitudes 19°00′–19°09′ N and longitudes 78°12′–78°18′ E and is represented on Survey of India topographic sheets 56 I/4, 56 I/8, and 56 J/5 at a scale of 1: 50,000.

Location map of the study area showing groundwater sampling locations in Dilawarpur Mandal, Telangana, India. The base map was prepared using Survey of India topographical sheets. The India national boundary shape file was obtained from the NRSC Bhuvan, and the DEM data used for elevation mapping were sourced from the USGS Earth Explorer. All spatial data were processed and maps were prepared using ArcGIS 10.8 (Esri Inc.).

The region experiences a semi-arid climate with pronounced seasonal variability. Summer temperatures frequently reach ~ 45 °C, while winter temperatures remain relatively mild, with minima around 22 °C. Mean annual rainfall during 2011–2021 is approximately 1,022 mm, most of which occurs during the southwest monsoon26,30. High summer temperatures combined with uneven rainfall distribution result in elevated evapotranspiration rates and recurrent seasonal water stress.

Geology and hydrogeology

Geologically, the study area forms part of the Archean crystalline terrain of Nirmal District, dominated by lithounits of the unclassified Dharwar Supergroup. The bedrock mainly consists of the Peninsular Gneissic Complex, including pink hornblende–biotite granite, pink granite, and grey hornblende–biotite gneiss, which are widely exposed across the watershed (Fig. 2a). These basement rocks are intruded by Paleoproterozoic dolerite dykes trending predominantly north–south and northeast–southwest.

(a) Geological map, and (b) Land-use/land-cover distribution.

Limited exposures of younger sedimentary formations belonging to the Pahal Super group (Mesoproterozoic) and the Penganga Group (Neoproterozoic) occur locally along lithological contacts. These units comprise quartzite, shale, and limestone and unconformably overlie the crystalline basement. The predominance of granitic and doleritic lithologies, which commonly host fluoride-bearing minerals such as fluorite, fluorapatite, and biotite, exerts a strong control on regional groundwater geochemistry.

Hydrogeologically, groundwater occurrence is primarily controlled by secondary porosity generated by weathering and rock fracturing. Productive aquifers are largely confined to the weathered mantle and the underlying fractured bedrock, with groundwater abstraction commonly extending to depths greater than 65 m. Spatial variability in the thickness of the weathered zone, fracture intensity, and structural interconnectivity governs groundwater storage and flow dynamics, as well as residence time, thereby enhancing water–rock interactions and exerting a strong influence on the evolution of major ion chemistry.

The predominant crops include paddy, maize, and pulses. Irrigation is mainly dependent on groundwater through borewells and shallow wells, supplemented by seasonal canal water where available. These practices strongly influence local groundwater demand and quality.

Land-use and land-cover (LULC)

Land-use and land-cover (LULC) characteristics were derived using Survey of India topographic sheets (56 I/4, 56 I/8, and 56J/5), IRS-P6 (2010) satellite imagery, and recent false-color composite (FCC) images from 2020 (Fig. 2b). Data processing and interpretation were carried out with support from the National Remote Sensing Agency (NRSA), Hyderabad, India.

The LULC classification indicates that agricultural land (48.27%) is the dominant land use, followed by water bodies (35.99%), shrub land (10.50%), forest cover (2.66%), built-up areas (1.60%), and fallow land (0.98%). These land-use patterns play a critical role in controlling spatial variability in groundwater recharge, abstraction intensity, and irrigation-related water quality across the watershed.

Materials and methodology

Sampling, analytical procedures and ionic balance

A total of 61 groundwater samples were collected from borewells across Dilawarpur Mandal during pre-monsoon and post-monsoon seasons 2020 to capture seasonal hydrochemical variability. Sampling was conducted using pre-cleaned 1 L high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottles following APHA standard protocols31. Samples were immediately sealed, labelled, and transported to the Centre for Materials for Electronics Technology (C-MET), Hyderabad, India, for laboratory analysis.

In situ measurements of pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were carried out using calibrated portable meters (HACH SensION and Systronics Conductivity Meter, Model 304). Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) were estimated from EC using standard conversion factors.

Major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, and K+) and anions (HCO3−, Cl−, SO42−, NO3−, and F−) were analyzed following APHA and BIS guidelines. Na⁺ and K⁺ were determined using flame photometry (Systronics Model 128), while Ca2+ and Mg2+ were measured by EDTA titration. HCO₃⁻ was analyzed using standard titrimetric methods.

Irrigation water quality indices

Groundwater suitability for irrigation was evaluated using hydrochemical indices that reflect salinity, sodicity, alkalinity, and soil permeability hazards. These include EC, %Na+, SAR, KR, SSP, RSC, PI, MHR, and the Irrigation Water Quality Index (IWQI). Each index captures a specific soil–water interaction, and using them together reduces uncertainty from relying on a single index.

Statistical and multivariate analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient

Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated among all irrigation indices (EC, %Na+, SAR, KR, MH, PI, SSP, RSC, and IWQI) for pre-monsoon and post-monsoon datasets. Positive “r” values indicate a direct relationship, while negative values indicate an inverse relationship36.

All variables were standardized prior to analysis to ensure uniform scaling. Statistical significance was assessed at the 95% and 99% confidence levels (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01). Correlation analysis identifies strongly interdependent indices, especially those related to salinity and sodicity, helping to clarify their combined effect on overall irrigation water quality.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA was applied to reduce dataset dimensionality and identify dominant hydrochemical processes controlling groundwater suitability37. All indices were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) to remove scale effects. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors were extracted from the covariance matrix, and PCs with eigenvalues > 1 were retained following the Kaiser criterion. Loadings of variables on each PC were used to quantify their contribution to total variance and distinguish primary controlling factors from secondary influences.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA)

HCA was used to group irrigation water quality indices based on similarity in behavior and influence on groundwater suitability. Ward’s linkage method with squared Euclidean distance was used as the dissimilarity measure. Standardization of all variables minimized scale bias. HCA provides insights into index groupings and their hydrochemical significance, facilitating interpretation of groundwater quality patterns.

To evaluate the relative contribution and seasonal sensitivity of key hydrochemical parameters influencing irrigation water quality, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) analysis was performed separately for the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon datasets. Geospatial mapping

Spatial distribution maps were prepared using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation approach in ArcGIS 10.8. This method was selected based on the principle that spatially proximate sampling locations tend to exhibit comparable groundwater quality characteristics, allowing for reliable estimation of unsampled areas. A fixed search radius was applied, and the power parameter was adjusted to achieve optimal interpolation performance by reducing prediction errors. The generated maps clearly depict the spatial variability and delineate zones of varying groundwater quality across the study region.

Total dissolved solids (TDS) exhibit a pattern similar to EC, ranging from 265–1490 mg/L (mean: 598 mg/L) in pre-monsoon and 240–1020 mg/L (mean: 481 mg/L) in post-monsoon season (Table 3). Higher pre-monsoon variability (CV: 38.23%) compared to post-monsoon (30.55%) indicates spatial heterogeneity influenced by evapotranspiration and lithological control. This spatial variability becomes relatively homogenized after monsoonal recharge41.

Among major cations, Ca2+ and Na+ are dominant. Ca2+ concentrations range from 40–144 mg/L (mean: 69 mg/L) in pre-monsoon and 26–118 mg/L (mean: 59 mg/L) in post-monsoon season (Table 3), primarily derived from silicate weathering and limited carbonate dissolution38. Magnesium occurs in moderate concentrations (18–118 mg/L in pre-monsoon and 14–64 mg/L in post-monsoon). The higher pre-monsoon variability (CV: 52.32%) indicates heterogeneous contributions from ferromagnesian minerals and ion exchange processes.

Sodium concentrations show wide spatial variation (22–225 mg/L in pre-monsoon; 32–166 mg/L in post-monsoon) and high CV values (67.04% and 43.85%, respectively), reflecting alkali feldspar weathering, cation exchange, and anthropogenic inputs such as irrigation return flow42 (Table 3). Potassium remains low (< 12 mg/L) in both seasons due to fixation by clay minerals, though elevated CV values (74.34% in pre-monsoon and 40.46% in post-monsoon) suggest localized enrichment linked to agricultural activities.

Among anions, HCO3− predominates, ranging from 120–390 mg/L (mean: 236 mg/L) in pre-monsoon and 86–380 mg/L (mean: 188 mg/L) in post-monsoon season (Table 3). This dominance indicates CO2-mediated silicate weathering as the principal geochemical process in the granitic aquifer. Chloride concentrations vary considerably (46–410 mg/L in pre-monsoon; 30–276 mg/L in post-monsoon), reflecting evaporative concentration, irrigation return flow, and localized domestic inputs43.

Sulphate concentrations are generally low to moderate (8–96 mg/L in pre-monsoon; 12–61 mg/L in post-monsoon) and are attributed to minor sulphate mineral dissolution and agricultural sources44. Nitrate exhibits very high spatial variability (CV > 60% in both seasons), with concentrations reaching up to 135 mg/L in the pre-monsoon and 132 mg/L in the post-monsoon season. This indicates anthropogenic contamination from fertilizer application, sanitation practices, and shallow recharge pathways45.

Fluoride concentrations range from 0.24–3.28 mg/L (mean: 1.01 mg/L) in pre-monsoon and 0.08–1.52 mg/L (mean: 0.65 mg/L) in post-monsoon season (Table 3). Elevated pre-monsoon levels reflect geogenic release from fluorite- and biotite-bearing minerals under alkaline conditions, enhanced by evapoconcentration.

Seasonal variation of irrigation water quality indices

Salinity hazard

Salinity hazard, evaluated using electrical conductivity (EC), is a critical factor governing irrigation water suitability because elevated salt concentrations reduce soil osmotic potential and restrict crop water uptake. The predominance of moderate to high salinity classes during pre-monsoon period reflects the cumulative effects of evapoconcentration, prolonged groundwater residence time, and intensive abstraction under semi-arid conditions46. The occurrence of very high salinity waters during this season indicates localized zones of hydraulic stagnation and limited natural flushing, which enhance solute accumulation in hard-rock aquifers.

Post-monsoon EC values generally shift toward lower salinity classes, demonstrating the diluting influence of monsoonal recharge47. However, the continued dominance of medium (C2) and high (C3) salinity classes in both seasons, based on the USSL classification48 (Fig. 3), indicates that recharge results in partial dilution rather than complete chemical renewal of groundwater. This persistence highlights the resilience of the aquifer’s geochemical framework, wherein seasonal recharge moderates’ salinity levels without fundamentally altering long-term salinity controls.

Sodium hazard indices (%Na+, SAR, KR, and SSP)

Sodium hazard indices are essential for evaluating irrigation water suitability because excessive Na+ in irrigation water can displace Ca2+ and Mg2+ from soil exchange sites, leading to clay dispersion, reduced permeability, poor aeration, and restricted root development. In semi-arid hard-rock aquifers, Na+ enrichment is commonly associated with silicate weathering, cation exchange, evapoconcentration, and irrigation return flow. Therefore, Na+-based indices are particularly relevant for assessing long-term soil–water interactions.

The %Na+, calculated using Eq. (2), ranges from 16.17–57.98% during pre-monsoon season and 21.10–43.82% during post-monsoon season (Table 5). According to the Wilcox classification (Table 4; Fig. 4), all samples in both seasons fall within the suitable category (%Na+ < 60), indicating low sodicity risk for irrigated soils. The higher upper range observed during pre-monsoon reflects evapoconcentration and enhanced ion exchange under declining groundwater levels, whereas the narrower post-monsoon range indicates partial dilution following recharge.

SAR values, computed using Eq. (3), vary from 0.61–5.08 in pre-monsoon and 0.86–3.16 in post-monsoon season (Table 5). Based on Richards’ classification, all samples fall within the excellent category (SAR < 10), suggesting negligible Na+-induced soil dispersion risk in both seasons (Table 4). The persistently low SAR values, even where EC is moderate to high, indicate that Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations remain sufficient to counterbalance Na+ effects and maintain soil permeability.

The combined influence of salinity and sodicity was evaluated using the USSL diagram44 (Fig. 3). During pre-monsoon season, 51% of samples plot in the C2–S1 field and 47% in C3–S1, indicating medium to high salinity with low sodium hazard. Only a minor fraction (~ 4%) occurs in C3–S2 and C4–S1 fields. It reflecting localized salinity stress rather than sodicity constraints. In the post-monsoon season, samples are restricted to C2–S1 (59%) and C3–S1 (41%), confirming seasonal dilution of salinity without any significant shift in sodium hazard status. The consistent dominance of the S1 class across seasons demonstrates that salinity, rather than sodicity, is the primary irrigation limitation in the study area.

Kelly’s ratio (KR), calculated using Eq. (4), ranges from 0.19–1.46 in pre-monsoon and 0.27–0.79 in post-monsoon season (Table 5). Based on Kelly’s criterion (Table 4), 95% of pre-monsoon samples and 100% of post-monsoon samples are suitable for irrigation (KR < 1). The few pre-monsoon samples exceeding unity indicate localized Na+ enrichment under evaporative conditions, which is effectively mitigated after monsoonal recharge.

SSP values, calculated using Eq. (5), range from 16.24–59.41 during pre-monsoon and 21.18–44.03 during post-monsoon season (Table 5). Based on SSP classification (Table 4), 77% of pre-monsoon samples are suitable, while 23% fall under unsuitable categories. In contrast, 85% of post-monsoon samples are classified as unsuitable despite lower absolute Na+ concentrations. This apparent inconsistency reflects the greater sensitivity of SSP to relative ionic proportions rather than absolute Na⁺ levels, suggesting that post-monsoon dilution of Ca2+ and Mg2+ can increase the relative dominance of Na⁺ in solution.

Carbonate hazard indices (RSC, PI, and MHR)

Carbonate species (CO32− and HCO3−) significantly influence irrigation water suitability by promoting the precipitation of Ca2+ and Mg2+ as carbonates, which in turn increases the relative dominance of Na+ on soil exchange sites and may impair soil structure49.

In the study area, RSC values range from − 10.47 to 0.55 meq/L during pre-monsoon season and from − 5.43 to 0.63 meq/L during post-monsoon season (Table 5). According to standard classification criteria, all samples (100%) in both seasons fall within the good category (RSC < 1.25 meq/L), indicating the absence of carbonate-induced sodicity risk (Table 4). The dominance of negative RSC values further suggests an excess of Ca2+ and Mg2+, characteristic of silicate weathering–controlled hard-rock aquifers50.

The PI, calculated using Eq. (7) following Doneen, integrates these parameters to evaluate potential long-term effects on soil hydraulic properties. PI values range from 41.87–77.33% during pre-monsoon season and from 47.21–74.27% during post-monsoon season (Table 5). Based on Doneen’s classification (Table 4), 97% of pre-monsoon samples and 100% of post-monsoon samples fall within Class II (25–75%), indicating moderate permeability conditions. The Doneen diagram (Fig. 5) confirms that groundwater from both seasons predominantly occupies the Class II field, suggesting moderate but persistent permeability constraints rather than severe structural degradation.

Doneen’s permeability index classification of groundwater for irrigation suitability during (a) pre-monsoon and (b) post-monsoon seasons.

The MHR, calculated using Eq. (8), assesses the relative dominance of Mg2+ over Ca2+ in irrigation water. Excess Mg2+ can adversely affect soil aggregation, reduce permeability, and suppress crop productivity51. Pre-monsoon MHR values range from 39.07 to 472, with 85% of samples classified as suitable (MHR < 50) and 15% as unsuitable (Tables 4 and 5). During post-monsoon season, MHR values range from 38.21 to 53.31, with 95% of samples falling within the suitable category. The improved post-monsoon suitability reflects dilution of Mg2+ concentrations and increased Ca2+ availability following recharge.

Irrigation water quality index (IWQI)

Seasonal variation of IWQI

To evaluate the integrated influence of key hydrochemical parameters controlling irrigation suitability (EC, Na+, HCO3−, Cl−, and SAR), the IWQI was computed using Eqs. 9–11.

During the pre-monsoon season, IWQI values range from 48 to 89, whereas post-monsoon values range from 59 to 92 (Table 5). Based on established IWQI classification criteria31, groundwater quality is categorized as excellent (IWQI > 85), good (70–85), poor (55–70), very poor (40–55), and unsuitable (< 40).

Accordingly, during pre-monsoon season, 23% of samples fall within the excellent category, 62% are classified as good, 55% as poor, and 10% as very poor (Table 4). In contrast, post-monsoon results indicate a clear improvement, with 39% of samples classified as excellent, 57% as good, and 4% as poor. Notably, no samples fall within the very poor or unsuitable categories during post-monsoon period.

The spatial distribution of IWQI values across the study area is illustrated in Fig. 6, highlighting zones of varying irrigation suitability. During pre-monsoon season (Fig. 6a), approximately 23.73% of the area is classified as good, 65.01% as moderate, 8.41% as poor, and 2.85% as very poor. Areas with lower IWQI values are primarily concentrated in the central and eastern parts of the study area, where intensive groundwater abstraction, limited recharge, and evapoconcentration effects are dominant.

Applications, limitations, and management considerations

Applications

This study proposes an integrated framework for evaluating irrigation water suitability in semi-arid regions by combining irrigation indices with multivariate statistical tools. The approach identifies key hydrochemical controls affecting irrigation sustainability, particularly sodium hazards influencing soil permeability. The results support crop selection, irrigation planning, and soil–water management. The framework is transferable to similar hydrogeological regions and contributes to Sustainable Development Goal 3 and Sustainable Development Goal 6 by promoting safe water use and sustainable water resource management.

Limitations

The present analysis focuses on major irrigation water quality indices based on two seasonal datasets (pre- and post-monsoon), which capture broad seasonal contrasts but do not fully represent short-term hydrological variations that may influence salinity and sodicity dynamics. Groundwater table fluctuation data were not available due to the absence of dedicated monitoring wells and consistent static water level records in the study area; therefore, the relationship between groundwater level fluctuations and irrigation water quality could not be evaluated in this study. In addition, localized aquifer heterogeneity and site-specific anthropogenic influences may not be fully represented at all locations. Consequently, application of this framework in other regions may require consideration of local lithology, recharge characteristics, groundwater level conditions, and agricultural practices.

Management considerations

Groundwater classified as excellent to good can be used for irrigation without restriction, whereas marginal-quality water may require blending, soil amendments, or the cultivation of salt-tolerant crops. Areas affected by elevated sodium hazard should adopt management practices such as gypsum application, improved drainage, and regulated irrigation to prevent soil structural degradation. Given the dominance of sodicity over salinity identified in this study, long-term management should prioritize maintaining a favourable Ca2+–Na+ balance and preserving soil permeability. Seasonal variability, particularly post-monsoon dilution effects, underscores the need for adaptive, site-specific irrigation management strategies.

This study assessed groundwater suitability for irrigation in a semi-arid region of Telangana, India, using multiple irrigation water quality indices integrated with multivariate statistical and regression analyses. The results indicate that groundwater is generally suitable for irrigation, with 85% of pre-monsoon samples and 96% of post-monsoon samples classified as excellent to good based on the IWQI.
