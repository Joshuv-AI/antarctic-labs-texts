Multi-modal prediction model for thymic epithelial tumors: Enhancing surgical decisions and recurrence risk assessment from CT datasets
Authors: Xing Wang, Fei Yang, Qiongliang Liu, Ran Wei, Ning Song, Zhengyang LYU, Qing Yan, Zhixiong Lan, Jiang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrecise prediction of histology (TETs vs. Non-TETs), Masaoka-Koga staging, and WHO classification is essential for surgical and postoperative decisions. Artificial intelligence (AI) models have shown promise in addressing these gaps, but existing studies often suffer from small datasets and single-task limitations.PurposeThis study aims to develop and validate a multi-task deep learning model for preoperative non-invasive prediction of histology and Masaoka-Koga stage in thymic epithelial tumors to support individualized risk assessment and treatment planning.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed 659 chest CT scans from TET patients with pathologically confirmed histology, Masaoka-Koga staging, and WHO classification collected between October 2014 and July 2025 from Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, and Huashan Hospital. A deep learning model was developed to classify histology into TETs and Non-TETs groups, Masaoka-Koga stages and WHO risk groups.ResultsThe model achieved high performance across tasks in the test set. For histology classification, AUC was 0.9674 (95% CI, 0.9347–0.9895), with accuracy of 91.73% (95% CI, 86.47%-96.24%). Masaoka-Koga staging stratification yielded an AUC of 0.9328 (95% CI, 0.8179–1.0000) and accuracy of 95.38% (95% CI, 89.23%-100.00%). WHO risk prediction reached an AUC of 0.8485 (95% CI, 0.7447–0.9347) and accuracy of 78.46% (95% CI, 67.69%-89.23%). Calibration analysis further showed low Brier scores for the histology and Masaoka-Koga models (0.0695 and 0.0456, respectively), supporting acceptable probability calibration for these two tasks.ConclusionThis multi-task AI model provides a non-invasive, accurate tool for preoperative TET assessment, potentially optimizing surgical strategies and personalized care.Keywords: Thymic epithelial tumors; Computed tomography; Deep learning; Radiomics; Multitask, learning

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are rare neoplasms arising from the thymic epithelium, comprising about 0.2–1.5% of all malignancies with an annual incidence of 1.3–3.9 per million global8ly [1], [2], [3]. Classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) into low-risk subtypes (A, AB, B1; >90% 5-year survival) and high-risk subtypes (B2, B3, thymic carcinoma; elevated recurrence and metastasis risks), TETs demand precise preoperative risk stratification to inform surgical and adjuvant strategies [4], [5]. The Masaoka-Koga staging system further guides interventions: stages I–II favor minimally invasive techniques like video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) to minimize morbidity, while stages III–IV require open resection for R0 margins, a factor linked to improved survival [6]. Differentiating TETs from non-TET mediastinal masses (e.g., cysts or hyperplasia, comprising up to 40% of anterior lesions) is equally vital, as it prevents overtreatment with total thymectomy in benign cases [7].

Current guidelines from NCCN and ESMO emphasize multidisciplinary preoperative evaluation, yet computed tomography (CT)—the primary imaging modality—often yields subjective interpretations with 60–70% sensitivity for invasion or lesion differentiation due to overlapping radiologic features [8], [9], [10]. Invasive biopsies, though confirmatory, pose risks of tumor seeding and sampling errors, potentially delaying management [8]. These limitations underscore the need for objective, non-invasive tools to unify TET detection, staging, and risk prediction, reducing variability and enhancing personalized care.

Prior AI efforts, including radiomics and deep learning on CT, have achieved AUCs of 0.85–0.95 for individual tasks like WHO stratification or staging but are hampered by small cohorts (<200 cases), single-center biases, and siloed analyses that limit generalizability [11], [12], [13], [14], [15].

We hypothesized that a hypothesis-driven multi-task deep learning framework, fusing radiomics (e.g., texture entropy, shape irregularity) with convolutional features from a large, multi-center CT dataset (>650 cases), could simultaneously outperform single-task models in predicting TET histology, Masaoka-Koga stages, and WHO risk groups. By leveraging shared representations across tasks and 3D volumetric analysis, this approach addresses imaging overlaps and rarity-induced imbalances, enabling seamless integration into routine workflows for hypothesis-tested precision oncology.

Methods

Data acquisition

A retrospective study included 659 patients (aged 6–83 years) with anterior mediastinal lesions who underwent chest CT scans at Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, and Huashan Hospital from October 2014 to July 2025. Scans were performed using Siemens (Healthineers, Germany) or Philips (Eindhoven, Netherlands) scanners in helical mode (120 kVp, 300 mA; collimation 38.4–80 mm; reconstruction 1.0 mm thickness/0.7–1.0 mm intervals; 0.6 s rotation; pitch 1.375). Enhanced scans followed IV injection of 90–120 mL iohexol (300 mg/mL, 3.5 mL/s or 1.5 mL/kg), with arterial phase imaging at 30 s post-injection via right upper limb vein. Radiation dose was described using scanner-generated dose metrics, including volume CT dose index (CTDIvol, mGy) and dose-length product (DLP, mGy·cm).

Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, received the Institutional Review Board approval from Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, and Huashan Hospital, and waived individual informed consent due to its retrospective design (No. 2023SQ221). All procedures followed the approved protocol.

Pathological examinations

All patients underwent surgical resection within 60 days after CT imaging. Four patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery, while none underwent neoadjuvant radiotherapy or targeted therapy.For the pathological examination, surgically resected specimens were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned using a microtome, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). All available tumor slides stained with H&E were independently reviewed by two different pathologists who were blinded to the clinical outcomes.

Furthermore, pathological examinations were aimed to distinguish: 1. TETs and Non-TETs; 2. WHO classification：Type A, AB, B1, B2, B3 and thymic carcinoma; 3. Masaoka-koga stages：Stage I, II, III and IV.

Preprocessing of CT images

Overall Framework

The proposed Thymus-Net model (Fig. 2) is designed to integrate complementary information from multiple domains for comprehensive thymic tumor analysis. The overall framework comprises three major components: a data preprocessing module, a dual-branch feature extraction module, and a feature fusion and classification module. This modular design ensures standardized data processing, facilitates deep feature extraction from diverse perspectives, and achieves accurate classification through an effective multi-level fusion mechanism.

Overview of the Data Processing and Model Inference Pipeline.

Model Architecture

Thymus-Net is built upon a large-scale foundation model [16] that has been pretrained on hundreds of millions of medical images and has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across various medical imaging tasks. The core architecture of this foundation model is based on the self-attention mechanism, originally proposed by Vaswani et al. in the Transformer framework [17], which revolutionized sequence modeling by effectively addressing long-range dependency challenges. In this study, we extend and adapt this mechanism to three-dimensional medical imaging, enabling Thymus-Net to capture global contextual information both within lesions and across surrounding anatomical structures, thereby overcoming the inherent limitations of conventional convolutional neural networks in modeling long-range dependencies.

Data Preprocessing

The purpose of data preprocessing is to convert raw three- dimensional CT images into standardized inputs suitable for model processing. The procedure includes the following steps:

Lesion Segmentation: The raw CT images are first subjected to 3D segmentation to precisely delineate the thymus region. This step can be performed either through manual expert annotation or by using automatic/semi-automatic segmentation algorithms such as U-Net [18] or SCA-UNET [14], yet all segmentation masks were ultimately reviewed and confirmed by two senior radiologists, and they were fully blinded to pathological results during tumor delineation. The segmentation results are stored as 3D masks, where voxel values of 1 indicate lesion regions and 0 indicate the background. For model input, the lesion mask served as an ROI reference for lesion localization and radiomics extraction, and the mask was checked before feature extraction to ensure consistency with the visible lesion.

Image Cropping: To focus on the lesion while preserving adjacent contextual information, the tumor mask was used to localize the lesion and define a bounding box centered on the segmented region. Instead of retaining only mask-positive voxels, an enlarged image patch around the lesion was cropped from the original CT data, thereby including both the tumor and surrounding peritumoral context. This strategy retained peritumoral tissue within the cropped patch while using the mask to identify the lesion boundary, resolving the distinction between ROI-based feature extraction and context-preserving image input.

Resampling and Standardization: The cropped image patches are resampled to unify voxel sizes and standardized to a 224 × 224 × 16 shape. This procedure ensures that all input data share the same spatial resolution and volume dimensions, eliminating potential inconsistencies due to varying image sizes and providing a stable foundation for model training.

Dual-Branch Feature Extraction Module

We designed a dual-branch feature extraction module to jointly model radiomics features and deep learning features.

To avoid data leakage, cohort partitioning was performed before model evaluation. Radiomics features were extracted on a per-patient basis, and no label-informed feature selection was performed using the full cohort. Any data-driven normalization parameters were estimated from the training set and then applied unchanged to the test set.

1)Radiomics Feature Extraction Branch:This branch focuses on extracting traditional and interpretable radiomics features [19] from pre-processed lesion image patches. Using the open-source PyRadiomics library, multiple groups of features were extracted from lesion masks, including first-order statistics, texture features (e.g., GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, NGTDM), and Shape2D features. These features were integrated into a high-dimensional radiomics feature vector, which was then transformed via a fully connected linear layer to produce a compact representation vector.

Statistical Analysis: Diagnostic performance was assessed on the held-out test sets using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score. The 95% confidence intervals for these metrics were calculated using 1000 bootstrap resamples of the test set. Model calibration was additionally evaluated using Brier scores, calibration curves, and calibration intercept/slope, with bootstrap confidence intervals calculated for calibration metrics.

Histology Classification

The model effectively distinguished thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) from non-TETs, both of which are categories of mediastinal lesions, using CT-derived features such as tumor texture and enhancement patterns. This differentiation is vital, as TETs (malignant) require total thymectomy to prevent recurrence, while non-TETs (often benign, e.g., thymic cysts or hyperplasia) may only need observation or minimal intervention. The classification identifies TETs for surgical treatment, reducing unnecessary procedures on benign lesions and supporting preoperative decision-making.

Fig. 3 illustrates performance. Fig. 3A shows case distribution: training set with 258 malignant (TET) and 268 benign (non-TET) lesions; test set with 65 malignant and 68 benign cases. Fig. 3B presents the ROC curve, achieving an AUC of 0.9674 (95% CI, 0.9347–0.9895) in the test set. Fig. 3C displays the confusion matrix from 133 test cases: 61 true positives, 7 false positives, 4 false negatives, and 61 true negatives. This yielded an accuracy of 91.73% (95% CI, 86.47%−96.24%), sensitivity of 93.85% (95% CI, 87.10%−98.61%), and specificity of 89.71% (95% CI, 81.33%−96.61%). Calibration analysis showed a Brier score of 0.0695 (95% CI, 0.0422–0.1044), calibration intercept of −0.2792 (95% CI, −1.1007–0.4582), and calibration slope of 1.2209 (95% CI, 0.9457–1.7587), indicating acceptable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

These results, validated on the test set, align with the study’s focus on preoperative precision, addressing the challenge of overlapping imaging features in mediastinal lesions. By leveraging radiomics and deep learning, including texture and shape features, the model enhances surgical planning, ensuring malignant TETs are targeted while sparing benign cases, potentially reducing overtreatment in anterior mediastinal masses. This automated approach strengthens personalized care for this rare malignancy.

Masaoka-Koga Staging Classification

The multi-model stratified thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) into Masaoka-Koga stages I-II and stages III-IV using CT-derived invasion indicators, such as peritumoral fat involvement and vascular proximity. This classification may support preoperative risk stratification by identifying imaging patterns associated with more locally advanced disease, while the final surgical approach should be determined together with clinical assessment and surgeon judgment.

Fig. 4A shows dataset distribution from 323 TET patients: training set with 203 cases (stages I-II) and 55 cases (III-IV); test set with 51 cases (I-II) and 14 cases (III-IV). Fig. 4B presents the ROC curve, achieving an AUC of 0.9328 (95% CI, 0.8179–1.0000) in the test set. Fig. 4C displays the confusion matrix for the 65-case test set: 51 true positives, 3 false positives, 0 false negatives, and 11 true negatives. This yielded an accuracy of 95.38% (95% CI, 89.23%−100.00%), sensitivity for stages I-II of 100.00% (95% CI, 100.00%−100.00%), and specificity for stages III-IV of 78.57% (95% CI, 53.85%−100.00%). Calibration analysis showed a Brier score of 0.0456 (95% CI, 0.0072–0.0945), calibration intercept of −0.6517 (95% CI, −6.2449–5.0090), and calibration slope of 1.3120 (95% CI, 0.9275–16.6217), suggesting favorable probability calibration in this test set.

Multiple independent studies documented limited diagnostic performance of unaided thoracic radiologists for Masaoka-Koga early/advanced staging, with reader sensitivity ranging 84.6%–92.3% and specificity 71.4%–77.6% [22]. Our results support the potential value of the model for preoperative Masaoka-Koga risk stratification. The findings should be interpreted as supportive evidence for risk assessment rather than as a stand-alone basis for surgical decision-making, and further validation in independent cohorts is warranted.

WHO staging Classification

The multi-task AI model categorized thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) into low-risk (WHO types A, AB, B1) and high-risk (WHO types B2, B3, thymic carcinoma) groups using CT-derived morphological and density-based features linked to tumor aggressiveness. This classification supports preoperative assessment of recurrence and metastasis risks, guiding tailored follow-up and adjuvant therapy decisions.

While the estimated AUC and accuracy suggest reliability, improving specificity could enhance recurrence risk prediction, supporting optimized therapeutic strategies for this rare malignancy.

Discussion

This study introduces a multi-task AI model trained on extensive multi-center CT datasets to tackle preoperative challenges in thymic epithelial tumors (TETs). By fusing radiomics and deep learning, it enables simultaneous TET identification, Masaoka-Koga staging, and WHO risk stratification, yielding performance comparable to single-task models. These capabilities promise to refine diagnostic precision, mitigate interobserver variability in CT reads, and foster personalized surgical and adjuvant strategies for this rare malignancy.

The model's clinical utility spans the TET perioperative period. Accurate TET vs. non-TET classification may help reduce unnecessary thymectomies in benign anterior mediastinal masses, avoiding invasive procedures. Masaoka-Koga staging predictions may support preoperative risk stratification related to surgical planning rather than serving as a stand-alone determinant of operative approach. WHO classification predicts recurrence and survival risk groups, informing surveillance versus adjuvant therapy. These outputs may complement radiologist assessment and support decision-making, but prospective validation and formal clinical utility analyses are still needed before routine deployment.

Benchmarked against prior work, our framework is non-inferior to fragmented single-task efforts. Prior radiomics on smaller cohorts (<200 cases) achieved AUCs of 0.85–0.92 for TET classification [11], [23], our larger, diverse 3D multi-task design boosts this. Masaoka-Koga staging predictions surpass spectral CT models (AUC ∼0.93) via holistic volumetric capture [24], while WHO stratification equal performs biased nomograms (external AUCs 0.80–0.90) through shared features and hybrid extraction [23], [25]. Reliance on accessible CT—unlike less available MRI [26] or PET/CT [27]—ensures broad applicability, with multi-task efficiency curbing computational overhead.

The broader implications extend to precision oncology: by reducing overtreatment, optimizing workflows, and aligning with NCCN/ESMO guidelines, our multi-modal could lower VATS-to-open conversions and enhance outcomes in high-risk TETs. In low-resource environments, it empowers specialists, addressing TET rarity and global disparities.

Limitations include retrospective design, introducing selection bias toward operable cases and underrepresenting inoperable/advanced TETs [4]. Single-modality CT focus, while practical, may miss metabolic nuances from PET/CT. Additionally, although the dataset is multi-center, external validation on independent cohorts remains absent, potentially curbing generalizability.

Future work includes prospective multicenter trials for real-world validation, surgical outcome assessment, and recurrence prediction via PET/CT integration [27]. Multimodal fusion with MRI for soft-tissue detail and histopathologic deep learning for subtype confirmation could sharpen prognostics [11], [28]. Future prospective studies should also include formal decision-curve analysis to quantify clinical net benefit across clinically defined risk thresholds.

In conclusion, this non-invasive multi-task AI tool optimizes preoperative TET evaluation, refining surgical and management strategies. Prospective integration into workflows promises enhanced outcomes for this rare malignancy.
