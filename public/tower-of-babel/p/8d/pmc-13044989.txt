Non‐Classical HLA and MIC Risk Score Is Associated With Acute Kidney Transplant Rejection Beyond Classical HLA Matching
Authors: Silja Tammi, Salla Markkinen, Ilkka Helanterä, Marko Lempinen, Jukka Partanen, Kati Hyvärinen, Jarmo Ritari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMatching of HLA‐A, ‐B and ‐DRB1 is the gold standard in histocompatibility testing for kidney transplantation. A good HLA match reduces the risk of alloimmunisation, thereby decreasing the likelihood of graft failure and potentially enabling reduced immunosuppression therapy. However, the role of the non‐classical HLA and MIC genes is not well established. We hypothesised that polymorphisms in these genes contribute additional risk of acute rejection after kidney transplantation, beyond what is explained by classical HLA mismatching. We developed and validated an oligogenic risk score based on ten polymorphisms in the HLA‐E, ‐F, ‐G, MICA and MICB genes associated with time to acute rejection using Cox proportional hazards analysis in a single centre retrospective cohort of 1025 Finnish adult kidney transplant recipient‐donor pairs. The score, particularly at the extreme ends, that is, the 1st and 10th deciles, was associated with rejection‐free survival in both the discovery cohort (Cox multivariable model HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.33, p‐value 4.350 × 10−4) and the replication cohort (HR 1.33, 1.10–1.62, p‐value 0.004). The score added value beyond the standard HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 matching or HLA class I and II eplet matching, differentiating high and low‐risk patient groups. The association remained when adding HLA‐C and HLA‐DQB1 matching to the analysis. The results demonstrate the value of considering the non‐classical HLA and MIC genes in risk assessment and even graft allocation in kidney transplantation, and the potential of using oligogenic and polygenic summary scores to predict transplant outcomes.

Introduction

Acute rejection (AR) remains a major complication after kidney transplantation (KT), affecting graft survival and overall patient outcomes [1]. Although AR is a multifactorial trait influenced by both genetic and non‐genetic factors, a key component involves the recipient's immune system recognising the transplanted kidney as foreign and mounting an immune response.

The occurrence of AR even in well‐matched donor‐recipient pairs [2] suggests factors beyond HLA compatibility. Identifying these non‐HLA factors is essential for a comprehensive understanding of transplant immunology. Improved histocompatibility can help reduce the need for immunosuppression, thereby mitigating side effects, such as infections and cancer risk. Furthermore, a good match prevents alloimmune reactions, improving graft longevity and reducing complications in potential re‐transplantations.

As the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I polypeptide‐related sequence A and B (MICA and MICB) and non‐classical HLA genes are involved in immune processes, including modulation of natural killer (NK) cell activity and regulation of T‐cell responses, they have been among the candidate genes to affect transplantation outcome. MICA and MICB act as stress‐inducible ligands for the activating receptor NKG2D found on NK cells, CD8+ T cells and γδ T cells [3]. HLA‐E primarily interacts with NK cells and specific T cells, modulating immune responses through inhibitory and activating signals via NKG2A and NKG2C receptors [4]. HLA‐G and HLA‐F promote immune regulation and tolerance, particularly in pregnancy and transplantation [5, 6].

Numerous studies have investigated anti‐MICA antibodies and HLA‐G expression levels in transplantation, associating them with reduced graft survival, increased rejection risk and, in the case of HLA‐G, a more tolerogenic immune profile [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Recent research also suggests that variation in these genes may associate with AR and other KT‐related complications. For example, MICA mismatching has been associated with antibody‐mediated rejection (ABMR) [12] and decreased graft survival [13]. The variant MICA A5.1, which contains a triplet repeat microsatellite polymorphism in the transmembrane region and leads to overexpression of MICA molecules, abnormal basolateral localisation and reduced levels of soluble MICA (sMICA) [14, 15], has been implicated in anti‐MICA sensitisation and increased proteinuria [15]. The MICA‐129 methionine‐to‐valine variant affecting NKG2D receptor affinity [16], has been associated with cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection‐free and AR‐free survival [17]. HLA‐E*01:01/01:03 (HLA‐E R107G) dimorphism, with the HLA‐E*01:03 allele exhibiting increased stability and expression [18], has been linked to CMV reactivation [19] and clinical rejection [20]. Finally, HLA‐G 3′ UTR polymorphisms, which influence the stability of mRNA and expression levels [21], have been associated with acute rejection [22, 23, 24], graft loss [25] and CMV reactivation [26]. However, findings often lack reproducibility due to small sample sizes and methodological variation. To our knowledge, there are no published studies on MICB or HLA‐F polymorphism in kidney transplantation.

Instead of single polymorphisms, oligogenic or polygenic risk scores (PRS) offer a more robust approach to associating genetic variation with complex traits. Genetic summary scores have also been applied to predict transplant‐related complications, such as ABMR [12], graft loss [27], CMV infection [28], non‐melanoma skin cancer [29] and age at kidney failure [30]. By identifying patients with high or low genetic risk for rejection, clinicians can tailor personalised follow‐up and immunosuppressive regimens. High‐risk patients may benefit from more intensive monitoring and early intervention, while low‐risk patients could receive reduced immunosuppression, minimising side effects and improving outcomes.

To investigate the role of non‐classical HLA and MIC genes in KT, we analysed the association of allelic variation and mismatching in these genes with AR in a retrospective single‐centre cohort of 1025 kidney transplantations, divided into discovery and replication cohorts according to the year of transplantation. We imputed the alleles using a population‐matched high‐accuracy reference and created an oligogenic risk score based on the variation and mismatching showing the strongest associations with AR in the discovery cohort. We then tested whether this score provided added value over the standard HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 matching and HLA class I and II eplet matching in the replication cohort.

Methods

Clinical and follow‐up data of recipients and donors were collected from the Finnish Transplant Registry. The cohort has been described in detail earlier [31]. The primary outcome was biopsy‐proven AR per Banff classification [32]. This included both antibody‐ and T‐cell‐mediated rejections, as well as borderline changes. All transplantations had negative complement‐dependent cytotoxicity crossmatch results. DNA samples from the recipients and donors were collected during standard histocompatibility testing prior to transplantation at the Finnish Red Cross Blood Service, Helsinki, Finland.

The study has been approved by the ethics committee of Helsinki University Hospital (HUS/1873/2018) and the Finnish National Supervisory Authority for Welfare and Health (V/9161/2019).

Genotyping and Imputation

The genotyping protocol has been described in detail earlier [31]. Briefly, genotyping was performed at the Finnish Institute of Molecular Medicine, Helsinki, Finland using Infinium Global Screening Array‐24 v2.0 with Multi‐Disease drop‐in (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The genotype data were imputed using the Finnish SISu v3 reference panel consisting of high‐coverage (read depth 25–30×) whole genome sequence data from THL Biobank cohorts (N = 1768) (http://sisuproject.fi/).

Allele Imputation and Mismatch Calculation

The recipients and donors were typed for the HLA‐A, HLA‐B and HLA‐DRB1 loci at low‐resolution level according to the clinical histocompatibility testing protocol prior to transplantation. To obtain consistent high‐resolution typing across all loci, the HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, ‐DRB1, ‐DQA1, ‐DQB1 and ‐DPB1 alleles were imputed from genotype data at high‐resolution (two‐field) level using HIBAG [33] v1.38.1 with a Finnish HLA reference [34]. Antibody‐verified eplet mismatches for the classical HLA genes were calculated based on the imputed HLA alleles using HLA‐Matchmaker [35]. The alleles of HLA‐E, HLA‐F, as well as MICA and MICB, were imputed at two‐field resolution and the alleles and UTR haplotypes of HLA‐G at four‐field resolution using HIBAG v1.38.1 and the combined Finnish and 1000 Genomes reference [36]. These imputation panels have been verified for accuracy against clinical‐quality genotyping data. The imputed alleles and their frequencies are presented in Table S1.

Imputation quality was evaluated using the distribution of posterior probabilities, which is informative about accuracy. Allele dosages were derived from these probabilities and used to calculate allele mismatches (0, 1 or 2) between the recipient and the donor in the direction of rejection (present in donor but absent in recipient). The imputed MICA alleles determined MICA A5.1 and MICA‐129 genotypes, and imputed HLA‐G 3′ UTR haplotypes determined HLA‐G 3′ UTR 14 bp genotypes. Eplet mismatches were calculated using the HLA Eplet Registry (https://www.epregistry.com.br/) [37] and were only feasible for MICA, as MICB eplets are not curated.

Statistical Analyses

The primary endpoint was time to AR post‐transplantation. Univariable analyses used Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests, while multivariable analyses used cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards models, censoring at last follow‐up. Missing data were excluded from the analyses. The dataset was divided into a discovery cohort (n = 682) comprising the earliest two‐thirds of the dataset, and a replication cohort (n = 343), consisting of the most recent one‐third.

A non‐classical HLA/MIC (nHM) risk score was developed in the discovery cohort to assess the combined association of key nHM polymorphisms with AR. Variables included imputed allele dosages and their mismatches in donors and recipients for MICA, MICA‐129, MICA A5.1, MICB, HLA‐E, HLA‐F, HLA‐G, HLA‐G UTR haplotypes, and eplet mismatching for MICA. The nHM variables and their coding are presented in Table S2. From 127 nHM variables with a standard deviation > 0.1, each was evaluated individually by multivariable Cox models adjusting for recipient and donor age and sex, year of transplantation, cold ischemia time, donor‐recipient CMV serostatus combination, HLA class I eplet mismatch sum and HLA class II eplet mismatch sum. Anti‐HLA sensitisation was excluded due to missing data.

Significant variables (p < 0.05) were normalised to categorical scale (−1, 0, 1) based on risk (Table S3). Missing class or class with unknown risk was assigned 0. Highly correlated variables (r > 0.8) were filtered to retain only the most significant, and only those with ≥ 10 samples per class were included. These were combined into a single nHM score (Figure 1).

The nHM score's association with AR was tested in both cohorts using multivariable Cox models, adjusting for baseline covariables and classical HLA mismatches (HLA‐A, ‐B and ‐DRB1, with and without HLA‐C and HLA‐DQB1). The added discriminatory value of the nHM score was assessed using time‐dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (time‐dependent AUC), Brier score and Uno's C‐index. Cumulative incidence functions (CIF) were estimated for AR and competing events (graft loss and death) using the Aalen–Johansen method. To account for competing risks, subdistribution hazard ratios were obtained from Fine–Gray models. Adjusted cumulative incidence curves were derived from these Fine–Gray models by predicting subdistribution probabilities for AR at each nHM score quintile, while fixing other covariables at their median (continuous) or reference category (categorical). To evaluate the score's added value beyond classical HLA matching in the replication cohort, a classical HLA score was created from standardised mismatch and eplet sums and included as a covariable in the Cox models alongside the nHM score, but with excluding the constituent variables of the classical score. Rejection‐free survival was then compared across nHM score extremes at varying classical HLA scores, controlling for other covariables.

Statistical analyses were performed using R v.4.4.2 and the ‘survival’ [38], ‘survminer’ [39] and ‘cmprsk’ [40] packages.

Results

To assess the potential role of MHC‐encoded non‐classical HLA and MIC genes in kidney transplant matching, we analysed the associations of polymorphisms and matching of MICA, MICB, HLA‐E, HLA‐F and HLA‐G with time to acute rejection after kidney transplantation in 1025 recipient‐donor pairs. Variables showing individual associations (p < 0.05) in the discovery cohort were combined into a single risk score to assess the combined, oligogenic association of nHM polymorphism. Finally, the added value of the nHM score beyond classical HLA matching in histocompatibility assessment was evaluated in an independent replication cohort.

Cohort Characteristics and Analysis Covariables

AR was detected in 199 out of the 1025 recipients (19%), of which 178 (89%) were T‐cell‐mediated rejections (TCMR) and 21 (11%) were antibody‐mediated rejections (Table 1). Graft loss was detected in 68 patients (7%). The most common diagnosis prior to transplantation was polycystic kidney disease (20%), followed by diabetic nephropathy with type I diabetes (15%), IgA nephropathy (10%) and unspecified chronic kidney disease (10%). The median follow‐up time was 37 months (IQR 18–62), and the median cold ischemia time was 20 h (IQR 17–23). Of the covariables, HLA I eplet mismatches, HLA II eplet mismatches and HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 mismatch sum were significantly different between the rejection and nonrejection groups (p‐value 0.031, 1.82 × 10−6 and 2.52 × 10−4, respectively). There were 16, 4, 19 and 2 missing entries for donor‐recipient CMV serostatus combination, and for HLA‐A, HLA‐B and HLA‐DRB1 mismatches, respectively. The median posterior probabilities for the imputed genotypes were 0.97, 0.98, 0.98, 0.998, 0.96, 0.98 and 0.99 for MICA, MICB, HLA‐E, HLA‐F, HLA‐G, HLA‐G 5′ UTR and HLA‐G 3′ UTR, and 0.82, 0.82, 0.90, 0.97, 0.96, 0.99 and 0.96 for classical HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, DRB1, DQA1, DQB1 and ‐DPB1.

Building and Validation of Non‐Classical HLA/MIC Score for Time to Acute Rejection

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for classical HLA, we identified ten nHM variables in the discovery data showing single associations of p < 0.05 (Table 2). Only one variable (recipient MICB*014:01 allele) remained significant after correction for multiple testing. The sum score of these ten variables was statistically significantly associated with reduced rejection‐free survival in the discovery data (hazard ratio [HR] 1.2, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.11–1.28, p‐value 2.8 × 10−6). The association was also observed when analysing the extreme values (≤ −5 vs. ≥ 0) of the score (HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.09–1.29, p‐value 5.316 × 10−5) or the extreme deciles (1st decile vs. 10th decile) of the score (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.33, p‐value 4.350 × 10−4) (Figure 2a,c). These results were consistent in the replication cohort for the score (HR 1.1, 95% CI 1.00–1.21, p‐value 0.040), the extreme values of the score (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.06–1.38, p‐value 0.005) and the extreme deciles of the score (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.10–1.62, p‐value 0.004) (Figure 2b,d). The analysis was adjusted for HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 mismatch sum and HLA class II eplet mismatches, both of which showed statistically significant associations with AR in our discovery cohort (Table S4), indicating that the effect was independent of classical HLA matching. The associations remained statistically significant also when adjusting for the extended classical HLA matching (HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, ‐DRB1 and ‐DQB1). The Cox proportional hazards models for the nHM score, extreme values of the score and extreme deciles of the score are shown in Tables S5–S14.

Overall, we found no strong evidence of non‐linearity in the association between nHM score and AR. The squared nHM score was not statistically significant (Tables S15 and S16), and ANOVA comparing the basic model with the squared‐term model showed no improvement in either cohort (p‐value 0.089 and 0.069 for discovery and replication, respectively). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models showed no evidence of non‐linearity, as the nonlinear spline components were not statistically significant. However, ANOVA comparing the basic and RCS models indicated weak evidence of improved fit in the replication cohort (p‐value 0.17 and 0.02 for discovery and replication, respectively) (Tables S17 and S18).

Time‐dependent AUC, Brier score and Uno's C‐index analyses (Tables S19–S24) showed that adding the non‐classical HLA/MIC score improved model performance statistically significantly in the discovery cohort. The continuous score increased AUC by 0.036–0.039 and Uno's C‐index by 0.040, with all AUCs > 0.70. Improvements in the replication cohort were consistent but smaller (AUC increase 0.005–0.014 and Uno's C‐index increase 0.015) and not statistically significant, although AUCs remained > 0.70 at all timepoints. Brier scores were slightly lower in both cohorts, indicating reduced prediction error, although changes were not statistically significant. When restricting analyses to the extreme nHM score deciles (1 vs. 10), overall discrimination was higher and the absolute improvements in AUC, C‐index and Brier score were larger than in the full‐cohort analysis. However, all differences remained statistically non‐significant, likely due to smaller sample size.

Cumulative incidence curves showed that AR occurs predominantly early after transplantation, whereas graft loss and death accumulate gradually and become substantial only later, particularly after 90 months (Figure 3a,b). The Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard ratios were only slightly lower than the corresponding cause‐specific estimates, and p‐values increased only marginally (Tables S25 and S26), consistent with the observation that competing events occur mainly in the later post‐transplantation period. This indicates that competing events have limited influence on early AR incidence and therefore are unlikely to meaningfully bias our primary results, although they increasingly shape long‐term cumulative incidence.

Adjusted cumulative incidence curves by nHM score quintiles (Figure 3c,d) showed progressively higher rejection probability with increasing nHM score, even after adjustment for covariates and accounting for competing events. The 1‐year cumulative incidence ranged from 6.4% in the lowest quintile to 17.8% (risk difference 11.4%, relative risk 2.8%) in the discovery cohort and from 7.0% to 12.4% (risk difference 7.4%, relative risk 1.8%) in the replication cohort (Table S27). Separation between quintiles emerged early, suggesting that nHM score primarily discriminates early AR risk while maintaining risk differences over time.

Independence of Non‐Classical HLA/MIC Score From Classical HLA Matching

We next assessed the added prognostic value of the nHM score beyond classical HLA matching in KT. For comparison, a classical HLA score was constructed by standardising and summing the HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 mismatch sum and HLA class I and II eplet mismatch sums (Figure 4a). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were then applied across increasingly extreme decile contrasts of the nHM score, with the classical HLA score included as a covariable, in addition to the basic covariables. The HR for the nHM score increased progressively toward the extremes, suggesting that the score has a stronger prognostic value in more extreme nHM values (Figure 4b). In contrast, the HR of the classical HLA score decreased in these comparisons, suggesting a diminishing influence in the presence of extreme nHM scores. The correlation between the nHM and classical HLA scores was weak (r = 0.29), suggesting that the nHM score provides a robust and largely independent signal, despite some shared variance.

To further explore this relationship, we examined differences in rejection‐free survival between the extreme ends of the nHM score (deciles 1–2 vs. deciles 9–10) at varying classical HLA scores, while holding all other covariables constant at their mean or reference values (Figure 4c–e). The separation in 2‐year survival probability of the nHM score was more pronounced under poor classical HLA match (classical HLA score set at mean + 2 standard deviations) compared to a good match (mean − 2 standard deviations), with a difference of 16 versus 11 percentage points, respectively.

Discussion

Since their discovery in the late 1980s to mid‐1990s [41, 42, 43], the non‐classical HLA and MIC genes have gained interest as potential candidates contributing to post‐kidney transplantation complications. However, the impact of individual genes or polymorphisms may be modest, and the combined effect of these genetic factors has not been thoroughly investigated. Their strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) with classical HLA alleles, and the resulting challenges in matching, further complicate the fine‐mapping of the role of non‐classical HLA, MIC and other MHC genes.

To investigate the association between the combined non‐classical HLA and MIC genetic factors and the time to acute rejection after kidney transplantation, we developed an oligogenic risk score based on ten factors within these genes that were linked to AR. We hypothesised that non‐classical HLA and MIC would jointly contribute additional risk independently of classical HLA. PRS, based on results of extensive whole genome association studies, has proven to be a promising approach for understanding the entire genetic risk component of complex multifactorial traits and diseases [44, 45]. The method combines both the risk and protective effects of genetic variants into a single score value that may be a useful addition to clinical decision making.

Previous studies have identified MICA mismatching as a possible risk factor for complications after KT [12, 13]. Interestingly, our findings indicated an association with MICB mismatching, particularly when there were two mismatches between the recipient and the donor, whereas no significant association was observed for MICA mismatching in our cohort. Although solid organ and haematopoietic stem cell transplantations have a fundamentally distinct nature, a similar pattern was recently reported by Amann et al. [46], who found that MICB mismatching was associated with decreased disease‐free survival and graft‐versus‐host disease and relapse‐free survival in 9/10 HLA‐matched haematopoietic stem cell transplantations. Given that the majority of rejections in our cohort were TCMR, it is possible that the observed association reflects mechanisms more relevant to cell‐mediated rejection, although further studies are needed to explore this possibility.

The evidence about the role of HLA‐G 3′ UTR 14 bp polymorphism in rejection after KT is contradictory, with some works indicating recipient or donor ins/ins genotype as a risk factor [24, 26], while others suggest del/del [22] or ins/del [23]. We did not observe any 14 bp genotype associations in our cohort, but mismatching was associated with AR. We also observed recipient MICA A5.1 variant as a risk factor for AR, which differs from the observation by Tonnerre et al. [15], where MICA A5.1 in donors was associated with anti‐MICA sensitisation and proteinuria after KT. However, our endpoint was acute rejection, predominantly TCMR. Tonnerre et al. have observed reduced soluble MICA (sMICA) shedding by the A5.1 variant [15, 47, 48]. Binding of sMICA to NKG2D has been shown to cause downregulation of the receptor and consequent impaired T cell and NK cell activation [49, 50]. The higher risk of AR associated with recipient A5.1 observed in our cohort could thus result from the impaired immune cell modulation due to reduced release of sMICA. This would be consistent with previous observations of a correlation between the presence of sMICA and a lower incidence of rejection in transplantation [50, 51].

Confirming our hypothesis, the risk score was statistically significantly associated with rejection‐free survival and effectively distinguished high and low risk transplantations. This association was independent of the traditional HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐DRB1 matching or HLA eplet matching and remained when adjusting for the extended HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, ‐DRB1, ‐DQB1 matching, suggesting that the association of nHM is not explained by LD between the classical HLA and nHM genes and thus by extended classical HLA matching. Competing risks analysis indicated that graft loss and death are unlikely to meaningfully bias the primary association. The score's added value over traditional matching methods was more pronounced in transplants with poor classical HLA matching compared to transplants with good matching. Our results suggest that poor classical HLA matching combined with an unfavourable non‐classical HLA/MIC risk score may result in an inferior rejection‐free survival.

Despite these findings, there are some limitations to our study. As high‐resolution allele typing was inferred by imputation from SNP data, there is a possibility of about 1%–2% error rate particularly in rare alleles, despite our analysis being based on dense MHC SNP data and highly accurate, population‐specific imputation models trained on clinical‐grade reference [34, 36]. We also acknowledge the limitation due to the lack of data for donor‐specific antibodies (DSA), which are known to play an important role in ABMR [52]. The absence of DSA data may limit the ability to fully characterise the recipients' immunological risk profile, which could influence the observed associations between the nHM risk score and acute rejection. Future replication studies should incorporate DSA status for a more comprehensive evaluation. Another limitation is the absence of PIRCHE scores for indirect T‐cell allorecognition in our analysis. Given that the majority of rejections in our cohort were T‐cell–mediated, PIRCHE could have enhanced the evaluation of HLA‐related alloimmune risk and provided a more complete understanding of the pathways contributing to acute rejection in this population.

Furthermore, the replication dataset in our study was relatively small and derived from a single, genetically relatively homogeneous Finnish population with distinct LD patterns, particularly within the MHC region, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to more diverse transplant settings. In addition, inclusion criterion of a p‐value below 0.05 was applied to select the score variables, and it remains to be confirmed whether all the variables identified in this study are truly the ones contributing to AR. Although time‐dependent discrimination metrics improved modestly when adding the risk score to the models, these differences were not statistically significant in the replication cohort. Taken together, larger and more heterogeneous multicentre cohorts will be required to verify the generalisability of our results and to further validate the oligogenic risk score. Recent research has also indicated a putative role for polymorphisms in the receptors interacting with the non‐classical HLA and MIC, for example NKG2A, NKG2C and NKG2D [53, 54]. However, these genes were not considered in the present study, which could have provided additional insights. Clearly, other genome‐wide associations must be included in future scores.

The statistically significant risk factor identified in this study, based on variables showing single associations, implies that while individual polymorphisms of the non‐classical HLA and MIC genes have small effects on time to AR, their combined impact can be more informative. This combined effect is comparable to the influence of classical HLA mismatches, highlighting the potential importance of considering non‐classical HLA and MIC genes in the transplantation setting. By incorporating these genes into risk assessment models, we could improve our understanding of the factors contributing to AR and enhance the precision of transplant compatibility evaluations. Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger cohorts and including additional polymorphisms, such as those in the NKG2 receptor family and other genomic markers shown to be associated with AR, to further refine our understanding of immune responses in transplantation.
