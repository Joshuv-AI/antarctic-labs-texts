Therapeutic vaccines targeting solid tumors: A series of clinical trial analysis over the past decade
Authors: Fengqi Qiu, Yilan Sun, Darren Wan-Teck Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Decade-long trials show cancer vaccines are safe, immunogenic, but weak as monotherapy.•ELISPOT-measured vaccine T-cell responses fail to predict clinical benefit.•mRNA vaccines and checkpoint inhibitor combinations are emerging translational strategies.•Future progress requires neoantigen personalization and functional immune biomarkers.

Introduction

Therapeutic cancer vaccines represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy designed to activate and expand tumor-specific immune responses in patients with established malignancies, in contrast to prophylactic vaccines that prevent virus-associated cancers [1]. Over the past decade, remarkable advances in tumor immunology, antigen discovery, and molecular delivery technologies have revitalized the field, leading to renewed clinical interest in vaccine-based approaches for solid tumors [2,3]. These vaccines aim primarily to stimulate cytotoxic CD8⁺ and helper CD4⁺ T cell responses capable of recognizing and eradicating tumor cells [4].

Solid tumors, however, pose substantial challenges for vaccine efficacy due to their immunosuppressive microenvironment, heterogeneity in antigen presentation, and mechanisms of immune evasion [5]. To address these barriers, diverse vaccine platforms have been investigated, including peptide-based formulations, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, virus vector–based systems, nucleic acid vaccines (RNA and DNA), and inactivated whole tumor cell vaccines [6]. Among these modalities, peptide vaccines have a long history and are most extensively studied, whereas mRNA-based vaccines have recently gained substantial traction, owing to critical breakthroughs in RNA stabilization and lipid nanoparticle delivery technology [7].

Despite frequent induction of measurable T cell immune responses, clinical outcomes have been modest, and durable objective responses remain uncommon [8,9]. The limited correlation between vaccine-induced immune activation and tumor regression highlights a critical need for improved understanding of immunological correlates of efficacy and predictive biomarkers [10]. Moreover, integration with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has emerged as a rational combinatorial strategy, given their synergistic potential to overcome tumor-induced immune suppression [11].

This analysis provides a systematic evaluation of therapeutic cancer vaccine trials targeting solid tumors published between 2015 and 2025. By examining 86 clinical studies encompassing multiple vaccine modalities, stages of development, and patient populations, this study aims to characterize evolving trends in vaccine design, clinical efficacy, immune response correlations, and safety profiles. Through this comprehensive assessment, we seek to clarify the progress achieved and identify key challenges that must be addressed to optimize the clinical utility of cancer vaccines in solid tumors.

Methods

Literature search and selection strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CENTRAL Cochrane to identify clinical trials reporting the efficacy of therapeutic vaccines to treat tumors published between January 1, 2015, and August 31, 2025. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords to capture studies on therapeutic vaccines for solid tumors. The core search query was: ("solid tumor"[Mesh] OR "Tumor" OR "Cancer") AND ("Therapeutic vaccine"[Mesh] OR "vaccine") AND ("Clinical Trial"[Mesh]) AND ("2015/01/01″[PDAT]: "2025/08/31"[PDAT]).

The study selection process adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. After removing duplicate records, the identified citations were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Clinical trials that evaluated a therapeutic vaccine intervention in patients with solid tumors were selected for full-text review. Studies were excluded according to the following predefined criteria: (1) focusing on hematological malignancies; (2) investigating prophylactic (e.g., preventative) vaccines; (3) involving non-cancer vaccines; (4) being non-clinical studies (e.g., pre-clinical or in vitro research), case reports, or reviews.

Following this screening process, a total of 86 clinical studies were deemed to meet all inclusion criteria and were subsequently included in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data extraction

Data were systematically extracted from all eligible studies. Based on vaccine platform, the included trials were categorized into RNA vaccines, DNA vaccines, peptide vaccines, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and inactivated tumor cell vaccines. For each category, the following parameters were summarized: the number of studies, the clinical trial phase, the proportion of personalized versus off-the-shelf vaccines, the proportion of studies enrolling patients with advanced tumors for palliative treatment, and the proportion of studies involving resected patients in the adjuvant setting. Additionally, the proportion of studies designed to activate T-cells or those targeting B-cell activation was determined.

Data regarding patients’ outcomes were extracted from the included publications. For studies investigating vaccines as a palliative intervention in advanced tumors, the following data were analyzed: the total number of patients enrolled in each vaccine category and the best overall response (BOR) according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. The number and proportion of patients achieving a complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD) were extracted. The disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients with CR, PR, or SD (DCR = [CR + PR + SD] / total evaluable patients), was calculated for each group. In studies evaluating vaccines as an adjuvant therapy following resection, the number and proportion of patients with and without relapse were recorded.

Furthermore, the number and proportion of patients demonstrating a positive T-cell response via ELISPOT assay following vaccination were extracted from each study where available. The definition of a positive ELISPOT response varies across studies. Among the 86 included studies, 51 utilized the ELISPOT assay to evaluate vaccine-induced T-cell immune responses. Regarding the criteria for a positive response, 7 studies defined it as a ≥ 3-fold increase in spot-forming units (SFU) compared to the control group, while 19 studies utilized a 2-fold increase standard. Additionally, 18 studies defined a positive response as a significant increase compared with a negative control. In others 6 studies, 5 studies required an absolute value of 10 SFUs higher than the control, 1 study set the threshold at an absolute value exceeding the control by 50 SFUs, and 1 study not clearly indicate the positive standard. The correlation between the rate of T-cell response and the DCR was assessed across the total included cohorts and within vaccine subgroups using Pearson correlation analysis.

Data on adverse events (AEs) were extracted from the safety-evaluable cohort of each study. AEs were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE version 4 or 5). The number of patients experiencing AEs of grade 1–2, grade 3–4, and grade 5 was summarized for each vaccine group. Severe adverse events (SAEs) were specifically analyzed, and the proportion of patients with SAEs was calculated. SAEs were defined as any event fulfilling one or more of the following criteria: death, a life-threatening situation, inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, a persistent or significant disability/incapacity, a congenital anomaly, or an important medical event that may have jeopardized the patient or required intervention to prevent one of the aforementioned outcomes.

Finally, we analyzed the incidence of specific AEs occurring in >1 % of patients within each vaccine cohort. For these AEs, the number and proportion of affected patients were calculated. Concurrently, the reported incidence rate for each specific AE within its respective cohort, as provided by the study, was also compiled.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.4. Categorical variables, including clinical trial characteristics (e.g., vaccine platform, clinical phase, personalized nature, therapeutic setting) and patient outcomes (e.g., BOR, relapse status, AE grades), were summarized using frequencies and percentages. For the analysis of efficacy in advanced tumors, the DCR was calculated for each vaccine group as (Number of patients with CR + PR + SD)/(Total number of response-evaluable patients). The correlation between the proportion of patients with a positive T-cell ELISPOT response and the DCR across studies was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. In the safety analysis, the incidence of each AE was calculated in two ways: 1) as the proportion of patients experiencing the event within the respective safety-evaluable cohort, and 2) as the reported incidence rate for the event within the cohort, as extracted from the primary studies. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overview of included trials on solid tumor vaccines

From January 2015 to August 2025, a total of 86 published clinical trials investigating vaccines targeting solid tumors were included. Patient recruitment across these studies Nspanned from 2003, beginning with a peptide-based vaccine trial, to 2024 (supplementary Table 1). Over the past decade, peptide-based vaccines represented the most extensively investigated modality, with 41 trials, followed by dendritic cell (DC) based vaccines (n = 14) and virus vector-based vaccines (n = 10). mRNA-based vaccines, facilitated by recent advances in RNA delivery technologies, have rapidly emerged, with 8 trials conducted within the last five years. DNA vaccines were investigated in 7 studies, whereas inactivated whole tumor cell vaccines were assessed in 4 trials. The others include 2 studies, one is based on autologous B cell vaccine, and another is targeting the activation of GM-CSF based vaccine. Most trials were early-phase studies, with Phase I accounting for 48 (56 %), followed by 22 Phase I-II (26 %), 15 Phase II (17 %), and only one Phase III trial (Table 1). Further, Our analysis revealed that the vast majority of the evaluated clinical trials utilized single-arm designs. Specifically, all trials investigating RNA-based (n = 8) and DNA-based (n = 7) vaccine platforms were exclusively single-arm studies. In contrast, controlled trial designs were implemented in only a small subset of other modalities: 5 out of 41 peptide-based studies, 1 out of 14 dendritic cell DC-based studies, 1 out of 4 whole-cell studies, and 1 out of 10 viral vector-based studies incorporated a comparative control arm (Supplementary Table 4). Regarding development strategies, off-the-shelf vaccines comprised 62 studies (72 %), while personalized vaccines accounted for 24 studies (28 %). Among the personalized design studies, mRNA vaccines represented the highest proportion (50 %) (Table 1). Regarding immunological mechanisms, 97 % of vaccine designs aimed to elicit T cell–mediated immune responses, including both CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ subsets, whereas only three studies focused on activating B cell-mediated immunity (Table 1).

In terms of patient populations, 68 (79 %) studies enrolled patients with advanced disease, whereas 18 (21 %) studies investigated adjuvant vaccination following surgical tumor resection. Moreover, 23 studies (27 %) evaluated vaccines in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Notably, within the mRNA vaccine group, the proportion reached 75 %, reflecting the highest frequency of ICI combination strategies in recent years' vaccine studies (Table 1).

The vaccine-induced cellular immune response was also assessed in these studies. A primary method for this assessment was the ELISPOT assay, which was used to measure T cell IFN-γ secretion. We observed that ELISPOT was the predominant method for evaluating vaccine-specific T-cell responses, employed in 59.3 % (51/86) of the analyzed studies (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, we identified that multi-parameter flow cytometry was the second most prevalent methodology, employed in 24.4 % (21/86) of the studies. This approach primarily evaluates markers such as CD107a, IFN-γ, and TNF-α to characterize the polyfunctional profile of vaccine-specific T-cell activity. Among the 1781 patients analyzed, 1189 exhibited a positive cellular immune response, corresponding to a response rate of 67 % (Table 4). To evaluate the clinical relevance of these findings, we investigated the correlation between cellular immune response rates and therapeutic efficacy. The analysis revealed no significant correlation between ELISPOT positivity and the objective response rate (ORR) (Fig. 1b) or disease control rate (DCR) (Fig. 1c). Similarly, there was no significant association between ELISPOT non-response rate and the progressive disease rate (PDR) (Fig. 1d). Additionally, the best overall response was analyzed and stratified by vaccine type, with subgroups categorized as mRNA (Fig. 1e), DNA (Fig. 1g), peptide (Fig. 1i), DC (Fig. 1k), virus vector (Fig. 1m), and inactivated tumor cell based vaccine (Fig. 1o). Correspondingly, we analyzed the correlation of DCR and immune responder status across vaccine subgroups. The results also demonstrated that the ELISPOT positive rate within each subgroup was not significantly correlated with DCR (Fig. 1f; h; j; l; n). These results suggest that the ELISPOT assay for T-cell immune response lacks both Positive Predictive Value (PPV) and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) for vaccine therapy outcomes. However, we were unable to extract and analyze data stratified by different tumor types to evaluate the association between ELISPOT responses and clinical outcomes of tumor vaccine therapy. This implies that the quantity of IFN-γ-secreting T-cells, measured peripherally, is an inadequate surrogate for anti-tumor activity within the tumor microenvironment. Treatment-related adverse events

The safety and tolerability profiles of the vaccines were evaluated across 79 studies encompassing five platforms: mRNA, DNA, Peptide, Dendritic Cell (DC), and Virus Vector. The mRNA vaccine cohort comprised 7 studies with 233 patients, of whom 94.8 % experienced AEs. In the DNA vaccine cohort (7 studies; 261 patients), 61.3 % experienced AEs. The peptide, DC, and virus based vaccine cohorts included 41 (n = 1304), 14 (n = 253), and 10 (n = 273) studies, with AE rates of 81.6 %, 78.3 %, and 77.7 %, respectively. A comparative analysis of AE severity profiles revealed that mild (Grade 1–2) AEs were the most common across all vaccine types. The incidence of these events was as follows: mRNA, 76 %; DNA, 52.5 %; peptide, 69.5 %; DC, 69.6 %; and virus vector, 73.6 % (Fig. 2a). Higher-grade adverse events (Grade 3–4) were generally less common, with frequencies ranging from 3.7 % to 11.6 %, except in the case of mRNA vaccines, which had a higher incidence of 18.9 %. Grade 5 AEs were rare (≤2 %), occurring only in the peptide and viral vector cohorts. Similarly, the reported rates of serious adverse events (SAEs) were low, with the highest incidence observed in the RNA vaccine group (8 %) (Fig. 2a). An analysis of adverse events with an incidence of greater than 1 % was performed for each subgroup. In the mRNA vaccine group, the most frequent AEs were pyrexia (52.4 %), chills (37.8 %), and fatigue (23.2 %), followed by injection site reactions (21 %), nausea (17.2 %), and headache (15.9 %) (Fig. 2b). The DNA vaccine group was characterized primarily by injection site reactions (43.5 %), with lower rates of fatigue (12.5 %) and pruritus (5.1 %) (Fig. 2c). Dendritic cell (DC) vaccines were associated with a relatively low overall frequency of adverse events compared to other vaccine platforms. The most prevalent AEs were fatigue (13.7 %), followed by pyrexia (7.9 %) (Fig. 2d). Peptide vaccines showed a high frequency of injection site reactions (55.1 %), in addition to anemia (27.2 %) and fatigue (17.8 %) (Fig. 2e). Virus vector vaccines were most frequently associated with fatigue (45.9 %) and injection site reactions (38.3 %); one case of pulmonary embolism (3.2 %) was documented as a severe event (Fig. 2f). Whole tumor cell vaccines were predominantly associated with injection site reactions (48.3 %) and fatigue (15.6 %) (Fig. 2g). Despite some heterogeneity in safety profiles across the platforms, injection site reactions, fatigue, and pyrexia were consistently the most common adverse events.

Adjuvant

The intrinsic limitations of peptide antigens—specifically their low molecular weight, rapid systemic clearance, and lack of innate danger signals—necessitate the use of potent adjuvants to bridge the gap between antigen delivery and the initiation of a robust T-cell response. As the use of adjuvants are common in peptide vaccines for augmenting immunogenicity, we analyzed the utilization of adjuvants in peptide-based cancer vaccines. Among 41 peptide vaccine studies, 14 employed Montanide ISA 51 (ISA 51) as an adjuvant, while 8 utilized poly-ICLC, a Th1/Th2-driving adjuvant. 4 studies used GM-CSF as an adjuvant, and 2 employed the water-in-oil Montanide ISA 720VG vehicle. One study each used the following adjuvants: OPT821(a potent immunological adjuvant derived from the soapbark tree Quillaja saponaria), CV8102 (a RNA adjuvant, developed by CureVac AG), adenovirus serotype-5-based vector, Raffinose Fatty Acid Sulphate Ester (a sulpholipopolysaccharide), MIS416 (a nontoxic microparticle adjuvant derived from Propionibacterium acnes that activates immune responses via the NOD2 and TLR9 pathways), glucopyranosyl lipid A (GLA, a synthetic TLR4 agonist adjuvant), OK-432(a lyophilized biological preparation containing cells of the Streptococcus pyogenes Su strain), aluminum phosphate, as well as Amph-modified Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonistic CpG-7909 DNA.

Discussion

This systematic evaluation of 85 clinical trials investigating therapeutic cancer vaccines targeting solid tumors over the past decade provides a comprehensive overview of evolving design strategies, efficacy outcomes, and safety profiles within this rapidly advancing field. The analysis demonstrates that the field remains predominantly composed of early-phase investigations, with peptide-based vaccines maintaining a central role. Concurrently, there has been a marked expansion in the clinical translation of mRNA-based vaccine platforms and a growing integration of vaccine regimens with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), collectively reflecting the ongoing paradigm shift toward synergistic combination immunotherapy strategies.

The pooled analysis demonstrated a disease control rate (DCR) of 53 % and an objective response rate (ORR) of 18 % (CR+PR) across the included studies, indicating moderate antitumor activity. These results suggest that although cancer vaccines may seldom lead to substantial tumor shrinkage as monotherapy, they can effectively stabilize the disease, potentially by inducing a state of immune equilibrium. These findings align with the understanding that the principal benefit of cancer vaccines may lie in promoting long-term immune surveillance rather than eliciting immediate cytotoxic effects [1]. Nevertheless, the high recurrence rate of 52 % in the adjuvant setting highlights the persistent challenge of eliminating minimal residual disease and establishing durable protective immunity. It should also be noted, however, that substantial heterogeneity among vaccine platforms and disease contexts complicates direct interpretation of these findings.

The efficacy of tumor vaccines is fundamentally limited by the low immunogenicity of self-antigens and the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment [12]. Therefore, adjuvants are not merely additives but essential components that "prime" the innate immune system to ensure a robust adaptive response. Our analysis reveals a diverse landscape of adjuvant utilization, reflecting a strategic shift from simple antigen stabilization to sophisticated immune modulation. The frequent use of Montanide ISA 51 (n = 14) and ISA 720VG (n = 2) underscores the importance of the "depot effect" in peptide-based therapies. These water-in-oil emulsions protect short-lived peptides from enzymatic degradation and ensure a sustained release of antigens to local antigen-presenting cells (APCs), effectively prolonging the "window of opportunity" for immune recognition [13]. Poly-ICLC (n = 8) is frequently used and acts as a potent TLR3 and MDA5 agonist, creating an inflammatory milieu rich in Type I interferons [14]. This is crucial for "cold" tumors, as it promotes the cross-presentation of exogenous peptides onto MHC class I molecules. Similarly, the use of CpG-7909 (TLR9 agonist) and GLA (TLR4 agonist) demonstrates a clear intent to mimic pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) to activate dendritic cells (DCs) more effectively than traditional mineral salts like aluminum phosphate, which primarily drive Th2-mediated humoral immunity and are often insufficient for clearing malignancies. The utilization of CV8102 (RNA adjuvant) represents a modern approach where the delivery vehicle itself acts as a signal. RNA-based adjuvants not only provide the necessary inflammatory signals via TLR7/8 but also may offer a more favorable safety profile compared to permanent oil-based depots, potentially reducing local injection-site reactions [15]. Ultimately, the ongoing evolution toward precision adjuvancy underscores a critical need for future research to focus on bioengineered agents capable of not only potent immune activation but also targeted remodeling of the suppressive tumor microenvironment.

In this systematic review, we observed that ELISPOT was the predominant methodology for evaluating vaccine-specific T-cell responses. While the high sensitivity of ELISPOT for detecting IFN-γ-secreting T cells makes it a mainstay for initial immunogenicity screening, its correlation with clinical prognosis remains poorly defined. Crucially, our findings reveal a significant dissociation between immunogenicity (as measured via ELISPOT) and actual clinical efficacy. This indicates that a robust T-cell response detected in the peripheral blood does not necessarily translate into objective tumor regression or tangible survival benefits. A primary reason for this lack of correlation is ELISPOT’s inability to account for the concomitant induction of immunosuppressive subsets. Evidence suggests that tumor vaccination can inadvertently lead to the activation and expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [16]. These cells can abrogate the anti-tumor effects of vaccine-induced effector T cells, yet their presence would remain undetected in a standard IFN-γ ELISPOT assay [17]. This paradox suggests that a "positive" ELISPOT result may not always translate to clinical benefit if a high Treg ratio is present within the tumor microenvironment [18]. This paradox underscores the necessity of monitoring suppressive subsets alongside effector responses to accurately assess the "net" immunomodulatory effect of a vaccine. To overcome the inherent limitations of single-parameter assays, recent clinical efforts have shifted toward multidimensional biomarkers. For instance, multiplex cytokine profiling and polyfunctional T-cell analysis (e.g., co-expression of IFN-γ, TNF-a, and IL-2) via flow cytometry provide a more nuanced understanding of T-cell quality rather than just quantity [19,20]. Furthermore, T-cell receptor (TCR) sequencing has been employed to track the clonal expansion and persistence of vaccine-specific clones in the peripheral blood and tumor tissue, offering a high-resolution map of the immune repertoire[21]. Although some studies have evaluated exploratory markers like serum HMGB1 or LDH levels as indirect indicators of immunogenic cell death, these results are often not formally reported due to their exploratory nature or lack of statistical power. Nevertheless, the integration of these multifaceted biomarkers is essential for identifying reliable correlates of protection in future tumor vaccine development. Beyond biomarker sensitivity, the structural complexity of the TME serves as a final barrier to clinical efficacy. Various immunosuppressive mechanisms can render even vaccine-activated T cells dysfunctional [22]. Moreover, vaccine-induced T cells often exhibit an exhausted phenotype or become sequestered in the tumor microenvironment[23], the lack of correlation between peripheral ELISPOT responses and clinical outcomes raises critical questions regarding the utility of this biomarker for predicting therapeutic success [24]. Future research must therefore shift its focus toward evaluating the functional quality, phenotypic stability, and trafficking capabilities of vaccine-induced T cells, rather than merely measuring the magnitude of the immune response.

The surge in mRNA vaccine trials during the last five years parallels advances in mRNA stabilization and lipid nanoparticle delivery systems, which have demonstrated robust antigen presentation and favorable safety in both infectious and oncologic contexts [25,26]. Preliminary data from individualized mRNA neoantigen vaccines combined with PD-1 blockade in melanoma and pancreatic cancer have shown promising early clinical responses [27,28]. These findings, together with our analysis, reveal that vaccines can increase the infiltration of T-cells into the tumor (priming the immune system), while ICIs can overcome the local immunosuppressive mechanisms that render these T-cells inert [29,30]. The synergy of this approach is an area of intense investigation and holds considerable promise for improving patient outcomes.

Among the vaccine modalities, peptide-based platforms remain the most frequently tested, supported by established safety and manufacturing scalability. However, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines continue to exhibit variability in potency due to differences in cell source, antigen loading, and maturation protocols [31,32]. DNA vaccines, while conceptually appealing for their stability and safety, have shown limited immunogenicity in humans, although novel electroporation-assisted delivery technologies may improve their performance [33]. The viral vector based vaccines retain strong immunostimulatory properties but face challenges related to pre-existing vector immunity and manufacturing constraints [34]. Meanwhile, the rapid emergence of mRNA based vaccines, with a significant number of trials initiated in just the last five years, highlights a paradigm shift in the field and shows a strong trend towards personalized strategies within the mRNA platform, representing 57 % of such studies. This suggests a move towards personal neoantigen-targeting therapies designed to elicit a more specific and potent T-cell response, a primary goal for the vast majority of all vaccine designs studied.

Overall, adverse event (AE) rates across vaccine types were generally mild to moderate, with pyrexia, injection-site reactions, and fatigue being most common. Nonetheless, distinct safety profiles have emerged for each platform. mRNA vaccines, while showing promise, also exhibited the highest rates of overall, Grade 3–4, and serious AEs, driven by systemic inflammatory symptoms like pyrexia and chills. This is consistent with the innate immune-stimulatory properties of RNA delivery systems [35,36]. However, the present included studies involved combination regimens with ICIs or chemotherapy, introducing confounding effects that limit the attribution of toxicity solely to vaccine administration. Similarly, rare but severe events, such as pulmonary embolism or grade 5 toxicities, may reflect treatment synergy rather than vaccine-specific effects.

The results collectively emphasize that while therapeutic cancer vaccines are safe and immunogenic, their clinical benefit remains modest when used as monotherapy. The integration with ICIs, radiotherapy, or other immunomodulatory agents appears critical for achieving meaningful tumor control [37]. Moreover, our findings suggest that future vaccine trials should incorporate standardized immune-monitoring platforms and adopt combinatorial designs by immune signatures predictive of response [38]. The lack of correlation between ELISPOT responses and clinical benefit highlights the urgent need for more functional immune correlates, such as single-cell transcriptomic profiling or T-cell receptor (TCR) clonality assessments. Looking ahead, the next generation of therapeutic cancer vaccines will likely leverage personalized neoantigen discovery, artificial intelligence-guided epitope prediction, and mRNA or self-amplifying RNA platforms optimized for tumor-specific delivery [39].

Several constraints warrant consideration. First, this analysis is limited to peer-reviewed publications, excluding ongoing or unpublished clinical trials. This introduces potential temporal bias and may underestimate recent advancements or efficacy data from active investigations. Second, substantial heterogeneity among vaccine platforms and disease contexts complicates direct interpretation of these findings. Furthermore, we were unable to extract and analyze data stratified by different tumor types to evaluate the association between ELISPOT responses and clinical outcomes. Therefore, whether ELISPOT positivity can predict therapeutic efficacy in patients with different tumor types receiving tumor vaccines warrants further investigation. Third, part of the analyzed clinical trials involved vaccines administered in combination with standard therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or chemotherapy, making it difficult to isolate the therapeutic contribution of the vaccine itself. Therefore, the observed efficacy outcomes may not fully represent the independent effect of vaccine therapy. Fourth, the reported adverse events include those potentially attributable to concomitant agents, particularly checkpoint inhibitors and cytotoxic drugs, thereby confounding the safety assessment. Nonetheless, these limitations do not detract from the overall validity of our findings, as the included trials collectively reflect the current clinical landscape and provide a substantial evidence base for understanding recent progress and challenges in therapeutic cancer vaccine development.

In conclusion, the field of therapeutic cancer vaccines stands at a pivotal point in development. While traditional platforms have laid a crucial foundation, emerging technologies particularly personalized mRNA-based vaccines together with an advanced understanding of tumor immunology, are driving a new era of innovation. The modest efficacy observed with vaccine monotherapy underscores the need for rationally designed combination strategies that integrate vaccines with ICI, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies to achieve synergistic immune activation and durable clinical responses. Moving forward, personalized biomarker-driven patient stratification and identifying truly immunogenic neoantigens that can elicit durable, tumor-specific T-cell responses will be essential to translating vaccine-induced immunogenicity into meaningful clinical benefit for patients with solid tumors.
