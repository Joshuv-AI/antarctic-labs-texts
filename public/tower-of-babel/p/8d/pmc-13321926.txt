Comparative Assessment of the Microbiological and Histopathological Picture of the Implantation Zone of a Nanocellulose-based Biocomposite In Vivo
Authors: SAULE AKHMETOVA, BERIK TULEUBAYEV, YEVGENIY KAMYSHANSKIY, DINA SAGINOVA, AMINA KOSHANOVA, YEKATERINA KOSSILOVA, VLADIMIR VINOKUROV, IVAN AVROMIDI, ZHANERKE AMIRKHANOVA, MIKE BARBECK, ILYA BELYAEV, DARYN DARYBAYEV, MADINA RASHOVA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimBone tissue defects remain one of the main challenges in modern traumatology, often requiring biocompatible materials to restore bone integrity and function. The development of nanostructured composites opens new opportunities for bone regeneration. In our previous study, a nanocellulose-based biocomposite showed the ability to accelerate bone repair and achieve complete closure of bone defects. The present study aimed to evaluate the effect of this material on the microbial composition and inflammatory response at the implantation site.Materials and MethodsTwenty-eight rats were used and divided into two equal groups (n=14). In group 1, the defect was filled with an autologous blood clot (ABC), and in group 2 – with a nanofibrilated cellulose-based biocomposite (NFC). The microbial composition at the implantation site was assessed by standard microbiological techniques using Chistovich yolk-salt agar, McConkey agar, blood agar, and Sabouraud medium. Histological evaluation of the inflammatory response was performed on days 14 and 30 after implantation.ResultsThe isolated microorganisms included Staphylococcus epidermidis, S. saprophyticus, S. warneri, Brevibacterium casei, B. epidermis, Rothia mucilaginosa, and R. kristinae, which represent normal flora. S. haemolyticus was isolated once. No fungal growth occurred on Sabouraud medium. Histological analysis revealed no necrosis or active inflammation. Mild lymphocytic infiltration in the NFC group on day 14 indicated a normal early post-implant reaction.ConclusionAt days 14 and 30 post-implantation of a nanofibrilated cellulose-based biocomposite, the microbiological profile of the defect site remained within physiological norms in both groups. Occasional hemolytic isolates were considered technical contamination. The nanocellulose-based biocomposite did not disrupt the local microbial balance, supporting its biocompatibility and safety for use in bone tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Today, various areas of traumatology, including adult and pediatric orthopedics, emergency traumatology, and purulent traumatology, are seeking modern and innovative solutions for bone reconstruction. This has led research toward the development of advanced bone- substitute materials, particularly biocomposites (1, 2).

Nanocellulose has emerged as a promising component in biocomposite materials owing to its favorable physicochemical properties (3, 4), including mechanical strength, and chemical inertness, and potential antimicrobial properties. Nanocellulose-based biocomposites exhibit superior mechanical performance, barrier properties, and antimicrobial effects, making them ideal candidates for a broad range of biomedical applications (5-7). Moreover, the high availability of nanocellulose from renewable sources further supports its suitability for large-scale biocomposite production (8).

The use of composite biomaterials has been associated with enhanced bone tissue regeneration rate and improved mechanical properties (9, 10). Therefore, nanocellulose-based biocomposites have great potential to become the material of choice for applications in regenerative traumatology (11, 12).

However, it is known that implants placed within the bone tissue may cause an inflammatory response, either due to the implant itself or as a result of microbial contamination (13-16). Bacterial adhesion to implants can lead to infection, with Staphylococcus species being the most common causative agents, resulting in post-implantation complications such as osteomyelitis (17, 18). Additionally, these infections are further complicated by pathogen resistance to antibiotics (19).

In our previous research, we developed and characterized a biocomposite material based on nanocellulose. Our results demonstrated its ability to achieve complete bone defect repair (20). These findings prompted further investigation of this material, with focus on the microbiological aspects of the implantation site.

The aim of the present study was to perform a microbiological and histological evaluation of the implantation site in an animal model with femoral bone defects, comparing a cellulose nanocrystal-based biocomposite with an autologous blood clot control.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of the biocomposite. In this study, we used a nanocellulose-based biocomposite composed of nanocellulose and dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (CaHPO4·2H2O). This biocomposite was developed at the Department of Colloidal Chemistry at the Gubkin Russian State University of Oil and Gas, Moscow, Russian Federation.

Surgical stage of the experiment. Twenty-eight male white outbred rats weighing 250-300 g were used in the experiment. The animals were housed under standard vivarium conditions, at a temperature of 22±2° C, relative humidity of 50-60%, with 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Karaganda Medical University (Protocol No. 18, September 20, 2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals Used for Experimental and Other Scientific Purposes and complied with the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, as well as the Order of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan dated December 11, 2020 No. KR DSM-255/2020.

Before surgery, hair was removed from the incision area to minimize the risk of complications during healing of the postoperative wound. Considering the anatomical features of the rats, the intervention was performed on the femur. The operation was performed under strict aseptic conditions.

The animals were randomly assigned to two equal groups (n=14 per group). Surgical intervention was performed under aseptic conditions in an operating room for laboratory animals. Zoletil (Virbac, Westlake, TX, USA) was used as premedication at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg intramuscularly. After reaching the surgical stage of anesthesia, the animals were fixed in a dorsal position on the operating table, and the surgical area on the anterior surface of the right thigh was treated with an antiseptic solution.

A longitudinal skin incision of approximately 10-mm length was performed, followed by blunt and sharp dissection to expose the anterior surface of the femur. In the projection of the middle third of the hip diaphysis, a standard defect with a diameter of about 2 mm and a depth of up to 1 mm was created using a dental micro-drill (21). The procedure was performed under visual control to minimize additional tissue injury.

In the first group (n=14), the defect was filled with an autologous blood clot (ABC). In the second group (n=14), the nanocellulose-based biocomposite (NFC) was implanted into the formed defect. The surgical wound was sutured in layers with nodular sutures using absorbable suture material Vicryl 5.0 (Ethicon Inc., Johnson & Johnson, Somerville, NJ, USA). All procedures were performed by a single operator to ensure standardization.

Postoperatively, animals received analgesia – intramuscular Ketonal injections (Sandoz, Ljubljana, Slovenia) at a dose of 0.04 ml/kg once daily for three days. A clinical assessment of the general condition of the experimental animals was carried out daily throughout the observation period, including body temperature, weight, motor activity, as well as wound. In the absence of signs of inflammation, healing was considered satisfactory.

Animals were euthanized on postoperative days 14 and 30 by anesthetic overdose and the operated limbs were harvested. The collected samples were recorded and sent for microbiological examination.

Media and cultivation. The preparation of nutrient media was carried out in a laboratory setting using dry industrial media and individual components, following all aseptic procedures. The following media were used in the experiment: blood agar, mannitol-salt agar, and dextrose Sabouraud dextrose agar.

Blood agar is a medium supplemented with defibrinated sheep blood (5%), designed for cultivating microorganisms from various clinical samples and their initial differentiation based on hemolysis type. Mannitol-salt agar is a selective medium prepared according to Chapman’s formulation used for selective isolation of Staphylococcus species. Sabouraud dextrose agar is used for cultivating yeast and filamentous fungi.

Seeding of surgical material on nutrient media. For microbiological examination, surgical specimens (n=14) were aseptically transferred into a sterile porcelain mortar and mechanically homogenized using a sterile pestle until a uniform consistency was achieved. Sterile physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl) was added to the homogenate at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v) to ensure appropriate dilution of the substrate for subsequent inoculation.

The resulting suspension was inoculated onto blood agar, mannitol salt agar, and Sabouraud dextrose agar. Each Petri dish was pre-labeled with sample ID, incubation conditions, and type of medium used. Blood agar and mannitol salt agar plates were incubated aerobically at 36±1 °C for 18–24 h. Sabouraud dextrose agar plates were incubated at 25±1 °C for up to 5 days to allow for the detection and growth of fungal organisms. Upon completion of incubation, macroscopic colony morphology was evaluated, and, if necessary, microscopic verification was performed.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight(MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometric analysis. Identification of microorganisms was performed using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry on a Bruker Daltonics platform (Bremen, Germany). Colonies of isolated microbes were collected from nutrient medium using a plastic microbiological loop and transferred onto a metal target plate. A saturated solution of α-hydroxycinnamic acid was then added, and after drying, the sample was analyzed in the mass spectrometer within ta protein mass range of 2–20 kD. Spectra were compared to the Bruker database, and identification reliability was assessed using score values: values between 2,300 and 3,000 indicated a highly probable identification of the strain; 2,000 to 2,299 indicated reliable identification of the genus with probable identification of the species; 1,700 to 1,999 indicated probable identification of the genus; and 0 to 1,699 indicated identification failure.

Histopathological examination. To assess overall tissue morphology, cellular composition, inflammatory response, and associated angiogenesis within the bone defect area, histological analysis was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Following macroscopic examination, representative femoral bone samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h at room temperature. Decalcification was carried out using “Biodec R” solution (Bio-Optica Milano Spa, Milan, Italy) for 24 h, followed by washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4),

After achieving optimal decalcification and softening of the bone tissue, frontal sections were made. The samples were refixed in 10% formalin at 4 °C for 24 h, washed in running tap water, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 90%, 95%, and 100%). Subsequently, the specimens were cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin.

Paraffin sections 5-μm thick were prepared using a sledge microtome (Leica SM2000R, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were rinsed three times in tap water, stained with hematoxylin for 40 sec, and then deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 96%, and 70%). Eosin staining was then performed, followed by final clearing in xylene and mounting with a permanent medium (22).

Statistical analysis. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Yates-corrected chi-square test (with continuity correction) and the Mann-Whitney U test. Multiple comparisons were performed using the Pearson chi-square test. Statistical analysis of the data was carried out using SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and STATISTICA 10 (StatSoft, Inc., TuIsa, OK, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Microbiological analysis of the implantation zone. Surgical material from the implantation zones of both groups, the NFC group and the ABC group, was analyzed. A total of 24 microbial isolates (100%) were recovered from the samples. Nine isolates (37.5%) demonstrated growth; however, no color change of the medium was observed, indicating the absence of mannitol fermentation. On blood agar, 15 isolates (62.5%) were recovered, demonstrating general bacterial growth. No yeast-like or mold-like fungal growth was detected on Sabouraud agar, indicating the absence of cultivable fungi in the samples analyzed.

Identification of isolates on a mass spectrometer revealed the presence of the following microbial species: Staphylococcus epidermitis (3 isolates, 12.5%), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (3 isolates, 12.5%), Staphylococcus warneri (2 isolates, 8.3%), Staphylococcus haemolyticus (1 isolate, 4.2%), Brevibacterium casei (4 isolates, 16.7%), Brevibacterium epidermitis (5 isolates, 20.8%), Rothia Mucilaginosa (3 isolates, 12.5%), Rothia kristinae (1 isolate, 4.2%), Arthrobacter spp. (2 isolates, 8.3%) (Figure 1).

On mannitol salt medium, bacterial colonies observed on both day 14 and day 30 were surrounded by transparent red or pale purple halos, indicating mannitol fermentation. This is a typical is characteristic of coagulase-negative staphylococci that are commonly found in the commensal skin microbiota of laboratory animals.

Comparative analysis with the ABC group revealed no statistically significant differences in the number, type, or growth characteristics of the isolated microorganisms. These findings suggest a comparable microbial landscape in the implantation zones of both experimental groups and suggest that the use of the NFC-based biocomposite does not increase the risk of bacterial contamination relative to the ABC.

These findings demonstrate limited microbial colonization of the implantation site, predominantly involving coagulase-negative staphylococci and other opportunistic bacteria, which maintained metabolic activity when cultured on selective growth media.

Comparative histomorphometric analysis of the inflammatory and necrotic pattern of the implantation zone in both experimental groups. On day 14, a single reactive cell infiltration, without evidence of necrosis, was observed at the site of the defect in both NFC and ABC groups. By day 30, none of the groups exhibited histological signs of inflammatory cell infiltration or infection, including polymorphonuclear cells, lymphocytes, macrophages, and multinucleated cells, or tissue necrosis, within the surgically created defects.

Discussion

In this in vivo study, we performed a combined microbiological and histological evaluation of bone defects after implantation of a NFC, compared with an ABC control group. This multifactorial approach allowed for a comprehensive assessment of both the biological safety of the material and the characteristics of the local tissue and microbial responses.

Our results demonstrated that NFC implantation did not lead to pathogenic microbial growth, necrosis, or suppurative inflammation. The microbial spectrum identified in both groups mainly included coagulase-negative Staphylococcus species (S. epidermidis, S. saprophyticus, S. warneri), as well as Brevibacterium and Rothia species-microorganisms that form part of the normal commensal flora of the skin and mucous membranes of laboratory animals. These findings are consistent with previous observations describing the microbial background associated with sterile orthopedic interventions (23).

The absence of fungal growth on Sabouraud agar at both 14 and 30 days post-implantation confirmed strict adherence to aseptic surgical technique and indicated that the implanted NFC material did not promote colonization by opportunistic fungi. Comparable results were reported in earlier studies involving biocompatible implant materials (24-27).

From a clinical perspective, the detection of coagulase-negative staphylococci and other commensal microorganisms warrants careful interpretation. These bacteria represent resident microbiota of the skin and mucosal surfaces, and their presence in the implantation site should not be interpreted as infection per se. Rather, limited colonization by such microorganisms may reflect a physiological microbial equilibrium at the host–implant interface. In orthopedic and dental implantology, coagulase-negative staphylococci are frequently isolated, but only virulent or biofilm-producing strains are linked with clinically relevant infections. The absence of inflammation, necrosis, or purulent changes in our specimens suggests that NFC implantation maintains microbial homeostasis and does not trigger pathogenic conversion of commensal flora. This observation underscores the biocompatibility and microbiological stability of NFC-based biomaterials, highlighting that the presence of commensal bacteria can coexist with normal tissue adaptation rather than signify infection.

Histological analysis further confirmed the favorable biological profile of NFC. On day 14, moderate focal and perifocal lymphohistiocytic infiltration was observed in the implantation zone, which gradually subsided by day 30. This transient cellular response is characteristic of early tissue adaptation to biocompatible implants. Similar early immunomodulatory patterns have been described in previous studies (28, 29). The absence of necrosis, purulent inflammation, or chronic encapsulation in both experimental groups supports the hypothesis that nanocellulose behaves as an immunologically inert material. Previous studies have demonstrated that nanocellulose-based composites exhibit excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductive properties while avoiding excessive inflammatory or fibrotic responses.

Although bacterial counts occasionally exceeded 10 CFU/ml-the conventional clinical threshold for infection-no histological or clinical evidence of infection was found. This finding suggests that, in the absence of virulent strains or tissue destruction, elevated colony numbers may still represent a physiological colonization state rather than infection (30). Growth patterns on mannitol salt agar confirmed the predominance of non-pathogenic staphylococci, forming small colonies with weak mannitol fermentation, indicative of the metabolic stability of commensal strains upon contact with biomaterials.

Clinically, the absence of local or systemic inflammatory signs, stable body temperature, preserved motor activity, and complete wound healing emphasize the biocompatibility of NFC. These results align with earlier reports demonstrating excellent tissue tolerance and structural similarity to the native extracellular matrix. Collectively, our findings corroborate previous studies showing that nanocellulose-based materials combine mechanical robustness with biological inertness, making them promising candidates for bone tissue engineering. Importantly, the lack of fungal growth and minimal bacterial colonization observed in this study further support that NFC does not disrupt the native microbiome and does not serve as a substrate for pathogenic organisms.

Study limitations. This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the observation period was limited to 14 and 30 days, which may not fully capture long-term remodeling and late host responses. Extended studies over 60-90 days including delayed histological and microbiological assessments are warranted. Second, our study did not include a comparison with clinically approved bone graft substitutes such as hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate, or collagen-based scaffolds, which would allow a more robust benchmarking of NFC performance against established biomaterials. Third, the experiments were conducted under sterile or near-sterile conditions, without simulating infected defects or systemic risk factors such as diabetes or immunosuppression. Evaluating NFC behavior under contaminated or infection-prone models would be crucial for understanding its applicability in real-world orthopedic and reconstructive scenarios.

Conclusion

This experimental in vivo study confirmed the microbiological safety and histological biocompatibility of the nanocellulose-based biocomposite (NFC) after implantation into living tissue. Throughout the observation period, the microbial profile at the implantation site remained within physiological limits, indicating that the material neither disrupted the normal microbiota nor supported pathogenic colonization. The absence of fungal growth and stable bacterial spectrum confirmed the aseptic integrity of the model and the microbiological neutrality of NFC.

Histological evaluation revealed mild, transient lymphohistiocytic infiltration at the early stages of healing, which subsided by day 30 without necrosis, fibrosis, or chronic inflammation. These findings reflect a normal tissue adaptation response and further underscore the excellent biocompatibility of the NFC. Comparable healing outcomes between the NFC and control groups confirm that the material integrates safely with host tissues while maintaining local biological stability.

Collectively, these findings highlight the biosafety, biological inertness, and regenerative potential of nanocellulose-based biocomposites. These properties make NFC a promising candidate for use as a scaffolding material for bone tissue engineering and reconstructive surgery. Future research should focus on long-term integration, mechanical stability under load-bearing conditions, and behavior in contaminated or biologically compromised environments.
