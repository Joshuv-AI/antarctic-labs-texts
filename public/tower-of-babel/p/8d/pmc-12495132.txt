Diagnosing and Managing Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher Disease: A Pediatric Struggle
Authors: Sajjad Ahmed Khan, Naphibanroi Lamar, Ishan Shrestha, Sagar Bhagat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrompt diagnosis of rare genetic conditions like Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher Disease enables timely care. Coordinated, multidisciplinary support and proactive prevention, particularly of issues like aspiration pneumonia, are vital to enhancing quality of life and outcomes, even as the disease continues to progress.

Introduction

The PLP1 gene codes for proteolipid protein, a crucial component constituting nearly half of the proteins in myelin and representing its most abundant element. Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher Disease (PMD) is a rare X‐linked hypomyelinating leukodystrophy resulting from mutations in the PLP1 gene. These mutations disrupt the myelination process of axons by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system [1]. The disease manifests across a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from the severe connatal form to milder adult‐onset types.

The connatal variant presents at birth with pronounced hypotonia, seizures, severe developmental delays, and respiratory issues such as stridor. Most individuals with this form do not attain independent walking or speech [2]. The classical form typically appears within the first year of life with hypotonia and delayed motor development. An intermediate form may show overlapping characteristics. Disease severity often correlates with the specific type of PLP1 mutation: null mutations lead to total loss of function and milder symptoms, gene duplications cause PLP1 overexpression impairing myelin formation, and missense mutations result in misfolded proteins, oligodendrocyte death, and pronounced hypomyelination [3].

PMD diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical findings, neuroimaging, and genetic analysis. MRI plays a central role, often revealing hallmark hypomyelination patterns with T2‐weighted images showing diffuse white matter hyperintensity across the cerebrum, brainstem, and cerebellum [4], along with the classic tigroid or leopard‐skin appearance due to islands of preserved myelin [5]. Confirmation is achieved through genetic testing identifying mutations in the PLP1 gene on chromosome Xq22.2 [6].

Management remains largely supportive, focusing on symptom control and prevention of complications. While progress in gene therapy and stem cell research offers future potential, these advances have yet to reach clinical application [7].

This case highlights a male infant who began showing developmental regression at 5 months, along with feeding difficulties and respiratory problems. Despite repeated medical evaluations and MRI findings indicating leukodystrophy, a conclusive PMD diagnosis was only made at 1 year. The delayed diagnosis followed a sequence of misdiagnoses, consideration of metabolic disorders, inadequate management, and avoidable complications, particularly aspiration pneumonia, which ultimately proved fatal. This case underscores the critical role of early genetic testing, the consequences of limited access to such resources, and the significance of a coordinated multidisciplinary approach for symptom management and complication prevention—measures that can extend lifespan and enhance the quality of life in individuals affected by PMD.

Case Presentations

Case History and Examinations

A 4‐year‐old male child was admitted to the emergency department after being transferred from a community hospital. He presented with a 5‐day history of fever, which was acute in onset and intermittent, with the maximum documented temperature reaching 104°F. The fever was not associated with chills or rigors and was briefly relieved by medication. In addition to the fever, the child developed a non‐productive cough. He also exhibited shortness of breath, which was attributed to aspiration during an attempt to drink medicinal syrup. There was no history of loss of consciousness, abnormal body movements, nasal bleeding, ear discharge, vomiting, or loose stools. On examination, the child appeared ill, cachexic, and had facial dysmorphism with low‐set ears. Anthropometric measurements revealed that his growth parameters were significantly below the third percentile in all domains. There were no signs of pallor, icterus, lymphadenopathy, cyanosis, clubbing, edema, or dehydration. The child had been born at full term on January 25, 2020, and although he initially achieved developmental milestones, regression started at 5 months of age with signs of hypotonia, poor head control, and delayed motor milestones. This led to multiple clinical evaluations and investigations, which initially resulted in misdiagnoses. Ultimately, the clinical presentation suggested an inborn error of metabolism, prompting further diagnostic investigations.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Given the clinical presentation, the differential diagnosis considered included PMD, inborn errors of metabolism, and other neurological disorders such as congenital hypothyroidism. The child underwent a tandem mass spectrometry (TMS) panel, which excluded common metabolic causes such as amino acid, organic acid, fatty acid oxidation, and purine metabolism disorders. An MRI of the brain revealed signs of leukodystrophy, which prompted further investigation into a potential genetic cause. At the age of 1 year, genetic testing revealed a heterozygous 451.48 kb duplication in the PLP1 gene on chromosome Xq22, which confirmed the diagnosis of PMD. In addition, imaging of the chest was performed, which confirmed the presence of aspiration pneumonia (Figure 1). The child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) with respiratory distress, hypoxia, tachypnea, and subcostal retractions. Mechanical ventilation was initiated to assist with oxygenation (Figure 2), and antibiotics were administered to treat the aspiration pneumonia. Supportive care, including nutritional support, was provided as part of the intensive care management.

Results (Outcomes and Follow‐Up)

Despite the initiation of mechanical ventilation, antibiotics, and other intensive care measures, the child's condition worsened over the course of the 5‐day hospital stay. The progression of aspiration pneumonia, compounded by the neurological decline associated with PMD, led to the child's death on the fifth day of admission. The multisystemic deterioration, including worsening respiratory symptoms and feeding difficulties, contributed to this poor outcome.

This case highlights the challenges posed by the diagnosis and management of rare genetic disorders, particularly when initial misdiagnoses delay the correct treatment. The child's progressive developmental regression, along with multisystemic involvement, underscores the importance of early and accurate diagnosis, as well as the need for multidisciplinary care to manage complications effectively. In this case, PMD, confirmed through genetic testing, caused significant developmental delays and spasticity, contributing to the child's decline. Although there are no curative treatments for PMD, early intervention could help mitigate some of the symptoms and prevent complications such as aspiration pneumonia, which was a key contributor to the child's death. This case also emphasizes the importance of improving awareness of rare genetic disorders in pediatric populations and the need for adequate preventive care, such as strategies to reduce aspiration risks, to improve outcomes for affected children.

Discussion

This case presents a rare instance of PMD in a male child who experienced developmental regression, feeding difficulties, and recurrent respiratory complications. Despite multiple healthcare interventions, the definitive diagnosis was delayed until 1 year of age, largely due to initial misdiagnoses and limited access to advanced genetic testing. The child ultimately succumbed to aspiration pneumonia at the age of five, highlighting not only the progressive nature of PMD but also the critical gaps in early recognition and supportive care. The case is clinically significant due to its fatal outcome from preventable complications, underscoring the importance of timely genetic evaluation and proactive multidisciplinary management in rare leukodystrophies.

Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher Disease is a rare X‐linked hypomyelinating leukodystrophy caused by mutations in the PLP1 gene. These genetic mutations lead to a fault in the axonal myelination of the oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system [1]. This leads to varying degrees of manifestations with a large array of severity. In this case, the patient was found to have a PLP1 gene duplication, aligning with a severe hypomyelinating phenotype. Progressive neurologic dysfunction, combined with multisystem involvement including feeding difficulties and compromised respiratory coordination, laid the groundwork for the fatal complication of aspiration pneumonia.

The current literature on PMD has failed to highlight the importance of unrecognized gastroesophageal reflux, dysphagia, neuromuscular weakness, and their link with aspiration and recurrent respiratory infections. The necessity of active respiratory care, in addition to neurological support, is rarely mentioned, and the impact of respiratory complications from dysphagia is severely underestimated. The untimely demise of this case stands as an important reminder of the extent of systemic vulnerability in these patients, extending beyond traditional neurological decline, and reminds us of the value of a multisystemic approach to treatment and complication prevention in the long‐term care of a case with PMD.

Standard PMD care must evolve to routinely include feeding evaluation, respiratory physiotherapy, and anticipatory guidance on infection risks. As outlined by Birnkrant in the context of pediatric neuromuscular diseases, strategies such as early dysphagia screening, multidisciplinary coordination, and proactive respiratory management—including airway clearance techniques and non‐invasive ventilation—are vital. These steps not only improve quality of life but can meaningfully extend survival in PMD [8].

The lack of availability of genetic testing was a key contributor to the delayed diagnosis in this case. Advanced molecular diagnostics, including PLP1 gene analysis, is often inaccessible in resource‐limited settings, leading to prolonged diagnostic uncertainty, misdiagnoses, inappropriate interventions, and preventable complications. Adding to this issue is the lack of structured counseling for caregivers, which can result in missed opportunities for early supportive care and complication prevention. There is an urgent need not only to expand equitable access to genetic testing but also to implement routine caregiver education and multidisciplinary planning as essential components of long‐term management in PMD [9].

In comparison with other reported cases of PMD, this case adds significant value by highlighting the consequences of delayed genetic diagnosis and insufficient multisystem management. For instance, Jang et al. reported a case of a 1‐month‐old male infant who presented with early‐onset nystagmus and developmental delay. With timely supportive interventions, including physical rehabilitation, the patient achieved motor milestones such as sitting with minimal support and grasping objects, despite having a PLP1 gene duplication [10]. In contrast, our patient never received such supportive intervention nor any physical rehabilitation, resulting in the patient being unable to attain the motor milestones and unable to sit with minimal support even at 4 years of age. Similarly, Usman et al. described an infant with early seizure‐like activity and respiratory issues who was managed proactively with tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube placement. The patient was discharged in stable condition following multidisciplinary inpatient care [11]. Unlike the aforementioned case, our patient did not receive the necessary respiratory care and protection, and thus he suffered from recurrent aspiration pneumonia, further stressing the need for early respiratory care and protection in such cases of PMD.

This case illustrates the need for broader research into systemic complications of PMD, particularly in understanding the timing and prevention of aspiration‐related events. More data are also needed on the efficacy of early gastrostomy tube placement, ventilatory support strategies, and integration of palliative care in managing complex leukodystrophies. Additionally, improving access to and affordability of genetic diagnostics should be prioritized in global health strategies, to enable earlier intervention and avoid preventable mortality in resource‐limited settings. This case serves as an important reminder that early recognition and early intervention in patients with suspected PMD can have a significant impact on both life expectancy and quality of life [9]. Aspiration pneumonia, a common and preventable complication, could have been avoided with proactive measures such as swallow assessments, feeding tube placement, and respiratory monitoring [8]. The lessons learned from this case emphasize the critical need for multidisciplinary care and early genetic testing, especially in resource‐limited settings, to avoid delays in diagnosis and improve patient outcomes. Ultimately, a more structured, early approach to managing multisystem complications could have extended the patient's life and mitigated some of the devastating impacts of this rare and progressive disease [12].

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical importance of early and accurate diagnosis in rare genetic disorders, particularly when progressive neurological decline and multisystemic involvement are present. PMD, diagnosed through genetic testing, led to significant developmental regression and spasticity in the child, ultimately contributing to his decline. Despite intensive care efforts, including mechanical ventilation and antibiotic therapy for aspiration pneumonia, the child's condition worsened due to the progressive nature of the disease and inadequate complication prevention. This case underscores the need for timely intervention, multidisciplinary care, and vigilant monitoring to manage complications like aspiration, which can significantly affect the quality of life and prognosis. Early recognition and preventive care are essential in optimizing outcomes for children with rare genetic conditions like PMD, ensuring that potential complications are managed effectively to improve both survival and quality of life.
