A versatile CRISPR-associated transposases-mediated toolkit for transcriptional regulation library
Authors: Yiwen Zhang, Hang Zhou, Xiangxiang Xu, Kaining Zhang, Guozhong Huang, Feiyu Fan, Dongdong Zhao, Qingyan Li, Xinna Zhu, Xueli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenome-scale and multigene transcriptional regulation are crucial technologies in metabolic engineering. However, in Escherichia coli, a stable and universal tool for whole-genome transcriptional activation, and an in situ tool for multigene regulation remain lacking. Here, we present CAGER, a versatile clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-associated transposases (CAST)-mediated gene regulation toolkit. Through rational mutagenesis, we mitigate the intrinsic transcriptional interference in the left end of CAST system derived from Vibrio cholerae. Using promoters or terminators as cargoes, CAGER constructs the genome-wide activation (3272 genes) or termination (3339 genes) libraries, from which new activation or inhibition targets relevant to cellular acetic acid assimilation are identified. Furthermore, with the aid of M13 phage and the promoter library, CAGER facilitates rapid in situ multigene expression diversification. Applied to lycopene synthesis, a library targeting seven genomic sites is constructed within 24 h, achieving a 73.6-fold yield increase. This work highlights the modifiability of CAST elements and broadens CAST’s application in transcriptional regulation.

Introduction

How to screen unknown effective targets and the combined regulation of known genes are both important issues in metabolic engineering. Reprogrammable clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-mediated genetic regulation has emerged as a powerful tool for synthetic biology research. Bacterial CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) is a mature technology for screening unknown effective targets toward auxotrophy [1], chemical tolerance [2], and bioproduction [3]. For bacterial CRISPR activation (CRISPRa), systems based on σ70 [4] or σ54-dependent promoters [5] were reported, while limited by promoter types, rarely reported CRISPRa systems were used for building genome-scale libraries.

Since the cellular metabolism network is highly regulated, the initial engineered strains constructed by researchers based on prior knowledge and rational design often require multiple rounds of optimization and iteration to meet production requirements, which is costly and time-consuming [6]. Achieving simultaneous optimization of multigene expression is a common technological requirement for streamlined metabolic engineering [7].

Multiplex automated genome engineering (MAGE) is a recombination-mediated genome-engineering platform that uses single-stranded DNA to introduce modifications across the Escherichia coli genome repeatedly [8]. To further improve the recombineering efficiency, selection stress such as selectable markers or CRISPR-Cas system has been combined with MAGE, leading to the development of methods such as CoS-MAGE [9], CRMAGE [10], or CFPO [11]. These methods require automated equipment or continuous manual operations to achieve dozens of rounds of electrocompetent cell preparation and electroporation for DNA introduction. However, except in cases where the aim phenotype is coupled with growth, high-yield strains often experience metabolic burden, such as excessive consumption of precursors or accumulation of products [12]. Therefore, the development of an in situ and easy-operating multigene expression regulation method with high efficiency is crucial to improve the probability of screening for target strains in a weak growth competitive position due to high production [13].

The CRISPR-associated transposases (CASTs) system is a novel gene editing tool that has been proven to be highly effective in genome-wide multiplex editing due to its independence from homologous recombination [14, 15]. Two widely used variants of the CAST system, I-F3 and V-K types, have been successfully applied in various species for gene dosage optimization via genome integration in metabolic engineering [14, 16]. ShCAST has been utilized to randomly interrupt genes encoding transcription factors guided by a crRNA library, leading to the screening of drug-resistant/sensitive genes in Pseudomonas aeruginosa [17]. In multigene regulation, adjusting the copy number of genes or gene clusters by CAST typically results in the overall increase or decrease of multigene expression [16], which does not allow for the independent simultaneous regulation of multiple genes. However, reorganizing multiple genes into one larger fragment regulated by distinct expression elements is usually limited to a relatively small library due to the low efficiency of plasmid construction and transformation in E. coli [18–20]. It is worth noting that the RE sequence of the CAST from Vibrio cholerae (VchCAST) system contains a potential transcriptional activation sequence that has been disrupted through truncation [16], suggesting the presence of hidden transcriptional interference sequences and demonstrating the modifiability of CAST elements. Accordingly, further exploration and modification of the CAST system could facilitate the development of new tools for multigene transcriptional regulation.

Here, we report the repurposing of VchCAST for the construction of transcriptional regulation libraries. First, we identified the transcriptional repression effect of VchCAST’s LE and effectively alleviated it through a comprehensive analysis and the introduction of combined mutations, which paves the way for the application of CAST in microbial transcriptional regulation. Next, VchCAST targets gene upstream regions across the E. coli genome, enabling up- or downregulation of target genes via promoter or terminator cargoes. The genome-scale transcriptional regulation libraries are proved to be convenient and effective for engineering targeted phenotypes. Finally, we employed M13 phages to transduce promoter libraries in situ, allowing VchCAST to integrate promoter variants at specific gene upstream regions and generate a diverse combinatorial library of gene expression levels. As a proof of concept, we constructed a multigene regulation library for optimizing the lycopene synthesis pathway within 24 h and successfully screened a high-yield strain.

Materials and methods

Strains and cultural conditions

Strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Escherichia coli strain DH5α that was used for general cloning was cultivated aerobically at 37°C in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth. Kanamycin (Km, 50 μg/ml), Streptomycin (Sm, 50 μg/ml), or tetracycline (Tet, 15 μg/ml) was added to the medium as required.

Plasmid construction and array synthesis

Plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Plasmids were constructed via recombination using the ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Arrays are synthesized by Genscript (Nanjing, China). Sequences of spacers and arrays are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Preparation and titer determination of phage suspension

Inoculate 50 ml LB (Km) with a fresh colony of BW25113 pir F+ carrying pM13 series phagemid, grow at 37°C, 250 rpm until just slightly turbid (OD600 < 0.05). Add 50 µl M13KO7 (New England Biolabs, N0315S) helper phage [final concentration of 1 × 108 plaque-forming unit (PFU)/ml] and grow overnight (14–18 h) at 37°C, 250 rpm. Spin culture at 4000 × g for 10 min. Transfer supernatant to a new tube and repeat spin. Pipet the upper 90% of supernatant into a new tube and filter the supernatant with 0.22 μm filters to obtain phage suspension. Phage suspension is prepared when it is in need.

Phage suspension was diluted at 109-fold and added into the culture (OD ≈ 0.1) of E. coli BW25113 pir, F+. After cultured at 37°C for 45 min, appropriate amount of the culture was spread on plates containing Km and incubated overnight at 37°C. The titer (PFU/ml) of phage suspension was calculated by multiplying the number of clones by the dilution ratio.

Design and preparation of crRNA plasmid library

The E. coli K12 MG1655 genome sequence and relevant protein- or RNA-coding gene annotation of U00096.3 were used for the crRNA library (32-mer) design. The protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) (the priority is CC > GT > CA > CT) were searched from the 100–120 bp regions upstream the start codon of genes. The spacer was 32 bp region downstream of each PAM. For a given gene, once three spacers were designed, the design process stopped. If three spacers could not be designed within that particular region, the design process also stopped. The spacers were designed to insert in the fixed sequence, “primer binding sequence-BsaI recognition site-spacer-BsaI recognition site- primer binding sequence,” i.e. “5′ AAGCCACTGTTCAGAAGCTTCAggtctcctaac -spacer- gtgatgagaccCGCGATATCATCGGACTCGGTCT 3′.” The pooled oligomers were synthesized on a microarray by GenScript Biotech (files for the oligos are available in Supplementary Data 1). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified and used to generate a plasmid library by Golden Gate Assembly [21] with BsaI-digested pCrRNA-entry as the backbone vector. We confirmed the quality of the library by Sanger sequencing of 10 colonies picked from the agar plate after transformation, 10 of which could be perfectly mapped back to the in silico design (data not shown).

Construction of genome-wide regulation libraries using CAGER

Strain carrying the pQTSC series plasmid was cultured overnight at 37°C, 200 rpm, and then transferred to LB liquid medium containing Sm at a 1:100 ratio. Collect the culture when OD600 reached 0.6 and prepare to electroporation-competent cells. Three hundred nanograms of crRNA plasmid library was electroporated into competent cells and incubate in LB at 37°C, 200 rpm for 2 h. The culture was then directly transfered to 2 ml LB (Sm and Km) and further cultured at 37°C, 200 rpm for 8 h to enrich cells carrying crRNA plasmid. To induce transposition, the culture was transferred to 20 ml LB [Sm, Km, and 100 ng/ml anhydrotetracycline (aTc)] at a 1:1000 ratio, 30°C, 200 rpm in 125 ml flasks. After ~6 h, the culture was transferred to the same induction medium as mentioned earlier, and introduced for another 6 h. Continuous transfers were carried out, generating passage 2 and passage 3 of library. The final transcriptional activation and termination libraries were the libraries in third passage.

O-nitrophenyl-beta-D-galactopyranoside assay

The overnight cultures in LB medium were inoculated at 1% and cultured at 37°C for 3 h. The Abs600 of cultures in log phase was measured by an Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader (TECAN). The cultures were then diluted at 2-fold or 10-fold in fresh LB medium. Diluted culture (20 μl) was mixed with 80 μl permeabilization solution (100 mM Na2HPO4, 20 mM KCl, 2 mM MgSO4, 0.8 mg/ml hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide, 0.4 mg/ml sodium deoxycholate and 5.4 μl/ml β-mercaptoethanol) in 1.5 ml microfuge tubes. The samples and the substrate solution (60 mM Na2HPO4, 40 mM NaH2PO4, 1 mg/ml o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactoside, 2.7 μl/ml β-mercaptoethanol) were pre-incubated at 30°C for 20–30 min. Six hundred microliters of substrate solution was added to each tube, incubated at 30°C, and the time recorded. After sufficient color developement, 700 μl of stop solution (1 M Na2CO3) was added, mixed, and the time recorded. Two hundred microliters of reaction mixture was transferred into 96-well assay plates (Corning, 3603) and measured Abs420 by an Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader (TECAN). Miller Units were calculated as: dilution fold*1000*(Abs420)/[(Abs600 of culture sampled)*(volume[0.02 ml])*(reaction time)].

Construction of multigene regulation libraries using CAGER

Strain carrying pQTSR plasmid and F plasmid was cultured overnight at 37°C, 200 rpm, and then was transferred to LB liquid medium containing Sm and Tet at an initial OD600 of 0.02. Phage suspension at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1000 and 100 ng/ml aTc were then added into the culture and changed to 30 °C, 200 rpm to start transduction and editing. When the OD600 of the aforementioned culture reached ∼1 (~4 h), transferring the first passage of cells at 2% ratio into fresh LB liquid medium containing Tet, Sm, aTc, and phage suspension at a MOI of 1000, maintaining 30°C, 200 rpm. Following the above operations, the number n (n = 2, 3, …) passages of libraries were generated.

Plasmid curing

Since that pQTS series plasmids harbors sacB gene, we could 1% transfer the culture of libraries to LB liquid medium containing 10% sucrose with culturing at 37°C, 200 rpm for 6 h, or diluted spread appropriate amount cells of libraries on the LB plates containing 10% sucrose with culturing overnight at 37°C to facilitate plasmid curing. To verify the efficiency of plasmid curing, the liquid culture was diluted spread on LB plates. The colonies grown on the above plates were streaked on Sm plates and the non-resistant plates, respectively, and incubated overnight. The plasmid-curing efficiency was calculated by dividing the number of colonies on the Sm plates by the number of colonies on the non-resistant plate.

PCR, colony PCR, qPCR, and RT-qPCR analysis

PCR reactions for DNA fragments were performed using KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO) in a 50-µl reaction containing 25 µl Mix, 1.5 µl of each primer (10 µM), 1 µl DNA template, and 21 µL of ddH2O. PCR amplicons were generated over 30 cycles were resolved by electrophoresis on 1%–1.5% agarose gels stained with SYBR Safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

PCR reactions for E. coli samples were performed using Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme) in a 15-µl reaction containing 7.5 µl Mix, 0.75 µl of each primer (10 µM), and 6 µl of diluted cell lysate supernatant. PCR amplicons were generated over 25 PCR cycles and were resolved by electrophoresis on 1%–1.5% agarose gels stained with SYBR Safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Quantitative Real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using 4 µl of diluted genomic DNA (gDNA) in 20-µl reactions containing 10 µl of Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN), 2 µl of 2.5 µM mixed primer pair, and 4 µl of water. Reactions were set up in 96-well white PCR plates (BioRad) and run on a LC96 Real-Time PCR system (Roche) with the following thermal cycling conditions: polymerase activation and DNA denaturation (95°C for 5 min), 40 cycles of amplification (95°C for 15 s, 57°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s), and a terminal melt-curve analysis (60°C–95°C in 0.2°C/s increments). For gDNA extraction, cells cured of the pQTSR plasmid were collected, and 0.5 ml (~2 × 109 cells) was processed using the HiPure Bacterial DNA Kit (Magen, D3146-02). Each gDNA sample was analyzed with three separate qPCR reactions involving three primer pairs: two pairs comprise one mini-Tn-specific primer and one genomic-specific primer probing for either the T-RL or T-LR integration orientation, and one pair comprises two genome-specific reference primers at the gapA locus. Primer pairs amplify products between 100 and 250 bp and were confirmed to have amplification efficiencies between 90% and 110% using serially diluted samples. Integration efficiency (%) for each orientation is defined as 100 × (2^ΔCq), where ΔCq is the Cq (genomic reference pair) – Cq (R-L pair or L-R pair); the total integration efficiency is the sum of both orientation efficiencies.

Strains were inoculated in 5 ml LB supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and grown overnight at 37°C, 220 rpm. Cultures were then diluted 1:100 in 5 ml LB supplemented with antibiotics and grown to OD600 0.5. Cultures were pelleted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was extracted using a Bacterial RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek). For quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR), 1 µg of RNA was converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme) in 20 µl reactions. qPCR was performed using the aforementioned method with 0.5 ng of cDNA as a template. gapA was used for normalization. Control samples without a template and without reverse transcriptase were analyzed to confirm gene-specific amplification and absence of gDNA contamination. Expression levels for each gene were calculated relative to gapA using the ΔΔCt method [22]. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Tn-seq of genome-wide library and data analysis

In the genome-wide libraries, two-point mutations were introduced in the transposon right end to form an MmeI restriction site (from 5′-TGGTGATA-3′ to 5′-TGGTGGAA-3′). Cells with the pQTSC plasmid cured were collected, and 0.5 ml (corresponding to around 2 × 109 cells) was used for gDNA extraction with the HiPure Bacterial DNA Kit (Magen, D3146-02). This procedure typically yielded 50 µl of 0.5–1 µg/µl gDNA, which is a mixture of the E. coli circular chromosome and crRNA plasmid.

Next generation sequencing (NGS) libraries were prepared in parallel in PCR tubes, as follows. First, 1 µg of gDNA was digested with 4 U of MmeI (NEB) for 12 h at 37°C in a 50 µl reaction containing 1 × CutSmart Buffer, followed by heat inactivation at 65°C for 20 min. MmeI cleaves the transposon 17–19 nucleotides outside of the terminal repeat, leaving 2-nucleotide 3′-overhangs. Reactions were cleaned up using 1.4 × Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS magnetic beads (Omega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and elutions were performed using 30 µl of 10 mM Tris–Cl, pH 7.0. MmeI-digested gDNA was ligated to a double-stranded i5 universal adaptor containing a 3′-terminal NN overhang (Supplementary Table S4) in a 20 µl ligation reaction containing 16.33 µl of MmeI-digested gDNA, 500 nM adaptor, 400 U T4 DNA ligase (NEB), and 1 × T4 DNA ligase buffer. Reactions were incubated at room temperature for 30 min before being cleaned up with magnetic beads as before.

Adaptor-ligated transposons were enriched in a PCR-1 step using a universal i5 adaptor primer and a transposon-specific primer containing a universal i7 adaptor as 5′ overhang. Reactions were 25 µl in volume and contained 16.75 µl of MmeI-digested gDNA, 200 µM dNTPs, 0.5 µM primers, 1 × Q5 reaction buffer, and 0.5 U Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). Amplification was allowed to proceed for 22 cycles, with an annealing temperature of 66°C. Reaction products were then diluted 20-fold into a second 20 µl PCR (PCR-2) containing indexed p5/p7 primers, and this was subjected to 10 additional thermal cycles using an annealing temperature of 65°C. After verifying amplification for select libraries by analytical gel electrophoresis, barcoded reactions were pooled and resolved by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA was isolated by Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen), and NGS libraries were quantified by qPCR using the NEBNext Library Quant Kit (NEB). The DNA libraries were sequenced by Novogene Co., Ltd on the Illumina platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

To identify transposon insertion sites, 100 bp sequences upstream of the start codon for all 4217 protein-coding genes in the E. coli BW25113 genome (NCBI RefSeq: GCF_000750555.1) were extracted using a custom Python script. Paired-end 150 bp Illumina reads from Tn-seq libraries were screened for the presence of the expected transposon end sequence. For each identified read, the 19 nucleotides immediately upstream of the transposon end (5′ flanking region) were extracted and aligned against the 100 bp upstream regions of all annotated genes using an exact-match criterion (mismatch = 0). Reads with unique matches were annotated with the corresponding gene ID, and the results were compiled into Excel files. The statistical data were shown in Supplementary Data 2 and 3. The distribution of integrated genes was plotted and used to generate Fig. 2f.

GFP fluorescence detection

Single colonies on agar plates were picked and inoculated into 96-well plates containing 800 μl LB medium at 37°C, 800 rpm. After 12 h, growing cultures were inoculated at 1:100 ratio into 800 μl LB medium. The cultures were grown at 37°C and 800 rpm. After 4 h, 200 μl of diluted cultures were transferred to a black 96-well plate, and the GFP fluorescence was measured at 509 nm after excitation at 475 nm using a plate reader (Tecan). Fluorescence was measured in arbitrary units (A.U.), while optical density was determined by absorbance (OD) at 600 nm. The intensity of GFP was calculated by GFP fluorescence/OD600.

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested from cultures by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min, washed with phosphate buffer solution [phosphate buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4], and resuspended in PBS. GFP fluorescence was analyzed by flow cytometry (FACS, BD FACSAria Fusion SORP) with the following parameters, GFP: excitation at 488 nm, emission fluorescence at 515 ± 20 nm; RFP: excitation at 561 nm, emission fluorescence at 610 ± 20 nm, and sample pressure of 40 psi. The nozzle diameter was 85 μm. Cells were captured on the signal channels of FITC (voltage 879 V), FSC (voltage 374 V), and SSC (voltage 256 V). All captured events were used for fluorescence analysis. Data were analyzed using the FlowJo_v10.8.1.

Colorimetric screening and assay for lycopene production

Cells after plasmid curing were adjusted to OD600 ≈ 1, then were diluted 105-fold to spread on LB agar medium containing ampicillin (Amp) and 0.02 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG). After incubated at 37°C for 36 h to produce red colonies, which were subsequently screened by eyes. The selected colonies were grown in 1 ml LB medium containing Amp in 96-well plates for 24 h, and then transferred to 1 ml LB medium containing Amp and 0.1 mM IPTG for 24 h to produce lycopene. Lycopene was extracted from 1 ml cells as follows: centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min, suspended in acetone (1 ml), incubated at 55°C for 15 min in the dark, centrifuged at 14 000 × g for 10 min, and transferred the acetone supernatant containing lycopene to a new tube. Lycopene concentration was analyzed using HPLC with a variable wavelength detector set to 480 nm and a Symmetry C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Waters). Methanol/acetonitrile/dichloromethane (21:21:8, by vol) was used as the mobile phase at 0.8 ml/min at 30°C [23]. The results represent the mean ± standard deviation of three independent experiments. Dry cell weight (DCW) was calculated as follows: 1 OD600 = 0.379 g DCW/L.

Results

Transcriptional interference of RE and LE

The CAST system integrates cargoes along with RE and LE sequences into the genome. To realize transcriptional regulation by CAST-mediated promoters’ integration, it is crucial to determine whether the RE and LE sequences possess any transcriptional interference activity. In several I-F3 type CASTs, known for their high efficiency in E. coli [24], the RE and LE are typically located adjacent to open reading frames in the host organisms (Fig. 1a) [25]. It implied that there may be undetected terminator or promoter elements present in RE and LE that influence the transcriptional levels of cargo promoters. Here, we focused on two highly efficient I-F3 CAST systems, VchCAST and CAST from Pseudoalteromonas translucida KMM520 (PtrCAST) [26]. Using Ptac promoter as the cargo and lacZ as the reporter gene, we detected the transcriptional effects of RE* (truncated RE, 57 bp) [16], LE and LE* (105 bp, minimal length with high transposition efficiency [27]) of VchCAST. Specifically, in R-Ptac-L orientation, the LE and LE* exhibited strong transcriptional repression on Ptac, almost completely suppressing the activity of Ptac (Fig. 1b). Conversely, RE* of VchCAST exhibited significant transcriptional enhancement activity when integrated in the L-Ptac-R orientation, resulting in a 27.5% increase in expression levels over Ptac (Fig. 1c). The results in Supplementary Fig. S1 revealed that the LE of PtrCAST (PtrLE) in the R-Ptac-L orientation showed a transcriptional enhancement, leading to a 32.8% increase in expression level compared with Ptac. This suggests that the PtrLE sequence may contain a hidden promoter with a transcriptional strength ~84.0% of that of Ptac (Supplementary Fig. S1). However, the RE of PtrCAST (PtrRE) in the L-Ptac-R orientation showed a transcriptional repression effect, causing a 20.9% decrease in Ptac intensity (Supplementary Fig. S1). These results correspond with the anticipated transcriptional effects based on the relative positions of RE and LE with respect to coding frames in their host genomes (Fig. 1a).

Redesigned LE to minimize the transcriptional inhibition

Considering the reported detailed analysis on the RE and LE of VchCAST [28], we focused on the modification of RE* and LE of VchCAST (RE* and LE in the following refer to those derived from VchCAST, unless otherwise noted). Since the RE* and LE, even when truncated, still exhibit significant transcriptional interference, we attempted to introduce mutations to RE* and LE in order to eliminate the transcriptional interference. Sequence prediction analysis by PromoterHunter (www.phisite.org) indicated that RE* in the orientation of L–R could potentially contain hidden promoters (Supplementary Fig. S2a). Three mutated RE* sequences (RE*V1–V3) were constructed by introducing mutations into −35 and −10 regions (Supplementary Fig. S2a). We tested the transcriptional activity of RE*V1–V3 using lacZ as report gene without any upstream artificial promoters. However, the results showed that these mutations did not eliminate the original transcriptional activation activity (Supplementary Fig. S2b). The strength of the hidden promoter in RE* was ~72.3% of PlacUV5 and 40.9% of Ptac (Supplementary Fig. S2b), which can serve as a reference for the transcriptional strength of RE in further investigations. Considering that the RE sequence has been truncated to 57 bp, which is almost the entire sequence required for transposition [27], allowing a small room for additional modification, we then focused on the mutational modification of LE.

We analyzed the mRNA secondary structure (www.unafold.org) of LE in the R–L orientation and found the presence of multiple stem-loop structures that could potentially inhibit transcription (Fig. 1d). Considering stem-loops region and transposition-essential sequences, we introduced mutations on both LE and LE* sequences, resulting in specific region mutations (LEV2–V4, LE*V2–V4) as well as combined mutations (LEV1, LE*V1) (Fig. 1e). Data showed that only when all selected sites were mutated did LE and LE* lose their transcriptional inhibitory activity (Fig. 1f). Subsequent transposition experiment validated that both LEV1 and LE*V1 had negligible impact on transposition efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S3). Due to the relatively lower transposition efficiency mediated by LE*, we decided to proceed with LEV1 in subsequent experiments. It is worth noting that LE of VchCAST has been reported to contain an IHF binding sequence (wavy line in Fig. 1e) with consensus sequence 5′-YAANNNNYTGATW-3′ (R–L orientation), which is closely associated with the transposition activity [28]. Our results demonstrate that the mutation from “W” to “C” in the last nucleotide of the consensus sequence does not significantly affect integration efficiency. In brief, through rational modifications, we eliminated the transcription interference effects of LE without compromising transposition efficiency, thereby indicating the modifiability of transpositional elements and the potential of CAST in transcriptional regulation.

Genome-wide transcriptional activation or termination library

The engineered LE sequence LEV1 can integrate promoters into the genome of E. coli with negligible transcriptional interference, while the wild-type LE sequence is more suitable for integrating terminators due to its natural strong transcriptional inhibition effect. As shown in Fig. 2a, we hypothesize that using promoters or terminators as cargoes could establish stable genome-wide transcriptional activation or termination library by targeting the upstream of genes. To assess the transcriptional effects of different cargoes (promoters or terminators), we constructed two reporter strains, BW25113 eda-purT::gfp and BW25113 eda-purT::Ptac-gfp. crRNA-4 (Supplementary Table S3) was used to guide the insertion of promoters with varying strengths or a terminator sequence (contains one tonB terminator and two fd terminators) upstream of gfp. Colonies with R–L orientation insertions were selected and cultured in LB medium for 4 h, after which green fluorescence was measured. The results indicated that, depending on the user’s requirements, several promoters (including inducible promoters) or the terminators could be selected for constructing transcriptional activation or termination libraries (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S4a).

The design rationale for genome-wide crRNA library involved searching PAMs (the priority is CC > GT > CA > CT) within the 100–120 bp regions on sense strands upstream of the start codon of genes and selecting 32 bp regions downstream of identified PAMs as the spacers. No more than three crRNAs were designed to target a single gene. We transformed the crRNA plasmid library (11 900 crRNAs targeting 4217 genes in E. coli BW25113; Supplementary Data 1) into BW25113 cells carrying pQTSC plasmid (Fig. 2a). pQTSC represents a series of plasmids expressing the VchCAST effectors, a counter-selection marker SacB, and carrying cargoes including promoters (Ptac used here, Supplementary Table S6) or terminators (one tonB terminator and two fd terminators used here, Supplementary Table S6). The RE*-Promoter-LEV1 and LE-Promoter-RE* are both functional for transcriptional activation. As a single crRNA plasmid library was employed to construct both transcriptional activation and termination libraries, the RE*-Promoter-LEV1 and RE-Terminator-LE designs were used here. After inducing cells with 100 ng/ml anhydrotetracycline (aTc) for transposition, the cargo was inserted at diverse gene upstream regions guided by diverse crRNA, generating the genome-scale transcriptional activation or termination library. To stop transposition at any time and prevent repeat integration [29], the counter-selection marker sacB on the pQTSC plasmid enables enrichment of pQTSC-cured cells by supplementing sucrose in medium. The efficiency of plasmid curing reached 100% after culturing cells in LB medium containing 10% sucrose for 6 h (Fig. 2c). Following plasmid curing, the library cells were genomically stable and could be used for integration efficiency verification or phenotypic screening. After the first passage (6 h per passage), colony PCR revealed integration frequencies of 25.0% and 20.8% for the transcription activation and termination libraries, respectively. After three passages, the integration frequencies increased to 83.3% for both libraries (Fig. 2d).

Tn-seq was used to analyze the insertion sites of cargoes in library (Fig. 2e). The end of the RE sequence was mutated to MmeI recognition site, and no significant influence on integration efficiency was observed (Supplementary Fig. S4b and c). The frequency of R–L orientation integration increased by 22.0% with mutated RE* and LEV1 (Supplementary Fig. S4d). By analyzing the data from NGS (200k reads per sample), we found that the transcriptional activation library covered 3272 genes and the termination library covered 3339 genes out of total 4217 genes (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Data 2 and 3).

Given its abundance and lower cost than glucose in terms of weight and energy content, acetate is being explored as an alternative feedstock for bioproduct production [30, 31]. Although acetate can serve as a carbon source for E. coli, the growth rate and biomass yield on acetate are lower than those on glucose [32]. Here, we used 10 g/l acetate as the sole carbon source to select for strains with enhanced growth. Cells from the genome-wide transcriptional activation or termination library were subcultured in the M9 medium supplemented with acetate and passaged twice, aiming to enrich the genetic modifications beneficial for acetate utilization (Fig. 2g). The genes with insertion frequencies in the top 100 in the libraries with and without acetate treated were listed separately and plotted as Venn diagrams (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Data 4). The genes that belong to the intersection of two parallel treated libraries and are not in the untreated libraries were focused on. Among them, the ynfH and yehQ genes were excluded due to the unobvious difference in insertion frequency before and after screening (Supplementary Table S7). By verifying each modification in the parental BW25113 wild-type strain, we found that overexpressing lptC, prfA, metN, ynfH, fadJ, and dmsC, respectively, or repressing pyrI or proX, could all improve the growth of cells in M9 medium with acetate as the sole carbon source to varying degrees, and the effects of overexpressing prfA or lptC and inhibiting pyrI are particularly obvious (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Fig. 2i). The prfA gene encodes the peptide release factor RF1, which facilitates the release of nascent polypeptide chains at stop codons. Overexpression of prfA increased cellular tolerance to acetate, potentially by enhancing the expression levels of various proteins involved in acetate metabolism or tolerance pathways. This hypothesis warrants further investigation, for instance through proteomic analysis. Similarly, overexpression of lptC, which encodes a component of the lipopolysaccharide transport system, and disruption of pyrI, a pyrimidine biosynthesis gene, both enhanced acetate tolerance. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that in Clostridium acetobutylicum, acetate stress upregulates a conserved membrane protein involved in lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis and downregulates genes responsible for converting glutamine to uridine triphosphate [33].

The efficiency of M13 phage-assisted CAST

Using electroporation to introduce promoter library into host cells requires repeated electroporation and competent cell preparation to achieve a continuous supply of different promoter variants. Inspired by the transduction mechanism of phages, we placed the RE-cargo-LE on an R6k replicon-controlled M13 phagemid, which functions as a suicide plasmid in E. coli strains lacking the pir gene. The phagemid can be packaged into M13 phage using helper phage (see “Materials and methods” section). When using promoter variants as cargoes, the infection of pir-negative E. coli cells with these phages enables in situ delivery of diverse promoter variants (Fig. 3a). In the E. coli cells carrying pQTSR series plasmid, which expresses VchCAST effectors, SacB, and crRNA(s), the received different promoter variants can be integrated into genomic site(s) guided by crRNA(s), enabling expression regulation of single multiple genes.

When using engineered phages to deliver cargoes, the transposition efficiency is not only limited by the efficiency of the CAST system, but also by that of phage delivery (Fig. 3a). Therefore, it is necessary to optimize the conditions for transposition. With targeting specific genome locus guided by crRNA4 in the strain BW25113 carrying F plasmid, using Ptac as the cargo and LEV1 as the end sequence, the initial cell density during infection, the amount of phage suspension added, and the temperature during transposition were systematically tested. Similar to plasmid curing in liquid medium, when the cell culture was directly diluted and spread on agar plates containing 10% sucrose, near all cells lost the tool plasmid after overnight incubation (Supplementary Fig. S6). The transposition efficiency was determined after stopping transposition by directly spreading cells on sucrose plates. We found that an 87.5% insertion frequency was achieved when phage suspension at a MOI of 1000 was added to the culture with an initial cell OD600 of 0.2 and induced with 100 ng/ml aTc at 30°C for 4 h (Fig. 3b). Surprisingly, when targeting eight different genomic sites using an CRISPR array of eight crRNAs, 66.6% of cells integrated at three sites and 16.6% of cells integrated at five sites after one round of transposition (infection for 4 h), and the overall efficiency showed an increasing trend in passaging supplemented with phage suspension (Fig. 3c). Our results demonstrate that the M13 phagemid delivery system is compatible with the VchCAST system and exhibits transposition efficiency comparable to that of the self-replicating donor plasmid [14].

Muti-gene regulation library verified by fluorescent protein

To explore whether replacing a single cargo sequence with a promoter library can achieve targeted gene expression regulation over a wide range. We selected constitutive promoters J23119 with high transcriptional activity as the original promoter and introduced two types of mutations at five key positions in the −35 and −10 regions with degenerate primers to generate 32 promoter sequences (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S7). When using this mini-promoter library as cargoes to target the upstream region of gfp gene, the first round of transposition f was ~82.7%, and which increased with subsequent passages (Fig. 4b). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) data showed that the proportion of R–L integration orientation (70.9%) in the first round was much higher than that of L–R integration orientation (11.9%), which is consistent with previous reports [27]. The promoters inserted upstream of gfp were PCR-amplified and sequenced, and it was found that the mutation sites and the types of mutated bases were consistent with that in the promoter library (Supplementary Fig. S7). The strength of generated promoter variants spanned nearly three orders of magnitude in terms of GFP fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4c), which is comparable to those of previous plasmid-borne promoter libraries or CRISPR activation/interference devices and large enough for reprogramming cellular metabolism [5].

Furthermore, we validated whether different promoter variants can be orthogonally integrated into distinct genomic sites. We designed plasmid pQTS-array2, encoding a CRISPR array targeting the upstream regions of gfp and rfp genes, to verify the diversity of integrated variants and the orthogonally transcriptional regulation of multiple proteins. qPCR data showed that the insertion frequency of each site was over 60% in the third passage (Fig. 4d). Three randomly selected clones that have been confirmed for double-site integration through colony PCR were further subjected to Sanger sequencing. The promoter variants integrated at both sites were distinct (Fig. 4e), and the fluorescence intensity of both GFP and RFP exhibited an over a 103-fold range (Fig. 4f). When plotting the fluorescence values of gfp and rfp as the x and y coordinates, respectively, it can be observed that the transposed cells are scattered almost randomly between the starting strain and the positive control strains (RE-PJ23119-LEV1-gfp, RE-PJ23119-LEV1-rfp) (Fig. 4g), indicating orthogonal regulation of gfp and rfp expression. Given the high integration efficiency and ideal expression regulation effects of the phage-assisted CAST-mediated expression regulation, we plan to apply it to the optimization of metabolic pathways in industrial strains.

Duodecuple-genes regulation via targeting seven sites for reprogramming lycopene biosynthesis

To employ CAGER in optimizing complex metabolic pathways, we focused on diversifying the metabolic flux in the 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate (DXP) biosynthesis pathway, tricarboxylic acid cycle, pentose phosphate pathway [27], and the expression level of the global regulator RpoS [34, 35], for overproduction of the isoprenoid, lycopene (Fig. 5a). Using the aforementioned promoter library based on promoter J23119, seven genome sites were targeted by CAGER to tune the transcription of 12 genes documented to increase lycopene yield, including ispA, dxs, idi, dxr, rpoS, sdhABCD, sucAB, and talB (Fig. 5b and c). In the genome of E. coli BW25113, a 1 bp overlap exists between the start codon of ispA and the stop codon of xseB, meaning that the insertion of a promoter targeting ispA would disrupt the coding frame of xseB. Thus, we inserted a short futile sequence to separate ispA and xseB, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S8. The modified strain was then transformed with plasmid pTrc99a-crtEIB to introduce the lycopene synthesis pathway, generating strain EIB.

Strain EIB was transformed with transposition tool plasmid and then cultured in LB medium supplemented with 100 ng/ml aTc and phage suspension to start transposition. During the process of building library through successive transfers, the integration ratio at all sites was gradually increased in the initial stage, except sucAB (Fig. 5d). We suspected that the integration of promoters within a specific intensity range upstream sucAB was detrimental to cell growth, resulting in the gradual elimination of this population and the decline of integration ratio over time. With successive transfers, the ratio of cells integrating promoter variants at more sites gradually increased (Fig. 5e). The promoters inserted upstream of seven sites were PCR-amplified and sequenced, and the mutation sites and type of mutant base were consistent with that in the promoter library (Supplementary Fig. S9). After curing the tool plasmid in LB liquid supplemented 10% sucrose, cells in the all five passages were collected and mixed with an equal proportion. Screening of variants was done by isolating colonies that produced intense red pigmentation on LB agar plates containing ampicillin and 0.2 mM IPTG, which were respectively used to maintaining pTrc99a-crtEIB plasmid and inducing crtEIB. Variants were isolated from ∼5 × 104 colonies (Fig. 5f) and subsequently screened in 96-well plates, strain EIBf exhibiting as much as a 73.6-fold increase in lycopene production relative to the EIB ancestral strain, reaching 2 281.9 μg/g DCW (Fig. 5g). Seven target sites in EIBa–EIBf were verified and sequenced, and in strain EIBf, five sites including ispA-dxs, idi, dxr, rpoS, and sdhABCD were inserted (Supplementary Table S8). Four out of six variants integrated the strongest promoter J23119 at the idi site (Supplementary Table S8). Correspondingly, transcriptional levels of idi were markedly increased in all six engineered strains (Fig. 5h), indicating that high expression of idi may be critical for enhanced lycopene production, which is consistent with previous reports [36, 37]. In contrast, the transcriptional level of sdhC appeared to be negatively correlated with lycopene yield, while rpoS required only moderate upregulation to support optimal production (Fig. 5g and h). The optimized chassis strain holds the potential to produce other valuable isoprenoids.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate the construction methods of genome-wide regulation (activation and inhibition) libraries and multigene regulation library using the modified VchCAST in E. coli, named the strategy as CAGER (Fig. 6). Our work shows that the LE sequence of VchCAST can be modified to LEV1 to eliminate its natural transcriptional interference while maintaining high transposition efficiency. The genome-wide regulation library can be used to discover new target related to the ideal phenotypes, and the multigene combinatory regulation library can be screened to explore the engineering potential of cellular metabolic networks.

Schematic diagram illustrating the working principle of CAGER. I. The crRNA plasmid library is introduced into cells that already contain a plasmid harboring CAST genes and a cargo. The cargo, which can be either a promoter or a terminator, is integrated upstream of various genes mediated by CAST proteins and different crRNAs. Since the pQTSC plasmid contains a counter-selection marker, sacB, and the crRNA plasmid is self-replicating, the genome-wide transcriptional activation or termination library is generated after curing the pQTSC plasmid. The remaining crRNA plasmid can then be used to track targeted genes by sequencing the N32 region in the crRNA. II. A promoter plasmid library, containing various promoter variants as cargo, is repeatedly introduced into cells via iterative M13 phage-mediated transduction. The cargoes are integrated upstream of multiple target genes mediated by CAST proteins and a CRISPR array. Since the pQTSR plasmid contains sacB, and the crRNA plasmid is based on a suicide plasmid, a library of strains with multigene regulation is generated after curing the pQTSR plasmid.

The genome-wide regulation libraries constructed by CAGER achieve gene overexpression or repression through modifications at the DNA level, which is more stable than the traditional CRISPRa/i or plasmid-based gene overexpression, in principle. Compared to gene activation mediated by Tn5 [38] or the combination of CRISPR and transcriptional factor [5, 39], CAGER offers site programmability and allows users to independently design and build libraries based on the specific genes they expect to regulate. Furthermore, CAGER can generally control gene activation levels by using constitutive promoters of different strengths or by changing the concentration of inducers of inducible promoters. The reason of that the library in our experiments does not cover all genes can be attributed to three main factors. (i) Limitation of the size of reads analyzed (200k reads per sample). When increasing the analyzed reads into 300k, the number of covered genes increased from 3272 and 3339 to 3511 and 3539, respectively. (ii) Limitation of integration efficiency of CAST. The integration efficiency targeting some genes is not high enough to insert the cargo during three passages. More passages potentially increase number of the covered gene. (iii) Transcriptional activation or termination of some genes may impair cell growth. Overexpressing membrane-associated proteins or proteins with high abundances of branched-chain amino acids [40], or transcriptional termination of essential genes [41], tended to impair cell growth.

Given that cells with high product synthesis capabilities always face a growth disadvantage and prolonged library construction periods can lead to unnecessary spontaneous genomic mutations, it is crucial to expedite the library construction process to obtain high-producing strains. The CAST system exhibits efficient multi-target integration capabilities without relying on homologous recombination, as demonstrated for simultaneous integrating at up to 16 sites [14]. M13 phage possess high DNA transfer efficiency, ~1012 colony-forming units/ml. Therefore, we combined these two powerful systems. To demonstrate its efficacy, we used the synthesis of lycopene, a non-growth phenotype, as a case study. CAGER can target seven specific sites in E. coli, modulating the expression of 12 genes and achieving the reprogramming of the lycopene synthesis pathway within 24 h. The lycopene production of the screened strain is 73.6-fold of that of initial strain. CAGER provides an easy to operate, in situ, and efficient platform for multigene expression regulation in metabolic network reprogramming. We anticipate that it will substantially accelerate the development of strains for industrial chemicals production.

When the CAST system integrates cargoes, the flanking LE and RE sequences are also integrated simultaneously. Our research confirms the presence of sequences in both RE and LE that influence transcription, and eliminated the transcriptional repression effect of LE in VchCAST while maintaining high editing efficiency through combinatorial mutations. Samuel H. Sternberg et al. performed the mutation assays on LE and RE of VchCAST and identified the IHF-binding sequences closely associated with transposition efficiency [28]. We validated that, under the conditions of our experiments, the mutation in the last nucleotide of the consensus sequence (in the R–L direction, 5′–3′) from “W” to “C” has negligible impact on transposition efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S3b). The designed RE sequence of VchCAST has been reported to successfully ligate the fusion expression of cargo genes with downstream genes [28]. In the future, if more flexible designs become available for RE and LE without transcriptional effects, it would be possible to construct promoter libraries on cargoes and design RBS libraries at the end of RE or LE to fine-tune target genes at both the transcription and translation levels. To broaden the application scope of CAST, it is essential to conduct further research on the LE and RE of the CAST system to gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between sequences and transposition functions.

The PAM of CAST is not as strict as that of the commonly used Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9, and even PtrCAST is naturally PAMless [26]. Consequently, there are typically many optional spacers upstream of the target gene when using CAGER, eliminating the need for prior target sequence insertion, as required in base editing-mediated BETTER [42]. Successfully orthogonal multiplex editing has been reported using VchCAST and PtrCAST [26]. We envision that CAGER has great potential to employed more CAST systems to regulate the expression levels of a large number of target genes and orthogonally diversify various kinds of genes with different kinds of promoters (e.g. constitutive and inducible promoters) for reprograming complex metabolism. In the future, the delivery pattern of M13 phage could also be applied to the genome-wide regulation library, resulted in multiplex genome-wide regulation library that may contribute to the discovery of more unreported valuable targets.
