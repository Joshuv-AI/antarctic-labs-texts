Relatively upstream factors associated with pressure injury occurrence and staging in older hospitalized patients: a prospective observational study
Authors: Kun Yang, Zhou Yang, Xin Li, Xianni Xie, Wen Peng, Jing Fang, Wen Zhou, Guofeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Braden Scale is widely used to assess the direct physiological risks of pressure injuries. However, there is limited research on the relatively upstream risk factors for the parameters assessed by the Braden Scale, such as pre-admission living environment, education, and chronic disease history, as well as proximal factors such as, incontinence and mobility.MethodsThis prospective observational study enrolled 86 older patients. Patient age, education, pre-admission living environment, diet, hypertension, diabetes, urinary and bowel function, relationship between caregivers and patients during hospitalization, and functional mobility were analyzed. Risk factor analysis and screening were conducted using a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The relationship between risk factors and pressure injury staging was analyzed using unconditional logistic regression.ResultsUnivariate analysis indicated that the risk factors for pressure injury in older patients during hospitalization included the patient’s living environment before admission (P = 0.032), urinary and fecal incontinence (P = 0.004), and mobility (P = 0.008). Further unconditional logistic regression results indicated that urinary and fecal incontinence were also associated with pressure injury staging.ConclusionIn this observational study, urinary and fecal incontinence were associated with both the occurrence and severity of pressure injuries in older hospitalized patients, whereas associations with pre-admission living environment and mobility were not sustained after adjustment. These exploratory findings suggest that graded continence assessment and care may be a worthwhile focus for future interventional research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07860-6.Keywords: Pressure injury, Nursing, Upstream risk factors, Pressure injury staging, Older patients

Introduction

Pressure injuries, also known as pressure ulcers, are local injuries caused by prolonged or intense pressure and shear forces acting on the skin and subcutaneous tissue at bony prominences and are a common complication in older hospitalized patients [1]. Pressure injuries are classified into stages I to IV according to the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) criteria, ranging from non‑blanchable erythema (Stage I) to full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone, tendon, or muscle (Stage IV). This staging system reflects the severity of tissue damage and guides clinical management. With the rapid aging of the global population, the number of hospitalized older patients continues to increase, and the prevention and management of pressure injuries have become important challenges in clinical nursing [2, 3]. Pressure injury not only significantly reduces patients’ quality of life, increases their pain, and prolongs their hospital stay, but may also cause serious complications such as infection and sepsis, exacerbating the consumption of medical resources and the burden of family care [4, 5].

Although numerous national and international studies have explored common risk factors for pressure injury in older patients, most of these factors are based on the patient’s physiological and disease status [6], with the most common being the Braden Scale. The Braden Scale [7] is a widely used tool for assessing pressure injury risk, comprising six subscales: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear. Typically applied upon admission and reassessed regularly, it guides preventive interventions. However, several additional long-term factors that predate admission also warrant consideration, including age, education, pre-admission living environment, chronic diseases, and continence [8, 9]. We acknowledge that in strict epidemiological terminology, “upstream” determinants refer to broad social and structural factors. However, in this study, we use the term ‘relatively upstream’ to refer to factors that are more distal than the immediate Braden Scale items, while also examining certain proximal clinical factors—specifically, incontinence—as part of the same comprehensive assessment. At present, research that specifically analyzes risk factors from a relatively upstream perspective and further explores their association with these factors and the severity (i.e., staging) of pressure injuries remains relatively limited.

Consequently, this study focuses on relatively upstream factors that may influence the occurrence of pressure injuries, aiming to complement traditional risk-scoring tools with a more comprehensive assessment across demographics, social ecology, chronic disease management, and nursing interaction. We specifically selected pre-admission living environment, continence status, and mobility as indicators of baseline frailty, functional mobility dependence, and accumulated care quality, respectively. Although patients with pressure injuries present at admission were excluded, these pre-hospital vulnerabilities may represent a ‘frailty substrate’ that increases susceptibility to new pressure injuries when patients are exposed to the acute illness and relative immobility of hospitalization. Further analysis was conducted on the correlation between the aforementioned risk factors and the subsequent staging of pressure injuries. In summary, this study aimed to determine whether these routinely available admission variables are associated with the occurrence and staging of hospital-acquired pressure injuries, and thus whether they may serve as potential supplementary risk indicators beyond traditional tools.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study was conducted from January 2025 to December 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second People’s Hospital of Jingzhou City (2025LW0005). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [10].

All patients were recruited from the geriatric and long-term care wards of the Second People’s Hospital of Jingzhou City, which specializes in managing older patients with multiple chronic conditions and high levels of functional mobility dependence on them. The screening was performed by two trained research nurses who reviewed the electronic medical records and conducted bedside assessments within 24 h of admission. Patients who met the inclusion criteria (age ≥ 60 years, expected hospital stay ≥ 72 h, and able to provide informed consent themselves or via a primary caregiver) and did not meet any exclusion criteria (pressure injury present on admission, end-of-life care, and incomplete clinical data) were invited to participate. Written informed consent was obtained before any data collection. A total of 86 patients were enrolled in the study. A flow diagram illustrating the recruitment process is presented in Supplementary Material 1.

The standard prevention protocol for all older patients included: repositioning every 2 h for bedridden patients, use of standard foam mattresses (no specialized pressure-redistributing surfaces were routinely used, except for patients with existing pressure injuries), daily skin inspection with moisturizing cream, and heel elevation using pillows for patients with limited mobility.

The primary outcome was the new onset of any pressure injury (Stage I–IV) during the hospital stay, detected by daily skin assessments in patients who were confirmed to be free of pressure injuries at baseline (admission).

Research tools and data collection

Data were collected using a self-designed questionnaire (Supplementary Material 2) on the risk factors for pressure injuries in older hospitalized patients. The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review and consultations with three senior geriatric nursing specialists. It was then piloted on 10 patients (not included in the final analysis) to assess its clarity and feasibility. Based on the pilot results, we refined the wording of a few items (e.g., mobility and incontinence descriptions) to enhance clarity. The final version of the questionnaire, which was used for data collection, included the following content:

Pressure injury assessment

Older patients without pressure injuries at admission were followed up during hospitalization. Diagnosis and staging were performed daily by two trained wound care nurses, and the observation period ended at discharge or after 28 days, whichever came first. To differentiate pressure injuries from incontinence-associated dermatitis, nurses were instructed to apply the NPIAP criteria, focusing on lesion location (bony prominence vs. diffuse perineal), shape (circumscribed vs. irregular), and the presence of non-blanchable erythema. The diagnosis and staging of pressure injury were strictly based on the latest NPIAP staging criteria (stages I-IV) in the United States.

Statistical methods

SPSS 25.0 software was used for the data analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Patients were divided into pressure and non-pressure injury groups, and single-factor analyses were conducted on various nursing-related risk factors using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test to identify statistically significant factors (P < 0.05). Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into an unconditional logistic regression model (entry/exit criteria 0.05/0.10) using the maximum likelihood ratio test. Finally, the variables entering the model were divided into subgroups based on the presence or absence of pressure injuries and pressure injury staging, and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s test.

Results

This study had two primary objectives: (1) to identify relatively upstream factors associated with the occurrence of pressure injuries in older hospitalized patients, and (2) to examine whether these factors are related to pressure injury staging. Accordingly, we first compared the characteristics of patients who developed pressure injuries with those who did not, and then analyzed the relationship between significant risk factors and pressure injury severity.

Demographics and incidence of pressure injury

This study included 86 hospitalized older patients with a mean age of 80.76 years (SD 8.41). Among patients with mobility dependence (n = 43) the incidence was 65.1%, and among those with incontinence (n = 64) the incidence was 60.9%. During hospitalization, a total of 46 patients developed pressure injuries, with an incidence rate of 53.5% calculated from the full sample of 86 patients, all of whom were at baseline risk due to the specialty of the recruiting wards (multiple chronic conditions and high functional dependence). The most common locations of pressure injuries were the sacrum (26 patients, 56.5%), heel (10 patients, 21.7%), greater trochanter (5 patients, 10.9%), and occiput (3 patients, 6.5%). Other locations (elbows, ankles) accounted for 2 patients (4.4%). Among them, there were 12 patients (13.95%) in stage I, 22 patients (25.28%) in stage II, 6 patients (6.98%) in stage III, and 6 patients (6.98%) in stage IV.

As shown in Table 1, univariate analysis identified three factors that were significantly associated with pressure injury incidence (P < 0.05): pre-admission living environment, urinary and fecal incontinence, and mobility. No significant differences were observed for age, education, hypertension, diabetes, or caregiver relationship between the pressure injury and non-pressure injury groups.

Association between risk factors and pressure injury staging (Objective 2)

The results demonstrated that urinary incontinence, as well as urinary and fecal incontinence, exhibited a significant association with pressure injury staging (P-values of 0.011 and 0.032, respectively; OR of 0.211 and 0.078, respectively). These findings are elucidated in Table 2 for variable assignment and Table 3 for the logistic regression results. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, no patients of severe Stage III/IV pressure ulcers were observed among patients who were continent or who experienced urinary or fecal incontinence. However, among patients with complete urinary and fecal incontinence, 12 patients (30.0%) developed Stage III/IV pressure ulcers.

Discussion

This study aims to explore relatively upstream factors associated with pressure injury risk in older patients during hospitalization and analyze the association between these factors and pressure injury staging. The results of this study demonstrate that pre-admission living environment, urinary and fecal conditions, and functional mobility are significant risk factors for pressure injury in older patients during hospitalization, and urinary and fecal incontinence is not only associated with pressure injury but also statistically correlated with its staging.

An unexpected and counterintuitive finding of the univariate analysis was that older patients who lived at home before admission had a significantly higher incidence of pressure injury during hospitalization compared to those from nursing homes (59.4% vs. 29.4%, P = 0.032). However, this does not necessarily indicate causality and may reflect unmeasured confounding factors. For example, older patients living at home before admission may have been admitted with more acute conditions or received less structured skin care. In contrast, nursing home residents may have received baseline preventive care [12]. Therefore, this association requires further validation.

Nevertheless, this result suggests that the pre-admission living environment should be considered an important warning signal of pressure injury risk during hospitalization assessment, and that more proactive preventive measures should be promptly initiated. Furthermore, severe functional mobility dependence is a well-established risk factor, consistent with previous findings [13–15]. Long-term bed rest or wheelchair use can lead to sustained local pressure, and reduced autonomous activity also affects blood circulation, collectively increasing the risk of skin damage [16, 17]. Therefore, encouraging and assisting patients to get out of bed and move around within a safe range, and implementing timed position changes, remain the cornerstones of pressure injury prevention. Further research revealed that sacral injuries were the most common and were often associated with both immobility and incontinence [18], which aligns with our main findings.

The most important finding of this study is that urination and defecation not only affect the incidence of pressure injuries but are also significantly correlated with their severity. The results showed that patients with complete incontinence had a significantly increased risk of developing stage III/IV severe pressure injuries. This association may be explained by incontinence, which leads to prolonged exposure of the perineum and buttock skin to a moist environment, weakening the skin barrier function [19]; digestive enzymes in feces and ammonia in urine cause chemical irritation and skin maceration [20]; and a humid environment increases the friction between the skin and bed sheets [21]. Together, these factors may accelerate the progression and increase the severity of pressure injuries once they develop. This observational finding suggests a potentially meaningful direction for nursing assessment by highlighting the relevance of incontinence severity beyond its mere presence. The data provide preliminary support for the hypothesis that graded and enhanced nursing interventions tailored to individual patients’ continence profiles may be a worthwhile focus for future evaluation.

The results of this study are consistent with several national and international studies, confirming that restricted mobility and incontinence are core risk factors for pressure injuries [22, 23]. However, previous studies have mostly evaluated these factors as general risks, with less in-depth exploration of their specific relationships with pressure injury staging. The present analysis of the association between incontinence and pressure injury staging partially addresses the limited evidence in this field. It provides preliminary observational data that may inform the exploration of a shift from simple binary risk categorization to tiered, severity-based risk stratification; however, further confirmatory studies are needed.

This study has several limitations. First, as it is a single-centre cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, inter-rater reliability has not been formally evaluated, which may affect the representativeness and generalizability of the results. Second, the chi-square test was primarily employed for univariate analysis, failing to account for potential confounding factors such as nutritional status and serum albumin level. Third, further prospective cohort studies involving larger sample sizes and multiple centers are required to verify the independent contributions of various risk factors and to explore the effects of additional nursing operations, such as turning frequency and bed sheet material. Fourth, we did not collect systematic data on the pressure injury prevention measures delivered to each patient (e.g., repositioning frequency, type of support surface, and use of heel protectors). Standard prevention protocols were implemented for all older patients; However, adherence was not monitored. Unmeasured variations in the quality of prevention may confound the observed associations between risk factors and pressure injury occurrence.

Conclusion

In this prospective observational study of older hospitalized patients, urinary and fecal incontinence were associated with both the occurrence and higher staging of pressure injuries in the multivariate analysis, suggesting that incontinence severity may represent a continuum of risk that merits further investigation. Pre-admission living environment and mobility were significantly associated with pressure injury occurrence in the univariate analysis; However, these associations did not persist in the adjusted model. Given the single-center design and limited sample size, these preliminary observations should be interpreted with caution. Rather than definitive practice directives, these findings suggest that comprehensive continence management and pre-admission functional assessment may represent promising targets for tiered prevention strategies, which require testing in larger, multicenter cohort studies.
