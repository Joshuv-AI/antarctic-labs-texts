GPT-4 as a source of patient information for cervical disc arthroplasty: a comparative analysis against Google web search
Authors: Paul G Mastrokostas, Leonidas E Mastrokostas, Ahmed K Emara, Mena Salman, Ian J Wellington, Elizabeth Ginalis, Jonathan Dalton, John K Houten, Amrit S Khalsa, Ahmed Saleh, Afshin E Razi, Mitchell K Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing use of artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots like ChatGPT for online patient education, Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) has emerged as a significant tool for providing accurate health information. This study aims to compare Google and GPT-4 in terms of (I) question types, (II) initial response readability, (III) ChatGPT’s ability to modify responses for increased readability, and (IV) numerical response accuracy for the top 10 most frequently asked questions (FAQs) related to cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA).Methods“Cervical disc arthroplasty” was searched on Google and GPT-4 on December 18, 2023. The top 10 FAQs were recorded and analyzed using the Rothwell system for categorization and Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) criteria for source quality. Readability was assessed by Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid grade level. GPT-4 was prompted to revise text for low-literacy readability. We used Student’s t-tests for a comparative analysis between GPT-4 and Google, setting significance at P<0.05.ResultsFAQs from Google predominantly related to technical details and evaluation of surgery, paralleling GPT-4’s focus, which also included indications/management. No significant differences were found in readability between GPT-4 and Google, displaying a similar Flesch-Kincaid grade level (13.06 vs. 12.24, P=0.41) and Flesch Reading Ease score (36.87 vs. 40.05, P=0.53). Upon prompting GPT-4 to improve the readability of its responses, GPT-4 showed a lower Flesch-Kincaid grade level (6.58 vs. 13.06 vs. 12.24, P<0.001) and a higher Flesch Reading Ease score (76.20 vs. 36.87 vs. 40.05, P<0.001). Numerically, 60% of responses differed, with GPT-4 suggesting a broader recovery period for CDA.ConclusionsGPT-4 has the potential to enhance patient education about CDA by customizing complex information for users with lower health literacy levels. This highlights GPT-4’s ability to address existing gaps in online resources, benefiting those with lower health literacy.Keywords: GPT-4, Google, cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA), patient education

Highlight box

Key findings

• Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) demonstrated significant potential in enhancing patient education for cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) by providing more readable and accessible information than traditional Google searches. Upon prompting, GPT-4 could simplify medical content to a sixth-grade reading level without omitting critical details, addressing gaps in online resources for users with lower health literacy.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots like ChatGPT provide instant access to medical information. This manuscript adds a direct comparison of GPT-4 and Google in the context of CDA, highlighting GPT-4’s superior ability to adjust readability and provide more consistent educational material.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings suggest a need for integrating AI-driven tools like GPT-4 into patient education resources to ensure accessible and accurate information, particularly for complex medical procedures. Healthcare providers and educators should consider leveraging such technology to enhance patient understanding and support informed decision-making.

Introduction

Background

ChatGPT, an artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot, has seen widespread use with over 100 million weekly users seeking online information (1). OpenAI introduced Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) on March 13, 2023, offering more precise answers across various domains through a ChatGPT Plus subscription (2). This latest iteration is not limited by data before 2021, thanks to its internet search capabilities, marking a significant step in providing high-quality information (3-9). Such advancements raise the question of AI chatbots’ potential in enhancing online patient education. Considering that 89% of U.S. citizens turn to Google for health information before doctor visits, this study uses Google as a standard to evaluate the responses from GPT-4 for accuracy and quality (10).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Several studies have scrutinized online medical information quality. Sezgin et al. found that ChatGPT’s postpartum depression advice aligned with the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, outperforming Google’s poorer quality responses (11). Another study performed by Wei et al. looked at frequently asked questions (FAQs) regarding head and neck cancer, comparing answers from the Google web search with those generated by GPT-4 (12). Wei et al. found that GPT-4 displayed decreased readability when compared to the answers provided by the Google web search (12). However, it is important to note the capability of ChatGPT to be prompted to revise original bodies of text to increase readability. A study conducted by Moons et al. assessed the ability of ChatGPT and Google Bard, another AI chatbot, to improve the readability of written patient information materials (13). While Bard was able to decrease the reading level of the content to the sixth-grade level, the AI chatbot oversimplified the text by omitting up to 83% of the content. In contrast, ChatGPT demonstrated the ability to decrease reading level without omitting pertinent information. Haver et al. noted GPT-4’s superior readability improvements over GPT-3.5, indicating its advancement in tailoring responses to a lower reading level (14). These findings highlight the capabilities of AI chatbots like ChatGPT and GPT-4 in providing accessible patient information, suggesting a shift towards more user-friendly medical content online.

The diversity observed in Google search outcomes, AI-driven chatbot responses, and the knowledge possessed by physicians, alongside the reality that the health literacy of the average American adult falls below the recommended sixth-grade level, invites a new discussion regarding the accuracy of information that patients might come across on the internet, particularly in relation to common procedures of the cervical spine (15,16). Cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA), a motion-preserving treatment for radiculopathy and myelopathy caused by conditions like degenerative disc disease, avoids fusion risks such as adjacent segment disease (17). With CDA’s use growing annually by 17%, patients increasingly seek information from platforms like GPT-4 to understand the procedure and eligibility before seeing a surgeon, highlighting the need to ensure the information’s quality (18).

Objective

While previous research has evaluated the quality of responses produced by AI chatbots for different medical conditions and treatment options, no studies to date have specifically examined how GPT-4 compares to Google in delivering information related to CDA. This study seeks to compare the two platforms based on the following factors: (I) the types of questions generated, (II) the readability of their initial responses, (III) GPT-4’s capacity to revise answers for improved readability, and (IV) the accuracy of numerical responses to the top 10 most FAQs about CDA. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-47/rc).

Methods

On December 18, 2023, a Google web search was conducted using the term “cervical disc arthroplasty”, and the 10 most FAQs related to this term were documented. These questions were collected from the “People Also Ask” feature that appears within the standard Google search results, and a newly installed Chrome browser was used to avoid personalization. On the same day, GPT-4 was accessed via the ChatGPT Plus platform with the “Browse with Bing” tool enabled, allowing the model to retrieve real-time information from the internet. The following prompt was entered into GPT-4: “Perform a search with the search term ‘cervical disc arthroplasty’ and record the 10 most FAQs related to the search term.” This prompt was designed to simulate a real-time search engine query, instructing GPT-4 to identify common patient-facing questions based on current web content, rather than generate original or inferred questions from its training data. The gathered questions and their answers were then categorized according to the Rothwell classification system. This system sorts questions into three categories: fact, policy, and value, and further subdivides them into 10 topics specific to CDA (Table 1) (19-22). To evaluate GPT-4’s capability in enhancing the readability of response text, the following instruction was entered into GPT-4 after the initial response was generated: “Rewrite the text for people with low literacy.” This approach aligns with a previous study that investigated the effectiveness of GPT-4 in improving the readability of text materials (23).

Websites linked to each question were assessed for quality using the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, which evaluates authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency (24). Two independent reviewers categorized the questions, and any disagreements were resolved by a senior author. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess the level of agreement between reviewers. Additionally, the top 10 numerical FAQs identified through Google searches were entered into GPT-4 to compare and evaluate the similarity of its numerical responses.To compare responses from both platforms, the analysis focused on readability metrics, including the Flesch Reading Ease score, Flesch-Kincaid grade level, and total word count. The Flesch Reading Ease score, which ranges from 0 to 100, is determined based on average sentence length and syllables per word, with higher scores indicating easier-to-read content. Scores from 90 to 100 usually signify content suitable for elementary school students, 60 to 70 for middle school students, and 0 to 30 for university graduates. Consequently, the Flesch Reading Ease score is inversely related to the Flesch-Kincaid grade level, meaning a higher reading ease score denotes a lower grade level requirement (25-27). Analysis of the shared responses was conducted in plain-text format utilizing a software that assesses readability (https://readability-score.com).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Interobserver reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Student’s t-tests were used to compare JAMA benchmark scores, Flesch-Kincaid grade levels, Flesch Reading Ease scores, and word counts across responses from GPT-4, Google, and different website sources. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FAQs in Google Web search and GPT-4 for CDA

Among the top ten FAQs from a Google web search on CDA, the predominant Rothwell category was fact, representing 7 of the questions, followed by value with 3 questions, and none in the policy category (Table 2, Figure 1). The most frequent topics among FAQs were technical details, comprising 4 questions, and evaluation of surgery, comprising 3 questions (Table 2, Figure 2). For GPT-4, fact was also the most common Rothwell category, comprising 4 questions, with policy and value categories each contributing 3 questions (Table 2, Figure 1). Among the GPT-4 generated questions, the leading topics were evaluation of surgery, technical details, and indications/management, each with 2 questions (Table 2, Figure 2). The interobserver reliability scores for Rothwell category and topic classification were 0.742 and 0.758, respectively, indicating a high level of agreement among observers (P<0.001 for all).

For the FAQs related to CDA answered through a Google web search, social media was the most frequently cited source, making up 5 of the 10 unique websites. This was followed by medical practice websites, which accounted for 4 out of 10, and commercial websites, constituting 1 out of 10 unique websites. The interobserver reliability score for categorizing these websites was 0.931 (P<0.001), indicating a high level of agreement among the observers.

Discussion

AI-driven chatbots are increasingly being explored as tools to provide patients with quick access to health information and basic medical guidance (28). ChatGPT, in particular, has been the focus of significant research due to its capacity to generate contextually appropriate responses to user input (29,30). This study aimed to evaluate GPT-4’s effectiveness in answering FAQs about CDA and to compare the readability of its responses with those generated through a Google web search. Google’s most frequently encountered question topics centered around surgical evaluation and technical aspects, while GPT-4’s responses largely addressed evaluation, technical details, and indications or management. Although GPT-4’s initial responses had readability levels—measured by the Flesch-Kincaid grade level and Flesch Reading Ease score—that were similar in complexity to Google’s, the responses differed in length. When prompted to simplify its answers, GPT-4 produced content with a significantly lower Flesch-Kincaid grade level and higher Reading Ease score, all while maintaining a comparable word count to its original output.

When prompted to generate the top 10 most FAQs about CDA, GPT-4 produced only two questions that overlapped with those identified through a Google web search. This mirrors findings from a previous study on anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, where just 2 of the top 10 FAQs were shared between platforms (21). A likely explanation lies in GPT-4’s reliance on Bing for web data, potentially sourcing FAQs from structured content on specific websites rather than utilizing Bing’s “People Also Ask” feature (8,9). This distinction in methodology may significantly shape the type of information patients encounter when using GPT-4. In contrast, traditional search engines like Google rely on machine learning and natural language processing to interpret user intent and retrieve relevant content from a vast array of indexed webpages. These systems adeptly detect search patterns to retrieve FAQs related to the original query (31,32). In contrast, GPT-4 derives its questions directly from pages containing specific information on the topic. This method suggests that GPT-4 might provide more contextually relevant and topic-specific FAQs compared to the broader search patterns of traditional search engines. It could lead to a more tailored and efficient information retrieval experience for users, especially in specialized fields like spine surgery. However, this also indicates that the quality and reliability of GPT-4’s output are heavily dependent on the source material it accesses. This underscores the importance of ensuring that the content GPT-4 pulls from is accurately curated and reliable.

GPT-4’s responses demonstrated a comparable Flesch-Kincaid grade level but a lower Flesch Reading Ease score relative to those generated by Google, consistent with previous findings that GPT-3.5 content often falls within collegiate to post-collegiate readability ranges (33-35). This indicates that, similar to its predecessor, GPT-4 may not be readily understandable to the average American adult—particularly given that both the National Institutes of Health and the American Medical Association recommend health information be written at or below a sixth-grade reading level (15,16). Notably, Google’s collegiate-level FAQ responses for CDA reflect the readability of cervical radiculopathy literature from web search studies, yet they are considerably higher than those for cauda equina syndrome (36,37). This highlights a disparity in the readability of online medical content, suggesting an insufficiency of simple, understandable information on CDA. The low search volume for CDA compared to more common procedures like cervical discectomy and fusion, which exceeds CDA by a ratio of 81:1, might contribute to the limited lower-level reading materials (38). This scarcity necessitates a call to action for primary CDA-focused websites to improve the readability of their content, making it accessible to a wider audience and meeting health literacy goals. Such enhancements are vital not only for patient understanding but also for the provision of online resources that effectively serve the information needs of a diverse population.

Although GPT-4 initially produces content with a readability level comparable to that of Google—typically at a collegiate level—it has the unique capability to simplify complex information when prompted. On average, this simplification lowers the reading grade level by 6.48 grades. While this is consistent with previous studies investigating the ability of ChatGPT to markedly improve the readability of content, this marks a significant improvement in the magnitude by which the average Flesch-Kincaid grade level can be improved. Compared to a study by Ayre et al. on the utility of GPT-3.5 in simplifying responses, GPT-4 has shown a 1.96-fold improvement in reducing the Flesch-Kincaid grade level (23). However, this study highlights an advancement in GPT-4’s ability to enhance readability over previous iterations. While we achieved a sixth to seventh grade reading level in this study, Moons et al. and Haver et al. had difficulty lowering the literacy level of materials to below a ninth-grade level using earlier versions of the chatbot (13,14). This demonstrates significant progress in GPT-4’s capabilities, reflecting its evolving algorithms and improved training. These findings are particularly relevant for patient education in complex medical procedures like CDA, where accessible information is crucial. GPT-4’s ability to simplify complex medical information could be pivotal in bridging the gap left by existing resources on Google in areas like CDA, which fail to cater to patients with lower literacy levels.

When comparing Google’s search results with GPT-4’s responses to CDA-related FAQs involving numerical data, 6 out of 10 questions yielded conflicting information between the two platforms. This mirrors findings from a prior study on total joint arthroplasty, which noted that 55% of numerical responses differed across platforms (39). Discrepancies were especially evident in technical metrics such as surgical success rates and implant longevity. For instance, in response to the question, “What is the success rate of cervical disc arthroplasty?”, Google cited a rate of 62.40%, whereas GPT-4 reported a figure exceeding 80%. Such inconsistencies are particularly important in a clinical context, as they may shape patient expectations and influence their willingness to proceed with surgery. In contrast, more straightforward questions—such as “How long do you stay in the hospital after cervical disc replacement?”—yielded consistent answers (1–2 days) across both platforms, suggesting some agreement on clearly defined clinical outcomes. These results underscore the importance of improving consistency and accuracy across digital information platforms, especially when presenting medical statistics that may directly affect patient decision-making.

Another important consideration is the risk of misinformation due to hallucinations—an established limitation of large language models like GPT-4. These models may occasionally generate plausible-sounding but inaccurate or fabricated information. In our study, 60% of numerical responses differed between GPT-4 and Google, underscoring the variability and potential inaccuracy in AI-generated health content. In clinical contexts, such discrepancies carry significant risks, as they may lead to patient misunderstanding, misplaced expectations, or inappropriate decision-making. Accordingly, any AI-derived health information should be reviewed and validated by medical professionals before being used in patient education. Ensuring the reliability of these tools is essential before broader integration into healthcare communication strategies. In addition to factual accuracy, the transparency of information sources is a key concern. Although GPT-4 provided direct hyperlinks in our dataset, the majority of citations originated from commercial or practice-based websites, with limited academic representation. This narrow and non-peer-reviewed citation pool raises concerns about the evidence base supporting GPT-4’s outputs. Moreover, GPT-based models are known to occasionally fabricate citations, mimicking the structure of real references while pointing to non-existent sources. These issues reinforce the need for future AI systems to prioritize verifiable, high-quality sourcing and citation transparency to ensure both accuracy and trustworthiness in patient education.

This study has several limitations. First, fluctuations in Google’s search results, due to ongoing content updates, may influence the consistency and accuracy of the information retrieved. Similarly, GPT-4’s use of Bing as its search engine can result in variable responses, potentially impacting the reliability of the data. Although efforts were made to reduce personalization by using a freshly installed Google Chrome browser, some degree of result tailoring by Google may still have occurred. Additionally, the requirement to log into GPT-4 could introduce user-specific biases based on prior interactions. Unlike GPT-3.5, which is freely accessible, GPT-4 is a paid service, which may reflect a more selective user base and influence the nature of its outputs. The phrasing of prompts used to query GPT-4 may also not fully replicate how patients naturally seek information. While validated readability measures like the Flesch-Kincaid grade level and Reading Ease score were employed, the study did not directly evaluate comprehension among non-medical users. These metrics measure textual complexity, not whether users can meaningfully understand or retain the information. Furthermore, we did not evaluate whether GPT-4’s simplified responses—generated after prompting for improved readability—omitted any clinically relevant or integral content. Future studies should incorporate human validation—such as Likert-based usability assessments, comprehension testing, or interviews—to determine the real-world effectiveness of AI-generated health information and to ensure alignment with patient needs and perspectives.

Conclusions

This study highlights GPT-4’s potential to enhance patient education through the provision of information on CDA. While the initial readability of GPT-4’s responses was comparable to Google’s, with a similar collegiate-level complexity, GPT-4 demonstrated a remarkable ability to simplify responses upon request, significantly lowering the Flesch-Kincaid grade level. This suggests that GPT-4 could fill gaps in online resources, particularly benefiting users with lower health literacy. Moreover, the study revealed discrepancies in numerical answers between Google and GPT-4, underscoring the need for consistent and accurate medical information online. Despite limitations such as potential biases due to personalized search results and the paid nature of GPT-4, the findings advocate for further refinement of AI chatbots to better align with patient language and understanding. The study calls for ongoing efforts to ensure that AI tools like GPT-4 not only provide accurate medical information but also present it in a manner that is accessible to all users, thereby supporting informed healthcare decisions.
