A Global Health Perspective on the 2026 AHA/ASA Stroke Guidelines: Lessons from the Indian Health System
Authors: Priyanka Boettger, Ayush Agarwal, Omar Alhaj Omar, Martin Juenemann, Samuel Sossalla, Michael Buerke, Emmanuel Carrera, Ivy Anne Sebastian, Jeyaraj D. Pandian, Padmavathy Narayanan Sylaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContemporary evidence-based guidelines have substantially improved outcomes after acute ischemic stroke in well-resourced health systems. However, their implementation remains uneven in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where infrastructure, specialist workforce, financing, and time-critical workflows often differ from the assumptions embedded in guideline development. The extent to which high-income reference guidelines can be translated into resource-constrained settings therefore requires systematic evaluation.SummaryWe conducted a structured systems-level review of the 2026 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guideline for the early management of acute ischemic stroke, using India as an illustrative LMIC case. Acute implementation-relevant recommendations were grouped into clinically coherent clusters across eight domains of care and assessed using a predefined capacity-burden framework covering infrastructure and technology requirements, specialist workforce intensity, workflow complexity and time sensitivity, and financial or consumable dependency. Recommendation clusters were classified as directly implementable, adaptable, or infrastructure-constrained. Feasibility varied substantially across domains. Low-burden components, including stroke awareness, prehospital assessment, prenotification, stroke severity assessment, dysphagia screening, glucose monitoring, and pragmatic blood pressure protocols, were largely directly implementable. Moderate-capacity recommendations, including non-contrast computed tomography-first triage, protocolized intravenous thrombolysis, telestroke support, interhospital transfer agreements, and organized stroke-unit principles, were generally adaptable through service redesign, task-sharing, protocolization, and regional referral models. In contrast, advanced perfusion imaging, mobile stroke units, comprehensive thrombectomy pathways, and endovascular thrombectomy remained predominantly infrastructure constrained. The Indian case demonstrated similar feasibility gradients, while highlighting persistent rural-urban, public-private, and financing-related inequities.Key MessagesMany contemporary acute ischemic stroke guideline components can be implemented or adapted in resource-constrained health systems when translated according to available system capacity. The principal barrier to equitable stroke outcomes is not the absence of evidence but the misalignment between guideline assumptions and real-world infrastructure, workforce, financing, and workflow capacity. A capacity-burden approach offers a pragmatic framework for prioritizing stroke interventions, distinguishing immediately scalable components from those requiring service redesign or longer-term investment, and supporting more equitable global stroke care.

Introduction

Stroke remains a major contributor to global mortality and long-term disability, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for the majority of stroke-related deaths and disability-adjusted life years worldwide [1–3]. Although age-standardized stroke mortality has declined globally, absolute numbers continue to increase, reflecting population aging and the growing prevalence of vascular risk factors, particularly in resource-constrained settings [4]. Despite the availability of robust evidence and increasingly detailed clinical practice guidelines, reductions in stroke mortality and morbidity have been substantially greater in high-income countries than in low-income and lower-middle-income countries, where health system capacity to deliver evidence-based care remains uneven.

Disparities are most pronounced in the acute management of ischemic stroke, a clinical context in which guideline-recommended interventions are highly time dependent and system sensitive. While intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy have significantly improved outcomes in well-resourced health systems, access to these interventions remains limited in many LMICs due to delayed presentation, restricted availability of neuroimaging, shortages of trained personnel, and financial barriers to care [5]. Large international observational studies, including INTERSTROKE, have consistently demonstrated higher stroke severity, lower rates of diagnostic evaluation and reperfusion therapy, and higher case fatality in LMIC settings compared with high-income countries [6]. These findings underscore a persistent gap between the availability of evidence and its effective delivery in routine clinical practice.

Clinical practice guidelines are intended to support translation of evidence into standardized care pathways. Recently, the World Stroke Organization (WSO) published a comprehensive systematic review and synthesis of global stroke guidelines, demonstrating substantial concordance across recommendations and classifying them according to minimal, essential, and advanced levels of stroke service provision [7]. This work represents an important step toward harmonization of global guidance. However, the synthesis also highlighted that most recommendations are derived from high-income settings and provide limited direction regarding prioritization, sequencing, and operationalization in health systems with constrained resources. As a result, consensus on recommended interventions has not resolved uncertainties regarding implementation in settings where infrastructure, workforce, and financing differ substantially from those assumed in guideline development [8].

Against this backdrop, contemporary high-income guidelines, most recently the 2026 acute ischemic stroke guideline issued by the American Heart Association and the American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) [9], continue to refine evidence-based recommendations across the acute stroke care continuum, including prehospital triage, imaging-based decision-making, reperfusion therapies, and early in-hospital management. These guidelines represent the current evidence benchmark. However, their recommendations are largely predicated on assumptions of timely emergency medical services, immediate access to neuroimaging, dedicated stroke-unit infrastructure, and endovascular capability. Such assumptions are not consistently met in many LMICs and in under-resourced regions within high-income countries, resulting in predictable implementation gaps despite robust supporting evidence [1, 10, 11].

From a global health perspective, addressing these gaps requires moving beyond further guideline synthesis and toward structured analyses of guideline translatability. This involves identifying which components of evidence-based recommendations are feasible under constrained conditions, which can be adapted through service redesign, such as protocol-driven care, workforce upskilling, or telemedicine-supported networks, and which remain dependent on longer-term investments in infrastructure and specialist capacity. Although resource-stratified approaches to stroke services have been advocated by international organizations, empirical analyses linking specific guideline recommendations to real-world system constraints remain limited [12–14].

India offers a particularly informative context in which to examine these challenges. With a high stroke burden and marked heterogeneity in stroke service provision, India encompasses advanced tertiary centers alongside hospitals with limited diagnostic and therapeutic resources [4, 15]. At the same time, several pragmatic stroke-care models have been implemented in resource-constrained settings, including physician-led stroke units, hub-and-spoke systems supported by telemedicine, and task-sharing approaches involving non-specialist health workers [16, 17]. These experiences provide valuable insight into potential pathways for adapting evidence-based stroke care under real-world constraints [18, 19].

The objective of this review was to examine how contemporary evidence-based recommendations for acute ischemic stroke intersect with health system capacity. Using the 2026 AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline as a high-income reference standard and India as an illustrative LMIC case, we conducted a systems-level analysis to assess feasibility, adaptability, and implementation barriers across key domains of acute stroke care. Rather than duplicating existing guideline syntheses, this review aims to inform context-sensitive translation of stroke evidence by identifying priority areas for adaptation and scalable implementation in resource-constrained health systems.

Methods

Study Design and Analytical Approach

We conducted a structured, systems-level review of contemporary evidence-based recommendations for the acute management of ischemic stroke, focusing on their translatability to resource-constrained health systems. The objective was not to synthesize or reappraise guideline evidence but to examine feasibility, adaptability, and implementation barriers of guideline recommendations across different levels of health system capacity. The review was informed by principles of global health implementation research and health systems analysis.

Guideline Selection

As a high-income reference standard, we used the 2026 guideline for the early management of acute ischemic stroke issued by the AHA/ASA [9]. This guideline was selected because it represents the most up-to-date, comprehensive synthesis of evidence for acute ischemic stroke care, encompassing prehospital triage, imaging-based decision-making, reperfusion therapies, and early in-hospital management.

To contextualize our analysis within the existing global guidance landscape, we drew on the WSO synthesis of global stroke guidelines, which classifies recommendations according to minimal, essential, and advanced stroke service levels [7]. This synthesis was used as a conceptual framework for resource stratification, rather than as a source of additional recommendations. Where relevant, interpretations of the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline were explicitly linked to the corresponding guideline section, class of recommendation (COR), and level of evidence (LOE) to ensure transparency and reproducibility of recommendation-level mapping [9]. Recommendation-level references were retained particularly for statements relating to prehospital triage and emergency medical services workflows, imaging pathways, reperfusion eligibility, blood pressure targets, glucose management, antithrombotic therapy, dysphagia screening, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, and stroke systems of care.

Analytical Framework

Guideline recommendations from the 2026 AHA/ASA document were grouped into eight predefined domains reflecting the acute stroke care continuum: (1) prehospital care, (2) emergency assessment and stroke-team activation, (3) diagnostic imaging and triage, (4) intravenous thrombolysis, (5) endovascular thrombectomy and transfer systems, (6) stroke-unit and in-hospital organization, (7) supportive care and complication management, and (8) telemedicine, registries, and equity monitoring. Intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy were analyzed as separate implementation domains because they differ substantially in infrastructure requirements, specialist workforce intensity, workflow complexity, and financial dependency.

Within each domain, individual recommendations were grouped into clinically coherent recommendation clusters. This approach was chosen to avoid artificial fragmentation of closely related implementation tasks while preserving the link to the original guideline section, COR, and LOE where available. The recommendation cluster, corresponding AHA/ASA section, COR, LOE, capacity assumptions, capacity-burden score, feasibility classification, and rationale for classification are reported in online supplementary Table S1a and S1b (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553505).

Feasibility classification was undertaken at the level of recommendation clusters rather than entire service packages. This recommendation-level approach was used to identify which components of evidence-based acute stroke care are directly implementable, which require adaptation through service redesign, and which remain infrastructure constrained in resource-limited settings. The categorization aligns conceptually with the WSO minimal-essential-advanced service framework but applies resource stratification to specific guideline components rather than to whole models of stroke service provision. The recommendation cluster, corresponding AHA/ASA section, COR, LOE, capacity assumptions, and implementation-relevant India linkage are reported in online supplementary Table S1a.

Feasibility Classification Framework

To enhance transparency and reproducibility, feasibility classification was operationalized using a predefined capacity-burden scoring framework. Each recommendation cluster was systematically evaluated across four health system capacity dimensions: (1) infrastructure and technology requirements, (2) specialist workforce intensity, (3) workflow complexity and time sensitivity, and (4) financial or consumable dependency. Each dimension was scored from 0 to 2, with 0 indicating low capacity burden, 1 indicating moderate capacity burden, and 2 indicating high capacity burden. Capacity-burden scoring framework for recommendation-level feasibility classification

Recommendation clusters were assessed across four predefined health system capacity dimensions: infrastructure and technology requirements, specialist workforce intensity, workflow complexity and time sensitivity, and financial or consumable dependency. Each dimension was scored from 0 to 2, yielding a total capacity-burden score from 0 to 8. Total scores of 0–2 were classified as directly implementable, scores of 3–5 as adaptable, and scores of 6–8 as infrastructure constrained. The framework was used to support transparent and reproducible classification of acute ischemic stroke guideline recommendations according to their implementation feasibility in resource-constrained health systems.

For each recommendation cluster, the four dimension-specific scores were summed to generate a total capacity-burden score ranging from 0 to 8. Recommendation clusters with total scores of 0–2 were classified as directly implementable, those scoring 3–5 as adaptable, and those scoring 6–8 as infrastructure constrained. Directly implementable recommendations were defined as those feasible with existing or modestly enhanced resources, requiring limited additional infrastructure, non-specialist or minimally trained staff, low workflow complexity, and low recurrent cost. Adaptable recommendations were defined as those with moderate capacity requirements that could plausibly be addressed through service redesign, task sharing, protocolization, telemedicine support, regional referral pathways, or phased implementation. Infrastructure-constrained recommendations were defined as those dependent on advanced infrastructure, highly specialized workforce capacity, tightly coordinated time-critical workflows, or substantial financial investment not widely available in resource-constrained settings. Predefined override rules were applied to preserve clinical plausibility. Recommendation clusters requiring both advanced procedural infrastructure and highly specialized workforce capacity were classified as infrastructure constrained irrespective of the total score. Conversely, clusters with moderate capacity burden were classified as adaptable when a realistic implementation pathway existed through regionalization, telemedicine, protocolized referral, or phased service development. Classification was therefore based on the total capacity-burden score, supported by predefined decision rules, rather than on a single determinant. Assessments were performed independently by two authors with expertise in stroke systems and global health implementation. Both assessors first scored all recommendation clusters across the four predefined capacity dimensions and assigned an initial feasibility category before consensus discussion. Inter-rater agreement for the initial three-category feasibility classification was assessed using linear weighted Cohen’s kappa, reflecting the ordinal structure of the categories: directly implementable, adaptable, and infrastructure constrained. Discrepancies were subsequently resolved through structured consensus discussion, with attention to the underlying capacity assumptions and the feasibility of pragmatic adaptation strategies. Borderline decisions and the rationale for final classification are reported in online supplementary Table S1a and S1b. For example, non-contrast computed tomography for initial stroke triage was classified as adaptable because it requires imaging infrastructure but can be implemented through shared access models, outsourcing, or protocol-driven prioritization. In contrast, endovascular thrombectomy was classified as infrastructure constrained because it depends on advanced imaging, specialized interventional facilities, trained procedural teams, and coordinated peri-procedural workflows. Dimension-specific capacity-burden scores, total scores, final feasibility classifications, and the rationale for classification are reported in online supplementary Table S1b.

LMIC Case Illustration

India was selected as an illustrative LMIC case to contextualize implementation pathways and system constraints. This choice was based on its high absolute stroke burden, the scale and heterogeneity of its health system, and the coexistence of advanced tertiary stroke centers with district hospitals and peripheral facilities that have more limited diagnostic and specialist capacity. India is also particularly informative because national stroke guidance explicitly addresses prevention, early recognition, referral, acute care, rehabilitation, and service delivery across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. It therefore provides a useful setting in which to examine how high-income guideline assumptions can be translated into resource-stratified pathways rather than adopted as uniform service standards. In addition, India offers empirical examples of pragmatic adaptation strategies relevant to resource-constrained systems, including early recognition and referral pathways, district-level stroke-ready care, protocol-driven acute management, physician-led or team-based stroke units, telemedicine-supported models, and phased development of advanced services. In this sense, India was considered an emerging implementation exemplar rather than a fully representative LMIC setting. Its combination of high disease burden, rural-urban and public-private variation, expanding tertiary stroke capability, and national policy efforts to build stroke care capacity across levels of the health system makes it useful for identifying which components of contemporary stroke guidelines can be scaled immediately, which require system redesign, and which depend on longer-term investment. The purpose of this case illustration was not to evaluate national performance or to present India as representative of all LMICs. Rather, India was used as a systems-level example of how contemporary high-income guideline recommendations interact with heterogeneous health system capacity in a large transitioning LMIC. Indian evidence was therefore interpreted cautiously, recognizing that data from registries, tertiary centers, and selected facility surveys may overestimate service availability in smaller public hospitals and rural areas. Evidence from peer-reviewed observational studies, implementation reports, national guidance, and systematic reviews describing stroke care delivery in India and comparable LMICs was used to inform feasibility classifications and adaptation pathways [4, 17, 20].

Data Sources and Synthesis

Relevant literature describing stroke care delivery, implementation barriers, and pragmatic care models in LMICs was identified through targeted searches of PubMed and reference lists of key reviews, including the WSO guideline synthesis and major LMIC-focused stroke systems publications [11, 17]. Formal quality appraisal of individual studies was not undertaken as the aim was to contextualize guideline recommendations rather than to estimate intervention effects. Findings were synthesized narratively, with emphasis on identifying recurrent system-level constraints, adaptation strategies, and cross-cutting implementation principles. Results are presented by domain of care, highlighting areas of alignment and tension between guideline recommendations and real-world system capacity. Results

Overall Translatability of Guideline Recommendations

System-level mapping of recommendations from the AHA/ASA 2026 acute ischemic stroke guideline demonstrated marked heterogeneity in feasibility across domains of care when assessed against resource-constrained health system contexts. All classifications were performed according to the predefined capacity-based framework described in the Methods section. When mapped against predefined capacity dimensions, including infrastructure requirement, workforce intensity, workflow complexity, and financial dependency, distinct domain-specific feasibility gradients emerged. Recommendations differed substantially in their reliance on infrastructure, specialist workforce, and system organization. Across domains, three recurring feasibility categories emerged: (i) recommendations that were directly implementable with modest resources, (ii) recommendations that were adaptable through service redesign or task reallocation, and (iii) recommendations that remained constrained due to dependence on advanced infrastructure or highly specialized capacity. Detailed mapping of guideline components and their classification across feasibility categories is provided in online supplementary Table S1. Before consensus discussion, the two independent assessors agreed on the feasibility classification in 21 of 26 recommendation clusters (80.8%). Linear weighted Cohen’s kappa for the ordinal three-category classification was 0.76, indicating substantial agreement. Disagreements were limited to borderline classifications between adaptable and infrastructure-constrained recommendations, particularly for vascular imaging, telemedicine-supported thrombolysis pathways, and interhospital transfer-system components. All discrepancies were resolved through structured consensus discussion before final classification. The complete recommendation-cluster mapping, including AHA/ASA section, COR, LOE, capacity-burden scores, final feasibility classification, classification rationale, pragmatic LMIC adaptation, India linkage, and candidate audit metrics, is provided in online supplementary Table S1.

Prehospital Care and Emergency Medical Services

Several prehospital recommendations were consistently identified as directly implementable in resource-constrained settings. These included early stroke recognition and structured prehospital assessment using validated stroke scales (Section 2.1, COR 1) [9], as well as pre-notification of receiving hospitals (Section 2.3, COR 1) [9]. Implementation of these measures primarily required protocol development and targeted training rather than substantial capital investment. In contrast, recommendations assuming universal access to coordinated emergency medical services with rapid ambulance response were variably constrained. In many settings, fragmented transport systems and delayed first medical contact limited timely access to stroke-capable centers. Notably, the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline states that, in areas with well-coordinated stroke systems of care and local hospitals proficient in thrombolysis delivery and secondary interhospital transfer, direct transport of patients with suspected large-vessel occlusion to a more distant thrombectomy-capable center does not improve 3-month clinical outcomes (Section 2.4, COR 3: No Benefit, LOE B-R) [9]. This recommendation underscores that system performance, particularly rapid thrombolysis delivery and efficient door-in-door-out transfer, may outweigh geographic bypass in resource-constrained settings. Accordingly, prioritizing optimized transfer processes and structured referral networks may be more effective than prolonged prehospital diversion. Adaptable strategies include alternative transport pathways, community-based referral mechanisms, and regional hub-and-spoke models to concentrate expertise.

Diagnostic Imaging and Triage

Recommendations requiring non-contrast brain imaging to differentiate ischemic stroke from intracerebral hemorrhage were broadly adaptable but not uniformly feasible. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends emergent brain imaging with non-contrast computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging for initial evaluation (Section 3.2, COR 1, LOE A) [9], with protocols aiming to achieve rapid imaging acquisition, ideally within 25 min of hospital arrival (Section 3.2, COR 1, LOE B-NR) [9]. Although computed tomography was available in many secondary and tertiary centers, continuous 24/7 access and rapid turnaround times were frequently limited. Importantly, the guideline further recommends that, in patients with suspected acute ischemic stroke and large-vessel occlusion, emergent vascular imaging with contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography and/or perfusion imaging should not be delayed in order to obtain serum creatinine measurement (Section 3.2, COR 1, LOE B-NR) [9]. In resource-constrained environments where laboratory turnaround may represent a critical bottleneck, this recommendation has particular relevance for minimizing avoidable treatment delays.

Advanced imaging-based selection strategies, which are recommended in guideline-based pathways for patient selection for endovascular thrombectomy and supported by recommendations for rapid vascular imaging (Section 3.2, COR 1, LOE A) [9], remained largely constrained by equipment availability, cost, and trained personnel. Adaptation approaches therefore included prioritization of non-contrast computed tomography for initial triage, streamlined imaging protocols, and selective referral of patients requiring advanced imaging to higher-level centers within organized referral networks.

Reperfusion Therapies

Recommendations for intravenous thrombolysis demonstrated mixed feasibility across settings. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends that eligible patients presenting within 4.5 h of symptom onset or last known well receive intravenous thrombolysis as rapidly as possible using either tenecteplase at 0.25 mg/kg (maximum 25 mg) or alteplase at 0.9 mg/kg, without delays related to additional multimodal imaging when not required for decision-making (Section 4.6.1, COR 1, LOE B-NR; Section 4.6.2, COR 1, LOE A) [9]. In resource-constrained settings, this supports implementation models centered on rapid non-contrast imaging, protocolized eligibility assessment, and simplified acute workflows. Population-level access remained limited by delayed presentation, uneven drug availability, and financial barriers. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline strongly recommends endovascular thrombectomy for selected patients with anterior circulation proximal large-vessel occlusion within 6 h of symptom onset and for selected patients within 6–24 h based on imaging and clinical criteria (Section 4.7.2, COR 1, LOE A) [9]. From a systems perspective, however, implementation of these recommendations remains uneven as they are intrinsically dependent on advanced imaging capability, interventional infrastructure, and specialized peri-procedural care. In many resource-constrained settings, these requirements limit access to a small number of tertiary centers. Consequently, translation into practice relies less on broad decentralization and more on system-level adaptation, including structured referral pathways, regional hub-and-spoke networks, and phased development of thrombectomy-capable centers.

Organization of In-Hospital Care

The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends treatment within an organized inpatient stroke unit supported by a specialty-trained interdisciplinary team to reduce mortality and disability (Section 5.1, COR 1, LOE B-R) [9]. In resource-constrained settings, full implementation of comprehensive multidisciplinary stroke units is often limited by workforce shortages and infrastructure constraints. However, the core principles of organized stroke care remain highly adaptable. Protocolized monitoring, early mobilization, dysphagia screening, and coordinated discharge planning can frequently be implemented through reorganization of existing wards and targeted staff training. In this context, physician-led stroke units, task-sharing approaches, and protocol-driven care pathways represent pragmatic models that preserve the benefits of organized stroke care despite limited specialist capacity.

Early Complication Management and Supportive Care

Recommendations addressing early management of complications, including fever, hyperglycemia, dysphagia, oxygenation, blood pressure control, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, were consistently identified as among the most feasible components of acute stroke care across settings. These interventions are largely protocol driven and require limited advanced technology, relying primarily on clinical training and standardized workflows. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends maintaining normothermia and actively treating hyperthermia (Section 4.4, COR 1, LOE B-R), avoiding intensive glucose lowering to near-normoglycemic targets (Section 4.5, COR 3: No Benefit, LOE A), providing supplemental oxygen only in hypoxic patients (Section 4.1, COR 1, LOE C-LD), and performing bedside dysphagia screening before oral intake (Section 5.2, COR 1, LOE C-EO) [9]. Blood pressure management is similarly protocolized; the guideline advises against intensive systolic blood pressure reduction to <140 mm Hg after intravenous thrombolysis (Section 4.3, COR 3: No Benefit, LOE B-R) and warns against such aggressive lowering following successful endovascular reperfusion, where harm has been observed (Section 4.3, COR 3: Harm, LOE A) [9]. In addition, intermittent pneumatic compression is recommended to reduce the risk of deep vein thrombosis in immobilized patients (Section 5.4, COR 1, LOE B-R), and early antiplatelet therapy is recommended as part of acute management and secondary prevention planning (Section 4.8, COR 1, LOE A) [9]. From an implementation perspective, however, feasibility in resource-constrained settings may still be influenced by limitations in nursing capacity, continuous monitoring, and availability of basic supportive devices, which may affect consistent delivery of these otherwise low-technology interventions.

LMIC Case Illustration: India

India provides a particularly strong empirical illustration of the feasibility gradient identified in this review. In the Indian Registry of Stroke Care Quality, which prospectively captured 7,337 ischemic strokes across 46 stroke centers between 2022 and 2024, brain parenchymal imaging was performed in all patients but vascular imaging in only 60%; the thrombolysis rate was 32%, median door-to-needle time was 37 min, and endovascular thrombectomy was performed in 6.5% of cases, indicating that even among participating stroke centers advanced imaging and endovascular care remain selectively available rather than universal [18]. At the national level, a geospatial analysis identified 566 intravenous-thrombolysis-capable and 361 thrombectomy-capable centers in India, corresponding to only 0.41 and 0.26 centers per million population, respectively; moreover, only 26.3% of the population lived within 1 h of a thrombolysis-capable center and 20.6% within 1 h of a thrombectomy-capable center, demonstrating major geographic inequities in access to reperfusion services [21]. Facility-level surveys reinforce the structural basis of this gap. Across 22 public tertiary medical colleges, non-contrast computed tomography was universally available, but 39% were not performing CT angiography, only 27% offered endovascular therapy, only 32% had stroke units, and 36% did not provide thrombolysis free of cost [15]. A broader 95-hospital survey likewise found that only half of hospitals had a dedicated stroke unit, with stroke-specific infrastructure and specialist services concentrated in private institutions [22]. Financing barriers remain substantial: a scoping review estimated stroke-related hospital costs up to 6 months after admission ranging from ₹19,428.86 in a government hospital to ₹118,040.15 in a private tertiary center, with substantial out-of-pocket expenditure across income groups [23]. Taken together, these data support the use of India as a genuine systems-level LMIC case study rather than an anecdotal illustration. They suggest that guideline components dependent on rapid non-contrast CT, protocolized thrombolysis workflows, and standardized supportive care are increasingly implementable, whereas universal CT angiography access, dedicated multidisciplinary stroke units, and thrombectomy networks remain concentrated in urban tertiary hubs. This pattern also helps explain why Indian stroke-system development has emphasized hub-and-spoke referral pathways, telestroke, and phased concentration of advanced services rather than uniform decentralization [4].

Cross-Domain Capacity Alignment Patterns

Across domains, translatability was not primarily determined by the clinical category of intervention but by the structural capacity assumptions embedded within individual guideline components. When examined against predefined capacity dimensions, including infrastructure requirement, specialist workforce intensity, workflow complexity, and financial dependency, distinct and recurrent alignment patterns emerged.

Recommendations categorized as directly implementable were generally characterized by low infrastructure dependency and limited specialist intensity, with reliance on protocolized workflows rather than advanced technology. Adaptable components frequently exhibited moderate workflow or workforce demands that could be mitigated through regionalization, task-sharing, or telemedicine-supported decision pathways. In contrast, constrained recommendations consistently combined high technological dependency, specialized workforce requirements, and tightly coordinated time-sensitive processes.

Importantly, these patterns were observed within domains as well as across them, indicating that feasibility gradients are not adequately captured by tier-based classification of entire service packages. Rather, translatability reflects multidimensional capacity alignment at the level of individual recommendations. This cross-domain consistency suggests that guideline implementation gaps arise less from domain-specific deficiencies and more from structural mismatch between embedded guideline assumptions and prevailing health system capacity.

Summary of Findings

Across domains, a substantial proportion of guideline recommendations were either directly implementable or adaptable in resource-constrained health systems, particularly those related to early recognition, protocol-driven care, and basic supportive management. In contrast, recommendations dependent on advanced imaging, endovascular infrastructure, and specialized workforce capacity remained constrained, as detailed in the recommendation-level mapping presented in online supplementary Table S1. Taken together, these domain-specific findings reveal consistent feasibility gradients across the acute stroke care continuum, which are synthesized in Figure 2. The Indian case illustrates how national guideline adaptation aligns with these gradients, reinforcing the relevance of resource-stratified implementation pathways for translating contemporary stroke evidence into routine care in LMIC settings. Across domains, feasibility patterns consistently reflected differential capacity requirements rather than intrinsic differences in evidentiary strength, underscoring system-capacity mismatch as the primary driver of translatability gradients. These gradients were structured along predefined dimensions, including infrastructure dependency, workforce intensity, workflow complexity, and financial requirements, indicating that translatability is determined by multidimensional capacity alignment rather than by domain classification alone.

Discussion

This systems-level review demonstrated that a substantial proportion of contemporary evidence-based recommendations for acute ischemic stroke were either directly implementable or adaptable in resource-constrained health systems, while others remain constrained by infrastructure and workforce requirements. Importantly, feasibility varied across recommendations according to their underlying capacity requirements rather than by clinical domain per se, indicating that the principal barrier to equitable stroke care was not lack of evidence, but the mismatch between guideline assumptions and health system capacity.

Guideline Synthesis versus Guideline Translation

Recent efforts by the World Stroke Organization (WSO) have established a high degree of global consensus across stroke guidelines and classified recommendations according to minimal, essential, and advanced service levels [7]. Our findings extend this work by shifting the focus from consensus to translatability. While global synthesis defines what should be delivered, implementation in resource-constrained settings depends on how individual recommendations align with real-world system capacity. Across domains, recommendations related to early recognition, protocol-driven workflows, and basic supportive care demonstrated consistently high feasibility as they rely primarily on training, standardized processes, and organizational efficiency rather than advanced technology. In contrast, recommendations dependent on advanced neuroimaging, comprehensive stroke unit infrastructure, and endovascular thrombectomy remained constrained in most low- and middle-income settings, reflecting persistent gaps in infrastructure, workforce, and financing [4, 24].

Importantly, even interventions classified as low technology within guideline frameworks are not inherently context neutral. Their effective implementation depends on reliable nursing capacity, continuous monitoring, and basic supportive infrastructure, which remain uneven across many LMIC health systems. Core components of early supportive care, including dysphagia screening, protocolized monitoring, and stroke-unit-based care, require coordinated workflows and trained personnel rather than technology alone, and in many settings these system prerequisites represent the primary implementation bottleneck. Environmental and infrastructural constraints may further modify feasibility. In regions with high ambient temperatures, maintaining normothermia can be intrinsically challenging, while inconsistent oxygen supply, limited blood pressure monitoring, and variable access to glucose control infrastructure may compromise adherence to recommended targets. As a result, even guideline components considered low resource can become operationally complex in under-resourced environments. Nevertheless, because they are less dependent on capital-intensive infrastructure, supportive care interventions remain among the most scalable and high-impact targets for health system strengthening, particularly when embedded within standardized protocols and workforce training strategies.

Capacity Alignment as the Determinant of Guideline Translatability

A central implication of this analysis is that translatability is determined less by the clinical domain of a recommendation than by the structural capacity assumptions embedded within it. Evaluating guideline components across key dimensions, including infrastructure dependency, workforce intensity, workflow complexity, and financial requirements, reveals that feasibility gradients emerge both within and across domains. This recommendation-level perspective contrasts with service-tier frameworks that classify entire levels of care as minimal, essential, or advanced. Instead, our findings suggest that implementation gaps arise primarily from multidimensional capacity misalignment rather than categorical differences between “basic” and “advanced” care, consistent with guideline implementability frameworks emphasizing feasibility and resource constraints as central determinants of uptake [25–27]. This distinction shifts the focus from replicating high-income service models toward aligning individual guideline components with attainable system capacity.

Learning from System-Level Approaches in AMI Care

A notable observation from this analysis is that many feasible and adaptable stroke-care components align with system-level principles long established in AMI care [12]. Regionalized hub-and-spoke networks, protocol-driven workflows, prehospital activation, and time-based performance metrics have enabled scalable AMI care in diverse and resource-constrained environments [28, 29]. These same principles are implicitly embedded within contemporary stroke guidelines and were reflected in the feasibility gradients identified in this review [30, 31]. The relevance of AMI system for stroke care lies not in therapeutic equivalence but in organizational design. In both conditions, outcomes are highly time dependent, and benefits accrue disproportionately from system efficiency rather than from incremental technological complexity [32]. Our findings suggest that prioritizing regionalization, referral pathways, and protocolized workflows may yield larger population-level gains than attempting to replicate comprehensive tertiary services across all settings.

India as an Illustrative LMIC Case

The Indian experience illustrates how evidence-based stroke care is translated under constrained system conditions. National guidance aligns with international recommendations but operationalizes care through resource-stratified pathways, including physician-led stroke units, hub-and-spoke referral networks, and telemedicine-supported decision-making [20, 33].

Empirical data highlight persistent structural limitations. Access to reperfusion therapies remains uneven, with advanced imaging and endovascular services concentrated in urban tertiary centers and limited population-level coverage within therapeutic time windows [2, 15, 18]. Workforce constraints and financial barriers further restrict access, particularly in public-sector and rural settings [17, 24]. These patterns reflect a consistent feasibility gradient: protocol-driven care, non-contrast imaging, and supportive management are increasingly implementable, whereas advanced imaging and thrombectomy remain regionally concentrated. Accordingly, stroke system development in India has emphasized regionalization and referral coordination rather than uniform decentralization of advanced services [17].

Equity Implications and Policy Adaptation

Guideline translatability is fundamentally an equity issue. In LMICs, access to evidence-based stroke care is structured by geography, health-sector segmentation, and socioeconomic status. Advanced capabilities are concentrated in urban tertiary and private institutions, limiting timely access to vascular imaging and reperfusion therapies for rural populations, public-sector patients, and lower-income groups [2, 6, 15, 17, 18]. Because many guideline-recommended interventions are highly time dependent and require early imaging-based triage, delays in access to appropriate facilities translate directly into reduced eligibility for thrombolysis and thrombectomy, reinforcing outcome disparities across population groups.

This has important implications for evaluating implementation of the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline [9]. Applying high-resource interventions as universal quality benchmarks without accounting for structural constraints risks misclassifying under-resourced systems as underperforming and may inadvertently widen disparities. An equitable approach therefore requires resource-stratified implementation linked to system capacity. Priority should be given to scalable, high-impact interventions, such as early recognition, rapid non-contrast imaging, protocolized thrombolysis, and organized ward-based care, while strengthening referral pathways for advanced diagnostics and thrombectomy [7, 11, 20]. To translate this principle into actionable implementation steps, we developed a practical prioritization framework that distinguishes interventions suitable for immediate scale-up from those requiring system redesign, phased regional investment, or selective pilot implementation (Table 2). Policy responses must therefore be explicitly redistributive rather than purely incremental. This includes public financing to reduce out-of-pocket barriers to imaging and reperfusion, regional stroke networks with defined transfer pathways and door-in-door-out targets, telemedicine-supported care for peripheral hospitals, targeted expansion of stroke infrastructure in underserved regions, and equity monitoring across sex, geography, socioeconomic status, and public-versus-private sector access. In this framework, guideline implementation becomes not only a technical challenge but a question of equitable access to time-critical care.

Emerging Tools and Pragmatic Innovation

While not yet central to guideline recommendations, emerging strategies may further modify feasibility. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends mobile stroke units, where available, for the transport and management of thrombolytic-eligible patients with suspected acute ischemic stroke to achieve faster treatment and improved outcomes (Section 2.5, COR 1, LOE A) [34, 35]. However, MSU models are inherently infrastructure intensive and require substantial capital investment, trained personnel, and coordinated emergency systems, limiting their scalability in many resource-constrained settings [35, 36]. Selective implementation examples demonstrate context-specific adaptation. In India, pilot MSU initiatives, such as the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR)-supported Mobile Stroke Care Unit in the North-East region, illustrate how targeted deployment in high-burden or geographically underserved areas may extend access without universal replication of high-income models [37].

The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline endorses the use of either tenecteplase or alteplase for intravenous thrombolysis in eligible patients within the 4.5-h time window [9]. From a global health and health-systems perspective, this recommendation has important implementation implications. In particular, the single-bolus administration of tenecteplase, compared with the infusion-based alteplase regimen, may reduce workflow complexity, shorten treatment preparation time, and lower staffing demands.

These differences are especially relevant in resource-constrained settings, where timely delivery of thrombolysis is frequently limited by delays in drug preparation, monitoring capacity, and availability of trained personnel rather than by lack of evidence-based therapy itself. In this context, tenecteplase may represent a more scalable thrombolytic strategy, particularly in systems where workflow efficiency and workforce constraints constitute the dominant barriers to reperfusion therapy. However, adoption remains influenced by local regulatory approval, cost considerations, and variability in national protocols, underscoring that the impact of pharmacological innovation depends on its alignment with health system capacity rather than efficacy alone [35]. Blood-based biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) have also shown promise for early differentiation between ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage, but limited availability, cost considerations, and lack of integration into current guidelines constrain their near-term applicability in most LMIC health systems [38, 39]. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline also expands recommendations to pediatric acute ischemic stroke, reflecting growing recognition of this population. From a global health perspective, implementation in resource-constrained settings is likely to be particularly challenging, given limited awareness, diagnostic delays, and restricted access to specialized pediatric stroke expertise and care pathways. Taken together, these innovations illustrate that technological advancement alone does not resolve implementation gaps; rather, their impact depends on structured alignment with health system capacity.

Implications for Policy and Implementation

Taken together, these findings support a resource-stratified approach to stroke system development that prioritizes high-yield, scalable interventions. To translate this approach into actionable implementation steps, we developed a practical prioritization framework that distinguishes interventions suitable for immediate scale-up from those requiring system redesign, phased regional investment, or selective pilot implementation (Table 2). Investment in emergency transport coordination, workforce training, protocol development, telemedicine-enabled networks, and basic stroke-unit principles is likely to generate substantial gains even in the absence of advanced infrastructure. Conversely, expansion of thrombectomy services and advanced imaging should be pursued through phased regionalization rather than universal decentralization. For policymakers, aligning stroke services with broader noncommunicable disease strategies, as observed in India, may facilitate sustainable implementation by leveraging existing platforms for prevention, acute care, and follow-up. For researchers, the results highlight the need for implementation studies that evaluate not only clinical effectiveness but also system performance, equity, and cost-effectiveness across diverse settings.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, feasibility assessments were based on published literature and reported system characteristics rather than direct measurement of service availability or performance. Second, India was used as an illustrative case and may not fully represent the diversity of LMIC health systems. Third, the analysis focused on acute ischemic stroke and did not address primary prevention, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or long-term rehabilitation in detail. Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across domains and alignment with prior LMIC-focused work support the validity of the conclusions.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that contemporary evidence-based stroke guidelines contain numerous components that are directly implementable or adaptable in resource-constrained health systems, alongside others that remain constrained by infrastructure and workforce requirements. Bridging the gap between evidence and outcomes will require moving beyond guideline synthesis toward structured, system-level translation. Lessons from AMI care, combined with resource-stratified frameworks and pragmatic national adaptations such as those observed in India, provide a viable pathway toward more equitable global stroke care.
