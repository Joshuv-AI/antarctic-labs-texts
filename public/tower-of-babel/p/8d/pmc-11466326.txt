An Unusual Case of Metronidazole-induced Encephalopathy in a Patient with Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia
Authors: Tina H. Dao, Kinza Khan, Christopher D. Jackson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Metronidazole-induced encephalopathy (MIE) is a rare toxic encephalopathy. We describe a reversible MIE case in a patient with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), treated with metronidazole for brain abscess, who developed dizziness, weakness, dysarthria, and severe dysmetria. His Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) brain revealed bilateral, symmetric lesions in bilateral symmetrical regions of increased intensity in the medullary olives, cerebellar dentate nuclei, and the dorsal pons, all characteristic of MIE. Upon metronidazole discontinuation, the patient experienced significant symptom improvement, with subsequent MRI showing resolution of the lesions.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male with HHT presented with progressive balance issues, weakness, speech issues and dizziness following a two-month course of metronidazole 500 mg three times daily and ceftriaxone for a brain abscess. On exam, he had severe dysmetria with finger-to-nose and knee-to-shin testing bilaterally and wide-based ataxic gait. His brain MRI showed prominent bilateral T2 hyperintensities in the medullary olives (Fig. 1A), the cerebellar dentate nuclei (Fig. 1B), and the dorsal pons (Fig. 1B). His history, physical exam, and MRI findings were most consistent with a diagnosis of MIE. The medical team discontinued metronidazole and evaluated for other causes of encephalopathy. Workup for other causes of encephalopathy to include infection and metabolic derangements were negative. His symptoms improved with discontinuation of metronidazole, and his repeat MRI taken 2 weeks after hospital discharge showed resolution of the MRI changes (Fig. 1C). The discontinuation of metronidazole that resulted in near complete or complete resolution of neurological symptoms corroborated the diagnosis of MIE.

Discussion

Metronidazole-induced encephalopathy is an uncommon neurological adverse effect that is diagnosed by clinical history and aforementioned characteristic MIE lesions on MRI.1 Although metronidazole-induced encephalopathy should be prioritized on the different diagnoses list when new neurological symptoms manifest upon the initiation of metronidazole, other differential diagnoses should also be considered, e.g. bilateral T2 hyperintensities of dentate nuclei are associated with enteroviral encephalomyelitis,2 methyl bromide toxicity;3 T2 hyperintensities of the splenium are associated with epilepsy resulting from overdose or withdrawal, demyelinating disorders, and acute infectious encephalitis;4 demyelinating and diffusion restriction with low apparent diffusion coefficient are associated with systemic lupus erythematosus, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, acute toxic encephalopathy (5-FU and methotrexate),4 and Wernicke’s encephalopathy,5 and demyelinating disorders like Canavan disease.6

This case offers unique insight into the potential interplay between HHT and MIE. In 85% of adult cases with metronidazole-induced encephalopathy, cumulative metronidazole doses ranged from 5 to 2000 g with an average of 125.7 g, lower quartile of 36 g, and upper quartile 110.8 g.7 This patient had a cumulative metronidazole dose of 90 g over two months. Metronidazole undergoes extensive hepatic oxidative metabolism.8 In this unique case, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasias could cause extensive intrahepatic vascular malformations and impaired hepatic metabolism that could decrease metronidazole metabolism, thereby resulting in the accumulation of metronidazole and the observed neurological symptoms in this patient with lower cumulative doses. Taken together, HHT might have contributed to the development of MIE in this patient. This challenges the conventional threshold for MIE development and highlights the importance of individual patient factors in drug-related neurotoxicity.
