The impact of American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status on the feasibility and safety of non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Sira Laohathai, Apichat Tantraworasin, Ratchaya Weerayutwattana, Imran Tahir, Jakraphan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NIVATS) avoids the risks of general anesthesia, one-lung ventilation and tracheal intubation, and has traditionally been reserved for low-risk patients [American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II]. Its feasibility and safety in patients with higher ASA physical status remain insufficiently defined. We compared perioperative outcomes between low- and high-ASA patients undergoing NIVATS.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients undergoing elective NIVATS for lung resection between September 2019 and December 2023. Patients were stratified into low-ASA (ASA I–II) and high-ASA (ASA III–IV) groups. Baseline characteristics and perioperative outcomes were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-squared test or Fisher exact test. Multivariable Poisson regression adjusted for sex and operation type was used to estimate the independent association of high-ASA status with key outcomes, expressed as the incidence rate ratio (IRR).ResultsA total of 663 patients were analyzed (low-ASA, n=517; high-ASA, n=146). High-ASA patients were older (median 66.9 vs. 42.1 years, P<0.001) and had a greater comorbidity burden. Conversion to intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) (2.1% vs. 1.4%, P=0.58), operative time (P=0.53) and overall perioperative complications (P=0.52) did not differ between groups. However, the high-ASA group had higher rates of reoperation (3.4% vs. 0.4%, P=0.007), longer hospital stay (P=0.003) and higher mortality (2.1% vs. 0.2%, P=0.03). After adjustment, high-ASA status independently predicted a prolonged hospital stay [IRR 2.18, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.02–2.34, P<0.001] and markedly higher mortality (IRR 12.21, 95% CI: 1.27–117.51, P=0.03), but not conversion to intubated VATS, ICU admission or postoperative complications.ConclusionsNIVATS is technically feasible and intraoperatively well tolerated in high-ASA patients, with conversion and immediate complication rates comparable to those of low-ASA patients. However, high-ASA status independently predicts prolonged hospitalization and a substantially increased risk of mortality. These findings underscore the importance of careful patient selection and intensive, tailored postoperative care for high-risk patients undergoing NIVATS.Keywords: Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NIVATS), American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA physical status), thoracic surgery, postoperative complications, surgical risk

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this retrospective cohort of 663 patients undergoing non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NIVATS), intraoperative safety—conversion to intubated surgery, operative time, and immediate complications—was comparable between low-risk [American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II] and high-risk (ASA III–IV) patients. However, high-ASA status independently predicted a longer hospital stay and a markedly higher risk of postoperative mortality (more than 12-fold higher than low-ASA patients).

What is known and what is new?

• NIVATS has traditionally been reserved for low-risk (ASA I–II) patients, and a higher comorbidity burden is generally associated with worse surgical outcomes.

• This study provides one of the largest single-center cohorts directly comparing NIVATS outcomes across the full ASA spectrum, showing that the excess risk associated with high-ASA status is concentrated in the postoperative period rather than intraoperatively.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• NIVATS can be intraoperatively well tolerated even in higher-risk patients, but postoperative surveillance and resource allocation should be intensified for high-ASA patients, and clinicians should counsel these patients about their substantially elevated risk of mortality when planning perioperative care.

Introduction

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has emerged as a cornerstone of modern thoracic surgery, offering a minimally invasive alternative to traditional thoracotomy (1,2). This approach, which involves performing procedures through small incisions, has demonstrated significant advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and a quicker return to normal activities (3,4). Traditionally, VATS has been performed under general anesthesia with one-lung ventilation (OLV) facilitated by a double-lumen endotracheal tube (2,5). This method, while effective for isolating the surgical lung, carries its own set of potential risks and complications, particularly in vulnerable patient populations (6).

In recent years, the paradigm of thoracic anesthesia has shifted with the advent of non-intubated VATS (NIVATS) (7,8). This innovative technique, which utilizes regional anesthesia and sedation while the patient breathes spontaneously, has shown promising outcomes (9). The benefits of avoiding general anesthesia and tracheal intubation are particularly significant for patients with compromised cardiopulmonary function (10). The conventional intubated approach can lead to various complications, such as intubation-related airway trauma, ventilation-induced lung injury, and residual neuromuscular blockade, which can be detrimental to elderly patients with pre-existing comorbidities (10,11).

Traditionally, NIVATS has been reserved for a select group of low-risk patients, typically those with a low American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status score (ASA I–II), who are considered to have minimal comorbidities (12). However, as surgical expertise and anesthetic techniques have advanced, there is a growing interest in extending the application of NIVATS to a broader patient population, including those with higher ASA scores (ASA III–IV) (13,14). This expansion raises crucial questions regarding the safety, feasibility, and comparative outcomes of NIVATS in high-risk patients. While the physiological and anesthetic challenges associated with a higher comorbidity burden are well documented in other surgical contexts, a direct comparison of perioperative outcomes and safety profiles in low vs. high ASA score patients undergoing NIVATS remains limited. The aim of this study was to systematically compare the clinical outcomes—including intraoperative complications, conversion rates to intubated VATS, length of hospital stay, and postoperative morbidity—between low and high ASA physical status patients undergoing NIVATS. To our knowledge, this is among the largest single-center cohorts to directly compare perioperative outcomes between ASA I–II and ASA III–IV patients undergoing NIVATS for lung resection (n=663). Whereas most previous NIVATS series have either excluded higher-risk patients or reported them only as small subgroups, our consecutive, real-world cohort permitted a direct and adequately powered comparison across the ASA spectrum. The incremental contribution of this study is therefore to identify which specific outcomes, and at which stage of care (intraoperative versus postoperative), higher ASA status begins to influence the safety of NIVATS, thereby informing patient selection and postoperative resource allocation in high-risk populations. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1446/rc).

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Vajira Hospital, Bangkok, from September 2019 to December 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Vajira Hospital (Approval No. 079/68), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Consecutive patients undergoing elective NIVATS for lung resection were included.

Patient selection and preoperative evaluation

All patients scheduled for wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy were considered. Eligibility required a predicted postoperative FEV1 >40%, preserved spontaneous breathing capacity, and absence of severe hypoxemia or respiratory failure. Patients with ASA V status, hemodynamic instability, or contraindications to regional anesthesia were excluded. Preoperative chest CT was performed to confirm lesion location, size, and resectability. Case eligibility and anesthesia planning were determined by a multidisciplinary team (thoracic surgeon, anesthesiologist, pulmonologist). Our exclusion criteria included difficult airway, tumor size >5 cm, clinical N2 disease, history of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy in the neck or chest, morbid obesity [body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m2], severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension, contralateral phrenic nerve palsy, predicted extensive pleural adhesion or previous ipsilateral thoracic surgery (15). During the study period, NIVATS was the default anesthetic approach for all patients who met these eligibility criteria, irrespective of ASA class; intubated VATS under general anesthesia with one-lung ventilation remained continuously available and was used when eligibility criteria were not met or when intraoperative conversion was required. No rigid age or comorbidity threshold was applied to high-ASA patients. Instead, suitability rested on the same eligibility criteria above, with the final decision reached by multidisciplinary consensus. All procedures were performed by a small, fixed team of 2 thoracic surgeons (both of them are familiar to NIVATS technique and have done more than 100 cases in their earlier experience) and 3 anesthesiologists experienced in NIVATS, which minimized operator-related variability but, as a single-center experience, may also have contributed to selection bias (see “Limitations” section).

Anesthesia and analgesia protocol

Procedures were performed under regional anesthesia with sedation, without muscle relaxants or endotracheal intubation.

The sedation and regional anesthesia regimens described above were applied as a standardized institutional protocol, with predefined agents and weight-based dosing ranges titrated to the RASS target above rather than to fixed doses. Local infiltration and the intercostal and vagus nerve blocks were performed under direct thoracoscopic vision using 2% xylocaine with adrenaline (1:200,000) 10 mL together with 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine into 2nd to 8th intercostal nerve and vagus nerve approximately 1–2 mL for each intercostal nerve and 3–4 mL at vagus nerve; the adequacy of regional blockade was judged intraoperatively by the absence of cough or diaphragmatic irritation during pleural and hilar manipulation and by maintained hemodynamic and respiratory stability. The protocol was delivered by the same small group of thoracic anesthesiologists throughout the 2019–2023 period and did not undergo substantive revision since the initiation of our NIVATS program in 2017; identical sedation and analgesia protocols were used for low- and high-ASA patients, with more conservative titration within the same ranges in higher-ASA patients according to hemodynamic tolerance. Thoracic epidural analgesia was not used routinely: intercostal and vagal blockade combined with local infiltration and multimodal systemic analgesia was our preferred to provide adequate analgesia while avoiding the sympathectomy-related hypotension, urinary retention and procedural risks of neuraxial techniques in this frequently elderly and comorbid population, consistent with prevailing NIVATS practice.

Patient positioning and surgical setup

Patients were placed in the full lateral decubitus position. The ipsilateral arm was elevated on an armrest. The surgical team was positioned anteriorly, the scrub nurse posteriorly, and the anesthesiologist at the head. A uniportal utility incision (3–4 cm) was made at the 4th–6th intercostal space, depending on lesion location, using an Alexis wound protector.

Surgical technique

After placement of the Alexis wound protector, a surgical pneumothorax was created to achieve adequate lung collapse. The patient’s spontaneous breathing and resulting lung movement were synchronized with the surgical maneuvers. This was followed by exploration and resection, including:

❖ Wedge resection: nodules identified by palpation or CT-guided marking and resected with endoscopic staplers.

Protocol for conversion to intubated VATS

Immediate conversion to intubated VATS was undertaken if there was (I) uncontrolled bleeding, (II) persistent cough or agitation, (III) refractory hypoxemia or hypercapnia, or (IV) inability to complete the procedure non-intubated. Conversion was performed with lateral intubation without repositioning when feasible. These conversion criteria were predefined and were applied uniformly across all cases, irrespective of ASA classification.

Postoperative management

Hemostasis was secured, an underwater air-leak test was performed, and a single 24–28 Fr chest tube was placed via the utility incision. Digital chest drainage was used. Chest tube removal criteria included output <200 mL/day and absence of air leak. Postoperative analgesia included multimodal therapy with NSAIDs with or without opioids. Patients were mobilized within 24 hours. Complications were recorded and graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. A standardized enhanced-recovery pathway—comprising multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia, early chest-tube removal using the criteria above, and mobilization within 24 hours—was applied uniformly to both ASA groups and was not modified according to ASA classification. Routine postoperative admission to a monitored or intensive care unit bed followed the same institutional protocol for all NIVATS patients regardless of ASA class; the observed differences in length of stay and ICU utilization therefore reflected the clinical course rather than a predefined ASA-based admission policy.

Data collection

Clinical, anesthetic, intraoperative, and postoperative data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and operative notes. Consecutive patients undergoing NIVATS during the study period were included. All data were independently cross-checked by two reviewers to minimize errors, and missing or ambiguous entries were verified against source charts.

Data points

We collected data on demographics and baseline characteristics (including age, sex, BMI, smoking status, comorbidities, ASA physical status, preoperative pulmonary function, and tumor size and location). We also extracted intraoperative data on the type of lung resection (wedge, segmentectomy, lobectomy), duration of surgery, estimated blood loss, oxygen saturation, and conversion to intubated VATS, as well as data on postoperative outcomes including hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, ICU LOS, chest tube duration, postoperative complications (classified by Clavien-Dindo), 30-day readmission, reoperation, and 30-day mortality. Complications of interest included pulmonary (atelectasis, pneumonia, respiratory failure), cardiovascular (arrhythmia, myocardial infarction), surgical (prolonged air leak, bleeding, wound infection), and thromboembolic events.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were perioperative mortality, conversion to intubated VATS, and postoperative complications. Secondary outcomes included chest tube duration, hospital length of stay, ICU admission, and ICU length of stay.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous, normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared with the Student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and the two groups (low ASA vs. high ASA) were compared using the Chi-squared test or the Fisher exact test.

Multivariable Poisson regression was conducted to evaluate the independent association of high ASA score with the clinical outcomes of NIVATS (including mortality, conversion to intubated VATS, hospital length of stay, ICU admission, and postoperative complications) after adjusting for known confounders. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The set of adjustment variables was deliberately parsimonious. Because the number of events for the key binary outcomes was small (for example, four deaths in total), the multivariable models were restricted to two pre-specified covariates—sex and operation type—to preserve an acceptable events-per-variable ratio and to avoid model overfitting, in accordance with established recommendations for regression modeling with sparse outcomes (16). Age was not entered as a separate covariate because it is strongly collinear with ASA class (high-ASA patients being substantially older), so that adjusting for age would have over-adjusted for the exposure of interest.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Patients in the high ASA group (n=146) were significantly older than those in the low ASA group (n=517) (median age 66.9 vs. 42.1 years, P<0.001). The distribution of sex was similar between groups (P=0.25). A significantly greater proportion of high ASA patients were smokers (28.1% vs. 13.9%, P<0.001). Comorbidities were more prevalent in the high ASA cohort, including dyslipidemia (30.8% vs. 11.4%, P<0.001), diabetes (24.7% vs. 7.4%, P<0.001), hypertension (39.0% vs. 16.8%, P<0.001), cardiovascular disease (6.2% vs. 1.7%, P=0.007), cerebrovascular disease (4.1% vs. 0.2%, P=0.001), and COPD (17.1% vs. 5.8%, P<0.001). No difference was observed in CKD (P>0.99) or BMI (P=0.46). The distribution of individual ASA classes was as follows: in the low-ASA group, 128 patients were ASA I and 389 were ASA II; in the high-ASA group, 132 were ASA III and 14 were ASA IV.

The type of surgical procedure (lobectomy, wedge, or segmentectomy) did not differ significantly between groups (P=0.68). Similarly, laterality (left vs. right side, P=0.14) and prior lung surgery (P=0.43) did not differ. However, a significantly greater proportion of high ASA patients underwent surgery for malignancy rather than benign disease (63.7% vs. 47.6%, P=0.001) (Table 1). The “Other” resection category (143 (27.7%) in the low-ASA group and 43 (29.5%) in the high-ASA group) comprised minor non-anatomical thoracoscopic procedures (e.g., pleural or lung biopsy, bullae or bleb resection, and excision of benign lesions); importantly, no pneumonectomy was performed in either group.

Peri-operative outcomes

The rates of conversion to intubated VATS were low and comparable between groups (2.1% vs. 1.4%, P=0.58). Overall perioperative complication rates did not differ significantly (P=0.52), and no differences were seen in operative time (median 40 minutes in both groups, P=0.53), induction time (P=0.97), blood transfusion (P>0.99), or estimated blood loss (P=0.26). Postoperative ventilator requirement (P>0.99) and immediate extubation rates (P>0.99) were also similar. The variable labeled “no blood transfusion” in Table 2 denotes the proportion of patients who did not require allogeneic blood transfusion [513 (99.23%) low-ASA and 145 (99.32%) high-ASA]; transfusion was therefore needed in only 4 (0.8%) low-ASA and 1 (0.7%) high-ASA patients, consistent with the minimal estimated blood loss (median 10 mL in both groups) and with the minimally invasive nature of NIVATS. Induction time was documented in the anesthetic record in rounded increments, which accounts for the narrow interquartile range; these values, together with the operative times, were re-verified against source records and confirmed to be accurate.

However, reoperation was significantly more common in the high ASA group (3.4% vs. 0.4%, P=0.007). Multivariable analyses

After adjustment for sex and operation type, high ASA status was not associated with a significantly increased risk of conversion to intubated VATS (IRR 1.57, 95% CI: 0.41–6.08, P=0.51) or ICU admission (IRR 1.62, 95% CI: 0.77–3.42, P=0.20). Likewise, the composite measure of postoperative complications did not differ significantly (IRR 1.16, 95% CI: 0.49–2.74, P=0.72).

In contrast, high ASA status independently predicted a prolonged hospital stay (IRR 2.18, 95% CI: 2.02–2.34, P<0.001). Most notably, the mortality risk was markedly higher in the high ASA group, with a more than 12-fold increase compared with low ASA patients (IRR 12.21, 95% CI: 1.27–117.51, P=0.03) (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide a comprehensive analysis of the perioperative outcomes of NIVATS in patients undergoing lung resection, stratified by the ASA physical status classification. Our primary objective was to determine whether a higher ASA score independently predicts adverse outcomes, thereby informing the risk-benefit assessment for using NIVATS in higher-risk patients. The results offer significant insights into the feasibility and safety profile of NIVATS, particularly when applied to patients with a greater burden of systemic disease.

As demonstrated in our demographic analysis (Table 1), the high ASA group (n=146) represented a significantly older patient population (P<0.001) with a substantially higher prevalence of comorbidities compared with the low ASA group (n=517). Specifically, the high ASA group had a greater proportion of smokers (P<0.001), hypertension (P<0.001), diabetes (P<0.001), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (P<0.001), and cardiovascular disease (P=0.007). This establishes a clear and significant baseline difference between the two cohorts, confirming that the high ASA group comprises patients who are clinically sicker and more physiologically challenged.

A key finding of our study is that, despite these significant baseline differences, several immediate perioperative outcomes were remarkably similar between the two groups. As shown in Table 2, there was no statistically significant difference in the rate of conversion to intubated VATS (P=0.58), operative time (P=0.53), or estimated blood loss (P=0.26). Furthermore, the overall perioperative complication rate did not differ significantly between the groups (P=0.52), a notable finding given the higher comorbidity burden in the high ASA group. This suggests that the NIVATS approach itself, in terms of surgical execution and immediate intraoperative safety, is feasible and equally well tolerated by both patient populations. Our results support the growing body of literature advocating for non-intubated thoracoscopic techniques as a viable alternative for selected high-risk patients, as this approach avoids the potential complications associated with single-lung intubation and general anesthesia.

Our findings should also be viewed alongside contemporary evidence that minimally invasive thoracic surgery can be performed safely in patients with significant cardiopulmonary compromise. Recent propensity-matched data confirm that non-intubated anesthesia for VATS in early-stage lung cancer yields perioperative outcomes at least comparable to intubated anesthesia, with reduced opioid use, fewer postoperative complications and shorter hospital stays (17); moreover, even technically demanding reconstructive thoracoscopic procedures have been performed safely in patients with markedly impaired pulmonary function (18). Our data extend this literature by demonstrating that the intraoperative safety of NIVATS—in terms of conversion, operative time and immediate complications—is preserved in ASA III-IV patients, while showing that the principal excess risk in this population is concentrated in the postoperative period. This distinction suggests that, for high-risk patients, the limiting factor is not intraoperative tolerance of the non-intubated technique itself but postoperative physiological reserve, supporting a strategy of liberal intraoperative application coupled with intensified postoperative surveillance.

However, a more nuanced picture emerges when other critical outcomes are examined. While immediate complications were comparable, the high ASA group experienced a significantly higher rate of reoperation (P=0.007). This finding, along with the significantly longer hospital stay in the high ASA group (P=0.003), suggests that, while the initial surgical period is tolerated similarly, the postoperative recovery and complication profile are markedly different. These findings are further corroborated and strengthened by our multivariable analysis (Table 3). After adjusting for confounding factors such as sex and operation type, we found that high ASA status was an independent and highly significant predictor of both a longer hospital stay (IRR 2.177, 95% CI: 2.02–2.34, P<0.001) and, most critically, a higher mortality rate (IRR 12.210, 95% CI: 1.27–117.51, P=0.03).

The twelve-fold increased risk of mortality for patients in the high ASA group is a particularly striking result and represents the most important clinical implication of our study. This finding underscores the fact that, while NIVATS can be successfully performed in sicker patients, their underlying systemic physiological fragility places them at a significantly elevated risk for severe adverse outcomes. The higher risk of reoperation in this group may be a contributing factor to the increased mortality and longer hospital stays, highlighting the need for heightened vigilance and intensive postoperative management in these patients.

Our study’s strengths include the use of a multivariable analysis, which allowed us to isolate the independent effect of high ASA status on outcomes. By controlling for variables such as sex and operation type, we provide a more robust and clinically meaningful assessment of risk. However, this study is not without limitations. The single-center nature of the data may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, although we controlled for key confounders, other unmeasured variables—such as the specific severity of disease within each comorbidity—could potentially influence the results. Furthermore, because NIVATS was the default institutional approach during the study period and case selection was guided by multidisciplinary clinical judgment rather than by rigid physiological thresholds, residual selection bias cannot be excluded. The small number of adverse events also limited the number of covariates that could be modeled and produced a wide confidence interval for the mortality estimate, which should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that, while NIVATS is a safe and technically feasible procedure for patients with high ASA scores, their postoperative course is markedly different from that of healthier patients. A high ASA status independently and significantly predicts a longer hospital stay, a higher rate of reoperation, and a dramatically increased risk of mortality. Therefore, while NIVATS can be offered to high-risk patients, this decision must be made with a clear understanding that their systemic health status, as reflected by the ASA score, remains a powerful determinant of their postoperative trajectory. These findings reinforce the importance of meticulous patient selection and underscore the need for comprehensive and tailored postoperative care plans for high-risk patients undergoing NIVATS.
