Development and validation of the mental internal friction scale (MIFS) for chinese college students
Authors: Youming Song, Zhicheng Chen, Yaning Zhang, Bing Li, Jingyi Chen, Yin Wang, Yuxin Li, Gang Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimMental internal friction refers to a state of mental burnout and lack of motivation caused by excessive cognitive depletion under strong pressure. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent among contemporary college students. However, there is currently no suitable quantitative tool available to effectively assess mental internal friction. Therefore, the present study aims to develop a mental internal friction scale (MIFS) and verify its reliability and validity within the Chinese college student population.MethodFirst, ten college students participated in semi-structured interviews. Then, 612 college students were recruited for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis. Subsequently, a new sample of 524 college students was recruited for confirmatory factor analysis, as well as reliability and validity evaluation. Finally, 161 college students were randomly selected from previous two samples to track the measurement and verify its retest reliability.ResultsThe developed scale comprised 21 items assessing four factors: avoidance, rumination, sensitivity, and indecision. The four-factor structure fit well (χ2/df = 2.527, CFI = 0.946, GFI = 0.920, IFI = 0.946, RMSEA = 0.054), explaining 60.967% of the total variance. All reliability indices—Cronbach’s α, split-half, composite, and retest reliabilities—exceeded the 0.70 criterion for both the overall MIFS and each of its four subscales. Furthermore, the scale demonstrated good content validity, criterion validity, convergent validity, and discriminative validity, and passed the cross-gender invariance test.ConclusionThe MIFS demonstrates solid reliability and validity among college students. It can serve as a quantitative measure of mental internal friction in Chinese college students.

Introduction

In July 2022, a video titled “How Eriju (the Second-Oldest Maternal Uncle) Cured My Mental Friction after Being Back in the Village for Three Days” (“Eriju”) went viral after being uploaded to the popular Chinese video-sharing platform Bilibili [1], popularizing the term “mental internal friction” and sparking widespread resonance, inspiring heated online discussions. In the video, the term “mental internal friction” was used to describe the narrator’s mental burnout. However, there is still no general consensus regarding its conceptual definition. Some researchers define it as a state in which an individual exhausts their psychological resources of self-control [2, 3]. Others use it to describe a state of mental burnout caused by excessive consumption of cognitive resources [4, 5], or a negative emotional state caused by ambivalence between doing and not doing, thinking and not thinking [6]. Still, other scholars believe that mental internal friction is a manifestation of internal struggle as an individual exercises self-control [7]. Clearly, mental internal friction is a complicated psychological state. To simplify the current discussion, we define mental internal friction as a state of mental burnout and lack of motivation caused by excessive cognitive depletion under strong pressure, often manifesting as emotional sensitivity, negativity, psychological indecision, and struggle.

The rise of mental internal friction on network platforms is not an accidental phenomenon but an inevitability of the times [8]. In contemporary meritocratic societies, performance has become the main driving force for people’s value output and creation [9]. However, the excessive pursuit of performance can lead to two significant issues. On one hand, it can result in the disappearance of the diversity of human actions, becoming more of a labor with low emotional value that sustains biological life. On the other hand, meritocratic societies transform social relationships into more competitive ones, increasing the instability of people’s living state [10]. Then, when facing the high load of life and work pressure, people have to weigh their psychological needs against their material needs repeatedly. When competitive relationships develop into irrational competition, people also have to continually assess the balance between their efforts and rewards. In short, mental internal friction is the inevitable result of people’s active choice of “self-exploitation” driven by social performance, which is consistent with the view of excessive self-control of mental internal friction [2, 7]. Specifically, in meritocratic societies, “efficiency” and “usefulness” have become the primary, if not the only, standards for many young people to measure everything [9]. This compels them to constantly self-regulate in daily life to maximize “instrumental value”, which leads to excessive self-control and then falls into mental internal friction over time.

The fermentation of the mental internal friction among the youth group is the result of combined effects of their psychological development characteristics and realistic situations. At present, mental internal friction has been considered as a unique symptom [9] and general psychological state [11] of young people, as they face social dilemmas and pressures. Especially for college students, on the one hand, they are in a stage of transitioning from immaturity to maturity and are prone to contradictory psychology such as self-confrontation, self-doubt, and self-conflict [11]. On the other hand, contemporary college students feel their lives are exceptionally “heavy”, bearing the expectations from society, family and themselves, as well as psychological pressures from various aspects such as economy, study, employment and interpersonal communication [12]. Moreover, the development of today’s network media technology has expanded the boundaries of their learning infinitely in time and space, with their learning content shrouded by vast knowledge and information. Consequently, their competitive learning environment has extended from offline to online [11], oscillating between rationality and irrationality. This makes them psychologically face the constant struggle between becoming the “king of involution (actively participating in or producing involution)” and choosing to “lying flat (acting self-indulgent, lazy, and rejecting societal expectations, similar to goblin mode)”, thus falling into continuous mental internal friction. In addition, contemporary college students, as the main force of online socializing [12], their mental internal friction is further exacerbated by the potential effects of online media in “selling anxiety” and “social comparison”. It is evident that the fermentation of mental internal friction among college students has intrinsic causes, while it greatly influenced by the realities.

The psychological and realistic basis of the fermentation of mental internal friction among college students also determines that the manifestation of mental internal friction is pluralistic. In fact, due to the characteristics of college students psychological development and the current social information environment, they constantly confront a series of decision-making and cognitive conflicts. These include wandering between “self-supremacy” and “identity anxiety” in self-awareness [11], choosing between “independence” and “conformity” in psychology [13], balancing “involution” and “lying flat” in academics [11, 13], coordinating between “material pursuit” and “spiritual pursuit” in life [13], and reconciling “social phobia” with “social butterfly” in social interaction [11, 13]. These manifestations coexist and are difficult to eliminate in the short term among college students, and even directly define the abnormal survival logic and culture of college students, seriously affecting the sustainability of the normal psychological development of college students.

These manifestations of mental internal friction among college students impede the timely release of their cognitive resources, the restoration of their self-control, and the resolution of their psychological confusion. Over time, these issues contribute to psychological burnout and insufficient spiritual dynamics, thereby leading to a series of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral problems. On the macro level, the long-term mental internal friction can inhibit the play of their subjectivity and hinder the formation of their self-identity, which will gradually slide them into value nihilism and promote their bad social mentality [14]. On the micro level, long-term mental internal friction will not only further develop into mental diseases such as depression and anxiety [5, 6], but also seriously affect their academic performance and interpersonal relationships [7].

In particular, the series of negative consequences resulting from mental internal friction may be further intensified within the context of Chinese collectivist culture. First, at the level of cognitive and behavioral strategies, collectivist culture tends to encourage emotion-regulation strategies such as expressive suppression [15–17] and stress-coping mechanisms like escape-avoidance [18], which may intensify negative emotions and self-perceptions, ultimately leading to more avoidance choices. Second, in terms of thinking patterns, the emphasis on self-reflection-centered self-cultivation in Chinese Confucian culture [19] contributes to a more pronounced tendency toward rumination among individuals in Eastern collectivist cultures [20, 21], potentially leading them to dwell excessively on past negative experiences and emotions. Third, regarding social cognition, the collectivist emphasis on interpersonal relationships [22, 23] may lead Chinese individuals to be more attentive to how their words and actions influence others, thereby prompting greater caution in their speech and behavior and consequently increasing their sensitivity. Fourth, from a value orientation perspective, the prioritization of collective interests in collectivist culture [24, 25] may introduce additional layers of complexity into personal decision-making, as individuals weigh social expectations, familial obligations, and potential consequences for others. This multifaceted deliberation can reduce decision-making efficiency and result in hesitation. According to the relevant descriptions in previous studies[9, 11, 12], the manifestations of mental internal friction are linked to varying degrees to the aforementioned cultural influences. Therefore, the impact of collectivist culture on mental internal friction appears to be both extensive and deep-seated. It is thus evident that understanding this phenomenon among Chinese college students holds significant theoretical and practical importance.

The existing research on mental internal friction has primarily focused on phenomenological descriptions and theoretical discussions. In addition to the diverse conceptual definitions of mental internal friction previously mentioned, a number of related constructs have emerged both online and within academic discourse, such as “goblin mode”, “lying flat”, “tanking”, “buddha-like” and “self-indulgence”. Specifically, “goblin mode” refers to an instinctive lifestyle in which individuals disregard traditional social roles and norms [26, 27]. “Lying flat” reflects a state of helplessness and resignation, which emerges after sustained efforts yield little or no result [28]. “Tanking” describes the coping strategy employed by individuals in disadvantaged positions, characterized by deliberate self-withdrawal following repeated setbacks [29]. “Buddha-like” represents an indifferent and detached life attitude, characterized by acceptance and a passive approach to circumstances [30]. “Self-indulgence” reflects a manifestation of failure in self-control, typically characterized by excessive pursuit of personal desires, pleasure, or satisfaction without regard for consequences, responsibilities, or others’ needs [31]. The distinctions between these constructs and mental internal friction mainly lie in their conceptual connotations and generation mechanisms. In terms of connotation, “goblin mode”, “lying flat” and “tanking” share commonalities in that they all represent negative behavioral patterns or coping strategies in response to reality [32]; “buddha-like” emphasizes a passive and indifferent attitude toward life; and “self-indulgence” highlights irrational manifestation resulting from impaired self-regulation. Mental internal friction, however, is distinct in that it reflects a state of psychological fatigue and lack of motivation caused by excessive cognitive depletion under intense pressure. From the perspective of generation mechanisms, “goblin mode”, “lying flat”, “tanking” and “buddha-like” all stem from a negative interpretation or deconstruction of reality; “self-indulgence” arises from deficiencies in self-control and self-monitoring; whereas mental internal friction originates from excessive self-regulation and unrealistically high self-expectations.

Previous studies have described individuals experiencing mental internal friction cognitively or emotionally as exhibiting four main characteristics: avoidance, rumination, sensitivity, and indecision. Avoidance refers to passive retreat or active withdrawal due to an excessive fear of failure and negative evaluation by others. For example, individuals in a state of mental internal friction may often comfort themselves through the lowering of their desires and passive withdrawal [9, 14], in that they become less interested in most things and often feel exhausted even without having taken part in any mental or physical activities [2]. Psychologically and behaviorally, they seem to have enter a “goblin mode”, acting self-indulgent, lazy, and rejecting societal expectations. Rumination refers to thinking repeatedly about past negative events one has experienced, and finding it difficult to avoid circular thought patterns. For example, in the comment section of the “Eriju” video, many netizens in a mental internal friction state wrote about repeatedly thinking negatively about hardships and setbacks they had once experienced [33]. Surveys have shown that about 40% of the anxiety experienced by individuals in mental internal friction comes from worrying about the future, while roughly 30% comes from ruminating over past experiences [34]. Sensitivity refers to being overly concerned about the judgment of others, resulting in a fear of both actively rejecting others and of expressing oneself. For example, they may think repeatedly about comments made by others [2] and become extremely sensitive and worried about conflict with others [5, 9]. Finally, indecision refers to a state of hesitation and indecision, which stems from the pressure one feels to meet high standards set by themselves, others, and society. For example, they may often seem to be psychologically pulled between or colliding with different ideas and viewpoints, similar to two characters arguing [7, 9], leading the individual into spiraling thoughts as they weigh various possibilities and outcomes of their actions.

It is worth noting that the four dimensions of mental internal friction here—avoidance, rumination, sensitivity, and indecision—are different from the corresponding constructs involved in previous studies. In previous research, avoidance more often referred to a negative coping strategy for stress [18]; rumination was relatively complex, with both state and trait representations [35], but regardless of the representation method, the essence was a discrepancy-focused thinking style [36]; sensitivity typically appeared in the form of emotional sensitivity [37], interpersonal sensitivity [38], and sensory-processing sensitivity [39], and was regarded as a personality trait of being highly alert to external information and having a strong ability to perceive information and details [40]. Regarding indecision, it usually appeared in the form of career indecision [41], reflecting a state where an individual is unable to make quick decisions, and has been proven to be highly correlated with personality [42]. From the specific manifestations, although the four dimensions of mental internal friction share certain similarities with their corresponding constructs, the latter generally exhibit more defined directionalities and a higher degree of internalization. Accordingly, mental internal friction is a relatively comprehensive and stable subclinical psychological state.

The lack of targeted quantitative assessment tools has significantly impeded the progress of empirical research on mental internal friction. As far as we know, only Liu et al. have studied the relationship between academic involution and mental internal friction of college students from a quantitative perspective [7]. However, Liu et al. adopted the self-regulation fatigue scale [43] to measure mental internal friction, focusing more on explaining mental internal friction from the perspective of self-regulation. In actual fact, in addition to the performance of self-regulation fatigue and inefficiency, mental internal friction also comprises a series of specific externalized atypical manifestations involving cognition, emotion, will, attitude, and values, including rumination, sensitivity, self-denial, and misanthropy. Furthermore, in contrast to mental fatigue caused by self-regulation fatigue, mental internal friction is more focused on the phenomenon of systemic decline and reduction of mental motivation as exhibited by individuals under strong pressure. It is evident that the application of the self-regulation fatigue scale to assess mental internal friction is insufficiently comprehensive and lacks the necessary specificity. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a more precisely targeted quantitative measurement tool for mental internal friction to address the current challenges in related research.

To summarize, although the concept of mental internal friction has gained rapid public awareness and attention, the current academic focus on mental internal friction remains primarily on phenomenology, due to a lack of targeted quantitative research tools. This greatly limits research and theoretical development. Therefore, to provide a suitable measurement and evaluation tool for future research in this area, the current study aimed to construct a structural model of mental internal friction specific to Chinese college students, as well as to develop and validate the mental internal friction scale (MIFS) in line with psychometric standards.

Method

Participants

Using a convenience sampling method, 1,350 paper questionnaires were randomly distributed to students at some universities in northwest China, and 1,210 questionnaires were returned, resulting in a recovery rate of 89.63%. After eliminating 74 invalid questionnaires due to obvious repeated response patterns, 1,136 valid questionnaires were retained (average age = 19.946 years, SD = 1.497, age range 16 to 28 years), with an effective rate of 93.88%. SPSS 22.0 was used to randomly divide the valid questionnaires into two samples. Sample 1 (n = 612) was used for item analysis and content validity evaluation, while sample 2 (n = 524) was used for evaluating structural validity, criterion validity, composite reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity. According to Carpenter’s recommendation, the minimum sample size for factor analysis should be five to ten times the number of items in the measure [44]. The initial version of the MIFS contained 56 items, so the minimum necessary sample size was calculated to be 280, which is much smaller than the actual sample sizes used for exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in our study. Additionally, drawing upon the studies [45, 46] of Luo et al. and Ma et al., the sample size in our study is deemed adequate for conducting the additional statistical analyses required in the research. Therefore, the sample size in the current study met statistical requirements. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Yan’an University and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants reviewed the informed consent form and were provided with written acknowledgments upon completion of the scale.

Sample 1

Sample 1 comprised 612 college students with a mean age of 20.137 years (SD = 1.477, age range 16 to 28 years). This sample included 168 (27.45%) males and 444 (72.55%) females, with 134 (21.90%) freshmen, 288 (47.06%) sophomores, 133 (21.73%) juniors, and 57 (9.31%) seniors and above. In terms of residential background, 207 (33.82%) came from urban areas and 405 (66.18%) from rural areas. Additionally, 131 (21.41%) were only child and 481 (78.59%) were non-only children. Regarding majors, 117 (19.12%) students were in liberal arts, 130 (21.24%) in engineering, 27 (4.41%) in art, 165 (26.96%) in science, and 173 (28.27%) in medicine.

Sample 2

Sample 2 comprised 524 college students with a mean age of 19.723 years (SD = 1.490, age range 16 to 27 years). This sample included 133 (25.38%) males and 391 (74.62%) females, with 198 (37.79%) freshmen, 198 (37.79%) sophomores, 88 (16.79%) juniors, and 40 (7.63%) seniors and above. In terms of residential background, 152 (29.01%) came from urban areas, 370 (70.61%) came from rural areas, and 2 (0.38%) did not report their home region. Additionally, 107 (20.42%) were an only child and 417 (79.58%) were non-only children. Regarding majors, 137 (26.15%) students were in liberal arts, 86 (16.41%) in engineering, 20 (3.82%) in art, 169 (32.25%) in science, and 112 (21.37%) in medicine.

Retest sample

A total of 161 subjects (average age = 18.789 years, SD = 1.555, age range 16 to 28 years) were randomly selected from both Sample 1 and Sample 2 for the retest sample. The tracking measurement was conducted three months (Up to 8 July 2023) after the initial completion of the MIFS to evaluate the retest reliability of the scale. The retest sample comprised 36 (22.36%) males and 125 (77.64%) females, with 127 (78.88%) freshmen, 10 (6.21%) sophomores, 19 (11.80%) juniors, and 5 (3.11%) seniors and above. In terms of residential background, 46 (28.57%) were from urban areas and 115 (71.43%) were from rural areas. Additionally, 21 (13.04%) were an only child and 140 (86.96%) were non-only children. Regarding majors, 71 (44.10%) students were in liberal arts, 4 (2.48%) in engineering, 76 (47.20%) in science, and 10 (6.21%) in medicine.

Development of scale

Open-Ended interview

Initially, a semi-structured interview outline was designed to gain deeper insights into the concept of mental internal friction as defined in previous studies. The interview outline included four specific questions: (1) What do you think mental internal friction is? (2) What are the common, specific manifestations of mental internal friction, in terms of cognition, emotion, and behavior? (3) Have you ever experienced or are you currently experiencing mental internal friction? (4) Please tell us about the specific causes of mental internal friction, according to your own experiences.

Following the development of the interview outline, 10 college students from the psychology department of a university in Shaanxi Province, China, were invited to participate in one-to-one semi-structured interviews. The participants had an average age of 21.100 years (SD = 1.136, age range 20 to 24 years), with an equal distribution of five males and five females. Among them, three were juniors and seven were seniors, all majoring in applied psychology.

Item pool

Initially, the interview data was preliminarily sorted, and a Doctor of psychology was invited to conduct a comprehensive appraisal of the sorted data. Next, the data was classified into the four groups (avoidance, rumination, sensitivity, and indecision) by the researchers, and 58 items were developed based on these groups and data. Thirdly, the 58 items were again identified, and necessary grammatical corrections were made. Finally, a two-way list was established. One psychology teacher, two Masters students in mental health education, and five psychology undergraduates were invited to review and revise the items. They were asked to eliminate or modify items with unclear meanings, i.e., items for which group members had more than one interpretation, or ambiguity, i.e., cases where it was difficult to determine the specific sub-dimension to which an item belonged, or substantial semantic overlap with other items, and to ensure that each item clearly corresponded to a distinct subdimension of mental internal friction. In the end, 56 items were retained (14 from the avoidance dimension, 15 from the rumination dimension, 14 from the sensitivity dimension, and 13 from the indecision dimension), including 51 forward-scored items and five reverse-scored items. To improve item discrimination [47], a six-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 to 6 (i.e., 1 = absolutely inappropriate, 2 = inappropriate, 3 = slightly inappropriate, 4 = slightly appropriate, 5 = appropriate, 6 = absolutely appropriate) was adopted. Higher scores indicate a higher degree of mental internal friction reported by the respondent.

Criterion tool

According to Fan et al., Chen et al. and Wang et al., perfectionism is closely related to individual mental internal friction [9, 12, 48]. Therefore, we adopted the multidimensional perfectionism scale (MPS) as developed by Hewitt et al. [49] and revised by Dai [50] as the criterion for the MIFS. The MPS contains 29 items in two subscales: high standard and adaptability. The high standard subscale contains 15 items (e.g., “Once a goal is set, it must be achieved, not settled for second best.”), and the adaptability subscale contains 14 items (e.g., “I often feel like I’m a disappointment to those around me.”). Items are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); the higher the total score, the greater the maladjustment caused by the respondent’s pursuit of perfectionism. In this sample, the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the MPS, the high standard subscale, and the adaptability subscale were 0.784, 0.610, and 0.661, respectively.

Data analysis

Data analysis was mainly performed using SPSS 22.0, Amos 21.0 and Mplus 8.3 data analysis software.

First, missing values in all valid questionnaires were replaced using the serial mean value method in SPSS 22.0, and the questionnaires were randomly divided into two samples.

Second, item analysis and content validity analysis of the MIFS were carried out using the data of sample 1. To be specific, the extreme grouping method was used to divide respondents into high (the top 27%) and low (the lowest 27%) groups based on the total scores of all respondents to the initial MIFS. An independent sample T-test (the critical ratio value) was conducted to confirm the group differences of each item [51]. Items with significant differences were retained. Besides, the Pearson product-moment correlation was used to calculate the item-total correlation coefficients (I-TCC) of each item, retaining items with correlation coefficients r > 0.30 [52]. Additionally, the suitability of the data for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was determined by examining whether the Kaiser Meyer Olkin (KMO) value exceeded 0.70 and whether the Bartlett sphericity test was significant [53]. Considering that principal components analysis (PCA) retains maximal information from the original observed variables [54], and the basic hypothesis of oblique rotation aligns well with correlations between factors in social science research [55], it is highly recommended by many statistical experts [56]. Therefore, we chose PCA and Promax oblique rotation for factor extraction and rotation. To ensure the reliability and validity of each item and maintain the simplicity of the scale structure, we established the following criteria for item exclusion, as these criteria were derived from relevant literature and involved considering eigenvalues greater than 1 for the factors [57] and assessing the scree plot: (1) the factor loading value of the item was below 0.50 [58]; (2) the loading value of the item on two or more factors was above 0.40 [59]; (3) the difference in factor loadings between two factors was no more than 0.25 [60]; (4) the commonality of the item was below 0.50 [61]; (5) a single factor contained fewer than four items [62]; and (6) the measure of sampling adequacy (i.e., MSA value) of the item was below 0.40. The EFA was repeated after each exclusion until the indicators of all retained items met the above exclusion criteria. Finally, to evaluate the content validity of the official MIFS, SPSS 22.0 was used to analyze the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients between the total score and its four subscales.

Third, the structural validity, criterion validity, composite reliability, convergent validity, and the discriminant validity of the official MIFS were accessed using the data of sample 2. Specifically, Amos 21.0 was used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to verify the structural validity of the official MIFS. Based on previous research [63], the selected model fitting indexes included the minimum discrepancy per degree of freedom (CMIN/DF), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). The criteria for judgment were CMIN/DF < 3, GFI, IFI, and CFI > 0.90, and RMSEA < 0.08 [64]. To further confirm whether the structure of the official MIFS is consistent with theoretical hypotheses, several potential factor combination models were constructed based on the previous study [46]. The nested model comparison standard proposed by Cheung and Rensvold [65] was used to determine whether the theoretical hypothesis structure can win in model comparison, that is, the CFI should increase by at least 0.010, or the RMSEA should decrease by at least 0.015. Using the Multidimensional perception scale (MPS) developed by Hewitt et al. [49] and revised by Dai [50] as the criterion, the criterion validity of the official MIFS was evaluated by analyzing the significance of the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients between the scores of the official MIFS and the MPS. The composite reliability, average variance extracted (AVE) value, and the square root value of AVE of the official MIFS and its subscales were calculated using the factor loadings of each item in CFA. The Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients between the total score of the official MIFS and its subscales were calculated using SPSS 22.0. The composite reliability of the official MIFS was evaluated by assessing whether the composite reliability values of the official MIFS and its subscales were above 0.70 [66]; the convergent validity of the official MIFS was evaluated by assessing whether the AVE values of the subscales of the official MIFS were above 0.50 [67]; and the discriminant validity of the official MIFS was evaluated by assessing whether the Pearson product–moment correlation coefficients between each single factor and the other factors were below its AVE square root [68].

Finally, all data were used for internal consistency reliability analysis, split-half reliability analysis, and cross-gender invariance test. The retest sample data were used for reliability analysis. Specifically, SPSS 22.0 was used to calculate Cronbach’s α reliability and split-half reliability for the entire dataset and Cronbach’s α reliability was calculated for the retest sample data. A threshold of 0.70 [69] was used as the judgment standard for all reliability coefficients. Additionally, following previous research, the cross-gender invariance [70] of the official MIFS was tested using Mplus 8.3. The nested model comparison criteria proposed by Cheung and Rensvold [65] was used to determine whether the official MIFS has passed the cross-gender invariance test.

The factors were labeled to reflect the common connotation of the items they contained. Discussion

Mental internal friction has become an important factor affecting the mental health of today’s college students [7, 34]. However, due to a lack of targeted quantitative tools, current research on mental internal friction is limited to phenomenological descriptions, seriously hindering the advancement of related research. Therefore, our study aimed to develop the MIFS, which demonstrated good reliability and validity.

First, we compared mental internal friction with other similar constructs, which enhanced the conceptual clarity of mental internal friction. At a macro level, mental internal friction shares certain similarities with constructs such as “goblin mode”, “lying flat”, “tanking”, “buddha-like” and “self-indulgence”. However, mental internal friction differs significantly from these constructs in both connotation and generation mechanisms. In terms of connotation, mental internal friction primarily refers to a state of psychological fatigue and motivational depletion caused by excessive cognitive exhaustion under intense pressure. In contrast, “goblin mode”, “lying flat”, and “tanking” generally represent negative behavioral patterns or coping strategies [32], while “buddha-like” and “self-indulgence” reflect an indifferent life attitude [30] and irrational behavior following self-control failure [31], respectively. Regarding the generation mechanisms, mental internal friction arises from excessive self-regulation and high self-expectations, whereas “goblin mode”, “lying flat”, “tanking”, and “buddha-like” stem from a negative interpretation or deconstruction of reality. “Self-indulgence”, on the other hand, results from weakened self-control and monitoring. Notably, although some scholars have approached mental internal friction from the perspective of self-regulation fatigue [7], based on the descriptions of mental internal friction in previous studies [9, 14], it encompasses a broader conceptual scope, particularly emphasizing a systematic decline in psychological motivation under high-pressure conditions. This further supports the argument that, from a psychometric standpoint, the MIFS demonstrates superior measurement validity.

At the micro level, although the four dimensions of mental internal friction exhibit surface-level similarities with previously established constructs, they demonstrate significant differences in terms of internalization depth. Existing literature indicates that mental internal friction primarily manifests through four typical sub-clinical states: avoidance [9, 14], rumination [33], sensitivity [2, 9], and indecision [7, 9], which together form the conceptual foundation of the MIFS. Specifically, avoidance reflects a decline in psychological motivation and behavioral vitality caused by mental internal friction; rumination indicates negative changes in thinking patterns caused by mental internal friction; sensitivity captures the adverse impact of mental internal friction on interpersonal cognition; and indecision represents a reduction in decision-making efficiency caused by mental internal friction. However, it is important to note that in prior research, avoidance has predominantly been characterized as a maladaptive coping strategy in response to stress [18]; rumination has been described as a discrepancy-focused thinking style [36]; sensitivity has largely been identified as a stable personality trait [40]; and indecision has most frequently been regarded as a personality-linked psychological state [42]. Therefore, compared to the four subclinical dimensions of mental internal friction, these corresponding constructs exhibit a higher degree of internalization and more stable behavioral manifestations.

Second, we developed the items for the MIFS to use in college students through semi-structured interviews and literature review. The official MIFS has a total of 21 items measuring four factors. The factor loading values of the 21 items ranged from 0.577 to 0.906, and the cumulative variance contribution rate of the four factors is 60.967%, which is consistent with the criteria proposed by Li et al. [72] where factor loading values should exceed 0.40, and the cumulative variance contribution rate should exceed 40.00%. After integrating and analyzing the implications of the specific items within each factor, factor 1 (six items) was labeled as “avoidance”, factor 2 (five items) as “rumination”, factor 3 (five items) as “sensitivity”, and factor 4 (five items) as “indecision”. Each factor has a clear meaning, a distinct definition, and a relatively balanced number of items. Moreover, their contents cover the connotations and perceptions of mental internal friction, making them suitable for measure and evaluate the degree to which college students experience mental internal friction.

Third, to test the scale’s reliability, we calculated the Cronbach’s α, split-half reliability, composite reliability, and retest reliability of the MIFS. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the MIFS and its four factors were all greater than or equal to 0.806; the half-part reliability coefficients of the MIFS and its four factors were all greater than or equal to 0.736; the composite reliability of the MIFS and its four factors were all greater than or equal to 0.804; and the retest reliability of the MIFS and its four factors were all greater than or equal to 0.779. All of these reliability coefficients exceed the recommended standard of 0.70 [69], indicating that the MIFS has good reliability from a variety of aspects.

Finally, regarding the scale’s validity, we tested the structure validity, content validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the official MIFS. The fitting indexes of the four-factor structure of the MIFS were superior to those of the other competing models and met the model fitting index judgment criteria proposed by Wen et al. [64], indicating good structural validity of the MIFS. Pearson product–moment correlation analysis showed that the correlation coefficients between the four factors and the total MIFS score were all greater than or equal to 0.343, reaching significance (p < 0.001), which demonstrates good content validity of the MIFS. Convergent and discriminant validity tests also confirmed their acceptability [73, 74]. Lastly, the MIFS passed the cross-gender invariance test. Together, these results indicate that the MIFS has good validity and can be used as an effective measurement and evaluation tool of mental internal friction experienced by Chinese college students.

Furthermore, to evaluate the criterion validity of the MIFS, we investigated the correlations between MIFS and MPS at the sub-dimension level. The results indicated a significant positive correlation between the adaptability sub-dimension of MPS and both the total score of MIFS and all its sub-dimensions. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between the high standard sub-dimension of MPS and both the total score of MIFS and its sensitivity sub-dimension. The adaptability sub-dimension of MPS reflects the unadaptability associated with an individual’s pursuit of perfectionism; higher scores indicate greater levels of unadaptability. The high standard sub-dimension reflects the extent to which individuals set high behavioral standards for themselves and others, as well as their acceptance of such standards imposed by others; higher scores indicate stronger perfectionist tendencies [50] According to previous research, perfectionism is linked to mental internal friction in at least two ways. First, maladaptive perfectionism leads individuals to worry about making mistakes, experience action hesitation, face the disappointment of expectations, avoid negative evaluations, and excessively fear failure [75], which consumes more psychological resources [76] and thereby induces mental internal friction. Second, the pressure stemming from perceived external and societal high standards causes perfectionists to constantly worry about disappointing others or facing criticism [77], which further increases their sensitivity. These evidences support the aforementioned results of the present study and aligns with previous perspectives indicating a close relationship between perfectionism and mental internal friction [9, 12, 48], thereby further confirming the good criterion validity of the MIFS. Regarding the lack of correlation between the total MPS score and the indecision sub-dimension of MIFS, and the correlation between the high standard sub-dimension of MPS and the total MIFS score in the present study, these may be attributed to two underlying factors: the absence of a significant relationship between the high standard sub-dimension of MPS and the indecision sub-dimension of MIFS; the presence of a significant relationship between the high standard sub-dimension of MPS and the sensitivity sub-dimension of MIFS.

In conclusion, the MIFS we developed not only exhibits excellent psychometric properties across multiple indicators, but also offers significant potential for advancing future research and clinical practice. In terms of research applications, the MIFS can be directly utilized to delineate the developmental trajectory of mental internal friction and to construct theoretical models linking it with other relevant variables, thereby enriching the empirical and theoretical understanding of mental internal friction in a systematic manner. Regarding clinical practice, the MIFS can serve as an effective tool for early warning of neuroses or psychosomatic disorders by identifying individuals with high scores, as mental internal friction has been associated with an increased risk of such conditions [5, 6]. Furthermore, longitudinal monitoring of MIFS scores in clinical populations may facilitate the assessment of disease progression and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions for psychological disorders such as anxiety and depression.

Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations exist in the current study. First, all subjects were college students from northwest China. Considering the possible cultural differences between northern and southern China [78] (similar to the differences between individualism and collectivism), there may be regional variations in both the intensity and nature of mental internal friction. Consequently, this could limit the applicability of the MIFS in southern regions. Future research should examine the validity of the MIFS among students in other regions of China (e.g., southern China) and diverse cultural contexts. Second, the phenomenon of mental internal friction is also common among general social workers [10] and college counselors [79]. Since the manifestation of mental internal friction may differ across populations, further studies should investigate whether the MIFS can be effectively applied to assess mental internal friction in these alternative groups. Third, although the overall convergent validity of the MIFS is acceptable, the AVE of the indecision factor is slightly below the ideal threshold of 0.50 [80]. This may be attributed to relatively low homogeneity of items within this dimension. Therefore, future research could refine this subscale by improving item consistency, such as revising existing items, adding more homogeneous items, or removing those with low homogeneity. Fourth, the total sample included a disproportionately high percentage of female participants (73.504%), which may compromise the generalizability of the MIFS to male college students. Although cross-gender invariance testing partially mitigates this concern, ensuring gender balance in future applications of the MIFS remains an important area for further investigation. Fifth, during the item analysis process, we prioritized maximizing the cumulative variance contribution rate of the scale factors. Consequently, the official MIFS contains a relatively large number of items (n = 21), which may hinder its practical utility. Therefore, future studies could utilize network analysis techniques to streamline the scale while maintaining its psychometric integrity. Sixth, the current study only conducted a cross-gender invariance test for the MIFS, which provides limited insight into its overall robustness. Therefore, it is essential for future research to examine the equivalence of the MIFS across other demographic groups such as academic majors, grade levels, and regions of origin in order to further reveal its psychometric properties. Finally, we proposed a four-factor structure of mental internal friction based on existing literature and semi-structured interview data; however, due to limited literature availability, further research is necessary to validate whether this structure accurately captures the construct of mental internal friction.

Conclusions

Drawing upon an extensive literature review and semi-structured interviews, we developed the MIFS to assess and quantify the experience of mental internal friction among Chinese college students. The MIFS utilizes a six-point Likert scale and comprises 21 items across four dimensions: avoidance, rumination, sensitivity, and indecision. Both the overall scale and its four dimensions demonstrated satisfactory reliability (including internal consistency, split-half reliability, composite reliability, and test–retest reliability) and validity (covering content validity, structural validity, criterion validity, convergent validity, and discriminative validity). Additionally, the MIFS exhibited cross-gender invariance. The scale can serve as a reliable and valid tool for evaluating the extent of mental internal friction among Chinese college students in both theoretical and practical contexts.
