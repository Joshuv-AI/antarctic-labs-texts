Epidural abscess signal (EAS) and vertebral body signal (VBS) scores: MRI-based quantitative tools for early differentiation of pyogenic and tuberculous spondylitis to reduce inappropriate empirical therapy
Authors: Zhibin Chen, Yuming Huang, Boxuan Xu, Yaowang Pan, Chuanrong Chen, Rongsheng Chen, Weihong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyogenic spondylitis (PS) and tuberculous spondylitis (TS) are two common types of spinal infections. Given the significant differences in their antimicrobial treatment regimens, early and accurate differentiation is critical for developing appropriate clinical management strategies. The study aimed to develop and validate MRI-based epidural abscess signal (EAS) and vertebral body signal (VBS) scores for differentiating PS from TS, guiding empirical antibiotic therapy before microbiological confirmation.MethodsThis study enrolled patients with definitive etiological or pathological diagnosis of PS or TS from two tertiary centers into two cohorts: a retrospective derivation cohort (n = 88; December 2021–December 2024) and a prospective validation cohort (n = 34; January–June 2025). The EAS score was calculated using T2-weighted imaging signal intensity, while the VBS score was derived from T1-weighted imaging signal intensity. Diagnostic performance of scores was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to determine optimal cutoff values. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to develop the prediction model and externally validated through the prospective cohort.ResultsPS patients consistently demonstrated significantly higher EAS scores and lower VBS scores compared to TS patients across both derivation and validation cohorts (all p < 0.001). ROC analysis within the derivation cohort established EAS ≥ 0.400 (AUC = 0.888, 95% CI 0.801–0.975) and VBS ≤ 2.000 (AUC = 0.870, 95% C I0.784–0.956) as optimal diagnostic thresholds for PS. Implementing these thresholds to guide empirical therapy in the validation cohort significantly improved clinical management. This intervention resulted in a marked reduction in inappropriate empirical therapy (5.9% vs. 23.4%), shorter mean hospitalization duration (23.4 vs. 28.7 days), and reduced total antibiotic therapy duration (20.2 vs. 25.2 days) compared to the derivation cohort managed by standard guidelines. Multivariate analysis confirmed EAS ≥ 0.400 (aOR = 50.04, 95% CI 3.15–794.06) and CRP ≥ 50 mg/L (aOR = 37.47, 95% CI 1.01–1384.31) as independent predictors of PS in the EAS subgroup. Similarly, VBS ≤ 2.000 (aOR = 84.10, 95% CI 9.81–721.18) and CRP ≥ 50 mg/L (aOR = 10.62, 95% CI 1.11–101.46) were significant independent predictors in the VBS subgroup.ConclusionThe EAS and VBS MRI-based scores enable rapid differentiation of pyogenic from tuberculous spondylitis, significantly reducing inappropriate antibiotic use and optimizing therapeutic decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06786-4.Keywords: Pyogenic spondylitis, Tuberculous spondylitis, Empirical therapy, Epidural abscess, Vertebral body, Quantitative scores

Introduction

Pyogenic spondylitis (PS) and tuberculous spondylitis (TS) represent the most frequent etiologies of spinal infections, comprising 50–70% and 20–40% of cases, respectively [1, 2]. The definitive diagnosis of PS and TS requires microbiological confirmation combined with histopathological examination. However, the culture-negative rate for PS ranges from 9 to 47% [3], while TS yields positive cultures in only 48% to 76% of cases, with results typically requiring a prolonged waiting period of 3 to 8 weeks [4, 5].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging method for early diagnosis of spinal infections due to its superior soft tissue contrast and sensitivity to water content changes, allowing detection of inflammatory edema, abscess formation, and early structural damage [6, 7]. The pathological features of PS and TS exhibit fundamental differences in tissue hydration: PS typically presents with acute purulent exudation (high free water content) and proteolytic disc destruction, while TS is characterized by caseous necrosis (high viscous/solid component) and granulomatous inflammation with fibrotic changes (lower free water content) [7, 8], Despite these distinct hydration-related MRI patterns, current clinical practice lacks a reliable quantitative tool to differentiate TS from PS.

This study developed and validated two quantitative MRI scoring systems, the epidural abscess signal (EAS) score and the vertebral body signal (VBS) score, to differentiate pyogenic from tuberculous spondylitis before pathological confirmation, thereby guiding empirical antibiotic therapy during the critical pre-diagnostic period.

Materials and methods

Cohorts and patients

This is a retrospective analysis from an ongoing perspective real-world study at two tertiary centers, including a derivation cohort (n = 88, retrospective) and a validation cohort (n = 34, prospective). Ethical approval for this study (2022-1-13, IEC-FOM-013-2) was provided by the Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The derivation cohort included hospitalized patients with a definitive pathogenically or pathologically confirmed diagnosis of PS/TS, identified via medical record review between December 2021 and December 2024 after the exclusion criteria were applied. The validation cohort will consecutively enroll hospitalized patients with suspected PS/TS who undergo MRI between January and June 2025, and these patients will undergo a multidisciplinary assessment (Orthopedics/Infectious Diseases) for empiric treatment indications, including severe or progressive neurological symptoms [9, 10]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) repeat admissions; (2) absence of definitive etiological or pathological diagnosis; (3) spinal infections of other etiologies; (3) presence of adjacent metallic implants potentially affecting MRI signal intensity and (5) incomplete imaging or clinical data. The definitive diagnostic criteria for PS required fulfillment of at least one of the following: (1) identification of pathogenic bacteria in intraoperative or percutaneous spinal specimens; (2) positive metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) results from spinal specimens; or (3) radiological evidence of spinal infection accompanied by positive blood cultures [9]. For TS, the definitive diagnosis required meeting at least one criterion: (1) isolation of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M-TB) from intraoperative or percutaneous spinal specimens; (2) histopathological confirmation of tuberculous granulomas; or (3) radiological evidence of spinal infection supported by positive PCR results [11, 12].

EAS score and VBS score

All MRIs in this study comprised non-contrast T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) spinal sequences. Images were optionally displayed with picture archiving and communication system (PACS)-based pseudo-color enhancement to facilitate visual delineation of abscess margins; however, all regions of interest (ROIs) measurements for EAS/VBS scores were performed on the original grayscale Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images, and pseudo-color display did not alter the underlying MRI signal intensity values used for score calculation [13].

The EAS score is derived from sagittal T2WI sequences by quantifying epidural abscess signal intensity relative to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). In patients with multilevel involvement, only one ROI-defined representative level per patient was analyzed to ensure independence of observations. The standardized process involves: (1) ROIs were placed on the sagittal T2WI sequences showing the largest cross-sectional area of the epidural abscess, positioned within the central abscess cavity (avoiding the abscess wall) and the adjacent normal CSF space of the superior segment; (2) measuring mean signal intensities (MSI) in both ROIs; (3) calculating the ratio (EAS score = MSI epidural abscess/MSI CSF). The VBS score evaluates vertebral body signal abnormalities on sagittal T1WI using an analogous method. The standardized process involves: (1) ROIs were placed on the T1WI sequences showing the largest extent of vertebral body abnormal signal, positioned at the center of the abnormal marrow region (avoiding any intravertebral abscess) and the adjacent normal CSF space of the superior segment; (2) measuring MSI in both ROIs; (3) calculating the ratio (VBS score = MSI abnormal signal area/MSI CSF). ROI area (mm²) was recorded for each measurement, and ROI areas were compared between the PS and TS groups (Supplementary Material 1: Table 1). For the EAS score, ROIs were delineated on T2WI only; EAS ROIs delineation on T1WI was not feasible and therefore was not performed. For the VBS score, the same vertebral ROI was propagated to the matched T1WI and T2WI, and diagnostic performance between sequences was compared using ROC analysis (Supplementary Material 2).

MRI was performed on scanners with different field strengths, including a 1.5-T system (Toshiba Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) and a 3.0-T system (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Because MRI signal intensities may vary across scanner vendors and field strengths, baseline inter-scanner differences can confound intensity-based MRI scores [14]. Therefore, the CSF signal was used as an internal reference to normalize signal intensity and account for inter-scanner variability [15]. To ensure a homogeneous reference, CSF ROIs were placed in regions free of nerve root pathways. This normalization approach helps reduce scanner-related bias and improves the robustness of the scoring system across platforms. To enhance the generalizability of the scores, we applied no exclusion criteria for main magnetic field strength or MRI manufacturer to increase score universality. Accordingly, we compared measurements across scanner platforms to confirm that vendor was not a potential confounder (Supplementary Material 1: Table 2). Two independent researchers, blinded to the spondylitis subtype, conducted all EAS and VBS measurements and calculations. The final analysis incorporated the averaged values from both observers’ measurements.

Clinical data and MRI image

Clinical data collected at admission included: (1) demographic: age, sex, body mass index (BMI); (2) characteristics: onset time, tuberculosis history, symptoms, comorbidities; (3) biomarkers: white blood cell(WBC) count, neutrophil(NE) count, C-reactive protein (CRP) level, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Clinical management metrics were evaluated, including time to definitive diagnosis (interval from admission to confirmed diagnosis), hospital length of stay, and time to treatment initiation (interval from admission to the first antimicrobial/antitubercular dose). The therapy duration was stratified as the duration of empirical therapy (prior to pathogen identification), duration of targeted therapy (guided by identification results), and total duration of therapy. Empirical therapy rates (proportion of patients receiving initial empirical treatment) and inappropriate empirical therapy rates (initial regimens mismatched to the confirmed type of spondylitis) were calculated.

All enrolled patients underwent MRI examinations during hospitalization, and all analysis were based on non-contrast sequences. MRI image analysis included level and number of involved vertebrae, vertebral destruction, abscess distribution, EAS score, and VBS score. Vertebral destruction was assessed based on the vertebral height destruction and endplate destruction. Abscess distribution patterns were classified into five categories. Detailed classifications and definitions of the MRI features, along with typical images, are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations summarized continuous variables, while percentages characterized categorical data. Demographic and clinical characteristics were analyzed using Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or the Fisher’s Exact test for categorical variables to compare the PS and TS groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of biomarkers, the EAS score, and the VBS score for pyogenic spondylitis by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index (maximizing sensitivity + specificity − 1). Inter-rater agreement between the two independent researchers was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). To address quasi-complete separation and reduce small-sample bias, multivariate analysis was conducted using Firth’s penalized likelihood regression. Due to high collinearity with WBC count, NE count (largest variance inflation factor [VIF] and smallest tolerance) was excluded from the multivariate model. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all analyses in this study. Data processing and statistical tests were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Stata version 18.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Clinical outcomes

In the derivation cohort, empiric therapy was administered according to standard clinical practice guidelines [9, 10, 14], whereas in the validation cohort, antimicrobial selection was guided by the EAS/VBS scoring system.

The validation cohort demonstrated significantly improved clinical outcomes compared to the derivation cohort: perfect empirical therapy selection (100% vs. 72.7%, p = 0.001), reduced inappropriate regimens (5.9% vs. 23.4%, p = 0.029) (Fig. 3), faster treatment initiation (2.0 ± 1.0 vs. 3.6 ± 3.3 days, p < 0.001), shorter antibiotic courses (20.2 ± 9.2 vs. 25.2 ± 12.2 days, p = 0.033), and decreased hospitalization (23.4 ± 9.3 vs. 28.7 ± 13.6 days, p = 0.039), with comparable diagnostic timeframes (Table 1). General characteristics

The PS and TS groups showed comparable characteristics in the validation cohort (Table 2). But PS had higher EAS scores (0.53 ± 0.11 vs. 0.32 ± 0.11, p < 0.001), TS had higher VBS scores (1.53 ± 0.27 vs. 2.22 ± 0.37, p < 0.001) and more frequent anterior subligamentous spread (64.3% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.035).

In the derivation cohort, ROI areas were comparable between the PS and TS groups for both EAS and VBS scores measurements (Supplementary Material 1: Table 1). Notably, there was no significant difference between the mean EAS and VBS scores between MR systems (Supplementary Material 1: Table 2). Moreover, interobserver and intraobserver reliability was excellent, with ICCs of 0.872 (95% CI 0.798–0.921) and 0.983 (95% CI 0.971–0.989) for the EAS score, and 0.929 (95% CI 0.890–0.954) and 0.955 (95% CI 0.932–0.971) for the VBS score, respectively.

Diagnostic performance

ROC analysis established EAS ≥ 0.400 (AUC: 0.888, 95% CI 0.801–0.975) and VBS ≤ 2.000 (AUC 0.870, 0.784–0.956) as optimal diagnostic thresholds for PS in the derivation cohort, demonstrating robust diagnostic performance when applied to the validation cohort compared with conventional biomarkers alone (Fig. 4). In the same derivation cohort, the VBS score yielded a higher AUC on T1WI (0.870, 95% CI 0.784–0.956) than on T2WI (0.760, 95% CI 0.654–0.868) (Supplementary Material 2). In the validation cohort, EAS ≥ 0.400 demonstrated 91.7% sensitivity (95% CI 64.6–99.6%) and 90.0% specificity (95% CI 59.6–99.5%). Concurrently, VBS ≤ 2.000 achieved perfect sensitivity (100%, 95% CI 83.9–100%) with 92.9% specificity (95% CI 68.5–99.6%).

Independent Predictors

Firth’s regression confirmed EAS ≥ 0.400 (aOR = 50.04, p = 0.006) and VBS ≤ 2.000 (aOR = 84.10, p < 0.001) as independent predictors of pyogenic spondylitis, with CRP ≥ 50 mg/L as a significant covariate, remaining significant for both scores (p < 0.05). Anterior subligamentous spread exhibited a protective effect against PS in the VBS subgroup (aOR 0.06; 95% CI 0.01–0.50; p = 0.010) (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the clinical implementation of the EAS and VBS MRI-based scoring systems, developed to differentiate pyogenic from tuberculous spondylitis, yielded substantial improvements in therapeutic management through their robust diagnostic performance. Most notably, the rate of inappropriate empirical therapy administration decreased significantly from 23.4 to 5.9% (absolute reduction of 17.5% points; p = 0.029). Furthermore, the interval between initial presentation and commencement of appropriate antimicrobial/antitubercular therapy was reduced by a mean of 1.6 days (from 3.6 ± 3.3 days to 2.0 ± 1.0 days; p < 0.001), representing a 44% reduction in treatment delay.

Despite the standardized diagnostic protocols for spinal infections [9, 10], the delay in obtaining definitive results and the risk of severe consequences from delayed diagnosis emphasize the need for early differentiation among infection types. In our study, the median diagnostic delay time was 15 days in the derivation cohort and 13 days in the validation cohort. Importantly, both the EAS and VBS scores can be calculated immediately after the initial MRI examination, enabling early differentiation. This ability significantly shortens the diagnostic delay and facilitates timely treatment planning, providing a novel approach for clinical decision algorithms (Fig. 5).

PS epidural abscess, composed of necrotic debris and proteinaceous exudate with elevated free water content due to bacterial proteolytic enzymes, presents as increased T2-weighted MRI signal intensity and bone marrow edema [16, 17]. TS exhibits caseous necrosis with lipid-rich components and reduced free water content, wherein trabecular bone lipid infiltration correlates with T1-weighted hyperintensity [18, 19]. Capitalizing on the distinct pathophysiological profiles of PS (T2-hyperintense abscess) and TS (T1-hyperintense vertebral body), the EAS and VBS scores provide objective criteria for quantifying MRI signal patterns that correspond to their pathological differences. The EAS and VBS scores reliably differentiated PS from TS, exhibiting good to excellent interobserver agreement. The two quantitative scores provide clinicians with an objective decision-making framework that may potentially reduce diagnostic delays caused by overlapping clinical presentations between these two conditions.

Previous studies on the early identification of PS and TS have primarily constructed models based on qualitative MRI features and biomarkers [20–22]. Wang et al. [23] established an eight-predictor MRI-based nomogram differentiating TS from PS, with multilevel vertebral involvement, vertebral destruction severity and abscess characteristics as key discriminators. In another approach, Tanaviriyachai et al. [24] developed a seven-factor MRI-based scoring system ranging from 0 to 29 points. Their model prioritized thoracic lesions, absence of epidural phlegmon, subligamentous spreading as primary discriminators. Notably, the role of subligamentous spreading as a TS predictor parallels our analysis results. In our derivation cohort, the AUC for the EAS score alone reached 0.888, while the AUC for the VBS score was 0.870. Interestingly, three false-negative PS cases in derivation and validation cohorts involved Escherichia coli infections, potentially attributable to low proteolytic enzyme levels impairing EAS score performance. However, this hypothesis warranting further validation.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the diagnostic specificity could not be fully evaluated due to insufficient false-positive cases in our cohort. While the dual-center design helps minimize selection bias, the relatively small validation cohort may limit generalizability to broader populations. The clinical application of the EAS score is constrained by its dependence on detectable epidural abscesses, and potential variability in cerebrospinal fluid signal intensity may affect measurements. The current manual scoring method also introduces possible measurement variability. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate optimal diagnostic thresholds across diverse clinical scenarios and to evaluate the scores’ performance for other spinal infection types. Automated measurement methods currently under development may help address standardization challenges.

Conclusion

The EAS and VBS MRI-based scoring systems effectively distinguish pyogenic from tuberculous spondylitis before microbiological confirmation, allowing clinicians to initiate appropriate antibiotic therapy earlier and reduce unnecessary treatment.
