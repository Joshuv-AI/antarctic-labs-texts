Risk factors for lung metastasis in children and adolescent patients with papillary thyroid cancer: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hancong Li, Liying Wang, Gongshuang Zhang, Yue Zhao, Xiangyu Kong, Huanzuo Yang, Xinyue Fu, Peiheng Li, Rui Huang, Jiaye Liu, Xiaofei Wang, Zhihui Li, Han Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric papillary thyroid cancer (pPTC) exhibits a higher incidence of lung metastasis (LM) compared to its adult counterpart. This study supplements the gap in pediatric management guidelines by exploring factors associated with LM in pPTC and characterizing relevant genetic alterations.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed pPTC patients under 20 years who underwent initial surgery at our center between December 2008 and December 2022. Clinicopathological features, treatment approaches, outcomes, and target-gene sequencing were reviewed to identify risk factors and genetic variations.ResultsAmong 114 pPTC cases, 17 developed LM. Risk factors associated with LM included younger age, male gender, larger tumor size, multifocality, extrathyroidal extension, lymphatic invasion, and the number of metastatic lymph nodes (NMLNs), with NMLNs > 14 identified as an independent predictor (OR = 18.20, p = 0.037). Treatment responses with radioiodine related to postoperative stimulated thyroglobulin (sTg) levels (p = 0.003). Genomic analysis identified 1007 somatic mutations, including in the BRAF, APC, RET, and ATM genes, with RET-NCOA4 fusions observed in the LM subgroup.ConclusionLM is common in pPTC, and NMLNs > 14 is a critical risk indicator. The genetic profile, particularly RET mutations, highlights potential therapeutic targets. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12020-025-04513-3.Keywords: Papillary thyroid cancer, Pediatric, Lung metastasis, Risk factor, Target sequencing

Introduction

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common endocrine tumor in adolescents [1]. According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, the incidence of pediatric papillary thyroid cancer (pPTC) increased by 9.56% annually from 2006 to 2013 [2], accounting for about 1.4% to 2.3% of all thyroid cancers [3, 4]. Despite this increasing prevalence, comprehensive management guidelines for pPTC were not established by the American Thyroid Association (ATA) until 2015, with most recommendations being adapted from adult guideline [5, 6]. However, this approach overlooked the distinct biological and clinical differences between pediatric and adult cases [7]. Reportedly, compared to adults, pediatric patients often represent more aggressively and experience higher rates of recurrence or persistence after surgery [8–11]. Furthermore, they also tend to have larger tumors, a higher incidence of bilateral and multifocal lesions, and a significantly elevated lymph node metastasis rate of approximately 40–90%, compared to 20–50% in adults [12–14].

Lung metastasis (LM) is a critical risk factor for poor prognosis in PTC patients. Although it occurs in only 1% to 4% of adult cases, LM remains the leading cause of mortality. A long-term study reported that the 10-year mortality rate of differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) with LM was13.8%, significantly higher than the group without LM [15, 16]. Alarmingly, up to 7%-30% of pediatric patients with PTC are affected by LM [5, 17–20]. While children generally exhibit better radioactive iodine (RAI) uptake than adults, only a small proportion of patients achieve an excellent response after serial I131 therapy, and some may experience treatment-related adverse effects, such as pulmonary fibrosis [17, 21–24]. Therefore, further studies to identify high-risk factors for lung metastasis in pediatric and adolescent PTC patients are essential for early prevention and treatment.

To date, only a few studies have examined the clinicopathological features of pPTC and explored the risk factors for LM [20, 24, 25], with this topic remaining poorly understood, particularly in Chinese cohorts [26, 27]. In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of pPTC patients at our center, aiming to elucidate risk factors for LM and treatment responses.

Methods

Our study was in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort Studies in Surgery (STROCSS) guidelines [28]. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly adhered to. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board approval of West China hospital of Sichuan University (Approval No. 2020 − 737). Additionally, the research protocol has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200066828), ensuring compliance with current best practices in clinical research.

Study design and patient selection

In this study, we analyzed data from a longitudinal database established in 2008 at West China Hospital, a large tertiary teaching hospital affiliated with Sichuan University that serves as a major healthcare center in Southwest China.

Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) undergoing initial thyroid surgery at our institution between December 2008 and December 2022 , (b) aged under 20 years at the time of surgery, and (c) confirmed postoperative pathological diagnosis of PTC, irrespective of its subtype.

Exclusion criteria encompassed: (a) a postoperative pathological diagnosis of follicular thyroid carcinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, or anaplastic thyroid carcinoma , (b) concurrent other malignancies, and (c) incomplete clinicopathological records or follow-up data.

Clinical management

Surgical procedures were performed by two board-certified surgeons, and histopathological diagnoses were independently reviewed and confirmed in a double-blind manner by two experienced pathologists. Based on intraoperative findings and histopathological reports, patients were staged in accordance with the 8th edition of the AJCC/TNM staging system [29]. Recurrence was defined as the detection of new biochemical, structural, or functional evidence of disease after remission [30, 31]. Minimal extrathyroidal extension (mETE) was defined as tumor extension into perithyroidal soft tissues or strap muscles, whereas gross extrathyroidal extension (gETE) referred to tumor invasion beyond these muscles [32].

Radioactive iodine (I131) therapy was administered to patients with inoperable locoregional disease, lymph node involvement, or distant metastasis [33]. Preparation for RAI therapy involved a three-week withdrawal of levothyroxine. Disease status assessment included measurements of serum thyroglobulin (Tg), thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), free T3 (FT3), free T4 (FT4), along with neck ultrasound and chest computed tomography (CT).

According to the institutional nuclear medicine protocol [30], a dose of 1.35 to 2.7 mCi/kg was administered to prepubertal patients. For post-puberty patients, the administered activity ranged from 100 mCi for simple remnant ablation to 150–200 mCi for postoperative residual neck lymph node or distant metastases.

Follow-up

All patients underwent postoperative follow-up, with evaluations scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery, and every 6–12 months thereafter. Routine monitoring involved assessments of Tg, TgAb, thyroid hormone levels, along with neck ultrasonography and chest CT scans, to monitor disease status and detect potential recurrence or metastasis [34].

Outcome measures

The response to RAI therapy in patients with LM was assessed according to the adult ATA guidelines and classified into four categories : (a) Excellent response (ER): negative imaging with Tg levels < 0.2 µg/L, or postoperative stimulated thyroglobulin (sTg) < 1 µg/L, and TgAb < 40 IU/ml; (b) Biochemical incomplete response (BIR): negative imaging but Tg > 1 µg/L, sTg > 10 µg/L, or rising TgAb levels; (c) Structural incomplete response (SIR): evidence of persistent or newly detected locoregional or distant metastasis, regardless of Tg or TgAb levels; (d) Indeterminate response (IR): biochemical or structural findings cannot be clearly classified as benign or malignant [31].

Genotyping

For genotyping, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at 5–20 μm and microdissected when appropriate. After deparaffinization, genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Multiplex polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) was then performed for amplification. Library preparation was carried out with the KAPA HyperPlus Kit, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Targeted gene sequencing was conducted on genomic DNA from tumor samples and paired normal tissues using a custom-designed, patented panel developed by our group (HX-thyroid panel, patent No. ZL 2020 1 0461327.4).

Data collection

Two authors independently extracted data using a custom-made template, with any disagreements resolved through discussion. Variables collected included age, gender, histological tumor type, tumor size, multifocality, presence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, nodular goiter, extrathyroidal extension, lymph node metastasis, number of metastatic lymph nodes (NMLNs), LM, and postoperative sTg levels. Researchers involved with data collection were not involved in the analysis.

Data synthesis and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed with the T tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression were conducted to identify the independent risk factors for LM. Variables with univariate p < 0.05 were included in the multivariate analysis. Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine the optimal cutoffs for predicting LM and treatment responses in pPTC. The inspection standards were α = 0.05, and p<0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the pPTC patients

This retrospective study included 114 consecutive patients who met the inclusion criteria (FigureS1). The cohort comprised 91 females (79.82%) and 23 males (20.18%), with a mean age at diagnosis of 16.14 ± 3.33 years (female-to-male ratio: 3.96:1). The median follow-up duration was 64.5 months (IQR, 44–104 months). Among them, 17 patients (14.91%) developed LM, of whom13 were diagnosed at initial presentation and 4 during follow-up.

Baseline demographic characteristics stratified by LM were summarized in Table 1. Notably, patients with LM were significantly younger at diagnosis (14.16 ± 3.29 years vs. 16.49 ± 3.23 years, p = 0.007) and more frequently male compared to those without LM (41.17% vs. 16.49%, p = 0.044). The LM group also presented more aggressive disease features, including larger tumor sizes (39.81 ± 14.66 mm vs. 23.20 ± 15.22 mm, p<0.001), higher rates of multifocality (93.75% vs. 34.73%, p<0.001), extrathyroidal extension (ETE) (88.24% vs. 41.05%, p<0.001), and a greater NMLNs (23 vs. 9, p < 0.001). Additionally, the postoperative sTg level was substantially elevated in the LM group compared with the non-LM group (1256.70 ± 1421.60 µg/L vs. 17.52 ± 33.89 µg/L, p = 0.008).

Predictors of LM in pPTC

Multivariate analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for LM in pPTC patients. After adjusting for age, gender, tumor size, ETE, and multifocality, the presence of more than 14 NMLNs emerged as a significant independent predictor of LM (OR = 18.20, 95% CI: 1.19-277.89, p = 0.037, Table 2). When patients were categorized by NMLN count (> 14 vs. ≤ 14), the incidence of LM was markedly higher in those with > 14 NMLNs (42.9% vs. 2.5%, p < 0.001, Fig. 1B).

Moreover, when combined with the previous study reporting NMLN counts in pPTC [20, 27], the analysis revealed a significant difference in NMLNs between the LM and control groups (summary MD = 10.70, 95% CI: 7.98–13.41, Fig. 1C), underscoring NMLNs as a crucial independent risk factor for LM.

Clinicopathological and treatment outcomes of LM in pPTC

Table S2 summarizes clinicopathological features and follow-up data of 17 patients with LM. The median cumulative RAI activity was 600 mCi (range, 250–900 mCi), with an average of 3 treatments per patient (range, 2–5). Two patients were lost to follow-up, leaving 15 available for the subsequent analysis. Post-therapy chest CT showed increased tumor size in 26.67%, reduction in 46.67%, stability in 20%, and lesions detectable only on I131 scans in 6.67% (Fig. 2A). Serum Tg dynamics [35] were assessed in 13 patients after excluding 2 cases due to TgAb interference and incomplete data; among these, two (13.33%) achieved ER, three (20%) had BIR, and 8 (66.67%) had SIR (Fig. 2B and C).

Genomic features in pPTC samples

To investigate the genetic landscape of pediatric papillary thyroid carcinoma (pPTC), 71 genes were selected for targeted capture sequencing using our proprietary panel (genes detailed in Table S4). After quality control, 64 paired samples with sufficient DNA were sequenced, revealing 1007 somatic variants, including 707 non-silent mutation. The most frequently mutated gene was BRAF in 29.69% of patients (19/64), with the prevalent BRAFV600E mutation present in 15 cases. This mutation is also common in adult PTC, typically with a higher frequency. Other notable mutations included NF1 (10.94%,7/64), which is implicated in the RAS/MAPK pathway dysregulation, as well as APC (6.25%), ATM (4.69%), KIT (3.13%), and MSH2 (3.13%).

Notably, the primary tumors from 8 patients with LM showed relatively few genomic variants. Among these, one sample carried a missense mutation in APC, and one carried a missense mutation in the KIT. In addition, a RET-NCOA4 gene fusion was identified in two LM patients; however, its biological and clinical significance requires confirmation in larger cohorts. Given the limited scope of our targeted gene panel, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In our retrospective cohort of 114 pPTC patients, 14.91% (17/114) developed LM. Risk factors associated with LM included male gender, younger age (< 16 years), larger tumor size (> 20 mm), multifocality, extrathyroidal extension, and metastatic lymph nodes. Multivariate analysis further identified NMLNs > 14 as an independent predictor of LM.

Although similar observations have been made in adults [36], pediatric studies exploring the association between lymph nodes metastasis and LM remain limited [4, 17, 20, 24]. Only a few studies specifically examined the exact number of metastasized lymph nodes [20, 27]. Our meta-analysis supports the significance of both the extent and precise count of metastasized lymph nodes in pPTC.

Age, gender, and tumor size were all confirmed as the risk factors of tumor aggressiveness and poor outcomes [20, 37]. Younger age was identified as an independent predictor of LM in a large cohort of 740 pediatric thyroid cancer patients [38] ,though other studies did not find a significant association [39]. Despite this, special consideration should be given to younger patients, as they tend to have more advanced diseases at the time of diagnosis [23]. Regarding gender, while some studies suggested female gender as a risk factor [38], others noted a higher LM prevalence among males when considering the female predisposition to predominance of DTC overall [40, 41]. Alternatively, several studies found no significant association between gender and LM among pediatric DTC patients [24, 39]. Similarly, the predictive value of tumor size is debated [39, 42], with some studies questioning its clinical utility [24]. In our cohort, younger age (< 16 years old), male gender, and tumor size (> 20 mm) were associated with LM, but they were not independent risk factors. Variable sample sizes and methodological variations may account for the inconsistency between prior studies. Consequently, further research is needed to identify and verify the risk factors for pPTC patients.

We also examined postoperative sTg as a predictor of treatment response in LM patients and identified a cutoff of ≤ 307 µg/L, which was associated with better outcomes (ER or BIR). In previous studies, the value of sTg in prognosis (such as distant metastasis, disease recurrence, and/or disease persistence) prediction was typically reported but did not focus on the treatment response of LM in pPTC patients. The optimal cutoff ranges from 2 µg/L to 156 µg/L [24, 30, 39, 43]. Differences in endpoints likely explain this variability. Our findings require external validation before clinical application.

Genetically, pPTC displays distinct features compared with adult disease [44–46]. The BRAFV600E mutation, associated with poor outcomes in 27%-83% of adult [46, 47], is less frequent (≈ 19%) in pediatric patients [48]. Notably, fusion genes such as RET/PTC, RET/NTRK, ETV6-NTRK3 and BRAF fusions (AGK-BRAF and AKAP9-BRAF) were more common in pediatric cases and have been linked to aggressive characteristics [39, 40, 48–50]. This observation is similar to other studies [44, 51–53]. In our study, 22 fusions were detected in our pPTC samples, and RET-NCOA4 gene fusion was identified in two LM patients. This fusion has been reported in high-risk pPTCs and represented a target of RET inhibitors [54]. For example, pratinib was approved by the China National Medical Products Administration in 2022 for inoperable advanced or metastatic RET-fusion thyroid cancer in patients ≥ 12 years old [55].

Radioiodine is the primary treatment for iodine-sensitive LM [56].

However, unlike in adults, predictive factors for treatment outcomes in pediatric patients are less well established due to limited sample sizes. Among the available indicators, postoperative sTg has been linked to an increased likelihood of incomplete response in this population [57]. Children with asymptomatic and stable I131-refractory disease are generally managed through observation, but for those with progressive disease, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) may be considered . Mahajan P. et al. reported lenvatinib’s effectiveness in three children with metastatic PTC resistant to RAI [58]. Furthermore, vandetanin has been validated for safety and efficacy in advanced pediatric medullary thyroid cancer [59]. In our study, one patient with LM showed limited benefit from RAI and experienced severe allergies on sorafenib, emphasizing the need to further investigate genomic and therapeutic investigations in pPTC.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power of our analyses, and larger cohorts are needed to validate these results. Second, the retrospective, single-center design of this study may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Third, the absence of.

whole-exome or whole genome sequencing may have hindered the detection of de novo variants in pPTC. In the future, multicenter prospective large-sample studies with longer follow-up periods will be required to confirm and extend our observations.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that pPTC typically displays more aggressive traits than its adult counterpart. NMLNs > 14 stand as a key independent predictor for LM. Genetically, pPTC shows distinct characteristics, notably a higher prevalence of RET variants and gene fusions. These insights are crucial for clinicians in assessing LM risk and tailoring treatments. However, due to the limited sample size, these findings should be cautiously applied and compared with broader studies and regional data.
