Efficacy and risk analysis of monotherapy and dual immunotherapy in dMMR/MSI-H metastatic colorectal cancer: a meta-analysis based on randomized controlled trials
Authors: Shengqian Wang, Renjun Gu, Jiajie Lu, Shenghao Wang, Sijia Tao, Xiaoyu Zhang, Lerou Chen, Yongming An, Xin Gu, Ziyun Li, Minye Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment landscape for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) exhibiting DNA mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H). However, the comparative efficacy and safety of monotherapy versus dual immunotherapy remain inadequately quantified. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the benefits and risks of these strategies by quantitatively synthesizing data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to inform clinical decision-making.DesignWe systematically searched major databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane, Embase) from inception to September 2025 for relevant RCTs. Outcomes included objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), duration of response (DOR), and adverse events. Statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3 and Stata 18.0.ResultsSix RCTs (1,460 patients) were included. Pooled analysis showed that PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy significantly improved ORR compared with chemotherapy (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.02–2.27, P = 0.04), based on two RCTs (n = 429), and demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events (OR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.08–0.23, P < 0.00001; 2 studies). Dual immunotherapy with nivolumab plus ipilimumab significantly improved ORR versus monotherapy (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.25–2.49, P = 0.001), based on one RCT (n = 582), with a significantly increased toxicity risk (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.22–2.44, P = 0.002). For PFS, KEYNOTE-177 (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.79) and CheckMate 8HW (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48–0.81) showed significant benefits, while SAMCO-PRODIGE 54 and CheckMate 9 × 8 did not demonstrate statistically significant PFS improvement. OS hazard ratios across four studies ranged from 0.74 to 1.03, with all 95% confidence intervals crossing 1.0, indicating no statistically significant survival benefit in any individual study. DOR was consistently longer with immunotherapy, with median not reached for pembrolizumab versus 10.6 months for chemotherapy in KEYNOTE-177.ConclusionsEffect estimates for PFS, OS, and DOR generally favored immunotherapy; however, confidence intervals were wide and statistical significance was not consistently observed across trials. PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy demonstrated a favorable safety profile and a statistically significant improvement in ORR compared with chemotherapy (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.02–2.27, P = 0.04). Dual immunotherapy significantly improved ORR compared with monotherapy (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.25–2.49, P = 0.001), but this benefit was accompanied by significantly increased toxicity (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.22–2.44, P = 0.002) and did not consistently translate into statistically significant PFS or OS benefits across trials. These findings highlight the need for personalized risk-benefit assessment. Future research should focus on biomarker-driven patient selection and strategies to overcome resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16033-y.Keywords: Metastatic colorectal cancer, dMMR/MSI-H, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading global cause of cancer incidence and mortality [1–3]. In the metastatic setting (mCRC), approximately 4–7% of patients exhibit deficient DNA mismatch repair (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status [4–6]. While these tumors are characterized by high mutational burdens and prominent immune cell infiltration, they respond poorly to conventional chemotherapy combined with targeted agents. For example, first-line regimens such as FOLFOX or FOLFIRI yield objective response rates (ORR) of only 31–37% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) of just 7.2–8.8 months [7–9]. These suboptimal outcomes underscore the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed the treatment landscape for dMMR/MSI-H mCRC. Monoclonal antibodies like pembrolizumab and nivolumab block the PD-1/PD-L1 axis to reinvigorate tumor-specific T-cell responses. This mechanism leads to durable antitumor activity [10, 11]. The phase III KEYNOTE-177 trial established pembrolizumab monotherapy as a standard first-line option. In this study, pembrolizumab significantly prolonged PFS compared with chemotherapy (median 16.5 vs. 8.2 months) and yielded a 5-year PFS rate of 34% [12–14]. Consequently, major guidelines from the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) now recommend anti-PD-1 monotherapy as a first-line treatment for this patient population [4, 15, 16].

Despite these advances, clinical challenges remain. Approximately 30% of patients exhibit primary resistance to PD-1 monotherapy, and 15–20% of initial responders develop secondary resistance [13, 15, 17]. Preclinical models suggest that adding a cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) blockade could overcome this resistance. This concurrent blockade enhances intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration while reducing regulatory T-cell populations [18]. Early clinical results support this approach; the CheckMate-142 trial demonstrated promising efficacy with first-line nivolumab plus low-dose ipilimumab, achieving a 2-year PFS rate of 72% [19]. To formally evaluate these strategies, subsequent phase III trials (such as CheckMate-8HW and SAMCO-PRODIGE 54) were designed to compare dual immunotherapy against single-agent ICIs or standard chemotherapy [20, 21].

However, the relative benefits and risks of different ICI regimens, including monotherapy, dual checkpoint blockade, and combinations with chemotherapy, have not been systematically quantified. This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials aims to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of these strategies in patients with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC, with the goal of informing personalized treatment decisions and guiding future research directions.

Selection criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) The article was published in English, and the study type was randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (2) The study subjects were patients diagnosed with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC; all included studies were required to explicitly report the criteria for determining dMMR/MSI-H status, including immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MMR protein expression or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for microsatellite loci. No restrictions were imposed on testing platforms or laboratory sources; however, potential heterogeneity arising from methodological differences was addressed in the limitations Sect. (3) The intervention group received at least one PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, with or without additional anticancer agents (chemotherapy, targeted therapy). The control group received standard systemic therapy (including chemotherapy with or without targeted agents) as per the respective trial design; (4) Outcome measures include progression-free survival (PFS), ORR, adverse events, overall survival (OS), and duration of response (DOR), all of which are either provided in detail or can be calculated; (5) Provide sufficient necessary data, including information on treatment protocols and patient efficacy.

Exclusion criteria (The study design can be excluded by any of the following): (1) Duplicate publications, with the earlier version taking precedence; (2) Non-randomized controlled trial literature, including reviews, meta-analyses, letters to the editor, case reports, commentary articles, conference abstracts, and research proposals; (3) Literature lacking outcome measures relevant to this study; (5) Subjects with other severe comorbidities (e.g., malignancies, HIV infection, or other conditions potentially affecting study assessment); (6) Literature for which the full text could not be obtained.

Study selection and data extraction

Data were extracted by two reviewers independently. Disagreements concerning data extraction were resolved by discussion. WSQ and LJJ independently screened titles, abstracts, and keywords to identify duplicate trials and clearly ineligible studies for exclusion. Subsequently, the full text of the studies was examined to ensure that they met the inclusion criteria. Disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved by a third reviewer. The data information includes: (1) First author, publication year, country, trial phase, and sample size; (2) Age, gender ratio, intervention measures, control group, and follow-up duration; (3) Primary outcome measures include progression-free survival (PFS) and ORR, with secondary outcome measures comprising adverse events, overall survival (OS), and duration of response (DOR).

Quality assessment

The risk of bias of methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB2) Tool [23]. This tool comprised six parts (randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result, and overall bias) and ranked the methodological quality as some concerns, low risk, or high risk. A third party (CLR or WSH) was consulted to assist in the final decision-making process.

The ROB 2 plot was generated using the revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB2) [24]. In addition to the RoB 2 assessment, the overall quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE framework (Table 3), following standard Cochrane methodology.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3 and Stata 18.0. For dichotomous variables, odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. For time-to-event outcomes, hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CI were extracted or derived from study reports. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials (e.g., different comparators, lines of therapy), the random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird) was prespecified as the primary approach for all pooled analyses. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I² > 50% or P < 0.05 considered substantial. Fixed-effect models were used in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness. For efficacy outcomes (ORR), OR > 1 favors the experimental intervention (immunotherapy), indicating a higher likelihood of objective response. For safety outcomes (grade ≥ 3 adverse events), OR < 1 favors the experimental intervention, indicating a lower risk of toxicity. In comparisons of dual immunotherapy versus monotherapy, OR > 1 for adverse events indicates increased toxicity with the combination regimen. All ORs are presented with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Due to inconsistent DOR reporting (e.g., median values without variance measures), quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible; DOR data were summarized descriptively. For pooled analyses with a small number of studies (n < 3), we recognize that pooled estimates are inherently unstable and heterogeneity cannot be reliably quantified. In such cases, we report τ² values and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) to better characterize the uncertainty around the pooled effect estimate, and we explicitly acknowledge the limited power and interpretive constraints of pooling a small number of trials.

Result

Selection of eligible studies

A total of 1,059 records were identified through database searching (PubMed: 313, Web of Science: 246, Embase: 171, Cochrane Library: 80). After removing 249 duplicates by automation tools, 810 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following the screening, 795 records were excluded based on the following predefined eligibility criteria (see Sect. 1.2): wrong comparator (n = 13), where the control group did not receive standard chemotherapy as required; wrong intervention (n = 91), where the experimental arm did not include a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor; not meeting population criteria (n = 501), primarily because studies enrolled patients with microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, non-metastatic disease, or mixed populations without separate data for dMMR/MSI-H; wrong study type (n = 140), including reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, commentaries, conference abstracts, and non-randomized designs; full text unavailable (n = 32); and duplicates identified manually (n = 19). As a result, 14 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility.

During full-text review, 8 articles were excluded due to insufficient outcome data (n = 5) or not being an RCT (n = 3). Ultimately, 6 randomized controlled trials met all inclusion criteria and were included in this meta-analysis. The literature screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 6 studies were included, involving 1460 cases in total, with 814 cases in the treatment group and 646 cases in the control group. Baseline data analysis revealed that the distribution of general patient characteristics across 6 RCTs was uniform, ensuring good comparability. Three studies were Phase III trials, while three were Phase II trials, all of which provided clinical trial numbers. A total of 2 studies compared the efficacy and safety of monoclonal antibodies versus standard chemotherapy regimens, while 2 studies compared the efficacy and safety of two antibodies used in combination versus monoclonal antibodies. Risk of bias among the included RCTs

This study assessed the 6 included randomized controlled trials using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2). The overall risk of bias assessment is as follows: one study was rated as low risk of bias, two studies raised some concerns, and the remaining three studies were rated as high risk of bias. The majority of included trials were open-label designs, which inherently carry elevated risk in domains related to deviations from intended interventions (D2) and measurement of the outcome (D4), particularly for outcomes such as PFS that involve investigator assessment. Among the 6 included trials, one was rated as low risk of bias (André 2025), two raised some concerns (André 2024, Diaz 2022), and three were rated as high risk of bias (Redman 2022, Taïeb 2023, Lenz 2024). High-risk ratings were primarily attributable to early trial closure, small sample sizes, protocol deviations, or substantial non-adherence. Detailed evaluation results are shown in Fig. 2; Table 2.

Meta-analysis

ORR

Pooled analysis of two studies comparing PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies versus standard chemotherapy, involving 429 patients (KEYNOTE-177 and SAMCO-PRODIGE 54), demonstrated a significantly improved ORR with immunotherapy (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.02–2.27, P = 0.04). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%, τ² = 0.00, P = 0.46), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.75 to 3.10. As shown in Fig. 3. Similarly, a study comparing dual immunotherapy (PD-1 + CTLA-4 inhibitors) versus PD-1 monotherapy, comprising 582 patients, showed a significant improvement in ORR with the combination (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.25–2.49, P = 0.001). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%, τ² = 0.00, P = 0.68), and the 95% PI ranged from 0.98 to 3.19, reflecting the uncertainty inherent in pooling a small number of studies. As shown in Fig. 4. In contrast, no statistically significant differences in ORR were observed in other exploratory subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 1), including Jason M. Redman 2022 (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.17–5.77, P = 1.00) and T. André 2024 (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 0.99–2.50, P = 0.06).

Adverse events

Our pooled analysis across two studies comparing PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies with standard chemotherapy (423 patients) found the incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events was significantly lower in the immunotherapy group [OR = 0.14, 95% CI (0.08, 0.23), I² = 0%, P < 0.00001, τ² = 0.00]. As shown in the Fig. 5. In contrast, pooling data from two studies comparing dual immunotherapy versus monotherapy (888 patients) revealed a significantly higher rate of grade ≥ 3 adverse events with the combination [OR = 1.73, 95% CI (1.22, 2.44), P = 0.002]. No heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0%, τ² = 0.00, P = 0.84), and the 95% PI ranged from 0.89 to 3.36. As shown in the Fig. 6. For other subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 2), the incidence of adverse events did not differ significantly in Jason M. Redman’s 2022 [OR = 0.28, 95% CI (0.01, 6.37), P = 0.42, τ² = 0.00], whereas André 2024 favored immunotherapy over chemotherapy [OR = 0.36, 95% CI (0.22, 0.60), P < 0.0001].

PFS

Six studies reported PFS. In two analyses comparing pembrolizumab versus chemotherapy, the KEYNOTE-177 (Diaz 2022) showed significant PFS improvement (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.79, P < 0.001). In contrast, the SAMCO-PRODIGE 54 trial (Taïeb 2023) reported a median PFS of 4.1 months with avelumab versus 6.2 months with chemotherapy (P = 0.03), with no HR provided. For dual immunotherapy versus monotherapy, CheckMate 8HW (André 2025) showed significant PFS improvement (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48–0.81, P < 0.001), whereas CheckMate 9 × 8 (Lenz 2024) did not (HR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.53–1.23, P = 0.3). In the CheckMate 142 IPI-NIVO versus chemotherapy comparison (André 2024), the HR was 0.23 (95% CI: 0.16–0.33, P < 0.001). Other combination (Ree 2024) showed no significant PFS benefit. Detailed data are in Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3.

OS

Four studies reported OS. In KEYNOTE-177 (Diaz 2022), the HR was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.53–1.03), corresponding to P = 0.066, indicating no statistically significant survival benefit. In SAMCO-PRODIGE 54 (Taïeb 2023), HR = 0.94 (95% CI: 0.57–1.53, P = 0.79). For dual versus monotherapy, CheckMate 9 × 8 (Lenz 2024) gave HR = 1.03 (95% CI: 0.64–1.66, P = 0.90). Other comparison (Redman 2022: HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.24–2.48, P = 0.67) also yielded non-significant results. All OS hazard ratios had 95% confidence intervals crossing 1.0, indicating no statistically significant survival benefit in any study. Detailed data in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4.

DOR

Duration of response was reported in 6 studies but could not be meta-analyzed due to heterogeneous reporting formats (e.g., median values without variance estimates or hazard ratios), which limits the validity of cross-study comparisons. In SAMCO-PRODIGE 54, median DOR was 16.7 months with pembrolizumab (range 5.7–33.4 + months) versus 7.3 months (range 4.9–11.9) with chemotherapy. Similar results were observed in other single-agent immunotherapy trials. In dual versus monotherapy comparisons, CheckMate 9 × 8 reported median DOR of 12.9 months with nivolumab plus bevacizumab/chemotherapy versus 9.3 months with control. These descriptive findings should be interpreted with caution given the limitations in data reporting. Detailed data in Supplementary Table 3.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of studies with high risk of bias, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding the three trials rated as high risk (Redman 2022, Taïeb 2023, Lenz 2024). For the comparison of PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy versus chemotherapy, both included studies (Taïeb 2023 and Diaz 2022) were rated as high risk and some concerns, respectively. Excluding the high-risk study left a single trial, precluding a meaningful pooled estimate. For the comparison of dual immunotherapy versus monotherapy, excluding the high-risk study (Lenz 2024) left the low-risk trial (André 2025), which showed consistent ORR benefit (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.17–2.45, P = 0.005) and increased toxicity (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.19–2.43, P = 0.003), supporting the robustness of the primary findings. For adverse events in the monotherapy versus chemotherapy comparison, excluding the high-risk study (Taïeb 2023) left the remaining study (Diaz 2022), which still demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events with immunotherapy (OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.07–0.23, P < 0.00001). Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the pooled ORR estimate for the monotherapy comparison after excluding studies where the control arm included targeted agents (KEYNOTE-177). The pooled ORR estimate remained consistent (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 0.53–2.60, P = 0.69) after exclusion, indicating that the inclusion of targeted agents in the control arm did not substantially alter the overall conclusion.

GRADE assessment of evidence quality

To systematically assess the quality of evidence for each outcome, we performed GRADE evaluations using the Cochrane GRADE approach. As summarized in Table 3, the quality of evidence was moderate for adverse events, low for ORR and PFS, and very low for OS and DOR. The primary reasons for downgrading were risk of bias (due to open-label designs and lack of blinding) and imprecision (small sample sizes, wide confidence intervals, or immature data). No publication bias was detected.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of six RCTs (1,460 patients) evaluated immunotherapy strategies for dMMR/MSI-H mCRC, revealing three primary findings. First, PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy significantly improves ORR and demonstrates a superior safety profile compared to standard chemotherapy. Second, escalating to dual immunotherapy significantly increases ORR over monotherapy, though this benefit comes at the cost of elevated toxicity. Finally, while substantial progression-free survival (PFS) benefits were observed in major trials like KEYNOTE-177 and CheckMate 8HW, overall survival (OS) did not reach statistical significance in any individual study.

The favorable safety profile of PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy supports its continued role as a primary first-line option. While dual immunotherapy enhances ORR - consistent with the synergistic mechanisms of CTLA-4 and PD-1 blockade - this benefit does not consistently translate into significant PFS or OS gains across all trials. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, response durability is a critical determinant of long-term success. For instance, CheckMate 8HW demonstrated sustained responses, whereas CheckMate 9 × 8 showed only marginal PFS improvements. Additionally, the clinical impact of dual therapy may be influenced by biological heterogeneity, such as BRAF mutations or Lynch syndrome status, which warrants further investigation in future stratified analyses.

Safety remains a pivotal concern in treatment selection. The low toxicity of PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy reinforces its suitability for vulnerable populations. Conversely, the significantly higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events with dual immunotherapy raises questions about its therapeutic index. Notably, the use of low-dose ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) in the CheckMate trials appeared to balance efficacy and safety more effectively than higher doses. This suggests that dose optimization remains a key strategy for improving the tolerability of combination regimens without compromising antitumor activity.

Several limitations of this meta-analysis should be considered. Methodological heterogeneity and variable risk of bias existed across the six included trials, complicated by the fact that MSI status determination was not centrally confirmed in all cases. Furthermore, substantial differences in control arm compositions - such as the inclusion of targeted agents in some trials but not others - limit direct cross-study comparisons. The lack of individual patient data also prevented an in-depth exploration of effect modifiers. Finally, the limited follow-up duration in several studies and the presence of crossover designs may have obscured potential long-term survival benefits, resulting in low-quality evidence for OS and DOR.

Moving forward, large-scale, blinded randomized trials with standardized control definitions are essential to definitively establish the comparative effectiveness of dual versus monotherapy. Future research should prioritize patient-level stratification based on molecular biomarkers, such as tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 expression patterns, and gut microbiome composition. Identifying these subsets will be crucial for maximizing clinical benefit. Moreover, elucidating the mechanisms of primary and acquired resistance will be fundamental to developing the next generation of combination strategies for dMMR/MSI-H mCRC.
