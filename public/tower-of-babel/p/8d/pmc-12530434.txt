Examining the implementation of home-based palliative care for patients with end-stage liver disease (HoPE): a protocol for a feasibility study
Authors: Chung Yau Nah, WanFen Yip, Kalaiyarasi Kaliyaperumal, Sek Hwee Yung, Siew Chin Chia, Allyn Hum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCirrhosis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis, or end-stage liver disease (ESLD), have a high symptom burden and an increased mortality risk. Yet, the uptake of palliative care in patients with ESLD remains low and variable. Despite robust evidence of the value of home palliative care in other advanced diseases, this has not been well studied in patients with ESLD. Hence, the primary aim of this study is to explore the implementation, feasibility and acceptability of home palliative care in patients with ESLD and healthcare professionals. We also aim to describe its impact on the quality of life (QoL), mood, symptom burden, caregiver burden and healthcare utilisation of patients with ESLD.Methods and analysisThe study is a single-centre, mixed-methods feasibility study. Eligible patients include those with decompensated liver cirrhosis who are admitted under the gastroenterology and hepatology service and fulfil the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicator Tools (SPICT) criteria. Recruited patients will be followed up by a multi-disciplinary homecare team led by a palliative care physician for 6 months. Both qualitative and quantitative measures will be used to evaluate the primary aim and include the uptake of the service by both eligible patients and physicians. Semistructured interviews with key stakeholder groups will be conducted to determine their perspectives and experiences. Secondary outcome measures include changes in health-related QoL using the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire, depression severity, goals of care discussions, patient symptom burden, caregiver burden and healthcare utilisation.Ethics and disseminationThis study will adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the research ethics committee of the National Healthcare Group (DSRB (Domain Specific Review Board) reference: 2023/00852). Results will be submitted for publication in international peer-reviewed journals.

Introduction

The global burden of cirrhosis has increased, with deaths and disability-adjusted life years rising between 1990 and 2017, culminating in approximately 1.33 million deaths in 2017.1 In Asia, particularly in Singapore, there is a relatively high prevalence of decompensated cirrhosis, at 175–200 per 100 000 population, with an age-standardised death rate of 3.7 per 100 000 population in 2017,1 making it among the top 20 causes of life years lost due to disease.2 Patients with decompensated cirrhosis or end-stage liver disease (ESLD) experience complications such as ascites, overt hepatic encephalopathy and variceal bleeding,3 with a median survival of less than 2 years.4 Only a minority of these patients will ever receive a liver transplantation, which is the only curative option. Patients with ESLD experience a substantial symptom burden, including pain, breathlessness, muscle cramps and sleep disturbances. Symptom prevalence may be as high as 80%, mirroring that of patients with other advanced and chronic conditions like cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and congestive heart failure.5 Additionally, nearly half of these patients exhibit frailty.6 Consequently, the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for patients with ESLD is markedly poor, with a decline that correlates with disease progression.5

Despite its high symptom burden and increased mortality risk, palliative care for patients with ESLD remains underused and integrated too late, typically just days before death.7 Barriers to early intervention include misconceptions where palliative care is misunderstood as solely end-of-life care, inadequate awareness and training for clinicians, unclear referral criteria and competing medical priorities.8 As ESLD progresses, healthcare utilisation, especially hospital admissions and emergency visits, increases significantly.9

Home-based palliative care could offer meaningful benefits by mitigating suffering for patients in the comfort of their own homes while optimising available healthcare resources. A small study found that implementing home palliative care increased the likelihood of dying at home and reduced emergency hospital visits.10 In Singapore, although referrals to home palliative care have grown over time, this expansion has largely been limited to patients with advanced renal disease.11 There are currently no studies examining the delivery and implementation of home palliative care in patients with ESLD. Further research is needed to improve access and uptake for patients with ESLD.

Aim

The primary aim of this study is to describe the implementation process and explore the feasibility and acceptability of home palliative care service among patients with ESLD and healthcare professionals.

The secondary aim is to describe changes in HRQoL of patients with ESLD, patient symptom burden, advance care planning discussions, depression severity, caregiver burden and healthcare utilisation of patients who have received home palliative care.

Methods and analysis

A single-centre, mixed-methods feasibility study methodology will be adopted to explore the (1) perceptions of home palliative care services among key stakeholders; (2) impact of the intervention on patient-related outcomes, acceptance and healthcare utilisation and (3) impact of the intervention on caregiver burden and acceptance of home palliative care among caregivers. Patients with ESLD, their caregivers and healthcare professionals will be involved. The study design, implementation and evaluation will be guided by the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance) framework.12 Patient enrolment started in June 2024 and is expected to end by June 2026.

Target population

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis admitted to the gastroenterology and hepatology service in a tertiary care centre who fulfil the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicator Tools (SPICT)13 criteria will be eligible for the study (table 1). This two-step screening tool first identifies patients with deteriorating health, followed by a second screen for life-limiting conditions. In the case of liver disease, these include events that define decompensated cirrhosis. While the original SPICT criteria include patients with a history of unplanned hospital admission, this study requires at least two unplanned hospitalisations in the previous year, as frequent hospitalisations are linked to increased mortality.14 Patients who develop cancer, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma, during the study may continue their participation. Eligible participants must be able to speak English or Mandarin.

The exclusion criteria include: (1) patients who are aged <21 years at the time of recruitment; (2) inability to provide written informed consent by either the patient or legally appointed representative; (3) patients who are being evaluated for a liver transplant, are on the transplant waitlist or are previous transplant recipients; (4) patients residing in long-term residential care facilities (eg, nursing homes); (5) patients who are on ongoing follow-up with or have received palliative care within 1 year from the date of recruitment; (6) patients with active cancer; (7) patients and/or caregivers not keen for home palliative care service; (8) patients and/or caregivers who are unable to speak English or Mandarin and (9) pregnant women.

Family caregivers of recruited patients will be included in the study if they meet the following criteria: (1) principal caregivers of the patient; (2) involved in the direct care of the patient’s daily and healthcare needs, or ensured provision of care to meet these needs; (3) can provide written informed consent; (4) aged >21 years and (5) able to speak English or Mandarin.

Sample size

Historical data from the same tertiary hospital showed that there were about 17 referrals to the home palliative care service for non-terminal care over a year, from May 2022 to May 2023, from the gastroenterology and hepatology department. Hence, the study team intends to recruit at least 20 patients in this feasibility study. A target of 20 patients allows the study team to focus on refining interventions in homecare with the use of assessment tools, while examining the perceptions of key stakeholders (ie, healthcare professionals, patients and caregivers) and the impact of the intervention.

Intervention—home palliative care service

The study team will engage recruited patients in basic palliative care discussions, covering disease trajectory, potential complications, management strategies and the integration of palliative care into their overall treatment. Patients will also receive an overview of home palliative care services, with these discussions occurring in the inpatient setting or outpatient clinics, with family caregivers included whenever possible.

Patients with ESLD who subsequently enrol in the study will receive specialist palliative care provided by a palliative homecare service comprising specialist palliative care physicians, nurses, medical social workers, music, art and physical therapists who provide physical, psychosocial and spiritual support (figure 1) as a multi-disciplinary team. The palliative homecare team will conduct the following interventions for patients based on needs assessment: (1) identification and management of symptoms, (2) education of the patient and/or caregiver on the disease process of cirrhosis and its expected trajectory, (3) goals of care discussions with the patient and caregivers via advance care planning, (4) anticipatory discussions surrounding acute events, such as acute variceal bleeding, to determine future care and treatment plans and (5) assessment of caregiver burden and provision of support such as other home-based services (eg, home nursing or home-based respite care).

The palliative homecare team will provide regular visits through scheduled home visits and video consults, adjusting frequency based on patient needs. As symptoms worsen—such as pain from ascites or confusion due to hepatic encephalopathy—visit frequency will increase to support care at home. Emergency visits may also take place outside of office hours; such support is available throughout the week. The follow-up period will last for 6 months. Patient care under the home palliative care service is fully funded by the Ministry of Health in Singapore. During these visits, various assessment tools will be used to identify needs, which include the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS) to assess symptom burden, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) to assess depression and the Zarit Burden Interview-12 (ZBI-12) to assess caregiver burden. A symptom guide comprising both pharmacological and non-pharmacological management of common symptoms seen in ESLD will be developed for the palliative homecare team as a reference.

The study team, consisting of healthcare professionals from departments of gastroenterology and hepatology and palliative care, will conduct engagement sessions to raise awareness about the integration of palliative care into ESLD management. These sessions, targeted at physicians in the gastroenterology and hepatology department, aim to highlight the benefits of incorporating palliative care, addressing key issues such as symptom burden, basic and specialist palliative care integration and home-based palliative services beyond tertiary hospitals.

Additionally, the team will lead an interactive session for the palliative homecare team, focusing on the ESLD’s disease trajectory, complexities, common clinical symptoms (eg, abdominal pain and nutritional needs) and patient and family needs. Discussions will also explore both pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical interventions (figure 1). Throughout the study, the palliative homecare team will maintain communication with patients’ primary physicians to coordinate care and address emerging needs.

During the study period, patients may still return to the hospital for scheduled outpatient visits in the specialist clinic. Patients may be admitted to the hospital for further care if required, such as in the event of acute variceal bleeding, where care at home may prove challenging.

Data collection

Outcome measures are both quantitative and qualitative, guided by the RE-AIM framework.

Quantitative measures

Reach

To describe the number of patients who are willing to enrol in the home palliative care service, several quantitative indicators will be measured. These include the uptake of basic palliative care discussions by eligible patients, the representativeness of the sociodemographic and clinical profile of patients who enrolled in the home palliative care service and the overall uptake rate of the home palliative care service.

Effectiveness

Objective outcome measures will be performed at the index home visit and at 3 and 6 months into the intervention, either in person or via a teleconferencing platform. The Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ), which is a liver disease-specific instrument that measures HRQoL across six domains, will be used.15 There are also validated versions of the CLDQ in Mandarin,16 which may be applied to Mandarin-speaking patients in our study. Patient symptom burden will be measured using the ESAS.17 It assesses both physical and psychological symptoms and has been widely used in studies involving patients with cirrhosis.18 19 The frequency of documented goals of care discussions and/or advance care planning will be monitored. Patients will also be screened for depression using the PHQ-9,20 with scores≥10 indicating a high likelihood of major depression.21 Lastly, caregiver burden among caregivers of these patients will be monitored using the ZBI-12, which is a 12-item scale that classifies burden as mild, moderate or high.22

The healthcare utilisation of patients will also be monitored during the study period of 6 months from enrolment into the study, and include: number of home palliative care visits, number of visits to the emergency department, number and duration of all unplanned and elective hospital admissions, reason(s) for admission, 30-day re-admission frequency, number of visits to the outpatient specialist gastroenterology and hepatology clinic, number of and reasons for all visits to medical facilities other than the acute hospital (eg, private medical clinic or government subsidised polyclinics) initiated by the home palliative care team, time from recruitment to death and referrals to other home-based care services, as well as concordance between site of care and place of death.

Adoption

To describe the adoption of the home palliative care service into the patient’s management plan, the study team will track attendance at the engagement session with gastroenterology and hepatology physicians. The uptake rate of gastroenterology and hepatology physicians, who refer patients to the home palliative care service, will be monitored.

Implementation

Quantitative measures include tracking attendance at engagement sessions with the palliative homecare team conducted by the study team, the time from application to acceptance by the palliative homecare team and the attrition rate, defined as the proportion of enrolled patients who dropped out of the study at 3 and 6 months.

Reviews will be conducted by the study team with the gastroenterology and hepatology department and the palliative homecare team on a 3-monthly basis to understand the recruitment of patients and their caregivers and to highlight and discuss any difficulties encountered. The study team will also meet regularly to highlight problems and issues encountered while implementing the study protocol and discuss potential solutions.

Qualitative measures

Implementation

Qualitative interviews, including in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) will be conducted with key stakeholders of the intervention to explore their views and perspectives on the implementation process. Specifically, IDIs will be conducted with patients and their caregivers, while FGDs will be conducted with healthcare professionals from the gastroenterology and hepatology department and the palliative homecare team. Participants for the IDIs and FGDs will be purposively sampled. These discussions will be facilitated by an experienced health services researcher, guided by the interview guides (online supplemental appendix tables 2 and 3), while ensuring consistency across interviews. To avoid interviewer bias, interviews will be conducted by a researcher who is not involved in the home palliative care service. This study aims to conduct four to five FGDs with 4–6 participants per discussion for each group of healthcare professionals. To minimise the possible dominant effect of one or two participants and to facilitate a free discussion, researchers will ensure job grade and role homogeneity within each group. Given the target sample size of 20 for this feasibility study, and bearing in mind budget and manpower constraints, the study team also aims to conduct at least eight IDIs with patients who have received home palliative care and their caregivers.

Maintenance

Qualitative interviews that have been previously described will be conducted with key stakeholders of the intervention to explore the resources necessary to ensure the sustainability of home palliative care for patients with ESLD.

Data analysis

Reporting of the findings will be guided by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials checklist.

Quantitative measures

This study will use SPSS (V.30) software to analyse quantitative outcome measures. Findings will be presented primarily through descriptive statistics and will assess baseline and postintervention levels of symptom burden, QoL, depression scores and caregiver burden among patients. A paired t-test will be conducted to evaluate changes in secondary outcomes at specific time points postintervention. However, as the study is exploratory, it is not designed to detect statistically significant differences. Patients who do not complete the outcome measures at predetermined intervals will be excluded from the paired t-test analysis.

Qualitative measures

The interview transcripts from the IDIs and FGDs will be reviewed by the study team, with the Braun and Clarke23 six-phase analytical process approach used to guide the analysis. The qualitative analysis will be led by a researcher within the study team who has extensive experience in performing qualitative research. The study will employ an inductive-deductive approach for data analysis. First, researchers will review the transcripts independently of each other. Regular meetings will subsequently be held to refine emerging codes through consensus. Coding categories will be shaped by existing literature and participant data, ensuring rigour. After initial coding, the coded data will be grouped into themes. A recursive review process will assess how themes align with the study’s goals, refining them further. Ultimately, the team will connect subthemes and themes into a coherent narrative that accurately represents the study’s findings.24

Ethics and dissemination

This study will adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the research ethics committee of the National Healthcare Group (DSRB (Domain Specific Review Board) reference: 2023/00852). Written informed consent will be obtained from patients, caregivers and interviewees before the enrolment and conduct of the study.

Results of the study will be presented at scientific meetings and submitted for publication in international peer-reviewed journals.

Patient and public involvement

It was not possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this feasibility study. However, their experiences and perception of the home palliative service, gathered through semistructured interviews, will guide future improvements in the service and shape the development of larger, prospective studies we plan to undertake.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study examining the feasibility and implementation of home palliative care service specifically for patients with ESLD. The importance of palliative care in patients with ESLD has been increasingly recognised in the last decade and has been acknowledged by major gastroenterology and hepatology societies in the form of guidelines and recommendations on its provision.19 Palliative care interventions in patients with ESLD have led to improvements in physical and psychological symptom control, enhanced QoL, lower rates of hospital readmissions, reduced procedure burden and decreased costs of care.7 However, palliative care in patients with ESLD remains underused. Barriers to the uptake of palliative care are complex and multi-factorial in nature and include prognostic uncertainty, perceived incompatibility of transplantation and palliative care, inadequate training at undergraduate and postgraduate levels and provider reluctance to initiate palliative care discussions.25 Besides a low level of awareness, many patients equate palliative care with end-of-life care.26 The engagement session with physicians from the gastroenterology and hepatology department and palliative care discussions with patients and/or their caregivers hopes to address these barriers and misconceptions surrounding the utility of palliative care in liver cirrhosis. Additionally, utilisation of palliative care services among patients with ESLD predominantly occurs in hospital settings, such as inpatient wards or outpatient clinics, with limited transition to community-based support.27 Home palliative care services address this gap by significantly improving the accessibility of palliative care, helping to ensure a seamless transition of care from tertiary-based palliative care services. Home palliative care services improve physical and psychological symptom burden, lower costs, rationalise resource utilisation, improve end-of-life care and narrow the incongruence between preferred and actual place of demise among the non-cirrhotic population with other advanced diseases.28 Additionally, home palliative care services support care in a familiar environment, with the intentional inclusion of family members in the care process.11 Besides enhancing QoL for patients, provision of caregiver support is also a core element of home-based palliative care which can potentially reduce strain for caregivers who often experience substantial psychological, physical, social and financial burden.22 Despite its potential, there is currently limited understanding of the role of home-based palliative care for patients with ESLD, and the specific elements of the care that benefit the patient and family. Perceptions and acceptability of such care among key stakeholders, including patients and healthcare professionals, remain poorly explored.

Strengths of the study

Much of the research surrounding palliative care and liver cirrhosis has been conducted in Western populations in the United States, Europe and Australia,7 with limited understanding of perceptions of patients and healthcare professionals on palliative care utilisation in multi-ethnic Asian populations. It is important to recognise that Asian cultural practices and norms and attitudes towards end-of-life issues are fundamentally different from those of the Western population.29 This feasibility study seeks to examine the barriers and facilitators to the implementation of palliative care in patients with ESLD and will be the first conducted in a multi-ethnic Asian population. Exploration of the perceptions experienced by patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals may help shape care and resources and support the design of strategies to encourage palliative care utilisation. Importantly, findings from this study are expected to guide healthcare professionals in designing outreach materials that emphasise the benefits of home palliative care, ultimately increasing its uptake and improving outcomes for patients and families.

Our study uses the SPICT criteria, an internationally recognised tool for early identification of patients with palliative care needs, demonstrating accuracy in predicting 12-month mortality among patients with cirrhosis.30

Potential limitations of the study

This study is a feasibility study with a small sample size of 20 patients with eight IDIs with patients and their caregivers. While it may be difficult to predict if thematic saturation will be reached with this sample size, it does allow researchers to comprehensively assess the implementation of home palliative care for patients with ESLD and identify challenges which can be addressed.

With these findings, the team plans a larger case-control study, where the comparative control arm will measure patient symptom burden, QoL, depression severity and healthcare utilisation. We also aim to conduct a greater number of IDIs and FGDs with the relevant stakeholders in future studies.

Presently, the study only recruits English-speaking or Mandarin-speaking patients, which may introduce selection bias. Nevertheless, this reflects the majority of the patient population, with 75.9% of the Singapore population being Chinese.31 In addition, the country has a high overall literacy rate of 97.6% among those aged 15 years and older,32 with only a marginal decline to 86.44% in those aged above 65 years.33 Taken together, these factors suggest that any resulting bias is likely to be minimal.

In summary, the adoption of palliative care in patients with ESLD remains low and variable, although patients with cirrhosis have an increased mortality risk and a high symptom burden, particularly those with decompensated cirrhosis. Even with robust evidence of the value of home palliative care in other advanced diseases, this has not been as well studied in patients with ESLD. Insights gained from this study are expected to aid the development of an integrated model of care with earlier implementation of palliative care in patients with ESLD.
