Single center experience in surgical treatment of traumatic acute pericardial tamponade: a consecutive series of 12 cases
Authors: Jingfeng Zhu, Shuang Li, Jianchao Liu, Yijun Shi, Aizhong Shao, Guowen Ding, Yangyong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

The pericardium is a tough, leathery-like sac wrapping around the heart, which cannot expand quickly. The pressure increases greatly if the intrapericardial fluid is more than 80 to 120 ml, resulting in sudden compression of the heart. The increased intrapericardial pressure affects atrial and ventricular wall, causing increased central venous pressure and increased heart rates as a compensatory mechanisms to deal with the falling blood pressure, thus the classic Beck’s triad (muffled heart sounds, raised central venous pressure, and hypotension) can be observed [1]. Thoracic trauma accounts for 25% of traumatic deaths, and primary cardiac injury is a major contributing cause [2]. Injury to the heart or one of the great vessels close to it causes intrapericardial blood accumulate rapidly, resulting in ACT (acute cardiac tamponade) [3].

ACT is a catastrophic condition potentially fatal due to obstructive shock [4]. Some patients present symptoms of ACT, but the blood pressure and heart rate still can be maintained with vasoactive drugs. However, delays in diagnosis and treatment can lead to catastrophic outcomes. Two main methods to treat ACT are pericardiocentesis and surgical pericardiotomy. Pericardiocentesis was reported to successfully manage ACT patients [5]. However, if inadequate decompression is observed or severe bleeding manifests, pericardiocentesis serves as a temporizing measure prior to definitive surgical intervention [6]. Moreover, surgical pericardiotomy was associated with significantly lower mortality rates compared to percutaneous pericardiocentesis [7]. In ACT patients, rapid surgical pericardiotomy is important for lifesaving [8]. Rapid surgical pericardiotomy can relieve pericardial pressure immediately while controlling of hemorrhage. However, the literature in surgical treatment of ACT is rare.

This study retrospectively analyzed 12 cases of ACT caused by different types of trauma, which were successfully treated in our center. The process in managing different types of ACT were summarized to share the knowledge of diagnosis and treatment in cardiac injuries to improve the overall survival of these patients.

Methods

The Ethics Committee of Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University approved this study, and individual consent was waived for this retrospective analysis. From 2013 to 2023, 12 cases of ACT treated by surgical pericardiotomy were obtained, and cardiac tamponade caused by open heart surgery was excluded. The clinical data were reviewed and collected from electronic medical record in our hospital database. The following information of ACT patients were collected: age, gender, mechanism of injury, diagnostic method, time from diagnosis to operation, location of incision, pericardial blood volume, location of cardiac rupture, time of operation, CPB support, time of hospital stay and final outcome.

Results

Basic characteristics of patients

The basic characteristics of 12 cases of ACT patients were listed in Table 1. There were 9 (75%) males and 3 (25%) females. The average age was 45.0 years (Range, 29 to 72 years). There were 5 cases of ACT caused by traffic trauma. And 4 cases resulted from sharp instrument injuries, including 3 cases of knife stab wounds,1 case of acupuncture in the right anterior chest wall. 2 cases of the knife stab wounds located in the right chest, and 1 case in the left chest. 3 cases of ACT were caused by iatrogenic injuries, including 2 cases caused by mistakenly puncture during cardiac catheter arrhythmia ablation, and 1 case of a pericardiocentesis drainage tube accidentally inserted into the left ventricle (Fig. 1). The preferred diagnostic method was CT scan. 9 cases of this cohort were diagnosed by CT (Fig. 2). However, 2 cases of ACT caused by iatrogenic injury were confirmed by angiography and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and transferred to operation room immediately without CT scan. 1 case was diagnosed by ultrasonocardiograph (UCG), and 1 case by UCG combined with CT.

Surgical approach

Once the diagnosis of ACT was confirmed, the patient was immediately transferred to the operation room for urgent surgery. The incision was determined according to the different types of injuries. And the median sternotomy was preferred, which facilitated the exposure of different parts of heart and rapid establishment of extracorporeal circulation if needed. If the location of the injury site can be identified, the original wound path would be selected for anterolateral thoracotomy. During the operation, vasoactive drugs were prepared before opening the pericardium, and a small pericardial incision was made to release intrapericardial pressure slowly. Vasoactive drugs were used according to the changes of the blood pressure. The blood clot was cleared out carefully to prevent massive bleeding. Once the laceration point was found, compression hemostasis should be used and monofilament sutures with pledgets enforcement in the interrupted or running fashions was used to close the laceration of heart or large vessels. Extracorporeal circulation support can be used if the injured tear cannot be clearly exposed. All patients were admitted to ICU (Intensive care unit) for at least 48 h to observe delayed cardiac rupture and other complications.

Surgical details and outcome

The surgical details and outcomes are listed in Table 2. The average time from diagnosis to operation was 36 min (rang 20 to 115 min). 1 case of prolonged time from diagnosis to operation was 115 min due to knife stab wound, and the CT showed small amount of air in the pericardial cavity (Fig. 3). The patient did not show symptoms of ACT, however the patient developed typical symptoms of ACT, and emergency operation was performed, and injured pericardial vessel was found resulting in ACT. Median sternotomy was performed in 9 cases, 2 cases of right anterolateral incision, and 1 case of left anterolateral incision. The average blood volume in the pericardial cavity was 321 ml (range, 200 to 500 ml). The different locations of injured heart and vessel were listed in Table 2. The mean operation time was 125 min (range, 80 to 280 min). 11 cases did not require cardiopulmonary bypass support, however because of the difficulty of exposure of the surgical field, old age and poor cardiac function, 1 case of operation was performed with the cardiopulmonary bypass support. The average time of hospital stay was 18.8 days (range, 7 to 41 days). All 12 patients were discharged without complications.

Discussion

ACT is fatal in trauma patients with chest or upper abdominal injuries, which has complex medical circumstances and a variety of etiologies. Patients may deteriorate unexpectedly in a short period of time, and cardiac arrest could occur under this situation, which makes it tricky to manage the disease [9]. Rupture of the cardiac chamber due to blunt cardiac trauma accounts for 36–65% of deaths at autopsy [10]. Only a small portion of ACT patients survived to hospital and were managed promptly and successfully. Therefore, all team member of the trauma should have a comprehensive understanding of the diagnosis and treatment of cardiac injuries to improve overall survival in this group of trauma patients. We presented 12 cases of ACT patients treated successfully and discharged without any complications at our center.

A series of tests may be required to confirm the diagnosis of ACT. Some patients with occult or penetrating cardiac trauma are generally stable, and these patients frequently have no distinct presentation on ECG and laboratory testing and do not exhibit the classic Beck’s triad, thus, diagnosis of ACT remains a challenge. Trauma patients suffer from unexplainable hypotension should be suspected of having ACT. A FAST (focused assessment with sonography in trauma) is an important method for the emergency diagnosis of ACT, particularly in hemodynamically unstable patients, but it relies on the operator’s experience and the patient’s overall condition, and may pose some diagnostic challenges if the patient has a combination of subcutaneous emphysema or soft tissue swelling in the chest or abdomen [11]. Furthermore, FAST is not quite available in most of emergency room in China, as well as the professional staff. CT is now frequently performed as the recommended examination method for trauma due to the widespread usage of CT, and CT scans are advised for patients who have multiple injuries from traffic accidents without typical Beck’s triad signs [12]. Moreover, CT scans also detect injuries to organs such as mediastinum, viscera, and large vessels in addition to ACT, and are particularly useful for patients with multiple traumas. However, compared with FAST, the drawback of CT scan requires to transit patients from emergency room to CT room, which may increase the risk of cardiac arrest. In this group, 9 (75%) cases was confirmed with the diagnosis of ACT by CT scan without any adverse events during transit. Thus, CT scan is a safe and sound method of examination if patient is managed appropriately.

Once the diagnosis of ACT was confirmed, the trauma team was notified and the patient was transited to operation room immediately from the CT room. We preferred the operation room based on the full range of equipment and professional nurses, meanwhile the patient vital signs could be maintained by professional anesthesiologist. Severe hypotension and cardiac arrest are common following induction of general anaesthesia in ACT patients [10], however, this can be managed uneventfully by the experienced anesthesiologists. In this series of cases, the average time from diagnosis to operation was 36 min. However, 1 case of prolonged time was 115 min due to knife stab wound, and the CT showed small amount of air in the pericardial cavity. Thus the patient was transferred to ICU for closed observation, and the trauma team was prepared to act immediately upon the observation of any signs of ACT.

Given the requirement of open-chest heart massage, thoracotomy was the fastest, simplest lifesaving procedure [8]. And enlarging original chest wound, right or left lateral thoracotomy facilitates the detection of cardiac injuries as well as other injuries. In this series, two patients with a right thoracic knife-stab injuries underwent a right anterolateral incision, and one patient with a pericardial drainage tube mistakenly inserted into the left ventricle underwent a left anterolateral incision. However, a median sternotomy is the standard procedure to assess cardiac injuries in hemodynamically stable patients [13], meanwhile CPB could be conveniently established if needed. Moreover, median sternotomy facilitate the exploration of every part of the heart and the great vessels closed to it, in particular with the blunt chest or upper abdominal trauma patients with unknown lacerated locations. 9 cases of patients underwent median sternotomy and one of them required CPB support. The reason was that the patient had poor preoperative cardiac function, and the rupture was located at the top of the right atrium and aortic root, which restricted the visualization of operative field. However, under the support of CPB, this patient was managed uneventfully.

For ACT patients with extremely unstable vital signs, which cannot be maintained by vasoactive drugs, surgical pericardiotomy should be performed rapidly to release intrapericardial pressure. Direct finger pressure with the surgeon’s non-dominant hand on the cardiac wound can transiently control the ongoing bleeding [14]. However, if the vital signs are stable, gradually expansion the pericardial incision and searching for the cardiac injuries are preferred procedure [13]. The accumulated blood should be drained slowly to avoid the sudden drop of intrapericardial pressure, which may result in dramatically increasing of blood pressure. The blood clot should be removed slowly to facilitate the finding of lacerated location of the heart or great vessels. However, if the bleeding is massive, causing the difficulty in exploration of the cardiac injuries, a large amount of gauze should be used to fill the pericardial cavity to transiently control the ongoing bleeding and a preliminary determination of the bleeding site according to the color of the newly gushed blood, such as the body vein, the right heart system or the pulmonary vein, or the left heart system, could be made, then the gauze could be gradually removed to locate the injuries of the heart or the great vessels close to it.

The rate of iatrogenic ACT caused by cardiac interventional procedures, such as atrial fibrillation ablation, permanent heart rhythm devices, left atrial appendage closure, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, varies from 0.089% to 4.8%, and iatrogenic ACT is associated with adverse prognosis, including high in-hospital mortality and need for surgical intervention [15]. The possibility of ACT is usually realized by cardiologists when they detect contrast medium extravasations or notice sudden changes in vital signs [16]. Our experience with iatrogenic ACT was that the interventional catheter, guidewire or drain catheter should be kept in the original position until it could be removed under direct surgical vision. On the one hand, it can block the tear to reduce bleeding, on the other hand, it is conducive to the rapid intraoperative search for cardiovascular laceration and timely hemostasis. All three patients in this group were operated on urgently with the catheter or guidewire in place, and intraoperative ruptures were found quickly and all were successfully treated.

Limitaiton

However, this article also has many limitations. Firstly, during the ten-year period, there were only 12 patients, which is a relatively small number of cases considering the large time span. Secondly, there is no comparison with patients treated by pericardiocentesis or conservative measures. However, we plan to collect relevant patient data soon and compare them so that we can present better results.

Conclusion

ACT has different etiologies and complex conditions, and is a catastrophic disease that can be fatal. However, rapid diagnosis, maintenance of vital signs and surgical pericardiotomy are lifesaving methods to treat ACT patients.
