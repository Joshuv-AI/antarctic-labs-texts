Investigating the mechanism behind warmed-over flavor in prepared pork products during their shelf life using GC-IMS
Authors: Tiantian Zhang, Shuaishuai Dai, Ben Niu, Chaoyi Shen, Huizhi Chen, Ruiling Liu, Hangjun Chen, Weijie Wu, Haiyan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeadspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) were used to investigate the potential reaction mechanism of the oxidation process that alters the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and fatty acid content of prepared pork products during storage. GC-IMS detected a total of 38 volatile flavor compounds, of which 16 were identified as key differential markers through orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with variable importance in projection (VIP) values ≥1. Correlation analysis with 10 fatty acids showed that oxidative decomposition of oleic and linoleic acids increased the content of hexanal, glutaraldehyde, butanal, and (E)-2-heptenal, resulting in a warmed-over flavor (WOF). These results demonstrate that lipid oxidation is the primary driver of flavor deterioration in prepared pork products. This study provides critical insights into the formation mechanism of WOF and establishes a theoretical foundation for developing effective strategies to control flavor loss in meat products.Keywords: Warmed-over flavor, Gas chromatography – Ion mobility spectrometry, Orthogonal projections to latent structures - discriminant analysis, Fatty acid oxidation, Prepared pork products

Introduction

With the rapid development of economic conditions, the consumption structure is changing, and home-cooked meals are gradually being replaced with commercially prepared premade dishes (Torstensson et al., 2021). Prepared dishes refer to foods that are derived from edible products in the fields of agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, fisheries, and other fields, which are characterized by their ease of processing and consumption (Yu, Liu, et al., 2024; Yu, Zhang, et al., 2024). Previously, prepared dishes were mainly used in the food and beverage industry. Stimulated by the pandemic, new consumer groups have emerged for prepared dishes and the demand for household consumption has gradually increased. Among these, meatballs, a traditional Chinese dish, have been industrialized because of their nutritious taste and simple processing. However, when meat products are reheated, they are prone to develop an undesirable flavor known as warmed-over flavor (WOF) (Parvin et al., 2020). The occurrence of WOF negatively affects consumer acceptability, thereby impairing product quality and diminishing purchase intention(Chen et al., 2024). Elucidating the formation mechanism of WOF in prepared pork products is therefore essential for improving flavor quality and meeting consumer expectations.

The aroma is a critical component of flavor perception and a primary determinant of consumer acceptance of meat products. Flavor formation is a complex process arising from multiple chemical reactions, including lipolysis, lipid oxidation, protein degradation, and the Maillard reaction (Bicas & Rodriguez-Amaya, 2021). Among these, lipids play a fundamental role as they undergo oxidation and degradation to generate key precursors of aroma compounds (Fu et al., 2022). Lipid oxidation products (such as aldehydes, ketones and alcohols) have high volatility and low thresholds, which significantly contribute to the specific flavor of meat products during the maturation and drying processes (Tatiyaborworntham et al., 2022). Importantly, the extent of lipid oxidation and the specific volatile compounds generated are heavily influenced by the composition of fatty acids, particularly their degree of unsaturation, which dictates the total yield of VOCs during oxidation (Beldarrain et al., 2022).

Moreover, fat oxidation is strongly related to the WOF generated during the reheating of meat products. WOF was evident in prepared pork products after 48 h of storage at 4 °C, and many studies have described them as similar to an undesirable flavor of flaxseed oil, stale, or wet cardboard, as well as bitter and sour tastes (Dang et al., 2024). Numerous studies indicated that fat oxidation is the main pathway for WOF formation (Liu et al., 2024), and that lipid oxidation products such as hexanal, octanal, nonanal, (E, E)2,4-decanal, 1-octen-3-ol and 2-pentyl furan are indicators of WOF formation. (Zhao et al., 2024) studied the flavor of duck meat after storage and reheating and identified nine characteristic volatile odor compounds: 2-methyl butyraldehyde, valeraldehyde, (E)-2-octenal, (E)-2-nonenal, octanal, heptanal, hexanal, and 2-pentyl furan, which are primarily produced by the oxidative degradation of linoleic acid, oleic acid, and arachidonic acid. (Zhao et al., 2022) investigated the fatty acid content and reheating VOCs of different pork breeds and demonstrated that C16:1, C18:1, and C18:2 could be essential fatty acids involved in the formation of WOF.

Traditional flavor detection methods include gas Chromatography (GC)) and gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS), gas chromatography- ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) is an affordable, non-destructive, and rapid detection technologies that can separate and effectively identify VOCs. Its color profile shows the flavors of different samples. In recent years, it has been increasingly used in food flavor testing. It has been applied to meat adulteration, the analysis and detection of flavor compounds, and the identification of oil oxidation. (Li et al., 2022) determined that the VOCs of boiled salted ducks could be analyzed using GC–MS and GC-IMS. The findings indicated that the VOCs detected by the GC-IMS technique were numerous and better suited for identifying key characteristic aroma compounds. (Cui et al., 2024) developed a GC-IMS methodology for the detection of both primary and secondary oxidation products in rapeseed oil, offering an enhanced reflection of the VOCs present, thus facilitating more effective monitoring of oil oxidation.

This study aimed to investigate the characteristics of VOCs in WOF and their formation pathways in prepared pork products subjected to refrigeration and subsequent reheating, a consumption scenario that is both common and scientifically underexplored in meat flavor research. Therefore, prepared pork products at different storage times (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 days) were used in this study. The quality of the prepared pork products stored for varying durations was examined by measuring the thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) values, volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), pH, and sensory evaluation scores. GC–MS and GC-IMS were used to qualitatively evaluate the fatty acid composition and VOCs of the prepared pork products. Furthermore, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) combined with variable importance for projection (VIP) was employed to identify characteristic flavor markers and key discriminatory volatiles that significantly contribute to WOF development during storage and reheating. The findings provide novel insights into the formation mechanisms of WOF in prepared pork products and establish a theoretical foundation for developing targeted strategies to suppress off-flavor formation and improve the flavor quality of meat products.

Materials and methods

Materials

White pig hind leg belly, sourced from Hema Fresh (Hangzhou), served as the raw material. The meat was refrigerated within 24 h post-slaughter and transported to the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences' Institute of Food Science's SUPOR Intelligent Processing Joint Lab. Salt, white sugar, chicken essence, monosodium glutamate, corn starch, high-gluten flour, white pepper powder and arowana refined first-class soybean oil were obtained from a local market (Hangzhou, China) and were all food-grade. Chloroform, methanol, sodium chloride, ammonium thiocyanate, ferrous chloride, barium chloride, trichloroacetic acid, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, n-heptane, boron trifluoride-methanol, and sodium sulfate anhydrous (all analytical pure) were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Cooking processing

Approximately, 13.8, 0.04, 0.55, 0.55, 0.39, 2.58, 3.94, and 0.04 % of eggs, salt, monosodium glutamate, chicken essence, sugar, starch, flour, and pepper, respectively, were added per 100 g meat. The meatballs were shaped in the meat grinder, each weighing 20 ± 0.5 g. All subsequent experiments were performed in triplicate.

The following process was used: (1) raw meat was cleaned and fresh pork was washed with water for 15 s; (2) Trimming and cutting: raw meat was cut into small pieces (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm) before putting it into the meat grinder to prevent the meat from being ground for a long time because machine heating affects the quality of the meat; (3) Auxiliary materials, eggs, salt, sugar, chicken essence, monosodium glutamate, cornstarch, high-gluten flour, and white peppers were added; (4) The meat was chopped, mixed, and homogenized using a Midea MJ-LZ25Easy225 meat grinder. The homogenization process consisted of 3 min at 3000 r/min followed by 1 min at 1800 r/min; (5) The prepared meat was shaped into meatballs with a mass of approximately 20 g and a diameter of approximately 2 cm, the formed meatballs are immediately fried in a 170 °C oil for 3 min, and the oil was removed and cooled to 28 ± 2 °C; (6) The cooled prepared pork products were kept sealed and refrigerated at 4 °C(Xu et al., 2022).

TBARS

TBARS levels were determined using the method of (Luo et al., 2021). A sample of 2.00 g was added to a test tube along with 3 mL of the TBA solution and 17 mL of the TCA-HCl solution. The mixture was vortexed and then boiled in a water bath for 30 min. After cooling, 5 mL of chloroform was added, and the solution was centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 532 nm.

TVB-N content

The volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content was measured according to the method of (Dong et al., 2021). Meat samples were chopped, dissolved in water, and then filtered. The filtrate was transferred to a Nessler flask, and the absorbance of the solution was recorded at 420 nm, using water as the blank.

pH

The pH value was measured using the method described by (Huang et al., 2022) with slight modifications. The assay was conducted using a pH meter by mixing 3.00 g of a homogeneous sample with 45 mL of boiled distilled water, shaking for 0.5 min, and left to stand for 30 min, after which the filtrate was collected, and the pH value of the sample was measured using a pH meter. Five replicates were performed for each sample.

Fatty acid composition

The fatty acid composition was analyzed according to a previously established method (Zhang et al., 2017) with modifications. Briefly, total lipids were extracted from 5 g of each sample using a chloroform:methanol (2:1, v/v) solution. After homogenization, the mixture was maintained at 45 °C with continuous shaking for 2 h and then filtered. To facilitate phase separation, 10 mL of brine was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 3000×g for 15 min. The lower organic phase was collected and concentrated under vacuum at 45 °C to obtain purified fat.

Fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were prepared by transesterification. Specifically, the extracted fat was refluxed with 7 mL of 2 % sodium hydroxide in methanol in a water bath. Then, 5 mL of boron trifluoride-methanol complex, 10 mL of n-hexane, and 25 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution were added sequentially. After stratification, the upper n-hexane layer containing FAMEs was collected and dehydrated with 5 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate.

The FAMEs were analyzed using an Agilent 7000D GC–MS system (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Separation was performed on a DB-23 capillary column (25 m × 200 μm × 0.25 μm) with the injector temperature set at 250 °C. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature 60 °C held for 3 min, increased to 180 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 2 min, then raised to 200 °C at 2 °C/min and held for 2 min, and finally increased to 230 °C at 2 °C/min and held for 2 min. The mass spectrometer was operated with an ion source temperature of 280 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, and mass scan range of m/z 40–500. MRM scanning mode, and the characterization was carried out by comparing the retention time with that of the standard. Fatty acids were quantified according to the chromatographic peak areas.

Analysis of volatile flavor compounds

The VOCs were analyzed using a Flavor Spec® GC-IMS instrument (Shandong Hai Neng Science Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Approximately 2.00 g of each prepared pork sample was sealed in a 20 mL headspace vial and incubated at 60 °C for 15 min to allow volatile equilibration. Subsequently, 500 μL of the headspace gas was automatically injected into the heated injector maintained at 85 °C.

Separation was performed on an MXT-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.53 mm ID) held at a constant temperature of 45 °C. High-purity nitrogen (≥ 99.999 %) served as the carrier gas. The carrier gas flow was programmed as follows: initial flow set at 2 mL/min for 2 min, then increased to 10 mL/min over 3 min, to 15 mL/min over the next 10 min, to 50 mL/min over 5 min, and finally to 100 mL/min over another 5 min. The drift gas flow was maintained at 150 mL/min, and the IMS detector was operated at 45 °C.

Qualitative analysis of the VOCs based on retention and drift times was performed using the GC-IMS system software VOCal. Quantitative analysis was performed by GC-IMS material peak volume.

Sensory analysis

The sensory assessment team consisted of 10 trained panelists (five males and five females) aged 20–35 years. The research protocol for the sensory evaluation team was reviewed and subsequently approved by the Science and Technology Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Reference No. ZAASES[2024]02), ensuring compliance with established ethical standards for human research. Panel members underwent comprehensive training to familiarize themselves with the sensory evaluation process, and all data were collected and utilized with the explicit consent of the evaluators.

Sensory evaluation link: The Samples were retrieved 12 h in advance, thawed at 4 °C, steamed at 100 °C for 5 min. The samples were placed on clean white plates and evaluated at 40 ± 2 °C. The panelists were instructed to observe the color and state of the samples under natural light, smell its taste, gargle with warm water, taste the samples, and evaluate the samples based on their flavor, color, taste, and appearance. The samples were evaluated using a 9-point hedonic scale (Zhao et al., 2022)，the criteria used for the evaluation are listed in Table 1, if the score was below 5, the sample was deemed inedible.

Statistical analysis

Each sample was measured independently in triplicate. The obtained data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), adopting a significance level of P < 0.05. OPLS-DA were conducted using SIMCA 14.1(MKS Umetrics, Umea, Sweden).

Results and discussion

TVB-N, TBARS, pH and sensory analysis

The TVB-N value represents the concentration of alkaline volatile nitrogen compounds in meat products and is a potential indicator of their freshness (Bekhit et al., 2021). The TVB-N content of the prepared pork products increased significantly with storage time (Fig. 1A). After 12 d of storage, The TVB-N value was 11.45 mg/100 g, below the national threshold for fresh cooked meat products (15 mg/100 g, GB 16869–2005), however, it increased by 51.65 % compared to the TVB-N value of 7.55 mg/100 g on the 0th day of storage. This may be due to the decomposition of biomolecules such as proteins and fats by microorganisms and enzymes during the storage process, resulting in alkaline nitrogenous compounds such as ammonia and amines, contributing to an elevated TVB-N value (Dong et al., 2021).

Changes in the quality of prepared pork products under different storage times. (A) TVB-N, (B) TBARS, (C) pH, (D) Sensory quality scores. Different letters in the same subplot indicate significant differences (P < 0.05).

The TBARS value is mainly used to measure the secondary oxidation products produced in foods, especially the products of fat oxidation reactions such as propylene glycol and other aldehydes (Wang et al., 2021). Compared to the 0th day of storage (Fig. 1B), the samples stored for 12 d underwent more pronounced oxidation, and the TBARS increased significantly (P < 0.05) with storage time, which may be attributed to the accumulation of peroxides in the prepared pork products during storage and the decomposition of hydroperoxides into free radicals, which continue to oxidize unsaturated fatty acids (Liang et al., 2021), leading to an increase in the TBARS value.

pH is a critical indicator of protein degradation and microbial growth, directly impacting the quality of meat products (Pabast et al., 2018). As storage time increased, the pH of the samples decreased to 6.74 ± 0.01 after the 12th d of storage compared to 6.94 ± 0.01 at the 0th d of storage(Fig. 1C). This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the accumulation of acids produced during protein degradation and fat oxidative decomposition (Wang et al., 2023), which resulted in a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in the pH of the prepared pork products.

Fig. 1D shows the results of the sensory evaluation. The sensory characteristics of the samples stored for 0 d were considered to be the best, and the samples exhibited a strong meat flavor, firm texture and uniform color; however, after 12 d of storage, the sample was rated below 5 points on the flavor rating scale, appearing as a rancid odor.

The purpose of this sensory evaluation was to visually indicate that as storage time increased, the quality of the prepared pork products deteriorated, and a peculiar smell appeared. Compared with the indicators of color, texture, and appearance, the change in flavor was more obvious with the prolonged storage time. This could be due to prolonged storage, leading to significant protein and fat oxidation in the sample, causing off-flavor and flavor degradation.

Analysis of volatile flavor compounds in prepared pork products

Qualitative analysis of flavor substances of prepared pork products

Flavor, a core element of food's sensory quality, directly influences its consumer acceptability. Volatile compounds were qualitatively analyzed using GC-IMS Library Search software, with comparisons made to the GC-IMS Library (Supplementary Table 1.). A total of 38 volatile compounds were identified, comprising 13 aldehydes, 4 ketones, 8 alcohols, 2 hydrocarbons, 4 esters, 2 pyrazines, and 5 other compounds. Concurrently, analytical data revealed that while the variety of volatile compounds in the prepared pork products remained constant throughout storage, alterations in their relative concentrations were observed, suggesting that shifts in the relative abundance of these compounds contributed to the evolving flavor profile of the prepared pork products.

Aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones are the main products of fat oxidation and the dominant volatile substances after reheating prepared pork products, among which aldehydes are the most prevalent (Guo et al., 2019). Its high content and low threshold significantly influenced the flavor of the prepared pork products.

Hexanal is the primary oxidation product of linoleic acid. Hexanal has a grassy flavor. Octanal and nonanal are monounsaturated fatty acids, primarily the oxidative degradation products of oleic acid (Chen et al., 2024), which have floral and waxed fat flavors, respectively. At low concentrations, these substances improved the flavor of the sample, contributing to grassy, floral, and fatty aromas. When the concentration is high, it produces a strong pungent and rancid taste in the oil, which affects the overall flavor of the sample. Some of the aldehydes were used as indicators to measure the degree of hot odor generation, such as pentanal, hexanal, butyraldehyde, octanal, heptanal, octanal, nonanal, and et al. (Tikk et al., 2008). Therefore, these aldehydes can be used as characteristic substances to produce odor during sample storage. Alcohols, as oxidation products of fatty acids, enhance the flavor of cooked meat, 1-Pentanol is the most commonly found alcohol in the samples of prepared pork products, which is formed through the breakdown of lipid hydroperoxides and contributes to the overall scent of meat, featuring strong, pungent, and balsamic aroma notes. The oxidation product of linoleic acid is 1-butanol (Domínguez et al., 2019), whereas 1-hexanol is formed by the reduction of linoleic acid oxidation product.

Ketones are produced by lipid oxidation and amino acid degradation and have an important influence on the aroma characteristics of food. These compounds can enhance the flavor and add notes of butter and spice aromas to the product. 2-Butanone provides a strong blue cheese scent. 2-Pentanone adds buttery, spicy, and blue cheese aromas, whereas 2-heptanone contributes spicy and acorn notes (Górska et al., 2017). Esters are the primary contributors to the floral and fruity aromas produced by esterification between alcohols and acid compounds. Acids are crucial in flavor development due to their low sensory threshold, imparting a distinct sour aroma to the prepared pork products (Lorenzo et al., 2014). Furthermore, acetic acid can interact with amino acids via Maillard reactions, generating complex aroma precursors and consequently enhancing the overall aroma profile of the prepared pork products. The contents of the other flavor substances were low; however, they all had a certain image of the flavor of the prepared pork products.

GC–IMS topographic plots of prepared pork products during storage time

Changes in the VOCs in the prepared pork products during storage were elucidated using HS-GC-IMS. The three-dimensional morphology diagram(Fig. 2A) reveals that samples subjected to different storage durations exhibited similar peak signals for volatile organic compounds; however, variations in peak intensities were observed. This indicates that while the types of volatile compounds present in the prepared pork products remained consistent across different storage periods, their relative concentrations differed. This may stem from interactions between lipid oxidation, the Maillard reaction, and amino acids (Fu et al., 2022).

Changes in volatile compounds in prepared pork products under different storage times. (A) GC-IMS 3D topographic plot, (B) Subtraction plot, (C) Gallery plot of the selected peak, (D) PCA plot, (E) VOC composition profile, (F) Clustered heatmap.

To illustrate the changes in VOCs and compare the differences between samples stored for different durations, we selected samples stored for 0 days as the reference, and the spectra of other samples were subtracted from the reference (Fig. 2B). The abscissa and ordinate indicate the ion migration times and normalized gas chromatography retention, respectively (Bharathi & Jayas, 2024). The abscissa 1.0 was the reactive ion peak (RIP). Each point adjacent to the RIP peak corresponded to a VOC. The background after deduction was white, indicating that the volatile concentrations of the two groups of samples were identical. The red background indicates that the other groups of samples had higher concentrations than the reference group, whereas the blue background indicates lower concentrations. Most volatile compounds identified in pre-processed meat products during storage exhibited retention times ranging from 200 to 1000 s and relative drift times between 1.0 and 1.8 s. A significant number of these compounds appeared with a red background, indicating that the content of most volatile substances after storage was significantly higher than that in the control group. These compounds were predominantly aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, which represent three pivotal classes of volatile compounds shaping the flavor profile of meat products (Tatiyaborworntham et al., 2022). Aldehydes, characterized by their low odor thresholds and origin in lipid oxidation, serve as the primary odorants that define the fundamental meaty and fatty aroma. Alcohols contribute as flavor-modifying agents, adding background complexity and nuance to the overall aroma profile. Ketones serve as characteristic flavor enhancers, imparting distinct creamy and fruity aroma that enrich the sensory complexity (Al-Khalili et al., 2025). The synergistic interaction and dynamic balance among these three compound classes collectively define the final sensory quality of meat products.

Gallery plots of prepared pork products during storage time

To define the changes in the prepared pork products during storage, fingerprinting profiles were used to visualize the changes in different VOCs (Fig. 2C). Each row represents a sample, and each column corresponds to a VOC. The degree of color indicates the content of the compounds, with brighter colors representing higher content. Changes in the VOCs contents of the prepared pork products after different storage periods were clearly observed using fingerprints.

To identify further differences in the VOCs in the prepared pork products during storage, principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2D) was performed based on the VOCs signal strength. In the PCA plot (Fig. 2D), PC1 explained 96.63 % of the variance, whereas PC2 contributed 1.95 %, resulting in a cumulative contribution of 79.6 %, which could explain the similarity between the different storage times.

As shown (Fig. 2E), the VOCs in the prepared pork products were mainly composed of aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, lipids, pyrroles, and acids, of which the contents of aldehydes, alcohols, and lipid contents were relatively high, accounting for 44.84 %, 24.63 %, and 16.80 % respectively. To show the differences in VOCs among different samples more comprehensively, we performed a hierarchical cluster analysis of the relative values of VOCs at different storage times (Fig. 2F). The results revealed substantial alterations in the volatile profile of prepared pork products, notably an increase in aldehydes such as hexanal, valeraldehyde, and (E)-2-heptenal, compounds known to contribute to undesirable flavors. Concurrently, levels of 1-penten-3-ol, 2-heptanone, and other compounds associated with off-flavors also increased. Conversely, the concentrations of ethyl formate (aromatic odor) (Bharathi & Jayas, 2024) and dimethyl sulfide (garlic aroma) (Zhou et al., 2016) declined significantly. These compounds are known to positively influence the sensory attributes of pork products.

Multivariate statistical analysis of VOCs in prepared pork products

To further explore the key VOCs that cause flavor changes during storage, an OPLS-DA model (Fig. 3A.) was developed for analysis. Analyzed by OPLS-DA, R2X = 0.983, R2Y = 0.99, and Q2 = 0.981, demonstrating that the model exhibits strong stability and predictive capability. To assess the potential for overfitting in the model, the validity of the OPLS-DA model was evaluated using a replacement test (Fig. 3B. Repeated, n = 200). The findings indicated that the Q2 values of the stochastic model in the replacement test were consistently lower than those of the original model; the regression line for Q2 intersected the Y-axis in the negative semi-axis. This suggests that the original model demonstrates strong stability and does not exhibit any signs of overfitting. These findings indicate that the OPLS-DA model exhibits a high degree of interpretability and strong predictive capability for prepared pork products at different storage times.

Analysis of free fatty acid content in prepared pork products

In this study, 10 fatty acids, including 5 saturated fatty acids (myristic acid C14:0, palmitic acid C16:0, lauric acid C12:0, stearic acid C18:0 and behenic acid C22:0), 2 monounsaturated fatty acids (palmitoleic acid C16:1 and oleic acid C18:1) and 3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (linoleic acid C18:2, linolenic acid C18:3 and arachidonic acid C20:4) were quantified by GC–MS multiple reaction monitoring mode, and the results showed a significant change trend of different fatty acids during the 12-day storage period.

The results of Fig. 4A showed that lauric acid, myristic acid and behenic acid decreased by 16.67 %, 11.62 % and 30.00 % respectively, while palmitic acid and stearic acid increased by 3.48 % and 31.20 %, respectively, and the results of Fig. 4B showed that the unsaturated fatty acids showed a downward trend: palmitoleic acid, oleic acid, linoleic acid, linolenic acid and arachidonic acid decreased by 11.76 %, 19.33 %, 25.45 %, 28.13 % and 18.52 %, respectively. The comprehensive analysis showed that the relative content of SFA increased by 10.32 % after 12 d of storage (Fig. 4C), while the relative content of UFA decreased by 21.39 % (Fig. 4D). This phenomenon is consistent with Chmiel's findings (Chmiel, Roszko, Adamczak, et al., 2019). The increase in the relative content of saturated fatty acids such as palmitic acid and stearic acid may be due to the hydrolysis of triglycerides by lipases in meat products, and after the decomposition of triglycerides, saturated fatty acids are prone to accumulation due to their high chemical stability, resulting in an increase in their relative content. Polyunsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid (C18:2) and linolenic acid (C18:3) readily undergo oxidative degradation during meat product storage and processing. This degradation generates a series of volatile compounds that significantly influence flavor, including hexanal, pentanal, (E)-2-heptenal, hexanal, propionaldehyde, pentanal, 1-butanol, and 1-pentanol (Cui et al., 2024). These findings align with the key volatile compounds identified in Section 3.2.4, which contribute to both the characteristic flavor profile of meat products and the off-flavors associated with lipid rancidity and staleness.

Correlation analysis of fatty acid content and characteristic volatile compounds

Prepared pork products undergo many reactions during oxidation; therefore, identifying signature markers and their odor-formation mechanisms is a complicated process. Alterations in fatty acid content during storage may significantly affect the WOF of prepared pork products. Therefore, 16 VOCs with VIP values exceeding 1 and 10 fatty acids that mainly affect the flavor of the prepared pork products were selected. The correlation between the signature markers and fatty acid content (Fig. 5) was analyzed.

Correlation analysis of the fatty acid content and characteristic VOCs of prepared pork products.

Linoleic acid, oleic acid and linolenic acid were negatively correlated with the contents of (E)-2-heptenal, hexanal, propionaldehyde, valeraldehyde, n-butanol, and n-amyl alcohol (P < 0.001), This is because most VOCs generated during the oxidation of the prepared pork products are byproducts of lipid degradation. During storage, many lipid radicals are produced under the influence of light, temperature, microorganisms, and other conditions, which can accelerate the oxidation and hydrolysis of lipids, This leads to the formation of various lipid oxidation products(Fu et al., 2022), contributing to WOF. Therefore, fat oxidation in the prepared pork products during storage is a critical factor in the development of WOF.

Potential formation mechanisms of WOF

Most VOCs in cooked meat products are typically produced via lipid oxidation and degradation (Bravo-Lamas et al., 2018). Both autooxidation and thermal oxidation of lipids occur via a radical oxidation mechanism upon exposure to oxygen or air. Under different reaction conditions, lipid oxidation produces different mixtures of oxidation products. Autoxidation of fatty acids is a key factor in rancid WOF of meat.

The fatty acids in prepared pork products mainly include palmitic acid, myristic acid, stearic acid, oleic acid, linoleic acid, linolenic acid and arachidonic acid, among which the unsaturated fatty acids linoleic acid, linolenic acid and arachidonic acid are easily oxidized and decomposed, resulting in the unsaturated chain of fatty acids breaking to form hydroperoxides and free radicals. As a result, short-chain fatty acids, aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, acids and other substances are produced (Merlo et al., 2021). Among the 16 substances with VIP > 1, it has been shown that aldehydes, (E)-2-heptenal, are considered as WOF components in meat products(Yu, Liu, et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 6, the oxidative degradation of linoleic acid to form hexanal and (E)-2-heptenal begins with the extraction of hydrogen atoms mediated by free radical initiators, forming linoleic acid radicals and subsequently adding with oxygen to form peroxy radicals (Domínguez et al., 2019). This peroxyradical further seizes the hydrogen atom from another linoleic acid molecule or other donor, producing a variety of hydroperoxide isomers. After intramolecular rearrangement, these isomers may undergo β-cleavage, breaking the bond between carbon 11 and 12, possibly 2-heptenal, and breaking the bond between carbon 12 and 13, possibly hexanal (Cui et al., 2024).

Conclusion

In summary, during storage, the TVB-N and TBARS values of the prepared pork products significantly increased, while pH levels and sensory scores markedly decreased. This indicates oxidative deterioration of the samples, resulting in a significant decline in overall quality accompanied by WOF. Volatile component analysis revealed elevated levels of aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones, which most significantly impacted product flavor. Concurrently, lipid oxidation intensified, manifested by increased relative saturated fatty acids and decreased relative unsaturated fatty acids. The oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids like linoleic acid was particularly pronounced, yielding compounds such as (E)-2-heptanal, (E)-2-hexenal, propionaldehyde, valeraldehyde, 1-butanol, and 1-pentanol. Collectively, these compounds significantly promote the formation of WOF. This study systematically elucidates that the primary mechanism underlying WOF formation in the prepared pork products during storage stems from lipid oxidation. However, WOF development may also be associated with protein oxidation, representing a limitation of this research. Future investigations should further explore this aspect to refine the understanding of the underlying mechanisms.
