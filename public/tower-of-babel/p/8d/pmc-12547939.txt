High-value utilization of brewers' spent grain in extruded corn-based foods: physicochemical, functional, and metabolomic insights☆
Authors: Yan Huang, Zhengjie Xie, Guohua Zhao, Yu Zhang, Jiajia Song, Xiaoqi Xu, Damao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The high-value utilization of brewers' spent grain (BSG), a protein- and polyphenol-rich by-product of the brewing industry, holds great promise for developing functional foods. In this study, BSG was incorporated into extruded corn-based products at varying levels (0–35 wt%), and its effects on physicochemical properties, antioxidant capacity, α-amylase inhibition, and metabolic profile were systematically investigated. The inclusion of BSG significantly improved water- and oil-holding capacities, total dietary fiber, and total phenolic content (TPC), with 30 wt% identified as an optimal formulation balancing functionality and sensory acceptability. Following in vitro digestion, the BSG-enriched extrudates exhibited significantly enhanced antioxidant activity (FRAP and DPPH assays) and α-amylase inhibitory potential, supporting their hypoglycemic potential. UHPLC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics further revealed key bioactive compounds, including 9-cis-retinoic acid and tryptophylproline, contributing to the enrichment of immune- and glucose-regulating pathways (e.g., intestinal IgA, PI3K-Akt, FoxO signaling). These results highlight the potential of BSG-fortified extrudates as value-added, fiber-enriched functional foods and provide mechanistic insights for their future nutritional development and clinical evaluation.

Introduction

With the global advancement of sustainable development strategies (Gutierrez-Barrutia et al., 2025), the high-value utilization of food processing by-products has emerged as a critical research focus within the framework of the circular economy. Solid by-products generated during beer production primarily consist of brewers' spent grain (BSG), brewers' spent hops (BSH), and brewers' spent yeast (BSY) (Olivares-Galván et al., 2022) with BSG garnering significant attention due to its substantial proportion, accounting for approximately 85 % of total by-products (Mussatto, 2014). It is estimated that the global annual production of BSG exceeded 180 million tons in 2020. (Rojas-Chamorro et al., 2020). Traditionally, BSG has been utilized as a low-value animal feed or disposed of through landfilling. However, its high organic matter content renders it susceptible to spoilage and microbial degradation if improperly managed, posing risks of environmental pollution (Mussatto, 2014). The development of advanced utilization strategies for BSG not only mitigates the environmental burden caused by waste accumulation but also provides the food industry with novel functional ingredients, aligning with the resource regeneration demands under the “carbon neutrality” paradigm.

BSG primarily composed of barley husk and aleurone layers, exhibits significant nutritional and chemical properties. It contains over 60 % dietary fiber, predominantly consisting of cellulose (28.7 %), hemicellulose (17.5 %), and lignin (16.9 %). Additionally, BSG is rich in protein (19–30 %) (Torres-Mayanga et al., 2019), including high-quality plant proteins such as gluten and globulin, and contains phenolic acids (approximately 2300 mg/kg) mainly comprising ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, sinapic acid, and vanillic acid. More than 85 % of these phenolic acids exist in insoluble bound forms (Jin et al., 2022) and have been scientifically demonstrated to possess bioactive properties, particularly antioxidant activity (Connolly et al., 2021). Leveraging these attributes, BSG has been explored as a functional ingredient across various food matrices, including bakery products (Ginindza et al., 2022), dairy products (Naibaho et al., 2022), and cereal-based products (Gutierrez-Barrutia et al., 2023). The study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with BSG exhibited significantly greater in vitro anti-diabetic potential (p < 0.05), along with markedly enhanced inhibition of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation (p < 0.05) and superior anti-inflammatory properties during intestinal digestion (Gutierrez-Barrutia et al., 2023). The underlying mechanisms may involve the adsorption of bile acids by dietary fiber, as well as the metabolic regulation mediated by phenolic compounds through the modulation of the PPARγ signaling pathway (Carnauba et al., 2025).

Despite the significant nutritional advantages of BSG, its high fiber content (>60 %) often compromises the textural properties of food matrices, resulting in increased hardness (up to 2.3-fold), reduced crispness (approximately 35 % decrease), and pronounced browning (ΔE > 5, where ΔE represents the magnitude of the total color difference between a sample and its reference standard (Patrignani et al., 2021). To address these challenges, a series of pretreatment techniques has been developed: physical modification (e.g., microwave and ultrasonic treatments) enhances water-holding capacity by altering fiber crystallinity; biological processing (e.g., enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation) degrades anti-nutritional factors while releasing bound phenolic compounds; and extrusion puffing, as a thermo-mechanical synergistic treatment, effectively improves the oil-holding capacity and expansion properties of BSG (Martín-García et al., 2020). Notably, the application of extrusion technology in starchy food systems has matured, leveraging high-temperature, high-pressure shear forces to significantly enhance the digestibility and nutritional bioavailability of dietary fiber-rich materials, with in vitro digestibility increasing by 18–25 % (Wen et al., 2024). However, existing research predominantly focuses on optimizing processing parameters, with insufficient exploration of the retention rate of bioactive functional components and the underlying mechanisms influencing digestive properties.

This study targets a BSG-corn grits composite system, employing single-screw extrusion technology to systematically investigate the effects of gradient BSG addition on the following parameters: (1) functional component transformation, with a particular emphasis on elucidating the structural stability of phenolic compounds during thermal processing and their changes in in vitro antioxidant activity; (2) An in vitro simulated digestion model was employed to systematically evaluate the effects of digestion phases on reducing sugar release, digestion efficiency, and functional activity of the samples, thereby elucidating their digestive properties and potential nutritional functions; and (3) texture-nutrition interaction mechanisms, employing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to characterize microstructural evolution and clarify the interfacial interactions between dietary fiber and starch molecules on digestive properties. By establishing a multidimensional evaluation framework, this study aims to provide theoretical support for the precise application of BSG in puffed food products, thereby advancing the innovative development of cereal-based functional foods.

Materials and methods

Materials and sample preparation

Fresh BSG used in this study was supplied by Chongqing Jiawei Brewery Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China), while corn grits were procured from a supermarket in Beibei District, Chongqing (Chongqing, China). Pepsin (>3000 U/mg) was obtained from Chongqing Chaoyou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., pancreatin (4000 U/g) from Tianjin Hynes Optimum Technology Co., Ltd., heat-stable α-amylase (Aladin) from Chengdu Selvins Biotechnology Co., Ltd., porcine bile salts from Chongqing Onke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and amyloglucosidase (100,000 U/g) from Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd. All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Fresh BSG was autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min, dried at 60 °C for 18 h in an oven, pulverized, sieved through a 40-mesh screen, and stored at −20 °C to preserve its stability and bioactivity.

Extrusion processing

BSG powder was blended with corn grits at varying ratios (25, 30, and 35 g/100 g) and processed using a small single-screw extruder (Grain 7) equipped with a 16-hole die (22 mm head diameter) to produce puffed samples. The extruded products were cooled at 25 °C for 12 h, ground through a 40-mesh sieve, and subjected to subsequent analyses.

Sensory evaluation

A panel of 25 untrained consumers (10 males and 15 females) was recruited from Southwest University to assess the sensory properties of the puffed products. Prior to evaluation, panelists received training on the experimental procedure and sniffing techniques. Samples were evaluated across five attributes—color, appearance, texture, flavor, and crispness—with each assessment lasting 5 min. Scoring was based on a 20-point scale, where ratings ranged from 0 (poor) to 20 (excellent), reflecting increasing preference or quality. Specifically, scores of 15–20 indicated excellent quality (e.g., yellowish-brown color with distinct luster, uniform cylindrical shape with smooth surface, mild and long-lasting flavor, loose and uniform pores, loud chewing sound without stickiness); 10–15 denoted good quality (e.g., yellowish-dark brown with notable luster, mostly uniform cylindrical shape, mild and lasting BSG flavor, relatively loose pores, loud chewing with slight stickiness); 5–10 represented moderate quality (e.g., yellowish-dark brown with average luster, fairly uniform oval shape with rough surface, short-lived BSG flavor, small and uneven pores, normal chewing sound with moderate stickiness); and 0–5 signified poor quality (e.g., dark yellowish-brown with dull luster, uneven and unmoldable shape, no hop flavor, few or no pores, low chewing sound with stickiness). Physical property analysis

Textural properties were determined using a texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus990000, UK) in TPA mode with a P/5 probe. Parameters included a pre-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, test speed of 1.0 mm/s, post-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, compression distance of 60 % of sample diameter, 5-s interval between compressions, contact force of 5.0 g, and sample length of 50 mm. Color parameters (L*, a*, b*) were measured with a colorimeter (CM-5010499, Konica Minolta, Japan), where L* indicates lightness (0−100), a* denotes red (+a*) or green (−a*), and b* reflects yellow (+b*) or blue (−b*). Color difference (ΔE) was calculated as(1)ΔE=ΔL2+Δa2+Δb2

Microstructural analysis was conducted using a scanning electron microscope (Phenom Pro10102, Netherlands) on nitrogen-sprayed cross-sections of dried, pulverized samples at 500 × −3000× magnification.

Chemical property analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a Spectrum Two analyzer (USA) with attenuated total reflectance (ATR) to record absorption spectra from 4000 to 400 cm−1 (10 scans, 4 cm−1 resolution). The TPC was determined by extracting 1 g of the sample with 10 mL of 80 % methanol via ultrasonication for 30 min, followed by centrifugation. A 0.2-mL aliquot of supernatant was mixed with 150 μL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, reacted for 5 min, neutralized with 200 μL of 25 % Na₂CO₃, diluted to 1 mL, and incubated at 30 °C for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm, with gallic acid as the standard (Yu et al., 2025).

Dietary Fiber extraction and α-amylase inhibition

Dietary fiber (DF) was extracted from samples before and after extrusion processing (0, 25–35 wt% BSG before and after expansion) using a modified method (Yu et al., 2025). One gram of sample powder (Non-extruded control groups and extruded treatment groups) was treated with 40 mL of phosphate buffer (0.08 M, pH 6.0) and 300 μL of heat-stable α-amylase at 95 °C and 150 rpm for 30 min. After cooling to 60 °C, the pH was adjusted to 7.5 with 1 M NaOH, followed by the addition of 100 μL protease and incubation at 60 °C for 30 min. The pH was then adjusted to 4.5 with 1 M HCl, and 300 μL of amyloglucosidase was added; incubation continued at 60 °C for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the residue was washed twice with 95 % ethanol and once with acetone. In vitro digestion and metabolomics

Simulated saliva (SSF), gastric fluid (SGF), and intestinal fluid (SIF) were prepared with salivary amylase (50 U/mg), pepsin (3000 U/mg), and pancreatin (trypsin ≥4000 U/mg, lipase ≥4000 U/mg, amylase ≥7000 U/mg). SSF contained 10 mM NaCl, 24 mM KCl, 10 mM NaH₂PO₄, 30 mM NaHCO₃, 6.67 mM urea, and 75 U/mL amylase; SGF included 22 mM KCl, 240 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM CaCl₂, 1 g/L pepsin (pH 4.5); SIF comprised 15 mM KCl, 240 mM NaCl, 1.5 g/L trypsin, and bile (12 g/L porcine bile salts, pH 8.0). Four grams of sample were incubated with 6 mL SSF at 37 °C and 1000 ×g for 5 min, adjusted to pH 1.5, mixed with 12 mL SGF, and incubated for 2 h. The pH was then adjusted to 8.0, 12 mL SIF and 6 mL bile added, and incubation continued for 2 h. Enzymes were inactivated at 100 °C, and samples stored at −20 °C. The digestion process was replicated three times. For metabolomics, digested supernatant was thawed at 4 °C, vortexed with 400 μL methanol, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and reconstituted in 150 μL 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 ppm). Filtrates were analyzed via UHPLC (Thermo Vanquish) with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column and Q Exactive Focus MS (Thermo Fisher) in positive/negative ion modes (spray voltages: 3.50/−2.50 kV, resolution: 70,000/17,500).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Data visualization was conducted using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Result and discussion

Effect of BSG on physical properties and sensory evaluation of corn-puffed foods

In BSG, the moisture content ranges from 63 % to 80 %, protein content from 15 % to 32 %, lipid content from 6 % to 10 %, cellulose content from 19 % to 70 %, and ash content from 0.2 % to 8 %. Regarding phenolic compounds (mg/100 g), ferulic acid is in the range of 35–336.3 mg/100 g, p-coumaric acid is 6.7–137.3 mg/100 g, catechin is 41.30 mg/100 g, caffeic acid is 0.8–13.7 mg/100 g, gallocatechin is 162.2 mg/100 g, and epigallocatechin is 50.59 mg/100 g (Olivares-Galván et al., 2022). The incorporation of BSG into extruded corn, ranging from 0 to 35 wt%, significantly alters their physicochemical properties, particularly through color modification driven by the inherent composition of BSG and processing-induced reactions (Table 2) (Sobukola et al., 2013). The brightness (L*) of the expanded product decreased from 85.37 to 64.55, the yellowness (b*) from 36.56 to 22.36, and the redness (a*) from 5.59 to 5.84 with increasing BSG content, resulting in an increase in the total color difference (ΔE) from 22.93 to 25.17. This darkening and shift in hue are primarily due to the presence of pigments in BSG, such as melanoidins formed during brewing, and the enhancement of Maillard reactions during the high-temperature extrusion process. These reactions occur between amino acids and reducing sugars abundant in both BSG and corn, producing brown compounds that intensify the snack's color. Texturally, BSG addition markedly increases the hardness (from 1448.70 g to 2016.59 g), chewiness (from 715.18 g to 1206.97 g), and resilience (from 0.29 % to 0.47 %) of the products, while springiness (from 0.93 % to 0.83 %) diminish, reflecting the structural impact of BSG's high fiber (accounting for 19 % to 70 %) and protein content (accounting for 15 % to 32 %) (Olivares-Galván et al., 2022). The insoluble fibers in BSG disrupt the starch matrix's gelatinization and expansion during extrusion, leading to a denser product with reduced porosity and smaller air cells, which elevates hardness and chewiness. Simultaneously, proteins denature and form cross-linked networks under extrusion conditions, reinforcing the snack's structure but compromising its elasticity and crispness—attributes critical to consumer preference. Sensory scores for structure and crispness decline as BSG increases, indicating that the denser, less airy texture deviates from the desired light, crispy mouthfeel (Ainsworth Insworth et al., 2007). However, at 25–30 wt% BSG, a balance may be struck, as crispness scores (e.g., 12.08 for 25 wt%) remain acceptable, suggesting that moderate incorporation could enhance nutritional value without excessively sacrificing sensory quality. Behavioral economics analysis reveals that when the concept of “healthy food” is introduced, the purchase intention reaches 81.97 %. Additionally, 13.93 % of the participants express skepticism regarding the health claims of such products, and the taste is the primary factor contributing to their hesitation (Joncer Naibaho et al., 2023). Overall, this evidence substantiates that functional attribute claims can effectively offset the sensory impairments resulting from changes in physical properties.

Mechanistic effects of BSG on water/oil retention, soluble solids, and dietary fiber in extruded corn products

Experimental results demonstrate that the addition of BSG significantly enhances the physicochemical properties of corn-puffed foods (Fig. 1). Extrusion puffing improves WHC through dual mechanisms: first, mechanical shear forces and high temperature-pressure conditions cause starch granule fragmentation and gelatinization, exposing more hydrophilic groups (Chuang et al., 2004); Secondly, during the extrusion puffing process, the dietary fiber in BSG undergoes microfibril network reorganization. This increases the specific surface area and forms a porous structure, thereby effectively entrapping water molecules. However, no significant difference was observed between the OHC and the extrusion puffing control group. The change in OHC may also be related to the formation of amylose-lipid complexes, which have been demonstrated to form in corn-whole wheat flour extrudates, characterized by a combination of X-ray diffraction and differential scanning calorimetry (Oliveira et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 1A and B, the WHC and OHC values of puffed samples containing 25 wt% BSG were 0.34 ± 0.01 g/g and 0.20 ± 0.02 g/g, respectively, with WHC being significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.05), which increased to about 0.40 ± 0.01 g/g, at 35 wt% BSG. The data in Fig. 1C shows that the WSI value of the non-extruded control group was 0.011 g/g (dry basis). After adding brewer's spent grains (BSG), the WSI of the samples significantly increased (p < 0.05), but there was no statistical difference between the 25 wt% and 30 wt% BSG groups (p > 0.05), which might be related to the higher proportion of soluble components in the BSG raw material. After extrusion treatment, the WSI of the samples containing 25–35 wt% BSG significantly increased to 0.20–0.22 g/g. However, it is worth noting that the extruded control group with pure corn matrix showed a higher WSI value. This might be due to the higher degree of gelatinization of corn starch during the extrusion process compared to the dietary fiber and protein in BSG (Gong et al., 2018). Overall, extrusion expansion treatment increased the WSI, and this increase might be attributed to the high temperature and shear force during extrusion promoting starch gelatinization and the structural degradation of BSG fibers and proteins, thereby reducing the proportion of insoluble components and improving the bioavailability of nutrients. FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2) indicates that the molecular structure of the unexpanded and expanded samples has changed. The expanded samples after extrusion processing exhibit characteristic peaks of cellulose (3286 cm−1), protein (1500–1700 cm−1), lignin (1240 cm−1), and hemicellulose (1030 cm−1) (Ribeiro-Sanches et al., 2024). The stretching signal at 3286 cm−1 represents the presence of hydroxyl groups, indicating the presence of cellulose and hemicellulose. The peak at 2927 cm−1 is related to the stretching of aliphatic C—H bonds in polysaccharides and lignin. Additionally, the peak at 1741 cm−1 indicates a change in the ester and carboxyl bond signals in hemicellulose, which is only present in samples containing BSG, suggesting a change in the amorphous region of hemicellulose, possibly due to deacetylation caused by the expansion process (dos Santos et al., 2015). Such hemicellulose deacetylation may enhance water affinity and modulate digestive resistance, thereby linking cell wall restructuring with functional output. The region from 1650 to 1660 cm−1 is attributed to the amide I band (α-helix structure) of proteins in BSG, with minimal peak changes, indicating that the expansion process has a negligible effect on protein content. The peaks at 1034 cm−1 and 998 cm−1 are related to the stretching vibration of CO and CH in hemicellulose. The FTIR results show an increase or change in the peaks related to cellulose and hemicellulose, which is directly related to the increase in cellulose content due to the addition of BSG. The total dietary fiber (TDF) content extracted from corn puffed foods with different addition amounts of BSG further confirmed the enhancement of its functionality. As depicted in Fig. 1D, irrespective of whether the extrusion-puffing treatment was applied or not, the TDF content in the samples with the addition of BSG was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.05). Specifically, for the sample containing 35 wt% BSG, the TDF content reached 0.34 ± 0.01 g/g after puffing, compared with the non-puffed 35 wt% BSG group (0.31 ± 0.015 g/g), the TDF content exhibited an 8 % increase after puffing. This phenomenon can likely be ascribed to the combined effects of high temperature, shear forces, and pressure during the extrusion-puffing process. These factors led to a reduction in the crystallinity of dietary fiber, rendering its structure more amorphous and porous (Gong et al., 2018; Raibagkar et al., 2024). Notwithstanding that the enhancement of TDF content resulting from extrusion-puffing was relatively modest (8 %), it may exert a more profound influence on the physicochemical properties of dietary fiber, such as solubility and water-holding capacity. Consequently, these changes can have a substantial impact on the functional attributes of dietary fiber.

Impact of BSG and extrusion on starch digestibility, α-amylase inhibition, and glycemic response modulation

According to its sensitivity to pancreatic amylase (in vitro), starch can be categorized into rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) (Dhital et al., 2017). Certain starches and starch-based products that are challenging to digest within the small intestine are classified as resistant starch. The reasons underlying the difficulty in starch digestion are diverse, including physical inaccessibility, starch retrogradation, or chemical modification. Resistant starch confers significant health benefits, such as reducing the risk of colon cancer, hemorrhoids, diverticular disease, and constipation, increasing fecal volume, regulating blood glucose levels, and decreasing blood cholesterol levels (Bojarczuk et al., 2022). Additionally, it exhibits prebiotic properties.

The research has demonstrated that phenolic substances in millet (ferulic acid: 112.56 ± 5.61 μg/mL, caffeic acid: 25.76 ± 1.01 μg/mL, p-coumaric acid: 3.35 ± 0.22 μg/mL) effectively inhibit the activity of α-amylase (Pradeep et al., 2018). Phenolic acids achieve this inhibitory effect by decreasing the content of rapidly digestible starch and increasing the proportion of resistant starch. These phenolic substances also constitute a major component in BSG (ferulic acid: 129.81 ± 26.75–1306.06 ± 32.25 μg/mL, caffeic acid derivatives: 4.27 ± 3.03–19.04 ± 0.07 μg/mL, p-coumaric acid derivatives: 53.16 ± 0.14–2977.67 ± 4.77 μg/mL) (McCarthy et al., 2013). Furthermore, this might also be attributed to the fact that corn contains a higher amount of resistant starch compared to BSG. Previous studies have indicated that extrusion and puffing may reduce the content of resistant starch. This is due to the fact that heat treatment, high pressure and shear force disrupt the starch structure (Alsaffar, 2011). Resistant starch is capable of resisting the action of α-amylase. Therefore, it is speculated that although the total phenolic content increases, the reduction in resistant starch content leads to a weakened inhibitory effect on α-amylase after extrusion and puffing, making it more susceptible to enzymatic digestion.

Studies have found that increasing the BSG content can significantly enhance the inhibition of α-amylase (Bazsefidpar et al., 2024). In the present study, the addition of 25 wt% BSG subsequent to extrusion puffing increased the α-amylase inhibition rate to 41.16 ± 7.18 % (see Fig. 3D). However, no significant difference was observed compared to the extrusion control group (p > 0.05). This phenomenon might be attributed to the relatively high content of phenolic substances in corn itself (Fig. 3A), and it has been proven in previous studies that among common staple foods (corn, wheat, oats and rice), corn has the highest total phenolic content (For example, vanillic acid 23.71 ± 0.67 μg/g, caffeic acid 7.96 ± 0.34 μg/g, p-coumaric acid 27.56 ± 0.6 μg/g, ferulic acid 11.06 ± 0.39 μg/g.)(Adom et al., 2002), followed by wheat, oats and rice. Additionally, it could also be due to the relatively higher resistant starch content compared to BSG. As the BSG content increased, the α-amylase inhibition rate gradually rose. For instance, the inhibition rate of the 35 wt% BSG extrusion puffing group reached 49.06 ± 6.47 %. The observed decrease in post-digestion α-amylase inhibitory activity (e.g., the α-amylase inhibition rate in the 35 wt% BSG extrusion group dropped to 20.33 ± 0.58 % after in vitro simulated digestion) (Fig. 3D and J) might be attributed to the changes in the types and contents of phenolic substances in the samples after in vitro simulated digestion (e.g., the content of caffeic acid decreased from 2.56 ± 0.25 μg/g to 2.46 ± 0.22 μg/g) (Morais et al., 2025). Additionally, different phenolic substances have varying inhibitory effects on α-amylase. Moreover, extrusion and expansion significantly increased the protein digestibility in vitro (Yan et al., 2025). Therefore, it is speculated that despite the increase in total phenolic content (TPC) (Fig. 3G), the interaction between proteins and polyphenols, possibly through the formation of protein-polyphenol-enzyme ternary complexes (He et al., 2015), reduced the inhibitory activity of polyphenols on α-amylase. Thus, the weakened α-amylase inhibitory activity after digestion might be a combined result of the composition and content of phenolic substances, protein content, and the interactions among various components.

In Fig. 3E, the digestion rate of all sample groups increased significantly after extrusion treatment. Specifically, the group containing 35 wt% BSG achieved a digestion rate of 59.03 ± 0.41 %, which was significantly higher than that of the non-extruded 35 wt% BSG group (14.04 ± 1.20 %) (p < 0.05). This improvement can be attributed to the disruption of the starch crystalline structure and the formation of an amorphous structure during extrusion. Amorphous starch is more readily degraded by digestive enzymes compared to crystalline starch (Huang et al., 2022). Additionally, extrusion facilitates the conversion of dietary fiber from an insoluble to a soluble form, further enhancing the digestion rate.

However, as the BSG content increased, the digestion rate gradually decreased (64.31 ± 6.63 % and 63.04 ± 2.09 % for the 25 wt% and 30 wt% BSG extrusion groups, respectively, p < 0.05). This may be due to the relatively high content of dietary fiber and protein in BSG (Torres-Mayanga et al., 2019), particularly the increase in insoluble or poorly soluble dietary fiber. These components may undergo cross-linking or denaturation reactions during the extrusion process, resulting in the formation of more intricate and indigestible structures (Li et al., 2024). Thus, an inverse correlation was observed between the BSG addition amount and digestibility.

The combined action of high temperature and pressure during extrusion accelerates the gelatinization of starch, generating easily digestible small-molecule sugars (such as maltose) and promoting sugar release (Schirmer Chirmer et al., 2015). As depicted in Fig. 3F, the content of reducing sugars in the supernatant increased after extrusion (the content of reducing sugars in the 25 wt% BSG group increased from 36.10 ± 3.96 mg/g to 98.72 ± 7.14 mg/g). Nevertheless, with the increase in BSG content, the content of reducing sugars showed a downward trend (82.53 ± 13.97 mg/g in the 35 wt% BSG extrusion group). This may be because the lower starch content in BSG could also lead to a reduction in the release of digestible sugars.

Release and bioactivity of phenolic compounds in BSG-fortified extrudates

According to previous studies (Mussatto et al., 2007), BSG contains 145.3 mg/L ferulic acid and 138.8 mg/L p-coumaric acid, etc. However, the content of phenolic compounds in BSG from different sources varies greatly (such as epicatechin (0.869 ± 0.199–2.891 ± 0.290 μg/g), gallic acid (2.766 ± 0.145–8.330 ± 0.958 μg/g), and catechin (0.009 ± 0.020–1.572 ± 0.579 μg/g) (Petrón et al., 2021).

Fig. 3A-C show the TPC, DPPH radical scavenging capacity, and FRAP antioxidant capacity of corn puffed snacks with added BSG. In this study, using gallic acid as the standard, the TPC range of the extrusion puffing group (25–35 wt% BSG) was 4.20 ± 0.063 to 5.98 ± 0.85 mg GAE/g sample, while that of the non-puffing group (25–35 wt% BSG) was 3.40 ± 0.16 to 4.57 ± 0.49 mg GAE/g sample. Although some phenolic compounds may be lost during the extrusion puffing process, the experimental results show that the puffing process did not significantly reduce the content of phenolic substances. At the same BSG addition level, the extrusion puffing process instead promoted the release and extraction of phenolic compounds and enhanced their biological activity. This might be due to the fact that phenolic compounds in plant tissues are usually present in bound forms (e.g., bound to sugars or proteins), but the high temperature during the extrusion puffing process may promote the breaking of these bonds, releasing free phenolic compounds. Additionally, the amount of phenolic compounds increases with the increase in BSG content (Gong et al., 2018).

Phenolic compounds have long been recognized as a major source of antioxidants (McCarthy et al., 2013), and the phenolic compounds in BSG also exhibit strong antioxidant activity (Petrón et al., 2021). The DPPH radical scavenging capacity of the groups with added BSG was significantly higher than that of the control group. With the increase in BSG content, the DPPH scavenging rate gradually increased, with the scavenging capacities of the 0、25、30、and 35 wt% BSG extruded groups being 12.51 ± 1.10 μg TE/g sample, 20.11 ± 3.04 μg TE/g sample, 36.06 ± 5.58 μg TE/g sample, and 40.23 ± 1.87 μg TE/g sample, respectively. The FRAP antioxidant capacities of the 0、25、30、and 35 wt% sample extruded groups were 0.75 ± 0.052 μmol TE/g sample, 0.81 ± 0.076 μmol TE/g sample, 0.97 ± 0.13 μmol TE/g sample, and 1.15 ± 0.21 μmol TE/g sample, respectively. Overall, the TPC, as determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method, closely mirrored the trends observed in DPPH radical scavenging capacity and FRAP antioxidant activity. As shown in Fig. 3G–I, following in vitro simulated digestion, the supernatants from the extruded samples exhibited significantly higher FRAP values compared to their non-extruded counterparts, with antioxidant activity increasing proportionally with BSG content. In the extrusion group, the FRAP antioxidant activities of 30 wt% and 35 wt% BSG reached 21.82 ± 0.38 and 22.27 ± 0.20 μmol TE/g sample, respectively, which were significantly higher than those of the 0 and 25 wt% BSG groups (11.59 ± 0.55 and 18.04 ± 1.36 μmol TE/g sample, p < 0.05).

Similarly, DPPH radical scavenging capacity increased markedly with rising BSG levels. The digested supernatants from the extrusion group consistently outperformed those from the non-extruded group. The 35 wt% BSG extrusion sample showed the highest DPPH scavenging activity (53.42 ± 1.51 μg TE/g). These enhanced DPPH and FRAP results highlight the robust antioxidant potential of BSG-enriched extrudates. After digestion, significant improvements were also observed in polyphenol release: the TPC of the 35 wt% BSG group reached 7.70 ± 0.24 mg GAE/g sample, compared to 6.07 ± 0.53 mg GAE/g in the non-extruded group (Fig. 3G). This suggests that gastrointestinal digestion facilitates the release of bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols, from the BSG matrix.

The mechanism behind this phenomenon may involve the interaction between dietary fiber, protein, and phenolic compounds, as well as their release during digestion. During extrusion, the structure of dietary fiber and protein may be partially disrupted or denatured, making these components more easily released into the supernatant during digestion. Particularly, the enhanced solubility of dietary fiber enables more phenolic compounds to be released into the solution, thereby enhancing their antioxidant activity. Additionally, the hydrolysis during digestion may further promote the release of phenolic compounds, increasing their bioavailability in the gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, the extrusion samples showed significantly higher polyphenol content and stronger antioxidant activity after digestion, suggesting that they may have higher bioavailability in vivo and be more effectively absorbed and utilized in the small intestine.

Structural modifications in BSG-blended extrudates

The SEM micrographs in Fig. 4 reveal distinct microstructural changes across the sample groups. The unexpanded control group (NE) exhibits a smooth, granular surface, characteristic of the unprocessed raw material (e.g., 50 μm scale images). In contrast, the extruded and expanded group (E), observed at 1000× magnification, displays a significantly rougher surface with micro-cracks, pores, and irregular topography. This transformation is likely due to the rapid vaporization of moisture under high-temperature, high-pressure extrusion conditions, inducing expansion and forming steam-release channels that disrupt the matrix. Additionally, starch gelatinization occurs during extrusion due to hydration and heating, resulting in a networked structure. Based on the analysis, corn-based extruded foods supplemented with BSG demonstrate superior antioxidant and anti-glycemic properties compared to the control group. These functional enhancements are likely linked to the microstructural modifications induced by extrusion, such as increased porosity facilitating the release of bioactive compounds from BSG, and altered starch structures slowing sugar release. Integrating results from physical properties, sensory evaluation, in vitro digestion, and functional performance, the sample with 30 wt% BSG was selected for further electron microscopy and digestion metabolomics analyses. This decision reflects its optimal performance across multiple parameters, including enhanced digestibility, elevated antioxidant activity, and acceptable sensory qualities.

Metabolomic insights into BSG-fortified extrudates

Our study employed a metabolomic approach to investigate the corn puffed foods supplemented with 30 wt% BSG, aiming to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced antioxidant capacity, anti-glycation properties, and health potential. By integrating the biological significance of functional metabolites with existing research, we explored their molecular basis and potential applications. Metabolomic analysis revealed that the base peak chromatograms of samples subjected to extrusion puffing exhibited trends similar to those of the control group, indicating the stability of the experimental model and suggesting that the observed metabolic profile changes were primarily driven by BSG supplementation. Out of 1262 total identified metabolites, 270 were significantly differential (126 upregulated and 144 downregulated, with further analysis unveiling pronounced differences in metabolite abundance. Our metabolomics analysis identified notable changes in specific metabolites-notably (S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)glutarate, tryptophyl-proline, and 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine-alongside enrichment of immune/metabolic pathways such as the intestinal IgA network, FoxO signaling, and PI3K/Akt signaling.

Tryptophyl-proline is a dipeptide produced during protein digestion. While most dipeptides are short-lived, tryptophan-containing peptides can influence immune homeostasis. Indeed, gut microbes metabolize tryptophan into bioactive ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) in epithelial and immune cells. AHR activation by tryptophan catabolites is well-documented to promote barrier integrity and regulatory immunity (for example, inducing IgA+ regulatory T cells). Thus, an increase in tryptophyl-proline may reflect enhanced tryptophan availability or proteolysis, feeding into these AHR-mediated pathways. Conceivably, tryptophyl-proline could itself or upon breakdown yield AHR agonists, or serve as a nutrient signal to immune cells. Such mechanisms are consistent with reports that dietary tryptophan and its microbiota-derived metabolites augment mucosal IgA and suppress inflammation (Seo et al., 2023).

4-Hydroxycinnamoylagmatine is a phenolic amide formed from p-coumaric acid and agmatine. This compound belongs to hydroxycinnamic acid amides (HCAAs), a class of plant secondary metabolites (e.g. barley hordatines) that accumulate under stress (Stevenson et al., 2018). In human gut context, dietary hydroxycinnamic acids (from grains, fruits) and microbial enzymes can generate similar amides. HCAAs are known antioxidants and have antimicrobial properties, suggesting that 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine might modulate oxidative stress or microbial growth in the gut. Its elevation here may indicate altered polyphenol metabolism or microbiota activity. Although specific data in mammals are limited, plant HCAAs generally attenuate inflammation and support barrier defenses. Thus, increased 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine could reflect a salutary response- for example, enhancing antioxidant capacity or selecting for beneficial microbes- which would merit targeted studies.

Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to elucidate the metabolic pathways influenced by the supplementation of 30 wt% BSG in corn puffed foods, revealing several significantly impacted pathways that contribute to the observed functional enhancements (Table 3). This analysis identified 48 pathways, among which the original p-value of the “Intestinal IgA network” pathway was 0.0011, and after FDR correction, it was 0.0916 (in the marginally significant range), but its Pathway Impact reached 1.0, suggesting that this pathway occupies a key topological position in the overall metabolic remodeling. Combined with the significant upregulation of key metabolites (such as 9-cis-Retinoic acid), we believe that this pathway has biological significance. Further studies with larger sample sizes are still needed to obtain more robust statistical significance. This pathway indicates that our metabolite changes are related to the mucosal antibody response. Secretory IgA is the main antibody on the intestinal surface, coordinating the symbiotic relationship between the host and microorganisms (Wu et al., 2017). Notably, metabolites derived from the gut microbiota have been shown to regulate IgA. For example, acetate (a short-chain fatty acid from fiber fermentation) stimulates dendritic cells to produce retinoic acid through the GPR43 receptor, thereby promoting B cells that secrete IgA. Although our study did not directly measure short-chain fatty acids, this concept has broad applicability: changes in the metabolite pool can enhance IgA through “metabolite sensing” pathways. For instance, tryptophan metabolites can also enhance IgA+ regulatory T cells and B cells by activating AHR. Overall, the association between our differential metabolites and the IgA pathway suggests that nutritional intervention may have enhanced mucosal defense. Total: number of metabolites in each pathway; Hits: number of significantly altered metabolites; P-value: significance by hypergeometric test (smaller = more significant); FDR: Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted false discovery rate; Impact: pathway impact score reflecting the topological influence of the detected metabolites.

The P-values for the “small cell lung cancer” and “gastric cancer” pathways were 0.00321 and 0.00627 respectively, with an FDR of 0.0916 (at the threshold). Notably, both of these pathways share the same key metabolite, 9-cis retinoic acid, with the “intestinal IgA network” pathway, and this metabolite shows a significant upward trend. While the enrichment of pathways labeled as “small cell lung cancer” and “gastric cancer” reflects shared signaling components (e.g., 9-cis-retinoic acid, FoxO, and PI3K-Akt pathways), these pathways are associated with immune and metabolic regulation in this study and do not imply direct anticancer effects. For instance, 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine, a phenolic amide, may contribute to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, supporting immune modulation rather than cancer-related signaling inhibition.

Additionally, the “Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism” pathway (P = 0.237, Impact = 0.4277) and “Tryptophan metabolism” (P = 0.5226, Impact = 0.1466) were affected, supporting the observed upregulation of Tryptophyl-Proline, which likely contributes to neuroprotection and immune modulation via serotonin and melatonin synthesis. The “FoxO signaling pathway” (P = 0.0102, Impact = 0.4) and “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway” (P = 0.1261, Impact = 0.25) were also enriched. The observed enrichment of FoxO and PI3K/Akt pathways provides mechanistic insight into immune-metabolic regulation. FoxO transcription factors (FoxO1/3) serve as metabolic “master regulators” in immune cells (Yan et al., 2020). For example, in macrophages FoxO1 controls polarization: deletion of FoxO1 shifts cells toward an anti-inflammatory M2 state with reduced glycolysis. In other words, FoxO1 connects metabolic flux to immune phenotype, acting as a “bridge” between metabolism and function. Importantly, FoxO activity is regulated by PI3K/Akt signaling: growth signals activate PI3K/Akt which phosphorylates (and inhibits) FoxOs, whereas nutrient deprivation (e.g. fasting or caloric restriction) attenuates PI3K/Akt and thus relieves FoxO inhibition (Okawa et al., 2021). Our pathway analysis suggests that the treatment impacted this axis. Indeed, prior reports show that overnutrition drives persistent PI3K/Akt/mTOR activation favoring pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages, whereas calorie restriction suppresses PI3K/Akt and activates FoxO and AMPK pathways to support quiescent or regulatory phenotypes. In this context, our data hint that the altered metabolites may feedback on PI3K/Akt-FoxO signaling. For example, increased tryptophan metabolites (through AHR) and antioxidant amides could mimic a “low energy” signal, while glutarate derivatives might impact cellular redox. The net effect would be modulation of immune cell metabolism (glycolysis vs. oxidative phosphorylation) via PI3K/Akt-FoxO, consistent with improved anti-inflammatory tone.

These interpretations align with existing studies on nutrition-microbiota-immune interactions. Short-chain fatty acids promoting IgA through GPR43 and tryptophan metabolites engaging AHR are well documented (Wu et al., 2017). Likewise, dietary restriction or insulin signaling modifications are known to toggle PI3K/Akt versus FoxO activity Our findings extend this knowledge by identifying specific metabolite fingerprints associated with those pathways. For instance, few studies have highlighted (S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)glutarate or 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine in a nutritional context; their emergence here suggests novel links. Moreover, detection of tryptophyl-proline supports the concept that protein digestion products can signal to the immune system, as suggested by reports of peptide-mediated lymphocyte activation (Seo & Kwon, 2023). Overall, the consistency of our pathway enrichment with known mechanisms (IgA induction by metabolites, FoxO-mediated macrophage metabolism, diet-regulated PI3K/FoxO balance) strengthens the validity of the results.

The metabolomic signatures we identified could serve as biomarkers or targets for dietary modulation of gut health. For example, increasing intake of prebiotic fibers and phenolic-rich foods may boost beneficial metabolites (like acetate or 4-hydroxycinnamoylamines) that enhance IgA-mediated barrier function. Similarly, ensuring adequate tryptophan intake (or administration of probiotic strains that produce AHR ligands) might promote regulatory immune responses via the FoxO/AHR axis (Seo & Kwon, 2023). The down-stream convergence on PI3K/Akt-FoxO suggests that overall caloric and macronutrient balance remains important: diets that avoid chronic insulin/IGF-1 overstimulation will favor FoxO-driven homeostasis. In practical terms, monitoring levels of these metabolites (e.g. glutarate derivatives or peptide markers) could inform personalized nutrition strategies. Our study thus provides a mechanistic rationale for functional foods or supplements aimed at increasing intestinal IgA (e.g. fermentable fibers, retinoids) and reprogramming immune metabolism (e.g. polyphenols, ketogenic elements that activate AMPK/Sirtuins).

In summary, the altered metabolites we observed highlight an intersection of host and microbial metabolism. The enrichment of IgA, FoxO, and PI3K/Akt pathways suggests that these metabolic changes feed into canonical immune and energy-sensing networks. This is in line with literature showing that gut-derived metabolites can tune antibody responses and that nutrient signals dictate immune cell metabolism (Okawa et al.,2021). The new links uncovered-notably involving (S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)glutarate, tryptophyl-proline, and 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine add to our understanding by pinpointing specific molecular mediators. Future work should explore whether dietary modulation of these metabolites can beneficially influence gut immunity. Altogether, our findings underscore the scientific value of integrating metabolomics with immunometabolic pathways and point toward potential nutritional interventions to improve gut immune health.

The “Biosynthesis of Various Other Secondary Metabolites” pathway involves non-essential secondary metabolites derived from plants, microorganisms, and fungi, including alkaloids, steroidal compounds, terpenes, and phenolics. Although these metabolites are not directly implicated in fundamental physiological processes, they play a pivotal role in modulating biological activities such as antibacterial, antiviral, antioxidant, and antitumor effects. Metabolomic analysis indicates that this pathway comprises 67 metabolites, of which eight are significantly differential (screening criteria: |log₂FC| ≥ 0.5 and FDR < 0.05), displaying a pronounced upregulation trend. Compared to the non-extruded group, the expression levels of these differential metabolites exhibit highly significant changes following extrusion treatment (P < 0.01), suggesting that the extrusion process markedly activates the metabolic activity of this pathway.

The “Staurosporine Biosynthesis” pathway is responsible for synthesizing staurosporine, a potent natural protein kinase inhibitor. This compound modulates cellular signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis by inhibiting multiple protein kinases, holding significant potential for applications in cancer research and neuroprotection. Metabolomic data demonstrate that this pathway includes 48 metabolites, with six identified as significantly differential (screening criteria: |log₂FC| ≥ 0.1 and FDR < 0.2), exhibiting a bidirectional regulatory pattern (three upregulated and three downregulated). Despite the balanced number of up- and downregulated metabolites, their overall expression changes exert a notable influence on pathway function. The elevated staurosporine levels suggest that activation of this pathway may be associated with antitumor and neuroprotective effects, particularly in regulating apoptosis and signal transduction (Sarkar et al., 2024).

The “Isoquinoline Alkaloid Biosynthesis” pathway generates isoquinoline alkaloids through the metabolism of aromatic amino acids, such as tyrosine. These compounds, widely distributed in plants and microorganisms, exhibit significant pharmacological activities, including analgesic, sedative, antibacterial, and antitumor effects. Activation of this pathway modulates various physiological functions by enhancing the expression of related metabolites. Metabolomic analysis reveals that this pathway encompasses 131 metabolites, with 11 identified as significantly differential (screening criteria: |log₂FC| ≥ 0.5 and FDR < 0.2), predominantly showing upregulation (nine upregulated and two downregulated). The overall activity of the pathway is significantly enhanced (pathway impact score = 0.16, P < 0.01), with its regulatory effects closely aligned with the pharmacological properties of alkaloids.

Conclusion

This research confirms that incorporating 25–35 wt% BSG into corn flour via single-screw extrusion can significantly enhance the functional and nutritional values of the resulting extrudates. Extrusion not only elevates the WHC (ranging from 0.34 ± 0.015 to 0.4 ± 0.012 g/g) and OHC (around 0.21 g/g), and increases the TDF content (from 0.27 ± 0.01 to 0.34 ± 0.014 g/g), but also facilitates the release of bound polyphenols. During the simulated intestinal digestion process, the total polyphenol content (TPC) in the digestion supernatant of the 35 wt% BSG group reached 7.70 ± 0.24 mg GAE/g sample. Moreover, the antioxidant activity is enhanced, and the inhibition rate of α-amylase approaches 50 %. Metabolomic analysis further discloses the upregulation of key bioactive compounds, such as (S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)glutaric acid, tryptophyl-proline, and 4-hydroxycinnamoylagmatine, along with the enrichment of health-related pathways, including the production of intestinal immunoglobulin A, FoxO, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways. Among the formulated samples tested, the 30 wt% BSG mixture strikes an optimal balance between enhanced bioactivity and acceptable sensory attributes. These findings underscore the potential of extrusion-cooked BSG-corn formulations as value-added, cereal-based functional foods and offer a mechanistic framework for subsequent product development and clinical evaluation.
