Challenging diagnosis of adult-onset still’s disease (AOSD) in a young male: a case report highlighting the importance of early recognition and management

Introduction

Adult-Onset Still’s Disease (AOSD) is a rare systemic inflammatory disorder first described in adults in 1971, recognized as a continuation of systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) when presenting in individuals over 16 years of age[1]. Recent consensus from the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and Pediatric Rheumatology European Society (PReS) suggests that sJIA and AOSD represent the same disease spectrum, sharing clinical and immunological features[2]. AOSD is characterized by a triad of high-grade fever, polyarthritis, and a distinctive salmon-colored maculopapular rash, with systemic manifestations such as hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy[3]. The global incidence of AOSD is estimated at 0.16–0.62 per 100 000 individuals, with a prevalence of 0.73–6.77 per 100 000[4]. It exhibits a bimodal age distribution, with peaks at 15–25 and 36–46 years, though cases in younger adolescents are increasingly reported[5]. The pathophysiology of AOSD involves dysregulated innate immunity, with overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1 and IL-6 driving systemic inflammation[6]. These cytokines are key therapeutic targets, with IL-1 and IL-6 inhibitors showing promise in refractory cases[7].

Diagnosing AOSD is challenging due to its non-specific presentation and overlap with infections (e.g., sepsis), malignancies (e.g., lymphoma), and autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE])[8]. The Yamaguchi criteria, the most widely used diagnostic tool, require at least five features, including two major criteria (fever >39°C for >1 week, arthritis >2 weeks, typical rash, leukocytosis >10 000/µL with >80% neutrophils) and exclusion of alternative diagnoses[9]. Laboratory findings typically include elevated ferritin (>1000 ng/mL), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and microcytic hypochromic anemia[10].

Treatment strategies for AOSD aim to control inflammation and prevent complications. Corticosteroids and NSAIDs are first-line therapies, with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) like methotrexate used for steroid-refractory cases[11]. Biologic agents targeting IL-1 (e.g., anakinra) or IL-6 (e.g., tocilizumab) are reserved for severe or resistant disease[2]. Despite advances, AOSD remains underdiagnosed, particularly in younger patients, where its overlap with sJIA complicates clinical decision-making.

This case report presents a 16-year-old male diagnosed with AOSD, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the importance of early intervention to achieve remission. By detailing the clinical course, laboratory findings, and treatment response, we aim to raise awareness of AOSD in younger populations and underscore its inclusion in the differential diagnosis of systemic inflammatory syndromes.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male, the second child of non-consanguineous parents, presented to a tertiary care hospital on 10 November 2024 with a 2-month history of high-grade intermittent fever (maximum 102°F). The fever was relieved by paracetamol, accompanied by profuse sweating, but lacked chills, respiratory symptoms, or gastrointestinal complaints. For the past 1.5 months, he experienced symmetric polyarthritis affecting large joints (knees, ankles) and small joints (metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal) of all four limbs, with morning stiffness lasting approximately 30 min. A non-itchy, evanescent salmon-colored maculopapular rash appeared 1.5 months prior, primarily on the trunk, dorsum of hands, extensor forearms, and feet, resolving within hours (Fig. 1). The patient denied oral ulcers, photosensitivity, hair loss, Raynaud’s phenomenon, or neurological symptoms suggestive of alternative autoimmune conditions. Initial consultations with multiple physicians yielded no improvement, prompting admission on 10 November 2024. After 6 days of persistent symptoms, he was referred to another tertiary care center on 16 November 2024 for specialized evaluation. His medical history included childhood pneumonia (not requiring hospitalization) and chickenpox at age 7. He reported occasional use of NSAIDs and prednisolone for joint pain over the preceding months, with each episode involving brief flares of joint pain (2–3 days) responsive to short-term analgesics, but no chronic medication use. He was a non-smoker, non-alcoholic, and had no history of recreational drug use or unprotected sexual exposure. His immunization history was complete, including vaccinations for COVID-19 and Hepatitis B.

On examination (16 November 2024), vital signs were stable: pulse 82 beats per minute, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, temperature 100°F, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, and SpO2 98% on room air. Dermatological examination confirmed an evanescent salmon-colored maculopapular rash on the dorsum of both hands, extensor forearms, and feet, consistent with AOSD (Fig. 1). The rash was non-palpable, non-pruritic, and resolved within hours, distinguishing it from persistent reticular lesions. Musculoskeletal examination revealed tender and swollen metacarpophalangeal joints (Grade II tenderness) without deformity or restricted movement. Ankle joints were swollen bilaterally but non-tender. Abdominal examination identified splenomegaly, with no hepatomegaly or ascites. Fundoscopy revealed Roth spots in both optic discs, an uncommon finding suggestive of systemic inflammation rather than infective endocarditis, given the absence of cardiac symptoms. No lymphadenopathy, cardiovascular, respiratory, or neurological abnormalities were noted. A bedside urine test was negative for albumin and glucose.

Laboratory investigations (Table 1, Table 2) revealed significant abnormalities. On 16 November 2024, hemoglobin was 9.8 g/dL (reference: 14–18 g/dL), white blood cell (WBC) count was 21,200/µL (reference: 4000–11 000/µL) with 93% neutrophils, ESR was 146 mm/1st hour (reference: 0–10 mm), CRP was 98.6 mg/L (reference: < 10 mg/L), and serum ferritin was 859 ng/mL (reference: 15–120 ng/mL). Peripheral blood smear showed microcytic hypochromic anemia with anisocytosis and target cells. Repeat complete blood count (CBC) on 17 November showed a further hemoglobin decline to 8.6 g/dL and WBC reduction to 16 800/µL (Fig. 2). Serum iron profile (20 November) indicated low serum iron (35 µg/dL, reference: 37–170 µg/dL), elevated ferritin (780 ng/mL), and reduced total iron-binding capacity (TIBC, 115 µg/dL, reference: 274–494 µg/dL). Additional tests excluded alternative diagnoses. Blood cultures (18 November) showed no growth, ruling out sepsis. Urine examination (24 November) revealed 10–12 dysmorphic red blood cells (RBCs) per high-power field, suggestive of glomerular involvement, a rare AOSD manifestation. Hemoglobin electrophoresis (23 November) was normal, ruling out hemoglobinopathies. Autoantibody tests, including anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), and anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), were negative. HLA-B27, perinuclear anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (P-ANCA), and cytoplasmic ANCA (C-ANCA) were also negative. Abdominal ultrasonography (27 November) confirmed splenomegaly and a mildly thickened gallbladder wall. Electrocardiography and echocardiography (22 November) were normal, excluding cardiac involvement such as endocarditis.

A bone marrow study (9 December 2024) showed erythroid hyperplasia, a decreased myeloid-to-erythroid ratio, active granulopoiesis, and normal megakaryopoiesis, ruling out leukemia (Fig. 3). Skin biopsy (12 December) from the evanescent rash showed non-specific inflammation without immunoglobulin or complement deposition, consistent with AOSD and excluding lupus or vasculitis. Treatment was initiated with oral prednisolone 60 mg/day for 1 month and naproxen 500 mg twice daily as needed. The decision to start corticosteroids early was driven by the severity of fever and arthritis, which warranted rapid inflammation control over NSAIDs alone. Due to partial response (persistent fever and rash), methotrexate 10 mg/week was added, along with folic acid 5 mg/day to mitigate side effects. Prednisolone was tapered over 21 days (40 mg/day for 7 days, 20 mg/day for 7 days, 10 mg/day for 7 days) to minimize steroid dependence. A transient rash reappeared during tapering, prompting temporary re-escalation of prednisolone to 20 mg/day for 7 days before resuming the taper. Cefuroxime 500 mg twice daily was prescribed empirically for 14 days due to initial diagnostic uncertainty regarding a possible occult infection, despite negative blood cultures. Esomeprazole 20 mg twice daily was given for 2 months to prevent gastrointestinal side effects. Following methotrexate initiation, fever, arthritis, and rash resolved completely, and the patient remains in remission.

Discussion

AOSD is a rare systemic inflammatory disorder with an annual incidence of 0.16–0.62 per 100 000 and a prevalence of 0.73–6.77 per 100 000 worldwide[4]. Its presentation in a 16-year-old male is uncommon, as 45–80% of cases occur in late adolescence or early adulthood[5,12]. The EULAR/PReS recommendations recognize sJIA and AOSD as part of the same disease spectrum, justifying the consideration of AOSD in adolescents with compatible features[2]. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of AOSD, particularly when atypical features like microcytic anemia and Roth spots mimic other conditions such as leukemia or infective endocarditis.

The patient’s clinical presentation – intermittent fever, symmetric polyarthritis, and evanescent salmon-colored maculopapular rash – is hallmark of AOSD[8]. The rash’s evanescent nature, resolving within hours, aligns with classic AOSD descriptions, distinguishing it from persistent reticular rashes seen in conditions like vasculitis or drug reactions[10]. Skin biopsy revealed non-specific inflammation, consistent with AOSD’s histological profile, which typically lacks immune complex deposition[10]. Laboratory findings, including neutrophilic leukocytosis (21 200/µL), elevated ferritin (859 ng/mL), ESR (146 mm/1st hour), and CRP (98.6 mg/L), further supported the diagnosis[13].

Diagnostic challenges arose from atypical features. Microcytic hypochromic anemia (hemoglobin 8.6 g/dL) raised suspicion for AML, but bone marrow findings of erythroid hyperplasia excluded malignancy[13]. The anemia was attributed to chronic inflammation, a common AOSD feature driven by cytokine-mediated suppression of erythropoiesis[7]. Roth spots, typically associated with infective endocarditis, were likely due to AOSD’s systemic inflammatory effects, possibly mimicking vasculitis, as no cardiac abnormalities (e.g., murmurs, abnormal ECG) were identified[14]. The presence of 10–12 dysmorphic RBCs in urine suggested glomerular inflammation, a rare but reported manifestation of AOSD-related renal involvement[15]. These findings underscore the need for a broad differential diagnosis, including hematological, infectious, and autoimmune conditions, when evaluating AOSD.

The Yamaguchi criteria were instrumental in confirming AOSD, with the patient meeting all major (fever, arthritis, rash, leukocytosis) and several minor criteria (sore throat, splenomegaly, deranged liver function)[9]. Negative autoantibodies (ANA, RF, anti-CCP, anti-dsDNA) and exclusion of sepsis, malignancy, and vasculitis solidified the diagnosis. The equal gender distribution in AOSD, unlike sJIA’s female predominance, suggests unique immunological pathways, though specific triggers (e.g., environmental factors, genetic polymorphisms) remain understudied[3].

Treatment was guided by evidence-based protocols. Initial use of prednisolone (60 mg/day) was justified by the severity of symptoms, bypassing NSAIDs alone, as recommended for acute AOSD with systemic features[11]. Naproxen was added for pain relief. Due to partial response, methotrexate (10 mg/week) was introduced as a steroid-sparing agent, leading to complete remission, consistent with its efficacy in AOSD[16]. Prednisolone tapering over 21 days minimized steroid-related complications, with temporary re-escalation addressing a transient rash flare. Cefuroxime was prescribed empirically during initial diagnostic uncertainty, as persistent fever raised concerns for occult infection, but negative blood cultures suggest it was unnecessary. Folic acid and esomeprazole were appropriately used to prevent methotrexate and steroid side effects, respectively.

Certain diagnostic tests were not performed due to resource limitations in the clinical setting. Glycosylated ferritin, though specific for AOSD when low (<20%), is not widely available, and its absence did not hinder diagnosis given the robust clinical and laboratory evidence[2]. Hand X-rays were omitted, as they are often normal in early AOSD and the patient’s joint symptoms were inflammatory rather than erosive[10]. Genetic studies and cytokine profiling (e.g., IL-1, IL-6 levels) were not feasible due to cost and infrastructure constraints but could provide insights into AOSD’s pathogenesis in future research, particularly regarding IL-1 and IL-6 dysregulation[6]. The consideration of AOSD in a 16-year-old aligns with EULAR/PReS recommendations, which advocate for a unified approach to sJIA and AOSD, recognizing their shared pathophysiology[2].

AOSD’s prognosis is generally favorable, with a mortality rate below 3%, though complications like reactive hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, fulminant hepatitis, or amyloidosis can occur[15]. This patient experienced no such complications, and his response to methotrexate underscores its role in achieving sustained remission. The case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration, including rheumatology, hematology, and dermatology input, to navigate AOSD’s diagnostic and therapeutic complexities, particularly in younger patients where the disease is less commonly considered.

This case also emphasizes the clinical relevance of AOSD in resource-limited settings, where advanced diagnostics like glycosylated ferritin or cytokine assays may be unavailable. Clinicians must rely on clinical criteria, laboratory markers, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses to guide management. Raising awareness of AOSD in younger populations can reduce diagnostic delays, which are critical given the disease’s potential for severe complications if untreated.

Conclusion

This case report illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of AOSD in a 16-year-old male, a demographic where the disease is infrequently reported. The patient’s presentation with fever, polyarthritis, evanescent rash, and elevated inflammatory markers, coupled with fulfillment of the Yamaguchi criteria, confirmed AOSD after excluding alternative diagnoses. Early recognition and treatment with corticosteroids, NSAIDs, and methotrexate were critical to achieving remission, highlighting the efficacy of tailored immunosuppressive therapy. Clinicians should consider AOSD in adolescents presenting with systemic inflammatory symptoms, even in resource-limited settings, to prevent diagnostic delays and improve outcomes. This case underscores the overlap between sJIA and AOSD, supporting their classification as a single disease spectrum. Further research is needed to refine diagnostic tools, such as glycosylated ferritin or cytokine profiling, and to optimize management strategies for younger patients with AOSD.
