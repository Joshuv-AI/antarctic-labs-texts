Determinants of successful midsagittal visualization of the fetal corpus callosum using 2D transabdominal ultrasound
Authors: Aylin Yılmaz, Didar Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate factors influencing visualization of the fetal corpus callosum (CC) during mid-trimester ultrasound examinations, with particular focus on gestational age, fetal presentation, and maternal body mass index (BMI).MethodsThis observational study included 860 singleton pregnancies undergoing mid-trimester ultrasound examination between 20 + 0 and 28 + 0 weeks’ gestation. Fetuses with central nervous system or major structural anomalies were excluded. Assessment of the corpus callosum was performed using two-dimensional (2D) transabdominal ultrasound. Visualization was defined as clear identification of the corpus callosum in the true midsagittal plane. Associations between CC visualization and gestational age, fetal presentation, and maternal BMI were analyzed using chi-square tests and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsThe corpus callosum was successfully visualized in 748 of 860 examinations (87.0%). Visualization rates decreased significantly with advancing gestational age (p < 0.001). Successful visualization was lowest in cephalic presentation (79.8%) and highest in breech presentation (97.7%) (p < 0.001). Maternal obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) was associated with reduced visualization rates compared with BMI < 30 kg/m² (80.2% vs. 89.5%, p < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, breech presentation remained an independent predictor of successful visualization compared with cephalic presentation (adjusted OR 9.35, 95% CI 4.25–20.57, p < 0.001). Maternal obesity independently reduced the likelihood of visualization (adjusted OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.32–0.76, p = 0.001). Each one-week increase in gestational age was associated with a 15% decrease in visualization likelihood (adjusted OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.78–0.92, p < 0.001). Gentle hand manipulation was required in 423 examinations (49.2%) and was significantly more frequent in cephalic presentation than in breech or transverse presentation (62.7% vs. 30.2% and 39.0%, respectively; p < 0.001).ConclusionTransabdominal ultrasound enables midsagittal visualization of the fetal corpus callosum in the majority of mid-trimester examinations. Fetal presentation, gestational age, and maternal obesity exert a significant influence on visualization rates; in particular, maternal obesity and increasing gestational age are associated with a reduced probability of successful assessment. Gentle hand manipulation was frequently required, highlighting the technical challenges of midsagittal corpus callosum imaging during routine examinations.

Introduction

The corpus callosum (CC) is the largest commissural structure of the brain and plays a central role in interhemispheric integration of motor, sensory, and cognitive functions [1, 2]. Abnormalities of the corpus callosum are clinically significant due to their association with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, and syndromic conditions [3, 4]. Accordingly, dependable prenatal evaluation of the corpus callosum represents an essential element in the comprehensive assessment of the fetal central nervous system.

Prenatal sonographic assessment of the corpus callosum can be performed using indirect or direct approaches. Total agenesis of the corpus callosum may produce indirect findings on axial planes, such as absence of the cavum septi pellucidi, colpocephaly, or a “tear-drop” configuration of the lateral ventricles [5, 6]. However, partial agenesis, dysgenesis, hypoplasia, or morphologic abnormalities may not generate consistent axial abnormalities [7, 8].

Direct visualization of the corpus callosum in the true midsagittal plane allows comprehensive assessment of its anatomical components, including the rostrum, genu, body, and splenium [9–11]. Accurate identification of the midsagittal plane is therefore essential for excluding partial or subtle abnormalities. Despite its diagnostic value, midsagittal imaging is not universally mandated in routine fetal anomaly screening protocols [12, 13]. Moreover, technical factors such as fetal presentation, advancing gestational age, maternal obesity, and operator-related challenges may limit consistent acquisition of the midsagittal view in daily clinical practice [14].

Although several studies have reported high visualization rates under structured training conditions, real-world clinical determinants of successful transabdominal midsagittal visualization remain incompletely defined.

This study aims to evaluate the technical feasibility of visualizing the fetal corpus callosum using 2D transabdominal ultrasound and to identify clinical and technical factors associated with successful midsagittal visualization, rather than to assess diagnostic accuracy or clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

This observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral center and analyzed data recorded during real-time fetal ultrasound examinations performed between 20 + 0 and 28 + 0 weeks of gestation.

Pregnancies complicated by suspected or confirmed central nervous system abnormalities, chromosomal anomalies, major structural malformations, multiple gestations, or abnormal amniotic fluid volumes (oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios) were excluded. All eligible examinations performed during the study period were consecutively included. A total of 860 examinations were included in the final analysis.

All ultrasound examinations were performed using real-time two-dimensional (2D) transabdominal ultrasound with a Samsung V8 ultrasound system (Samsung Medison, Seoul, South Korea) equipped with a convex probe. All examinations were performed by two experienced maternal–fetal medicine specialists following a standardized institutional protocol.

Assessment of the fetal corpus callosum was performed in the true midsagittal plane. This plane was identified by visualization of standard midline anatomical landmarks, including the cavum septi pellucidi, third ventricle, brainstem, and cerebellar vermis. Visualization was defined as clear identification of the corpus callosum in the true midsagittal plane, allowing visualization of its anatomical components (rostrum, genu, body, and splenium).

To obtain the true midsagittal plane, a systematic approach was applied as previously described [14]. The midsagittal plane was acquired using appropriate insonation angles through the anterior or posterior fontanelle, depending on fetal head position, in accordance with current neurosonography recommendations [15]. In selected cases, a fetal profile or coronal approach with subsequent rotation to the midsagittal plane was employed. Fine transducer adjustments and side-to-side movements were performed to optimize image quality. When necessary, gentle manual manipulation was applied to facilitate acquisition of the midsagittal view.

Cases in which a midsagittal plane could not be obtained or the corpus callosum could not be clearly identified at the initial examination were classified as unsuccessful visualization. Visualization status was determined based on the initial examination attempt, even if visualization was achieved during a subsequent reassessment.

Gestational age was determined based on first-trimester crown–rump length measurement and categorized into three groups (20–22, 23–25, and 26–28 weeks) for subgroup analyses. Fetal presentation at the time of examination was recorded as cephalic, breech, or transverse. Maternal body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters and categorized as < 30 kg/m² or ≥ 30 kg/m². The requirement for gentle manual manipulation was also recorded.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2025/010.99/22/26) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) and were compared using the Pearson chi-square test.

Trend analysis across gestational age groups was evaluated using the linear-by-linear association test. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyses or considered clinically relevant were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of corpus callosum visualization. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In the present study, transabdominal midsagittal visualization of the fetal corpus callosum was successfully achieved in 87% of routine mid-trimester examinations. This finding demonstrates that direct assessment of the CC in the true midsagittal plane is feasible in the majority of cases under standard clinical screening conditions. Earlier studies reported lower visualization rates; however, more recent publications have documented substantial improvements, largely attributed to advances in ultrasound technology, enhanced image resolution, standardized scanning protocols, and structured sonographer training [16, 17].

Importantly, when analysis was restricted to the early mid-trimester subgroup (20–22 weeks), visualization rates in our cohort were comparable to those reported by Fernando et al., who demonstrated a 92.1% complete visualization rate [16]. It should be noted, however, that methodological differences in visualization criteria may influence reported rates. In their study, complete and partial visualization were scored using a graded scale, whereas in our study visualization was defined strictly as clear identification of the corpus callosum in the true midsagittal plane, with partial visualization classified as unsuccessful.

The strict definition of successful visualization used in this study may limit comparability with studies using partial or graded criteria. However, this approach provides a clear and consistent assessment of complete corpus callosum visualization, which may be particularly relevant in studies focusing on technical feasibility.

In our study, increasing gestational age was independently associated with a reduced likelihood of successful midsagittal corpus callosum visualization. This finding suggests that earlier assessment within the mid-trimester period may offer more favorable technical conditions for imaging. With advancing gestation, increasing fetal size and more stable fetal positioning may limit the flexibility required to obtain optimal insonation angles during transabdominal scanning.

Regarding fetal presentation, visualization rates were lowest in cephalic presentation and highest in breech presentation. In multivariate analysis, breech presentation remained a strong independent predictor of successful midsagittal visualization compared with cephalic presentation. This finding suggests that fetal presentation is an important determinant of midsagittal imaging feasibility, likely due to more favorable insonation angles when the fetal head is positioned away from the maternal pelvis. In contrast, cephalic presentation, particularly when the head is deeply engaged or posteriorly positioned, may limit optimal transabdominal alignment and restrict image acquisition.

Previous studies have shown that increased maternal BMI and excessive gestational weight gain are associated with adverse obstetric outcomes and may also affect the technical performance of prenatal ultrasonography [18, 19]. In our cohort, maternal obesity was significantly associated with reduced success of transabdominal midsagittal corpus callosum visualization, with women having a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² showing approximately half the odds of successful visualization compared with those with BMI < 30 kg/m². This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that increased maternal body habitus may impair ultrasonographic image quality through greater tissue attenuation, reduced signal penetration, and diminished spatial resolution [20, 21]. These limitations may reduce the clarity of midline structures and complicate acquisition of a true midsagittal plane during routine second-trimester examinations. Importantly, our strict definition of successful visualization may have further accentuated the technical impact of maternal obesity.

Although international guidelines do not mandate routine midsagittal imaging in low-risk fetal anomaly screening [12, 13], ISUOG guidelines for targeted neurosonography identify the midsagittal plane as the reference plane for comprehensive assessment of midline brain structures, including the corpus callosum [15]. The guideline further emphasizes that adequate visualization of the midsagittal plane may require gentle manipulation of the fetal head to optimize insonation through the anterior or posterior fontanelle [15].

In our cohort, external gentle manual manipulation was required in nearly half of all examinations and was significantly more frequent in cephalic presentations, likely reflecting technical challenges related to fetal head engagement within the maternal pelvis and restricted insonation angles. Gentle external abdominal pressure may help improve probe–fetal alignment and facilitate acquisition of the true midsagittal plane. In clinical practice, gentle manual manipulation may be considered when acquisition of the true midsagittal plane is technically challenging.

Non-visualization of the corpus callosum on a single examination should be interpreted as a technical limitation rather than an indication of abnormality. In routine clinical practice, repeat assessment may be performed when visualization is unsuccessful at the initial examination. However, repeat evaluations were not systematically recorded and no standardized follow-up protocol was applied in the present study. Therefore, follow-up data were not available for analysis.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may limit generalizability to different clinical settings with varying levels of operator experience and equipment quality. Although the examinations were performed by two experienced maternal–fetal medicine specialists following a standardized protocol, interobserver variability was not systematically assessed across the entire cohort. Cases were distributed between the two operators, and each case was evaluated by a single operator without duplicate assessment, precluding formal analysis of interobserver agreement. Second, the study design reflects routine clinical practice conditions, and therefore certain potentially relevant technical variables, such as exact fetal head orientation or degree of head engagement, were not quantified. The effect of detailed fetal head position (e.g., occiput anterior vs. occiput posterior) on visualization success was not evaluated, as this parameter was not systematically recorded and may represent an important technical factor influencing midsagittal imaging. The absence of systematic follow-up data represents an important limitation of the present study and restricts interpretation of the clinical significance of non-visualization.

Nevertheless, the large sample size, inclusion of consecutive routine screening examinations, multivariate adjustment for relevant clinical factors, and incorporation of operator-dependent gentle manipulation as a real-world variable represent important strengths of the present study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, transabdominal ultrasound enables successful midsagittal visualization of the fetal corpus callosum in the majority of mid-trimester examinations. However, the rate of successful visualization declines with advancing gestational age and increasing maternal obesity, whereas breech presentation is associated with a significantly higher likelihood of successful midsagittal visualization. The frequent requirement for gentle manual manipulation indicates the technical challenges involved in acquiring midsagittal images during routine mid-trimester examinations. Awareness of these determinants may help anticipate technically challenging examinations and guide appropriate scanning strategies during routine obstetric ultrasound.
