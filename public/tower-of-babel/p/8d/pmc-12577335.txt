Clinical effect of dexmedetomidine combined with landiolol hydrochloride on cerebral oxygen metabolism and inflammatory response in patients with severe craniocerebral injury
Authors: Peng Zhou, Di Wen, Jiyuan Bu, Yang Wang, Jun Chen, Li Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo study dexmedetomidine’s clinical effect and safety combined with landiolol hydrochloride on cerebral oxygen metabolism and inflammatory response in patients with severe craniocerebral injury.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 66 patients with severe craniocerebral injury underwent surgery between January 2018 and December 2023 in the neurosurgery department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. The patients only treated with dexmedetomidine before anesthesia induction were classified as the control group, while the treatment group was treated with dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride. The levels of the cerebral oxygen metabolism indexes and inflammatory factors were obtained from all patients for analysis at five-time points: before anesthesia induction (T0), immediately after surgery (T1), 12 h after surgery (T2), 24 h after surgery (T3), and 72 h after surgery (T4). The cerebral injury-related indexes were obtained at 14 days after treatment. The cerebral oxygen metabolism indexes included the blood oxygen saturation of jugular vein bulb (SjvO2), the arteriovenous oxygen content difference (AvdO2) and the cerebral oxygen extraction rate (CerO2). The inflammatory factors included the serum concentration of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). The cerebral injury-related indexes included the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score and the acute physiology and chronic health status II (APACHE II) score.ResultsCompared with T0, the SjvO2 were increased at T1, T2, T3, and T4 in two groups, while the CerO2 and the AvdO2 were decreased (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, the SjvO2 were increased at T3 (P = 0.049) and T4 (P < 0.001), the AvdO2 and CerO2 were decreased at T1, T2, T3 and T4 in the treatment group (P < 0.001). Compared with T0, the serum concentration of TNF-α, IL-6, NSE and GFAP were increased at T1, T2, T3 and T4 in two groups (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, these inflammatory factors were decreased at T1, T2, T3 and T4 in the treatment group (P < 0.001). After treatment, the GCS score was increased in two groups, while the APACHE II score was decreased (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, the GCS score and the GOS score were higher in the treatment group, and the APACHE II score was lower (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions between the two groups. Compared with the control group, the 1-year survival rate of patients was higher in the treatment group(P = 0.022).ConclusionDexmedetomidine combined with landiolol hydrochloride used for patients with severe craniocerebral injury could reduce postoperative cerebral oxygen metabolism, alleviate inflammatory reactions, and improve brain function, which improves both short-term and long-term prognosis without increasing the incidence of adverse reactions.Keywords: Dexmedetomidine, Landiolol hydrochloride, Severe craniocerebral injury, Cerebral oxygen metabolism indexes, Inflammatory response

Introduction

Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a common acute and critical neurogenic condition in clinical practice, with rapid disease progression, high rates of disability and mortality, and the potential to cause secondary cerebral edema, cerebral ischemia, and hypoxia, disturbances in cerebral oxygen metabolism, and cerebral circulatory disorders within a short period, ultimately leading to poor outcomes [1–3]. Emergency craniotomy is a primary treatment modality for TBI in clinical practice and can reduce mortality to some extent. However, perioperative surgical stress, ischemia-reperfusion injury, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress can lead to further neurological damage and affect prognosis [4]. Therefore, appropriate brain protection therapy during the perioperative period is needed to ensure surgical efficacy while reducing cerebral oxygen metabolism, controlling systemic inflammatory responses, and inhibiting oxidative stress damage to the greatest extent possible to improve neurological function in patients with TBI and reduce the incidence of postoperative complications [5]. Dexmedetomidine is an analgesic and sedative that can mitigate cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury and stress, and it has specific neuroprotective properties [6]. Landiolol hydrochloride is a novel ultra-short-acting β1-receptor blocker, which can reduce infarct size and improve neurological function in rats with cerebral infarction [7]. Studies have shown that the combination use of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride could reduce cerebral perfusion pressure and increased the reabsorption of intracranial fluid by lowering MAP without altering cerebrovascular resistance, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects [8]. However, the effect of combination use has not been reported in TBI. As the Lund concept had suggested, dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride had different mechanisms of action, and their combined use might have a specific synergistic and complementary effect in TBI. To optimize the perioperative management of TBI and reduce the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions in patients, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical information of previous patients underwent a combination of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride treatment.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with severe TBI who underwent surgical treatment at the Department of Neurosurgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2018 and December 2023. The study included comprehensive clinical data collection, encompassing imaging staging, clinical presentation, and surgical treatment strategies, to establish a foundation for clinical diagnosis and management. Before anesthesia induction, the patients only treated with dexmedetomidine (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Approval No.H20090248, Batch No.190525BP) at a dose of 1 µg/kg/h by intravenous infusion for 10 min, followed by a continuous intravenous infusion at 0.4 µg/kg/h, were classified as the control group. Besides the use of dexmedetomidine, the patients also treated with landiolol hydrochloride (Nanjing Hanchen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Approval No.H20203669, Batch No.20230804) at a dose of 0.01 ~ 0.04 mg/kg/min by continuous intravenous infusion, were classified as the treatment group. The research protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. (2022)454). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria

The clinical diagnosis conformed to the relevant diagnostic criteria in the 2016 American “Guidelines for the Management of Severe Traumatic Brain Injury (Fourth Edition)” and was confirmed by cranial CT/MRI to have brain injury. Patients are admitted for surgical treatment within 12 h after the onset of the disease. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3–8. Postoperative follow-up CT confirmed that no further surgery was needed.

Exclusion criteria

Patients who were brain dead or in a deep coma with dilated pupils before surgery. Patients with severe multiple injuries or compound injuries. Patients with severe dysfunction of essential organs such as the heart, lungs, liver, and kidney were also excluded. Patients are allergic to the drugs involved in this study. Patients with severe hypovolemia (Systolic blood pressure < 90mmHg, heart rate (HR) > 120 bpm, moist or mottled skin, urine output < 0.5 ml/kg/h, blood lactate > 4mmol/L). Patients with a history of neurological or psychiatric diseases. Patients with a history of drug abuse or alcoholism.

Conventional treatment strategies

Per the 2016 American “Guidelines for the Management of Severe Traumatic Brain Injury (Fourth Edition),” all enrolled patients were provided routine treatments and nursing care upon admission. They also received treatments to reduce intracranial pressure and hemostasis, control blood pressure and glucose levels, prevent infections, and maintain homeostasis. Active preoperative preparations were carried out, and emergency general anesthesia was administered for craniotomy to remove hematomas and devitalized brain tissue, followed by standard significant craniectomy decompression surgery. All the patients were induced with anesthesia by intravenous injection of midazolam (0.1 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.2–0.4 µg/kg), propofol (1.0–1.2.0.2 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg). Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol (4–6 mg/kg/h). Cisatracurium (3–4 mg) and sufentanil (10 µg) were intermittently added during the operation. The end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure was maintained at 30 ~ 34 mmHg and the bispectral index at 40–60 during the operation. The intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine and landiolol was stopped at the end of the surgery. If the mean arterial pressure (MAP) was less than 60 mmHg during the operation, and factors such as deep anesthesia and insufficient adequate circulating blood volume were excluded, a single intravenous injection of ephedrine 5–10 mg could be given. If the HR was less than 50 bpm during the operation, and cardiac depression caused by deep anesthesia was excluded, a single intravenous injection of atropine 0.3–0.5 mg could be given, which could be repeated (with a cumulative dose not exceeding 2 mg). After the operation, the patients were transferred to the intensive care unit for monitoring and treatment. The decision to extubate the endotracheal tube or to perform a tracheostomy to maintain airway patency was made based on the patient’s level of consciousness and respiratory condition.

Cerebral oxygen metabolism indicators

Blood samples of 2 mL from the jugular bulb and radial artery were collected at five-time points: before anesthesia induction (T0), immediately after surgery (T1), 12 h after surgery (T2), 24 h after surgery (T3), and 72 h after surgery (T4). Blood gas analysis was performed to detect parameters including hemoglobin (Hb), arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂), arterial oxygen saturation (SaO₂), jugular venous oxygen partial pressure (PjvO₂), and jugular bulb oxygen saturation (SjvO₂). Based on these parameters, the following calculations were made: cerebral arterial oxygen content (CaO₂), cerebral venous oxygen content (CjvO₂), arteriovenous oxygen content difference (AVDO₂), and cerebral extraction rate of oxygen (CERO₂). Brain injury markers

At each time point of T0, T1, T2, T3, and T4, 5 ml of peripheral venous blood was drawn from the patients. After centrifugation for 10 min, the serum was separated and stored in a −80℃ freezer for the following tests. Neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Inflammatory markers

Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were detected by ELISA.

Efficacy evaluation and patient follow-up

Compare the changes in GCS scores and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) scores of the two groups before and 14 days after treatment. Compare the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) scores between the two groups 14 days after treatment. Record the occurrence of drug adverse reactions such as bradycardia and hypotension in the two groups after surgery. Follow up the survival conditions of the two groups half a year and one year after treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 23.0. For measurement data, normality and homogeneity of variance were tested. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between groups, independent samples t-test was used, and for comparisons within groups, repeated measures analysis of variance was applied. Count data were expressed as percentages (%) and compared between groups using the χ² test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines

This study further explored the changes in TNF-α and IL-6 at different time points (T0 to T4) between the two groups to assess their differences and their dynamic changes over time. At T0, the levels of TNF-α in the control and treatment groups were 10.70 ± 0.38 pg/ml and 10.82 ± 0.36 pg/ml, respectively, with no statistical difference between the two groups (P = 0.185). At each time point from T1 to T3, the level of TNF-α in the treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group (P < 0.001), indicating that the increase in TNF-α in the treatment group was more moderate compared to the control group. At T4, the levels of TNF-α in both groups decreased to near baseline levels, with no statistical difference between the two groups (P = 0.876). At T4, the levels of IL-6 in the two groups decreased significantly, and the level of IL-6 in the treatment group (36.57 ± 1.05 pg/ml) was significantly lower than that in the control group (45.15 ± 1.76 pg/ml) (P < 0.001). Incidence of adverse reactions

During the treatment period, six patients (18.2%) in the control group experienced hypotension, while one patient (3.0%) in the treatment group had hypotension, with no statistical difference between the two groups (χ²=2.557, P > 0.05). In the control group, four patients (12.1%) had bradycardia, and one patient (3.0%) in the treatment group had bradycardia, with no statistical difference between the two groups (χ²=0.866, P > 0.05).

Efficacy and prognosis

Next, we conducted an in-depth comparative analysis of the two groups of patients to assess the impact of different treatment regimens on patient prognosis. The study showed that the treatment group had significant advantages in several key indicators. First, in terms of GCS scores, the scores of the control and treatment groups before treatment were 5.5 ± 1.4 and 6.0 ± 1.3, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups. However, 14 days after treatment, the GCS score of the control group only increased from 5.5 ± 1.4 to 6.0 ± 1.9, while the GCS score of the treatment group significantly increased from 6.0 ± 1.3 to 10.5 ± 2.9. The GOS score of the control group was 2.8 ± 0.8, which was significantly lower than 3.4 ± 0.8 of the treatment group. This significant difference between the two groups indicates that after receiving treatment, the recovery of neurological function in the treatment group was significantly better than in the control group. Secondly, regarding APACHE II scores, the scores of the two groups before treatment were 23.9 ± 4.5 and 23.2 ± 4.2, respectively, with little difference. But 14 days after treatment, the APACHE II score of the control group decreased from 23.9 ± 4.5 to 20.7 ± 4.0, while the score of the treatment group significantly decreased from 23.2 ± 4.2 to 15.3 ± 4.7. This indicates that after treatment, the severity of the condition in the treatment group was more effectively controlled and improved.

Regarding survival and mortality rates at 6 months and 1 year, the treatment group also showed significant advantages. At 6 months, the survival rate of the control group was 87.9%, and the mortality rate was 12.1%, while the survival rate of the treatment group was as high as 97.0%, and the mortality rate was only 3.0%. Although the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant, the high survival rate of the treatment group is still noteworthy. At 1 year, the survival rate of the control group decreased to 63.6%, and the mortality rate increased to 36.4%, while the survival rate of the treatment group remained at 87.9%, and the mortality rate was only 12.1%. At this time, the difference between the two groups became statistically significant (P = 0.022), further confirming the long-term prognostic advantages of the treatment group. Discussion

Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) has a high incidence and mortality rate and has always been a global concern. Surgical intervention can effectively remove intracranial hematomas and necrotic tissue caused by trauma, relieve intracranial hypertension, and significantly reduce the mortality rate of TBI patients. After TBI, the normal self-regulation function of the brain is impaired. On the one hand, the primary traumatic impact compresses brain tissue, leading to impaired venous return in the brain, which can quickly lead to cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, causing excessive release of central catecholamines, a sharp increase in brain tissue metabolism and further disruption of the oxygen supply and demand balance in the brain due to insufficient blood supply. On the other hand, ischemia-reperfusion injury, surgical anesthesia stress, and accompanying inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress damage, and neuronal apoptosis during the perioperative period interact with each other, further exacerbating brain damage and affecting neurological prognosis [1–3]. Among them, ischemia and hypoxia are the core links in the pathological process of TBI. The brain tissue is susceptible to hypoxia due to its high oxygen consumption in the metabolic process. Therefore, improving brain tissue oxygen metabolism and maintaining the oxygen supply and demand balance in the brain tissue of TBI patients is particularly important in the treatment process [9, 10]. Studies have shown that the blood return of brain tissue mainly passes through the internal jugular vein, so in clinical practice, the SjvO₂, AVDO₂, and CERO₂ indicators calculated from blood sampling at the jugular bulb are often chosen to reflect the blood flow and oxygen metabolism status of brain tissue [10, 11]. SjvO₂ indirectly reflects the overall brain blood flow and oxygen metabolism status. The lower the level of SjvO₂, the more severe the cerebral ischemia and hypoxia. SjvO₂ < 40% indicates cerebral ischemia and hypoxia; AVDO₂ reflects the balance of brain oxygen supply and demand. AVDO₂ < 4 ml O₂/100 ml blood indicates that brain oxygen supply is more significant than brain oxygen demand, and AVDO₂ >8 ml O₂/100 ml blood indicates that brain oxygen supply is less than brain oxygen demand; CERO₂ reflects the oxygen uptake and utilization of brain tissue. The higher its level, the higher the brain oxygen utilization. This study shows that the SjvO₂ of all patients after surgery is above 50%, indicating that neither intervention has led to an imbalance of brain oxygen supply and demand. The SjvO₂ level of the two groups of patients increased immediately after treatment, and the levels of AVDO₂ and CERO₂ decreased to different extents, indicating that different treatment regimens can improve the brain oxygen metabolism of TBI patients. However, the comparison between groups found that the levels of AVDO₂ and CERO₂ in the treatment group were lower than those in the control group after treatment, confirming that the combination of dexmedetomidine and laniclor can further improve the brain oxygen metabolism of TBI patients and maintain the balance of brain oxygen supply and demand.

Traumatic brain injury leads to neuronal damage, triggering a neuroinflammatory cascade that promotes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6, which disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and facilitate the release of brain injury biomarkers into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and subsequently into the bloodstream through the damaged BBB. Numerous studies have shown that the detection of peripheral blood biomarkers can predict the severity and progression of traumatic brain injury [12–14]. NSE is a soluble protein primarily found in neuronal cells and is present in very low amounts in normal blood. In the early stages of traumatic brain injury, NSE is released from the cytoplasm of damaged neuronal cells into the interstitial space and CSF. It then enters the bloodstream through the damaged BBB, making it a sensitive and specific biomarker for traumatic brain injury. GFAP is the only specific marker protein expressed by astrocytes. After brain injury, astrocytes undergo gliosis, and GFAP is released in large amounts into the CSF and then enters the peripheral blood through the damaged BBB [15]. This study’s results show that serum TNF-α, IL-6, and the expression of NSE and GFAP proteins are significantly increased in patients with traumatic brain injury after surgery, indicating that brain injury is inevitable in brain surgery. After treatment, the combination of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride, compared to dexmedetomidine alone, reduced the increase in the above indicators and had a specific neuroprotective effect.

Dexmedetomidine is an α2 receptor agonist with analgesic, sedative, anti-sympathetic, and stress response effects. Numerous studies have shown that dexmedetomidine has protective effects on organ functions, including neuroprotective functions [16]. Multiple studies have shown that dexmedetomidine can inhibit the release of inflammatory mediators and neuroendocrine hormones, reduce ischemic brain damage, stabilize hemodynamics, and maintain intracranial homeostasis [17]. Landiolol hydrochloride is a highly selective β1 receptor blocker, with rapid onset and short half-life, and continuous infusion in the body is not easy to accumulate, which can resist the circulatory effects caused by harmful stimuli to the central nervous system. Studies have shown that β1 receptor blockers reduce the sympathetic excitability of the central nervous system, lower cerebral blood flow, and cerebral metabolic rate in rats with cerebral stroke, and exert neuroprotective effects [18]. When dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride were used in combination, they reduced cerebral perfusion pressure and increased the reabsorption of intracranial fluid by lowering MAP without altering cerebrovascular resistance, thereby reducing the hydrostatic pressure between capillaries. Just as the Lund concept had suggested, dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride had different mechanisms of action, and their combined use had a specific synergistic and complementary effect. Therefore, in this study, we applied the combination of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride to patients with severe traumatic brain injury. The results showed that compared with the control group, the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and NSE and GFAP protein expression in the blood of patients in the treatment group after surgery were significantly reduced, and the GCS score and APACHE II score at 14 days after surgery were improved considerably. Moreover, the follow-up for one year found that the one-year survival rate of the combined treatment group was significantly higher than that of the control group. The possible mechanisms are: (1) Inhibiting the release of neurotransmitters from the synaptic terminals, thereby inhibiting the brain cell signaling pathways, reducing cerebral blood flow, cerebral metabolic rate, and cerebral oxygen consumption, and maintaining the balance of cerebral oxygen supply and demand; (2) By inhibiting the activity of the Toll-like receptor/myeloid differentiation factor 88/nuclear factor-κB pathway, down-regulating the expression of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 [19], reducing central inflammatory reactions, inhibiting BBB damage, and thus inhibiting the excessive release of NSE and GFAP proteins; (3) Acting on the medullary α2 receptor, activating the cholinergic system, reducing sympathetic tension, inhibiting the release of endogenous catecholamines, reducing stress response, stabilizing hemodynamics, and controlling the incidence of adverse reactions; (4) Dilating peripheral blood vessels, controlling mean arterial pressure, reducing capillary hydrostatic pressure, controlling cerebral edema, and reducing intracranial pressure; (5) By reducing cardiac output and hepatic and cerebral organ blood flow, the hepatic and renal clearance rate of general anesthetic drugs is reduced, the half-life of the drugs is prolonged, the anesthetic effect is enhanced, and the sympathetic excitatory response of the central nervous system caused by harmful stimuli is inhibited. In addition, in this study, neither group of patients had severe adverse reactions, and the incidence of adverse reactions was not statistically different (P >0.05), indicating that the addition of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride based on conventional treatment did not significantly increase the incidence of adverse reactions and had good safety.

This study is limited in its retrospective design, small sample size, and interference from confounding factors, such as other medications during the treatment process. Although some patients met the inclusion criteria after admission, they were ultimately excluded from the study due to undergoing major emergency treatment during the perioperative period, which may have caused potential selection bias in this study.

In summary, the combination of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride can reduce postoperative cerebral oxygen metabolism, alleviate inflammatory reactions, and improve brain function in patients with severe traumatic brain injury, which improves both short-term and long-term prognosis without increasing the incidence of adverse reactions. However, the cellular metabolic mechanisms of severe traumatic brain injury are complex, and the sample size of this study is limited. Moreover, many factors affect the prognosis of traumatic brain injury in clinical practice. Further research is needed on the effects of the combination of dexmedetomidine and landiolol hydrochloride on brain tissue metabolism and long-term prognosis in patients with severe traumatic brain injury, as well as their specific mechanisms of action.
