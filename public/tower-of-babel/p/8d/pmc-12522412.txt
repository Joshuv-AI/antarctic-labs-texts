Effect of proanthocyanidins application on push-out bond strength of root canal filling after different final irrigation procedures
Authors: Funda Fundaoğlu Küçükekenci, Ahmet Serkan Küçükekenci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to investigate the effects of using EDTA, phytic acid (IP6), chitosan, and proanthocyanidin (PAs) as final irrigation solutions on the push-out bond strength (PBS) of root canal filling.Materials methodsIn the present study, 56 extracted human incisors were utilized. All teeth were decoronated at the cementoenamel junction. Following the root canal chemomechanical preparation, the teeth were randomly divided into 7 groups according to the final irrigation procedures: Group 1: Control, Group 2: EDTA, Group 3: IP6, Group 4: Chitosan, Group 5: EDTA + PAs, Group 6: IP6 + PAs, Group 7: Chitosan + PAs. The root canals were filled using the cold lateral condensation technique with gutta-percha and resin-based root canal sealer. Samples were obtained horizontally cut from the apical third of the teeth and subjected to push-out bond strength (PBS) using a universal testing machine. Data was statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey HSD test (α = 0.05).ResultsThe highest value was obtained in Group 6 (12.14 ± 0.47 MPa), and the lowest value in Group 1 (5.80 ± 0,44 MPa) (P < 0.05). Group 6 has shown a higher PBS value than Group 5 (11.72 ± 0.14 MPa) and Group 7 (11.94 ± 0.42 MPa) (P > 0.05). There were statistically significant differences between all other groups, except Groups 3 and 4 (P < 0.05). The failure type was mostly adhesive, and no cohesive failures were observed.ConclusionThe use of EDTA, IP6, and chitosan for final irrigation has enhanced the bond strength values of resin-based root canal sealer to dentin. Furthermore, the use of PAs after EDTA, IP6, and chitosan increased the bond strength values more than when these solutions were used alone.Keywords: Chitosan, Final irrigation, Phytic acid, Proanthocyanidins, Push out bond strength

Introduction

Irrigation protocols are essential in endodontic treatment, primarily for the disinfection of the root canal system, the removal of necrotic tissue, and the elimination of biofilms. Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) has long been recognized as the cornerstone of irrigation solutions due to its potent antimicrobial properties and ability to dissolve organic tissue, including dentin matrix proteins [1, 2]. However, NaOCl alone is not sufficient for optimal cleaning; it must be used in conjunction with other agents, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), to effectively eliminate inorganic debris and the smear layer [3, 4]. The use of EDTA as an irrigation solution in endodontics is widely recognized for its chelating properties, but it also poses significant detrimental effects on dentin microhardness and structural integrity. EDTA removes calcium ions from dentin, resulting in demineralization and a consequent reduction in microhardness [5]. Previous studies indicate that EDTA significantly improves bond strength when used in conjunction with other irrigants such as NaOCl due to the effective removal of the organic phase from dentin and the enhanced cleanliness of the dentinal surface that promotes better adhesion [6, 7]. However, Barutcigil et al. indicate that while EDTA enhances infiltrative bond strengths, it could also lead to adverse structural changes in the dentin matrix that potentially impair sealer infiltration if used inappropriately [8]. This situation leads to a complex scenario where the benefits of bond strength augmentation must be weighed against the potential durability and structural integrity concerns of dentin [9].

Phytic acid, scientifically known as inositol hexakisphosphate (IP6), is an organic compound that serves as a primary storage form of phosphorus in many plant seeds [6]. Research suggests that phytic acid could offer a gentler alternative to EDTA for chelating calcium and enhancing the bonding of root canal sealers to dentin [9]. Hazar et al. observe that phytic acid, due to its ability to create a more ideal mineral gradient in dentin surfaces, may enhance bonding strength compared to EDTA, which can completely demineralize the dentinal surface [10].

Chitosan is a biopolymer derived from chitin, which is primarily sourced from the shells of crustaceans such as shrimp and crabs, as well as certain fungi. Chitosan is formed through the deacetylation of chitin, resulting in a cationic polysaccharide characterized by its positive charge, which enhances its interaction with negatively charged surfaces (such as dental hard tissues) and biological fluids [11]. In dental applications, chitosan has been explored as an alternative to EDTA. Research indicates that chitosan may provide a gentler chelation effect compared to EDTA while also offering additional advantages such as antibacterial properties and improved tissue compatibility [12, 13]. The ability of chitosan to penetrate deeper into dentinal tubules has been suggested to enhance its efficacy in promoting dentin regeneration and improving adhesive bonding [13, 14].

To enhance the bond strength of root canal sealers to dentin, various applications and methodologies have been investigated, each addressing different aspects of the bond formation process [10, 15–18]. Proanthocyanidins (PAs) are a class of natural compounds categorized as polyphenols, specifically flavonoids, found abundantly in various plants, particularly in fruits, seeds, vegetables, and tree bark. These compounds are known for their impressive antioxidant properties, and studies have highlighted their potential health benefits, including anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects [19, 20]. Due to these characteristics, they have drawn interest in dentistry, especially in enhancing the bond strength of root canal sealers to dentin. Kalra et al. showed that PAs can significantly affect the mechanical properties of dentin by facilitating the cross-linking of collagen fibers. Such cross-linking enhances the structural integrity of dentin and improves the bonding interface between dentin and resin-based materials [20]. Similarly, PAs were shown to effectively stabilize collagen and contribute to higher shear bond strength when used as a pretreatment for dentin before the application of adhesive systems [21].

To the author’s knowledge, there are no studies regarding the effect of the PAs application after final irrigation protocols with IP6, chitosan, and EDTA on the push-out bond strength (PBS) of root canal filling to dentin. Therefore, the present in vitro study aims to evaluate the effect of PAs application after different final irrigation protocols on the PBS of root canal filling to dentin. The null hypothesis was that the different final irrigation protocols and PAs application would not affect the PBS of the root canal filling to dentin.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and were approved by the Ordu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Number: 2025/72).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using a sample size calculator software program (G. power 3.19.2) based on the previous study [22]. When a = 0.05, b = 0.56, and 1 - b = 0.80 were established as a result of the power analysis test, it was decided to include 8 teeth for each group in the PBS test, resulting in a total of 56 teeth being utilized in the present study.

Sample preparation

A schematic presentation of prepared samples and PBS test is given in Fig. 1. A total of 56 freshly extracted, single-rooted, caries/resorption-free, and fully developed maxillary incisors were used. The presence of a single canal was confirmed using three-angle radiographs. Until used, the teeth were stored in a 1% thymol solution and cleaned of debris with a periodontal curette. All procedures were performed by a single operator. The teeth were decoronated below the cemento-enamel junction using a water-cooled diamond separator, and the root lengths were standardized to 10 mm. The working length was determined as 1 mm shorter than the length at which a #15 K-file (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was visible at the apex. Canal preparation was performed at the working length using the crown-down technique with a reciproc blue R 25 (WDV, Munich, Germany) file in reciprocation mode with a WDV gold endodontic motor (WDV, Munich, Germany). For irrigation, 10 mL of NaOCl was used in each canal. After chemo-mechanical preparation, the teeth were randomly divided into seven groups (n = 8):

Group 4: Irrigated with 5 mL 0.2% chitosan for 3 minutes each.

Group 5: Irrigated with 5 mL 17% EDTA and 6.5% PAs for 3 minutes each.

Group 6: Irrigated with 5 mL 1% IP6 and 6.5% PAs for 3 minutes each.

A 0.2% chitosan solution utilized in the study was prepared in the Department of Chemistry at Ordu University, Faculty of Science. Chitosan (0.2 g; Sigma Aldrich, MO) was weighed using a precision balance and subsequently added to 100 mL of 1% acetic acid. The mixture was then stirred with a magnetic stirrer until a homogeneous solution was achieved [22]. PAs (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was dissolved in deionized water to obtain a final concentration of 6.5% by weight; 95%, All irrigation procedures were performed using an irrigation needle (Kerr Hawe Irrigation Probe, KerrHawe SA, Biggio, Switzerland), which was inserted 1 mm short of the working length inside the canal. A volume of 5 mL was used for each solution. At the end of the irrigation protocol, all teeth were rinsed with 5 mL of distilled water for 1 min and dried using sterile paper points (Diadent Group International Inc., Cheongju, Korea). The root canals were filled using AH Plus root canal sealer (Dentsply DeTrey, Konstanz, Germany) and gutta-percha with the cold lateral compaction technique. Excess gutta-percha was removed, and the canal orifices were sealed with glass ionomer cement (3 M EPSE, Seefeld, Germany). The samples were stored in an incubator at 100% humidity and 37 °C for one week. The roots were vertically embedded in a block of auto-polymerized acrylic resin (Meliodent, Bayer Dental, Leverkusen, Germany). The roots were sectioned horizontally using a water-cooled precision cutting machine (Mecatome T180, Presi Metallography, Eybens, France) starting 2 mm above the apex, obtaining 2 mm-thick slices from the apical third with water cooling.

Push-out bond strength test

A universal testing instrument (Autograph AGS X, Shimadzu Co, Japan) was used to conduct the PBS test. One sample was selected from the apical third region for the PBS test (N = 56, n = 8). PBS test was applied from apical to coronal with a 1 mm diameter plugger at a rate of 0.5 mm/min until the canal filling was dislocated (Fig. 2). The bond failure force recorded in Newtons (N) is the peak force displacing the filling. The N value was converted to megapascals (MPa) for each sample by dividing the N value into the total bonding area was calculated as π (r1 + r2) h, where h is the thickness of the sample, r1 is the apical radius of the root canal, r2 is the coronal radius of the root canal, and π = 3.14 [17, 23]. Failure types were evaluated with a stereomicroscope (Leica SP1600; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at ×25 magnification, and classified as follows: (1) Adhesive (failure at the interface between sealer and dentin); (2) Mixed (failure in both the sealer and dentin).

Experimental design of PBS testing

Statistical analysis

According to the Shapiro–Wilk tests of normality, the groups were normally distributed. Statistical analysis (IBM SPSS Statistics, v20.0; IBM Corp) of PBS data was performed using a one-way ANOVA and the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test. The chi-square test was used to evaluate the association between the type of failure and the irrigation protocols of the present study (α = 0.05).

Results

According to the results of the PBS test, the highest value was obtained in Group 6 (12.14 ± 0.47 MPa), and the lowest value was obtained in Group 1 (5.80 ± 0,44 MPa). Group 6 has shown a higher PBS value than Group 5 (11.72 ± 0.14 MPa) and Group 7 (11.94 ± 0.42 MPa), but this difference is statistically insignificant (P > 0.05). The difference between Groups 3 (9.01 ± 0.08 MPa) and 4 (9.01 ± 0.40 MPa) was also statistically insignificant (P > 0.05). There were statistically significant differences between all other groups (P < 0.05) (Table 1). The percentages of each type of failure are given in Table 2. The chi-square test revealed no statistically significant association between the observed failure type and the irrigation protocols (P = 0.699). The most common failure mode was adhesive (58.9%), followed by mixed failure (41.1%), and no cohesive failures were observed (Fig. 3).

Discussion

According to the results of the PBS test, the application of EDTA, chitosan, IP6, and proanthocyanidin used in the final irrigation increased the PBS value of the root canal filling. Therefore, the null hypothesis has been rejected.

In dentistry, adhesive materials can be evaluated with bond strength tests. Due to its ability to effectively replicate clinical loading conditions, the PBS test method is frequently used. Unlike other methods, such as micro tensile and pull-out tests, the push-out test applies a shear load at the interface of the cement and dentin, which is directly relevant to the stresses experienced during the functional use of endodontically treated teeth [24, 25]. Nagas et al. indicated that the PBS test provides valuable preclinical information about the adhesive properties of various materials, making it a reliable technique for measuring the bond strength of root canal filling materials to root dentin [25]. The simple design and objective measurements make it advantageous for researchers to conduct comparative studies on various sealers and filling materials [26]. Thus, the PBS test was applied to the samples in the present study.

In root canal treatment, the efficiency of irrigation solutions is critical in managing the smear layer that forms during canal preparation. NaOCl, widely used for its antimicrobial properties, is effective in cleaning root canal systems; however, it may not fully remove the smear layer, leaving residual debris that can compromise the sealing ability of root canal fillings [27, 28]. In contrast, EDTA has emerged as a more effective alternative due to its chelating properties, which enhanced the removal of inorganic components of the smear layer [29, 30]. Previous studies indicate that EDTA not only aids smear layer removal but also significantly increases the push-out bond strength of root canal sealers applied to dentin surfaces [31, 32]. The capacity of EDTA to promote dentin surface cleanliness is supported by its ability to change mineral content and enhance bonding potential with root canal sealers, leading to improved long-term success of root canal treatment [29, 33]. For instance, previous studies demonstrated that when 17% EDTA is used following NaOCl, it optimally prepares the dentin surface by not only removing smear layers but also affecting the calcium-to-phosphorus (Ca/P) ratio, which is beneficial for subsequent bonding procedures [32, 34]. In the present study, the group that used EDTA in the final irrigation showed a higher PBS value compared to the control group irrigated solely with NaOCl. This situation may be attributed to the fact that EDTA removes the smear layer, allowing the root canal filling material to penetrate more deeply and thus increasing the PBS value. The results of the present study, that significantly higher PBS values in groups irrigated with EDTA compared to those irrigated solely with hypochlorite, are consistent with previous studies [35–37].

The use of 1% IP6 for 3 min is a suitable alternative to EDTA in removing the smear layer during root canal treatment [9]. A concentration of 1% IP6 has been shown to optimize its chelating properties, aiding in the effective removal of the smear layer and mineral content from dentin surfaces compared to lower concentrations. This removal not only facilitates better penetration of endodontic sealers but also promotes a more conducive environment for the healing and regeneration of the pulp-dentin complex [38, 39]. For this reason, IP6 was used at a concentration of 1% in the present study. The present study has demonstrated that when 1% IP6 is applied for 3 min, it achieves higher PBS values compared to groups irrigated with EDTA. This enhanced performance may be attributed to the fact that IP6 causes less dentin erosion than EDTA, resulting in less disruption to the bonding surfaces necessary for effective adhesive properties [10]. In contrast, excessive application of EDTA can lead to significant dentin erosion, which negatively impacts bond strength by compromising the integrity of the dentin structure [14, 22, 40]. For this reason, the irrigation time was standardized to 3 min for all groups to ensure consistency in the present study. However, this duration may have contributed to greater dentin erosion caused by EDTA. This increased erosion could be a reason for the lower PBS values observed when compared to those treated with IP6. IP6 may serve as an effective irrigant that can cleanse dentin surfaces while maintaining the structural integrity required for strong bonding of root canal sealers. Koçak and Ünal reported that when comparing the PBS values of calcium silicate sealers, the groups irrigated with EDTA exhibited higher PBS values than those irrigated with IP6 [22]. They attributed this finding to the possibility that EDTA may have removed the smear layer more effectively than IP6. However, we observed the opposite result in our study. This discrepancy may be due to the different adhesion capacities of the tested sealers. There is consensus across several studies that IP6 should be investigated as a viable option in endodontic irrigation protocols; however, direct comparisons with EDTA regarding push-out bond strength are required to reach definitive evidence [9, 10, 41].

Chitosan has garnered attention as an irrigant in root canal treatment due to its potential effects on the PBS of root canal fillings. Rane et al. evaluated the incorporation of chitosan nanoparticles into bioceramic sealers and showed that these nanoparticles enhance the sealing ability due to their hydrophilic nature, which improves their interaction with dentin surfaces and leads to better bonding and lower apical leakage [42]. Furthermore, Enggardipta et al. demonstrated that adding hydrophilic chitosan nanoparticles to epoxy-resin-based sealers increased their wetting ability, promoting better adhesion to irregular canal walls [43]. Mathew et al. showed that chitosan was more effective than traditional irrigants like EDTA in removing the smear layer, which is essential for achieving optimal bond strength with root fillings [44]. In addition, Sarrafan et al. emphasized the importance of smear layer removal in correlating with enhanced bond strength, suggesting that the use of effective irrigants like chitosan can lead to significant improvements in the mechanical properties of the adhesive interface [45]. Previous studies indicate that chitosan at a concentration of 0.2% effectively reduces bacterial viability and biofilm formation, making it a suitable alternative to traditional irrigants like NaOCl and EDTA [46, 47]. Ferreira-Reguera et al. showed that a 0.2% concentration of chitosan provides effective cleaning and opens up dentinal tubules, allowing for better penetration of the sealing materials used during root canal therapy [48]. The 0.2% concentration is optimal as it balances efficacy with safety, ensuring that it effectively targets bacteria without deleterious effects on the surrounding tissue [49]. So, chitosan was used at a concentration of 0.2% in the present study. According to the results of the present study, significantly higher PBS values were observed in the group with 0.2% chitosan as the final irrigation solution compared to the group with EDTA irrigation. This difference could be attributed to several factors. Firstly, chitosan possesses inherent antimicrobial properties, which may contribute to a cleaner root canal environment, facilitating better adhesion of the sealing material to the dentin surface. Secondly, chitosan’s molecular structure can enhance the wetting properties of sealers, leading to improved penetration into dentinal tubules and a more intimate bond between the sealer and dentin. Additionally, the potentially lower erosive effect of chitosan on dentin compared to EDTA may contribute to maintaining the structural integrity of the dentin interface, thereby promoting better bond strength [14]. Finally, the chelating ability of chitosan may differ from that of EDTA, affecting the removal of the smear layer in a manner that enhances bonding capabilities. Considering these factors, the use of chitosan as an irrigant can provide significant advantages in terms of enhancing push-out bond strength in root canal treatments.

The use of PAs in final irrigation solutions has shown promise in significantly increasing the PBS of root canal fillings [50]. PAs are recognized for their chelating and antibacterial properties, which may enhance the effectiveness of root canal filling by promoting a cleaner dentin surface and improving the bonding interface. Kumar et al. indicated that incorporating PAs could lead to better bonding due to their ability to effectively remove smear layers and reduce dentin erosion when compared to traditional chelators like EDTA [50]. Furthermore, it has been indicated that the application of chelating agents can expose more collagen fibers within the dentin structure, which facilitates increased bonding area and adhesion for root canal sealers [51]. In the previous study, PAs were utilized as part of the irrigation procedure; enhancements in the mechanical bond strength were observed, likely due to their ability to create a more favorable substrate for adhesive interaction by restructuring the dentin surface [52]. Specifically, the presence of PAs may not only enhance smear layer removal but also improve dentin wettability, which is crucial for optimal bonding performance. In the present study, 6.5% of PAs were used. The concentration of 6.5% PAs has been found to optimize the balance between antibacterial efficacy and biocompatibility. Higher concentrations may offer increased antibacterial activity but could pose risks of cytotoxicity. Thus, the 6.5% concentration appears to be a clinically effective dose that minimizes adverse effects while maintaining a high inhibitory effect on problematic bacteria [53]. According to the results of the present study, the groups that used 6.5% PAs as an additional final irrigation solution showed higher PBS values. This result is consistent with the findings of previous studies [50, 54]. The results may be attributed to how improved PBS when utilizing PAs is due to their multifaceted role in enhancing the bonding interface through both chemical interactions with dentin and physical alterations to the dentin surface during the irrigation process.

In the previous studies evaluating the PBS of root canal fillings, the focus has generally been on the apical third, as the bond in this area is of critical importance [55, 57]. The apical region presents significant challenges due to the complex morphology resulting from isthmuses and lateral canals, making thorough cleaning more difficult. Effective cleaning in this area is essential, as it often determines the prognosis of root canal treatment. Ng et al. indicated the importance of proper disinfection and sealing in apical areas to enhance treatment outcomes, highlighting that structural complexities of the apical third can significantly affect the adhesion and sealing properties of root canal fillings [55]. Similarly, Vera et al. reported that achieving a good seal in the apical area is paramount for the success of root canal therapy, as inadequate cleaning and sealing could lead to persistent infection and treatment failure [56]. Furthermore, the apical third is often the site of intricate canal systems, which can harbor microorganisms that are resistant to conventional cleaning and irrigating techniques. Zhang et al. reported that proper sealing of the apical region correlates with improved long-term treatment prognoses, supporting the notion that strong PBS values in the apical third are crucial for successful endodontic outcomes [57]. Therefore, the PBS values ​​of root canal fillings were obtained by testing on samples obtained from the apical third in the present study.

The predominant failure observed in the present study was adhesive failure (58.9%), a result that aligns with previous research emphasizing the critical role of adhesive interactions in the success of endodontic treatments [58, 59]. The lack of cohesive failures in the presented data might suggest that even with various irrigation methods, the overall mechanical properties may remain intact, albeit with diminished adhesive capacity leading to a higher incidence of adhesive failures.

According to the results of the present study, using chitosan and IP6 as final irrigants, along with the addition of PAs to the irrigation protocol, increases the PBS of root canal fillings. The integration of PAs in different final irrigation solutions potentially contributes to an increase in PBS by ensuring effective smear layer removal and enhancing the bonding conditions between root canal sealers and dentin surfaces. However, further in-vivo and in-vitro studies are needed to support these findings.

In this study conducted under in vitro conditions, only the apical triple region was evaluated. The apical triple region and lateral canals can exhibit significant variation, which can affect the removal of the smear layer and, consequently, sealer penetration, thereby impacting the PBS. The inability to standardize this aspect in extracted teeth represents a limitation of our study. The analysis of the sealer-dentin interface is crucial for understanding the efficacy of endodontic treatments. This analysis typically involves evaluating how well the endodontic sealers penetrate the dentinal tubules and adhere to the dentin walls, impacting the seal’s overall effectiveness and the likelihood of treatment failure [60, 61]. Analysis of the sealant-dentin interface requires a comprehensive approach combining microscopy techniques such as Scanning Electron Microscopy and Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy with mechanical testing to assess adhesion and penetration effectively. So, another limitation of the present study is that analysis of the sealant-dentin interface was not performed.

Conclusion

The use of EDTA, IP6, and chitosan for final irrigation has enhanced the bond strength values of resin-based root canal sealer to dentin. Furthermore, the use of PAs after EDTA, IP6, and chitosan increased the bond strength values more than when these solutions were used alone.
