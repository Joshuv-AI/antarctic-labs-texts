The influence of posture on the three-dimensional mandibular position relative to the maxillary coordinate system in centric relation
Authors: Chunxiao Jin, Junning Liu, Aihemaiti Muhetaer, Mengxun Li, Xinjie Cai, Cui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the influence of postural changes along three spatial axes on the three-dimensional position of the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points relative to the maxillary coordinate system in Centric Relation (CR).MethodsMandibular movements were recorded in 18 healthy participants using a high-precision jaw movement analysis system while they wore customized 3D-printed Lucia jigs. Three-dimensional coordinates of the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points were captured. Measurements were performed under various postural conditions, including anterior/posterior body tilt, left/right head tilt, and left/right head rotation.ResultsPostural changes significantly affected mandibular position within the measurement system (P < 0.05). During anterior-posterior body tilt, the recorded lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points shifted posteriorly along the Y-axis, with displacements up to 0.96 mm (P < 0.05). Lateral head tilt induced ipsilateral displacement along the X-axis (0.16–0.43 mm, P < 0.05), while lateral head rotation resulted in contralateral X-axis shifts (0.09–0.18 mm, P < 0.05). Across all postural conditions, the three mandibular landmarks showed predominantly coordinated displacement within the measurement system.ConclusionsPostural changes significantly affect mandibular position relative to the maxillary coordinate system. Anterior-posterior body tilt and lateral head tilt induce mandibular movement in the same direction, while head rotation produces movement in the opposite direction. The relative positions of the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points remain largely stable, consistent with a coordinated displacement pattern.Keywords: Centric relation, Three-dimensional positional coordinates, Postural changes, Jaw movement analysis system

Background

Establishing a stable and reproducible mandibular position is essential in prosthodontic treatment [1, 2], particularly in full-arch rehabilitation, severe tooth wear, and other complex restorative situations where the original occlusal relationship has been lost [3–5]. Centric Relation (CR) is widely used for this purpose because it is considered a repeatable mandibular reference position independent of tooth contact [6–9].

Various clinical methods have been used to record CR, including operator-guided manipulation, neuromuscular deprogramming approaches and functional movement-based techniques [10–13]. Despite methodological differences, most CR recording techniques assume a stable patient posture during measurement. However, in clinical practice, CR may be recorded in upright, semi-supine, or supine positions depending on the method used – for example, deprogramming devices are commonly used in upright posture, whereas operator-guided manipulation is often performed semi-supine or supine [14]. Moreover, unintentional head tilting or rotation related to operator position or patient comfort may also occur, potentially altering neuromuscular balance and gravitational loading of the mandible and introducing unrecognized variability into CR records. Previous studies have suggested that postural changes may influence mandibular position. Dental chair backrest inclination has been reported to alter mandibular position by using interocclusal contact registration with an autopolymerizing anterior device and photographic superimposition, particularly in more reclined positions [15]. A recent study using intraoral scanners and bilateral virtual occlusal records also found discrepancies in the maxillomandibular relationship recorded at maximal intercuspal position (ICP) among different dental chair positions [16]. These effects have typically been assessed using indirect or static approaches, such as intraoral measurements, occlusal appliance evaluation, or digital intraoral scans. However, measurements at ICP may be inherently influenced by tooth contacts, intraoral scanning requires sequential acquisition and occlusal alignment, and manual measurements are subject to operator dependency [17]. Moreover, most existing evidence is derived from two-dimensional analyses or isolated postural conditions, limiting comparability across spatial axes and failing to capture coordinated three-dimensional mandibular behavior [18–20]. Therefore, systematic three-dimensional evaluation of posture-related mandibular displacement during CR recording– where occlusal interference is minimized –remains limited. In particular, the extent to which body tilt, head tilt, and head rotation affect the spatial coordinates of key mandibular landmarks relative to the maxilla has not been fully quantified using real-time, objective motion analysis techniques [15, 21].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the influence of body and head posture on the three-dimensional mandibular position relative to the maxillary coordinate system during CR recording using a jaw-tracking system and a customized three-dimensionally printed Lucia jig. The null hypothesis was that changes in body and head posture would not significantly affect the three-dimensional mandibular position during CR recording.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study was conducted in the Department of Prosthodontics, Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University, between September 2025 and November 2025. Prior to the experiment, ethical approval was obtained from the institution’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. WDKQ2025B59). All participants provided written informed consent. A priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.0.10 for a repeated-measures ANOVA, with an effect size of 0.5, α error probability of 0.05, and power of 0.8. The analysis indicated a minimum sample size of 14 participants was required.

Thirty-nine volunteers with normal occlusion and no obvious temporomandibular disorder (TMD) symptoms were initially recruited. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–25 years, good general oral health, complete second molar-to-second molar dentition without indirect restorations, balanced maxillomandibular relationship, normal mandibular range of motion, and ability to chew comfortably [18]. Exclusion criteria included a history of myospasm, myositis, muscle contracture, polyarthritis, acute trauma, or infection in the TMJ region [22]. After screening, 18 volunteers met all criteria and were included in the motion analysis (10 men, 8 women; mean age 23.1 ± 2.2 years).

Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure included digital data acquisition, deprogramming, and jaw motion tracking under various postural conditions.

First, the dental arches of each participant were scanned using an intraoral scanner (Element 5D; iTero, Alachua County, USA), and the data were exported as Standard Tessellation Language (STL) files. Based on the scan, a personalized modified Lucia jig was designed and 3D-printed for each participant [11, 20, 23] (Fig. 1a).

The personalized modified Lucia jig (a) and the jaw movement analysis system (b)

A jaw movement analysis system (JMA, Zebris Medical GmbH, Isny, Germany) with ultrasound transducers was used to record mandibular position. As documented in the manufacturer’s official technical manual, the system achieves a spatial accuracy of 0.05 mm. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the transmitter was mounted on a prefabricated metal bite fork, which was attached to the labial/buccal surfaces of the mandibular teeth using a temporary crown and bridge composite material (Structur 2 SC, VOCO GmbH, Cuxhaven, Germany) and zinc polycarboxylate cement (Shanghai Dental Material Factory, Shanghai, China). The receiver was fixed securely to the participant’s head via a facebow (Fig. 1b). Prior to clinical measurement, the system underwent standardized hardware calibration to define the absolute spatial coordinates between the reference facebow and tracking pointers. Four calibration pointers were inserted into the mandibular transmitter and sequentially aligned with the bores on the head-mounted receiver assembly, with each point confirmed via the system’s record button. Subsequently, the facebow was adjusted so that its reference plane aligned with the participant’s Frankfort horizontal plane. Each participant underwent the same setup process to ensure consistency across the study cohort. Accordingly, the mandibular signal was obtained from a tooth-mounted transmitter, whereas the maxillary reference was defined by the head-mounted receiver assembly. The condylar points displayed by the system were therefore treated as electronically defined reference points within the system model.

After device placement, participants were seated in an upright position and wore the modified Lucia jig for 30 min to achieve muscle relaxation [1, 24]. Mandibular positions were subsequently recorded under different combinations of body and head postures. For the sagittal plane, four reclining angles – 45°, 90°, 135°, and 180° – were tested by adjusting the dental chair (A-dec 200, A-dec Inc., Newberg, USA) according to a pre-established protocol. A medical protractor reading was used to verify and lock each posture angle before recording. After a 30-minute rest period, coronal head tilts of + 45°, 0°, and − 45° were evaluated while participants remained seated upright. Following another 30-minute rest, horizontal head rotations of + 45°, 0°, and − 45° were assessed in the same sitting position (Fig. 2).

Subject postures under experimental conditions. (a) Sagittal plane body positions: 45° (45° Forward Tilt), 90° (Upright), 135° (45° Backward Tilt), and 180° (Supine). (b) Coronal plane head positions: head tilted 45° to the left (45° Left Tilt) or 45° to the right (45° Right Tilt) while maintaining an upright body posture. (c) Horizontal plane head positions: head rotated 45° to the left (45° Left Rotation) or right (45° Right Rotation) while maintaining an upright body posture

Throughout the experiment, the personalized modified Lucia jig remained in place, and all procedures were conducted by the same experienced dentist to ensure consistency. In each postural condition, light tapping was performed at a rate of three taps per two seconds, with the mouth open approximately 10 mm. After each tapping cycle, participants were instructed to hold the position momentarily, during which the system automatically recorded the three-dimensional coordinates of the system-defined lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points [21]. Each condition was measured three times, and the mean values were used for subsequent analysis.

A standardized coordinate system was defined for data interpretation relative to the maxillary reference system, following the native manufacturer definition (Fig. 3): the X-axis (coronal) represented mediolateral movement (left: +, right: −), the Y-axis (sagittal) represented anteroposterior movement (posterior: +, anterior: −), and the Z-axis (vertical) represented vertical movement (superior: +, inferior: −). The coordinate data from all recordings were exported for final analysis.

Measurement points of the mandible and the defined coordinate system

Statistical analysis

The collected data from each group were statistically analyzed using statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, v.22; IBM Corp., Chicago, USA). A two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement was used to assess the test-retest reliability. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA), with Mauchly’s test to evaluate sphericity and Greenhouse-Geisser correction applied when sphericity was violated, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Friedman test, with Dunn’s post-hoc multiple comparisons. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied as appropriate. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at 0.05.

The test-retest reliability of the three-dimensional coordinate measurements was excellent. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), calculated based on three consecutive repeated measurements per condition, ranged from 0.954 to 0.989 across all mandibular landmarks, postural conditions, and spatial axes.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the influence of body and head posture on the three-dimensional mandibular position during CR recording. A key finding was that postural changes significantly affected the mandibular-maxillary relationship captured by this recording system. To be specific, postural changes significantly affected the positions of the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points relative to the maxillary coordinate system. The null hypothesis that changes in body and head posture would not significantly affect the three-dimensional mandibular position during CR recording was fully rejected based on the present results. Anterior-posterior body tilt and lateral head tilt induced mandibular movement predominantly along the corresponding axes, whereas head rotation produced contralateral shifts. These observed displacements may reflect dynamic re-equilibration of the stomatognathic system in response to altered gravitational vectors and neuromuscular conditions.

The mandible is suspended within a complex muscular system that maintains cranio-cervical-mandibular equilibrium [14, 25, 26]. Postural changes may disrupt this dynamic balance by shifting muscle length-tension relationships and redirecting gravitational forces [27, 28]. The observed mandibular repositioning could be explained by neuromuscular re-equilibration within this integrated system.

During forward tilt, the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points shifted anteriorly, whereas backward postures resulted in posterior displacement. This pattern suggests that with either forward or backward tilt, the gravitational equilibrium acting on the mandible may be altered, producing a significant change in the sagittal gravitational component, whose direction corresponds to that of the body tilt [29–31]. This result differs from the findings of Ohmure et al. [32], who reported that the condyles moved posteriorly when the head was inclined forward. The discrepancy may arise from differences in head posture definition between the two studies. Although both postures involved forward head positioning, the study by Ohmure adopted a pure anterior translation of the head, whereas the present experiment involved a forward tilt of the entire body in the sagittal plane. These two postural conditions likely elicited distinct neuromuscular responses. In forward head translation, the activity of the masseter and digastric muscles may increase to stabilize the mandible, potentially pulling the condyles slightly backward [29]. In contrast, during forward body tilt, gravitational loading plays a more dominant role. This posture may primarily induce the lower cervical flexion and contraction of the infrahyoid muscles (particularly the sternohyoid), which draw the hyoid bone downward [33, 34]. The downward displacement of the hyoid bone further drags the mandible anteriorly, consistent with an overall anterior displacement of the mandible. This demonstrates how different postures reconfigure the muscular balance to establish a new equilibrium [35, 36].

A comparison between the upright (90°) and supine (180°) positions showed a significant posterior shift of the lower incisal point, which is consistent with the findings of Coelho et al. [15]. The condyles, however, did not undergo a corresponding posterior translation. Instead, they were displaced inferiorly relative to the maxilla (moving towards the feet direction). This inferior shift may be associated with the need to maintain stable contact with the bite jig. Consistent with the change in the orientation of gravitational vectors within the coordinate system, together with the muscular activity likely required to sustain anterior contact, the mandible may adjust its position. These combined influences contribute to the coordinated compensatory displacement that was observed.

During coronal head tilting, the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points shifted laterally toward the tilting side due to the increased gravitational component along the coronal axis (X-axis). This pattern is consistent with the sagittal gravitational component shift observed during anterior-posterior body tilt.

Horizontal head rotation mainly induced a characteristic contralateral shift along the X-axis, though the overall magnitude of displacement was inherently smaller than that caused by gravitational changes during coronal head tilting. When the head turned left, the left condylar point moved antero-medially while the right condylar point and the lower incisal point were displaced postero-laterally, with the reverse occurring during right rotation. This finding aligns with Katayama et al. [21], who reported significant mandibular deviation beyond approximately 20° of rotation. The movement may stem from hyoid-mandibular coupling. Specifically, asymmetric activation of the sternocleidomastoid and hyoid muscles during head rotation shifts the hyoid bone toward the counter-rotating side, thereby potentially pulling the mandible in the same direction. These adjustments are further guided and limited by capsulo-ligamentous constraints [21, 26, 37]. While the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points moved consistently along the X-axis, only the lower incisal point exhibited significant positional differences across all three rotation angles. Neither condylar point showed statistically significant displacement between any pair of angles, though the trend of displacement remained consistent. This differential statistical significance can be attributed to the lever-arm amplification effect. The lower incisal point is located significantly farther from the center of rotation (the cervical spine) than the condylar points, making incisal displacements larger and more easily detectable. Furthermore, this limited condylar mobility reflects the anatomical constraints imposed by the articular capsule, ligaments, and disc, which strictly limit lateral condylar excursion and prevent detectable asymmetry even under pronounced angular deviations [38–40].

A fundamental finding was that the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points exhibited predominantly coordinated displacement across all postural conditions, with no statistically significant interaction effects in most spatial axes and uniformly small effect sizes overall. This overall pattern is broadly consistent with predominantly translational movement within the measurement framework, rather than substantial rotational displacement between landmarks. Minor individual variations in displacement magnitude are to be expected, attributable to inherent individual differences in muscle tone, ligament elasticity, and TMJ morphology. Overall, this coordinated movement pattern should be interpreted cautiously, as it reflects the behavior of the tracked landmarks within the measurement system.

This study utilized a three-dimensional mandibular motion analysis system to capture jaw kinematics. This system enabled simultaneous, real-time tracking of the lower incisal point and bilateral condylar points, allowing detailed visualization of mandibular movement trajectories during postural changes [21, 41]. Compared with radiographic or static registration techniques, this approach provided dynamic, radiation-free, and time-resolved data that more closely reflected physiological mandibular behavior [42]. The continuous recording capability also allowed for precise quantification of subtle displacements and coordination patterns between landmarks, which were challenging to assess using static imaging methods.

To record CR, a personalized jig fabricated via 3D printing was employed. While conventional methods such as hand-formed autopolymerizing resin jigs and Gothic arch tracers are well-established [9, 38, 43], they are susceptible to operator-dependent variability and surface irregularities inherent in manual fabrication. In contrast, the digitally designed jig improved standardization by ensuring consistent thickness, smooth contact surfaces, and stable mandibular positioning, thereby reducing potential measurement bias [20, 23]. Furthermore, all measurements were conducted in CR rather than ICP. This approach minimized the influence of occlusal interferences and more accurately represented the true musculoskeletal reference position of the mandible, thereby enhancing the reliability and comparability of positional assessments across postural conditions [9].

Occlusal tactile sensibility has been reported to be as low as 16.1 ± 5.33 μm for natural dentition, 23.3 ± 7.42 μm for implant-supported prostheses, and 64.0 ± 39.59 μm for complete dentures [44]. In the present study, postural changes induced mandibular displacements that frequently exceeded these perceptual thresholds. Sagittal body tilt resulted in lower incisal point shifts ranging from 646 to 869 μm along the Y-axis, increasing to 963 μm in the supine position. In addition, lateral head tilts and rotations produced mediolateral displacements of 163–433 μm and 89–184 μm, respectively, with tilt-induced deviations generally greater than those caused by rotation. From a prosthodontic perspective, these posturally induced mandibular displacements particularly in the anteroposterior direction may still be considered physiologic within the concept of freedom in centric (0.5–1 mm), which allows limited mandibular freedom around CR [45, 46]. Although appropriately designed freedom in centric may compensate for most postural variations and reduce occlusal precontacts, standardized patient positioning during jaw registration remains essential to ensure measurement and transfer reliability. In addition, not all clinicians and technicians can consistently design and fabricate restorations with an optimal long centric scheme, and even within this range, residual discrepancies may still contribute to discomfort in occlusion-sensitive patients [47, 48]. Moreover, mediolateral deviations induced by both head tilt and rotation may still be clinically perceptible. This is particularly relevant in complex restorative cases with extensive occlusal reconstruction, where freedom in centric may be considered as a potential occlusal design strategy (e.g., full-mouth rehabilitation) [49]. However, the clinical translation of these findings remains to be validated in actual prosthodontic treatment scenarios.

To enhance the accuracy and reproducibility of clinical records, it is strongly recommended to adopt and maintain a standardized patient posture, particularly in full-arch rehabilitation and complex occlusal reconstruction, throughout the entire recording and transfer process. The Dawson bimanual manipulation technique explicitly mandates lowering the patient’s head to a near-horizontal position, a step performed for the primary purpose of achieving reliable stabilization via the clinician’s forearms or chest. Furthermore, clinicians should remain aware that during routine procedures, when the operator works from the patient’s right side, the patient may unintentionally adopt a slight rightward head tilt or rotation, introducing asymmetric positional bias. Acknowledging and controlling for such posture-related discrepancies is essential not only for achieving morphological precision in restorations but also for ensuring their long-term functional harmony in daily use. From a methodological perspective, future studies comparing CR recording techniques should incorporate postural standardization to prevent confounding of technical outcomes.

Several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, participants were healthy young adults with normal occlusion and no temporomandibular disorders, which enhances internal validity but limits generalizability to patients with occlusal wear, joint pathology, or musculoskeletal dysfunction. Second, measurements were obtained under static conditions that do not fully represent dynamic functional activities such as mastication, speech, or swallowing. Finally, although the kinematic system provided high-precision trajectory data, it did not record muscle activity directly, leaving the neuromuscular mechanisms underlying postural effects to be inferred. Moreover, the condylar points analyzed in this study were system-defined reference points identified by the electronic facebow system, and all measurements were acquired relative to a calibrated maxillary coordinate system rather than direct measurement against maxillary anatomical landmarks. Accordingly, the observed changes are best understood as posture-related positional changes within the measurement framework. Future studies should include more diverse samples and integrate electromyographic analysis to clarify neuromuscular mechanisms of posture-related mandibular adaptation. Longitudinal and interventional research is also needed to assess whether standardized postural control during CR recording can enhance clinical efficiency and facilitate prosthodontic and temporomandibular disorder management.

Conclusions

Based on our findings, and within the limitations of this study, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Body and head posture significantly influence the three-dimensional position of the system-defined lower incisal point and condylar points relative to the maxillary coordinate system during CR recording.
