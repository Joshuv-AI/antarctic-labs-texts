Non-ablative transurethral laser treatment for collagen remodeling with functional recovery in an in vivo model of stress urinary incontinence
Authors: Hwarang Shin, Minh Duc Ta, Myungji Kang, Yeachan Lee, Hyun Wook Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress urinary incontinence (SUI) is a common condition characterized by involuntary urine leakage under increased intra-abdominal pressure, such as coughing and physical exertion. The current treatments, including pelvic floor muscle training and surgical interventions such as mid-urethral sling procedures, remain limited by variable efficacy, invasiveness, and potential complications. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a novel, non-ablative transurethral 980 nm laser treatment as a minimally invasive, non-implantable alternative using a vaginal distension (VD)-induced SUI guinea pig model. Twelve female guinea pigs were randomly assigned to control, SUI, and laser treatment (SUI+LASER) groups. The VD model was induced by inserting a 10 Fr catheter into the vagina and expanding it for 1 h. After the VD, the SUI+LASER group received 980 nm irradiation (10 W, 5 s) applied transurethrally. Leak point pressure increased by 65% in the SUI+LASER group from day 0 to day 14. Histological analysis revealed significantly enhanced collagen deposition and muscle thickness in the SUI+LASER group, suggesting improved functional muscle recovery. These findings indicate that transurethral 980 nm laser treatment could effectively promote urethral tissue regeneration in the SUI model. Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI), the involuntary leakage of urine, is a common condition that significantly affects the quality of life for millions worldwide1,2. Among the various types of UI, stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is the most prevalent condition, especially in women. SUI occurs when increased intra-abdominal pressure from physical activities, such as coughing, sneezing, and exercising, leads to urine leakage2. This condition is commonly associated with weakened pelvic floor muscle or external urethral sphincter caused by childbirth, hormonal changes, and aging3,4. Additionally, obesity has emerged as a significant contributor to SUI. Excessive body weight increases intra-abdominal pressure, which places additional strain on the pelvic floor muscles and external urethral sphincter2.

The treatment of SUI encompasses both conservative and surgical approaches. Conservative treatments, such as pelvic floor muscle training and lifestyle modifications, are often the first-line options but may offer limited efficacy in moderate to severe cases5,6. The surgical interventions, including mid-urethral sling procedures, provide higher success rates but are invasive and carry potential risks, such as infection, pain, and recurrence7–9. Recently, laser-based treatment, which include CO₂ and Er: YAG ablative lasers, has emerged as minimally invasive intravaginal ablation. Nonetheless, the ablative laser treatment may result in significant gynecologic complications, such as vaginal burn, localized erythema, and chronic pain6,10. In fact, these laser treatments are primarily linked to vaginal rejuvenation and cosmetic procedures11–13. Therefore, the intravaginal laser treatment of SUI may lead to inaccurate tissue targeting and limited treatment effectiveness due to indirect treatment through the vagina. It is essential to directly target the urethral sphincter muscle through the urethra to maximize therapeutic efficacy.

Among available laser wavelengths, 980 nm diode lasers exhibit favorable photothermal characteristics for non-ablative endoluminal applications, owing to their relatively high absorption by water and hemoglobin14,15. Under controlled irradiation conditions, 980 nm laser exposure can induce localized mild hyperthermia capable of modulating collagenous and muscular tissues without causing epithelial ablation, and its optical penetration properties distinguish it from other wavelengths in deeper tissues14,16. These properties make the 980 nm wavelength particularly suitable for transurethral laser delivery aimed at reinforcing the urethral sphincter.

Based on these considerations, the goal of the current research is to develop and evaluate a new non-ablative transurethral laser treatment for SUI as a new minimally invasive and non-implantable procedure. It was hypothesized that the proposed non-ablative transurethral laser treatment could synthesize the collagen and increase muscle size without thermal damage to the urethra. An optical device was designed to deliver laser light to treat SUI in the guinea pig urethra. Both optical and thermal simulations were initially conducted to determine the optimal laser irradiation condition prior to in vivo tests. The in vivo tests were performed to assess the efficacy of the proposed endoluminal treatment and to evaluate the urethral functional changes (leak point pressure), histological changes, and gene expression in the urethra of the guinea pig. Hyperthermia (~ 44–46 °C for seconds) can promote tissue remodeling by partially denaturing collagen triple helices and enhancing fibroblast-mediated matrix turnover via TGF-β/SMAD signaling, leading to neocollagenesis without mucosal injury17. In skeletal muscle, mild heat stress engages the IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis to increase translational capacity and myofiber growth18–20. Moreover, photothermal exposure in the hyperthermia range has been reported to attenuate excessive inflammation by downregulating (TNFα/IL-6 and enhance IL-10, thereby creating a regenerative microenvironment that favors matrix remodeling and myogenesis over fibrosis21. In current study, the RT-qPCR process was conducted to confirm the gene expressions of the urethra related to muscle hypertrophy/protein synthesis (IGF, IGF-1R, PI3K, AKT, mTOR, EGFR, ERK, and STAT3) collagen/ECM remodeling (TGF-β, SMAD2/3/4, COL1A1, COL3A1, and Fibronectin), and inflammation/immune modulation (TNFα, NF-κB, IL-6, and IL-10) after laser treatment (Fig. 1). In addition, the current study builds upon our previous study in a porcine animal model, where non-ablative transurethral 980 nm laser treatment was demonstrated to be histologically safe and capable of inducing favorable tissue remodeling effects without mucosal injury22.

Materials and methods

Laser light delivery optical device

A 980 nm laser system (EsoLight Z360, TeCure, Inc., Busan, Republic of Korea) was employed to deliver laser light in the continuous-wave mode. A balloon-integrated optical device (BOD) was applied for concentrically delivering the laser light. A 400 μm core-diameter of a diffusing fiber (DF) was fabricated by the CO2 micromachining technique23–25. The DF was cut 3 mm in length to irradiate the laser light circumferentially. A balloon catheter (outer diameter = 3 mm) was integrated with the fabricated DF. A plastic catheter was then used to connect the DF with the distal end of the balloon by using epoxy to prevent any damage for the DF tip surface. The proximal end of the balloon was sealed with 1.2 m tubing to remove air from the BOD for deflation and to supply cooling liquid (water) for BOD inflation. The interior of the balloon was inserted into the cooling liquid (10 ℃) to lower the temperature of the urethral mucosal tissue prior to laser irradiation by using an inflator (balloon pressure = 1 ~ 1.5 atm).

Optical device beam profiles measurement

Before experiments, HeNe laser light (λ = 632 nm, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, New Jersey, USA) was applied to visualize light distribution along the BOD. The visualization of the light distribution was captured by a digital camera (D5100, Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan). To confirm the spatial profile emissions, both longitudinal and polar emissions from the BOD surface were measured by employing a goniometric sensor system (PD-300–3 W, Ophir, Jerusalem, Israel)26–29.

Numerical simulation

To determine the optimal irradiation conditions for endoluminal laser treatment, numerical simulation was conducted to predict the spatio-temporal variations in temperature and thermal damage in the guinea pig urethral tissue. The simulation was performed in a cylindrical 3D geometry (xyz plane). The outermost part of the geometry represented the urethra (outer diameter = 4 mm), while the inner regions corresponded to a BOD (outer diameter = 3 mm; thickness = 0.1 mm), including cooling liquid. The irradiation condition of 10 W for 5 s was selected and evaluated in the simulation to predict both temperature distribution and thermal damage in the urethral tissue. The detailed descriptions of the entire simulation were shown in the supplementary materials and methods.

Animal preparation

The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Pukyong National University (Approval number: PKNUIACUC-2024-50). Figure 1 presents schematic illustrations intended to depict the experimental procedures rather than exact guinea pig anatomy. Twelve female guinea pigs (Dunkin Hartley Guinea pig; age = 8 weeks; weight = 500–550 g; nulliparous) were purchased from Samtako (Osan, Republic of Korea). After one week of acclimatization, the animals were randomly divided into three groups to compare treatment efficacy (CTRL = control, SUI = stress urinary incontinence, and SUI+LASER = stress urinary incontinence treated with transurethral laser irradiation; N = 4 per group). The SUI group underwent vaginal distension without laser treatment to evaluate injury-induced functional impairment, while the SUI+LASER group received transurethral laser irradiation following VD to assess treatment effects. The sample size (N = 4 per group) was determined to balance statistical reliability and ethical considerations in line with the 3Rs principle (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement). The selected sample size was supported by variability estimates from pilot guinea pig experiments for leak point pressure, collagen and muscle distribution analysis. During the experiments, all the animals were anesthetized using a respiratory anesthesia system (R500-A-4 L, RWD Life Science, China) in a chamber. Isoflurane (Terrell™ Isoflurane, Piramal Critical Care, Bethlehem, PA, USA) in oxygen (0.8 L/min) was used for anesthesia, and anesthesia was continuously maintained throughout the vaginal distension procedure, laser treatment, and functional assessment. At the end of the experiment, all animals were humanely euthanized in accordance with the guidelines of the IACUC. Euthanasia was carried out using an overdose of isoflurane followed by carbon dioxide inhalation, in accordance with the current edition of the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest and lack of reflexes. Additionally, this study was confirmed in accordance with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). The experimental design was adapted from our previous study in a porcine animal model22, with protocol modifications to account for the anatomical and physiological characteristics of guinea pigs. These adjustments included scaling balloon inflation volumes, adapting saline infusion rates, and optimizing laser delivery parameters for the smaller urethral dimensions.

In vivo test

An SUI guinea pig model was established by balloon-induced vaginal distension (VD) adapted from established rodent30,31 and porcine protocols with parameter adjustments for guinea pig anatomy and tissue compliance based on preliminary trials​. Under anesthesia, a 10Fr foley catheter was inserted into the vagina, and a balloon was inflated with 2 ml of saline at infusion rate of 0.5 mL/s to distend the vagina. Distension was maintained for 1 h to induce acute injury to the external urethral sphincter followed by balloon deflation and removal. A CTRL group underwent no vaginal distension. To validate SUI induction, leak point pressure (LPP) was measured immediately before and after VD, and histological analysis (hematoxylin and eosin and Masson’s trichrome) in pilot animals confirmed thinning of the external urethral sphincter and decreased collagen density. A digital camera (D5100, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to identify any structural changes in the urogenital system at 14 days, and an ultrasound imaging system (E-cube8 with 21.5-inch Full HE LED monitor, C508NT, Alpinion medical systems Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea) was used to observe the bladder capacity in the guinea pig to compare any difference among three groups (CTRL, SUI, and LASER). For urodynamics tests in all the groups, leak point pressure measurement was conducted on day 0 (before vaginal distension) and day 14 (after vaginal distension and laser treatment). A urodynamic catheter (MMS U 73901, Cheongwoon, Suwon, Republic of Korea) was inserted into the guinea pig bladder dome in the supine position through the urethra. After the bladder was emptied, 3 ml of saline was injected to fill the bladder. The abdomen was compressed gently and manually to increase abdominal pressure until fluid leakage occurred. Upon the leakage, the pressure in the abdomen was immediately removed, and the peak pressure was measured through the urodynamic catheter. The urodynamic catheter was connected to a biological signal acquisition system (Biopac MP 36, BIOPAC Systems, Inc., CA, USA) with a pressure transducer (SS13L, BIOPAC Systems, Inc., CA, USA). Vaginal expansion was defined as an increase in the vaginal introitus diameter associated with mucosal protrusion and redness compared to baseline (Day 0). Morphological changes were captured using standardized digital photography under identical lighting, distance, and animal positioning to ensure consistency in visual assessment.

In vivo laser treatment

A BOD was initially inserted into the urethra of each guinea pig with SUI, and cooling liquid was then injected into the BOD to inflate the balloon in the BOD by using an inflator. The cooling liquid maintained at 10 ℃. 10 ℃ colling liquid was selected based on numerical simulation (Fig. 2), which showed that it protects the urethral mucosal surface while allowing the submucosal tissue to reach ~ 45 ℃ for reversible hyperthermia without irreversible thermal damage. For in vivo experiments, cooling liquid was pre-aliquoted into 50 mL conical tubes and placed in an ice-containing beaker. The temperature was monitored with a thermometer until it reached 10 ℃. The cooling liquid was then immediately delivered into the balloon via the inflator to ensure the temperature during laser irradiation. In the SUI+LASER group, laser irradiation was performed immediately after completion of the VD procedure, within 5 min after balloon deflation. After the balloon inflation, 980 nm laser light was irradiated onto the luminal surface of the urethra at 10 W for 5 s, which was selected from numerical simulations. Following the laser treatment, all guinea pigs were monitored for 14 days to evaluate treatment efficacy in terms of muscle hypertrophy, collagen synthesis, and inflammation.

Histological analysis

14 days after laser treatment, the treated urethral tissues were fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde (HT401128; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h at 4 ℃. The fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin blocks and sectioned at 4 μm. The sectioned slides were deparaffinized with xylene (UN1307; Duksan general science; Seoul; Republic of Korea) and dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol (000E0880; Duksan general science; Seoul; Republic of Korea). After deparaffinization and dehydration, the tissue slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome (MT) to evaluate the morphological changes and collagen distribution in the guinea pig urethral tissue. All the stained slides were observed using the Motic Digital Slide Assistant System (Kowloon; Hong Kong). Mitotic cells were identified in HE-stained images based on morphological criteria, including condensed and hyperchromatic chromatin, absence of an intact nuclear membrane, and visible mitotic figures corresponding to prophase, metaphase, anaphase, or telophase. Mitotic cells were defined as urethral tissue cells satisfying the specified morphological criteria and were evaluated for qualitative analysis. The ImageJ program (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to quantify the mucosa thickness, urethral skeletal muscle layer thickness, and collagen distribution in the guinea pig urethra. Muscle thickness was defined as the perpendicular distance between the inner and outer borders of the skeletal muscle layer in histological cross-sections, as identified in MT-stained images. The measurement reflects changes at the tissue-layer level rather than individual myofiber size. All the stained slides were randomly selected from five different areas and then quantified using the color deconvolution plugin in ImageJ (N = 4).

RT-qPCR analysis

14 days after laser treatment, the treated urethral tissues were harvested to evaluate chronic tissue responses, including muscle hypertrophy, collagen synthesis, and inflammation. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed to examine mRNA expressions. The extracted urethral tissues were homogenized, and RNA was isolated using TRizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using 5 µl of Hisense™ cDNA synthesis mater mix (CDS-100, Cells safe Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Table 1 lists primer sequences for PCR. To detect the mRNA expression level, qPCR analysis was performed using HiSense™ QGreenBlue qPCR master mix (RTQBLR-05, Cells safe Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea) according to the following conditions: initial step at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s. Target genes were pre-specified to probe hypothesized repair axes: TGF-β/SMAD-ECM remodeling, IGF-1/PI3K/AKT/mTOR-dependent protein synthesis, EGFR/ERK/STAT3-related proliferation, and TNFα/NF-kB/IL-6/IL-10-mediated inflammatory balance. Gene expression was quantified as fold change relative to the control group, and the relative expression was calculated using the 2− ddCt method with β-actin as the reference gene.

Statistical analysis

All data were represented as mean ± standard deviation. For nonparametric statistical analysis, both Kruskal-Wallis (KW) and Mann–Whitney U with Bonferroni correction (MU) tests were performed to compare between two groups by using the software SPSS (SPSS, Inc.). P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Figure 3 characterizes the emission profile of a fabricated BOD. The BOD fiber was applied to a 400 μm core optical fiber (Fig. 3A). To assess the beam profile, both longitudinal and polar emissions from the BOD surface were measured using a goniometric sensor system (Fig. 3B). Figure 3C displays a 3D polar emission profile from proximal (P) end to distal (D) end of the DF. The P-D length (LPD) was approximately 7 mm, with an axial radial measurement distance of 1 mm. The normalized intensity at 1 mm was higher than those at other positions. The mean normalized angular intensity deviation was less than 5% (Fig. 3D.96 ± 0.03). Figure 3E depicts the longitudinal emission along the LPD, revealing an asymmetric light distribution with two intensity peaks near the middle (i.e., 2 and 4 mm positions). The intensity difference between these peaks was 17%. The two intensity peaks can lead to the rapid formation of the uniform distribution, as shown in29. The intensity dropped by 19% at 3.5 mm position, compared to the highest peak.

Evaluation of temperature distribution and thermal damage in numerical simulation

Figure 2 represents the numerical simulations of temperature distribution and thermal damage in BOD and guinea pig urethral tissue during laser irradiation at 10 W for 5 s. In the longitudinal views (Fig. 2A, left), the temperature increased in the urethral tissue along the active length of BOD (x-axis: -2 mm to 2 mm and y-axis: -2.5 mm to 2.5 mm). The BOD temperature remained lower than that of the urethral tissue due to the cooling liquid inside (15.5 ~ 23.2 ℃). It was determined that the initial injection of cooling liquid was at a temperature of 10 ℃. The cross-sectional views (Fig. 2A, right) display a symmetric and uniform temperature distribution in the urethral tissue at 10 W for 5 s. After the laser irradiation, the urethral temperature of guinea pig reached 44.8 ℃, inducing reversible hyperthermia (initial urethral temperature = 37 ℃), with no irreversible thermal damage (maximum log(Ω) = -15). Figure 2B represents temperature profiles along the x-axis (± 1.5 mm) after the irradiation. The lowest temperature occurred inside the BOD because of the cooling liquid (initial temperature = 10 ℃) which corresponded to the middle region (pink area in Fig. 2B). The boundary between the BOD and urethral tissue exhibited a steep temperature gradient (x position = ± 1.5 mm). Figure 2C shows the temporal temperature changes at the measured point of urethra (MP; Fig. 2A). The maximum temperature was calculated 44.8 ℃ at 5 s, which was the end of laser irradiation. After the end of the laser irradiation, the temperature gradually decreased over time. Based on the numerical simulations, the laser irradiation condition of 10 W for 5 s was selected for in vivo testing in order to achieve hyperthermia (temperature of ~ 45 ℃32 without causing irreversible thermal damage.

Assessment of functional ability in in vivo guinea pig model

Figure 4 shows in vivo monitoring and functional evaluation of guinea pig urethra. According to Fig. 4A, CTRL group exhibited no morphological changes in the urogenital system (urethra, vagina, and anus) between day 0 and day 14. On the other hand, both SUI and SUI+LASER groups displayed vaginal redness and expansion after vaginal distension using a Foley catheter (inlet in Fig. 4A). On day 14, the vaginal expansion persisted in the SUI group, whereas the SUI+LASER group showed recovery in vaginal shape and color. No bladder area changes were detected in ultrasound images. (Fig. 4A). Leak point pressure (LPP) was measured by saline injection into the guinea pig bladder and monitored via laptop (Fig. 4B). On day 0, there was no significant difference in LPP (P = 0.8; CTRL = 1.84 ± 0.67 cmH2O, SUI = 1.60 ± 0.53 cmH2O, and LASER = 1.22 ± 0.46 cmH2O). However, after laser treatment, LPP increased significantly in the SUI+LASER group, as shown in Fig. 4B. Especially, a considerable increase in pressure was measured to be 2.00 ± 0.18 cmH2O on day 14 (KW P < 0.05 and MU P < 0.005 for SUI vs. LASER; LPP in SUI group = 1.08 ± 0.83 cmH2O). In addition, LPP was increased by 65% in the SUI+LASER group from day 0 to day 14. In turn, these results indicate that functional ability was restored in the SUI+LASER group, as opposed to the SUI group without affecting the urogenital system and functional ability of the urethra.

Histological analysis of transurethral laser treatment

To verify potential therapeutic effects, an in vivo test was conducted by using 980 nm laser light. Figure 5 presents histological changes associated collagen synthesis and muscle hypertrophy on day 14 post-transurethral laser treatment. Figure 5A reveals the increased mitotic cell proliferation in SUI+LASER group, compared to CTRL and SUI groups. It was conceived that the SUI+LASER group activated the cell proliferation in urethra by the 980 nm laser. Figure 5A, B show similar blood vessels count across all the groups (CTRL, SUI, and LASER) with no significant neovascularization changes (KW P = 0.3; CTRL = 12.8 ± 3.7, SUI = 13.64 ± 4.69, and LASER = 15.72 ± 2.31). Collagen distribution and muscle thickness were assessed using MT-stained tissues on day 14 after the laser treatment (Fig. 5C). There was no difference in urethral lumen area in all the groups (CTRL = 0.26 ± 0.06 mm2, SUI = 0.26 ± 0.09 mm2, LASER = 0.33 ± 0.09 mm2). The SUI+LASER group exhibited greater collagen deposition (blue staining) than the SUI group. Additionally, skeletal muscle thickness (purple staining) was lowest in the SUI group. The SUI group had the smallest muscle fiber size and length (Fig. 5C). According to Fig. 5C, D, the urethral mucosa thickness showed insignificantly different in all the groups (Fig. 5D; CTRL, SUI, and LASER; P = 0.9), indicating no thermal damage in the SUI+LASER group. Collagen distribution in the submucosa increased in the SUI and SUI+LASER groups, compared to CTRL group (CTRL = 43.5 ± 0.3%, SUI = 47.3 ± 0.3%, and LASER = 45.2 ± 0.4%). Notably, collagen distribution was significantly higher in the SUI group (47.3%) than the CTRL group (43.5%; P < 0.05). Figure 5F, G illustrate urethral skeletal muscle thickness and distribution. The SUI group had the thinnest skeletal muscle (458.14 ± 16.33 μm), compared to CTRL (569.65 ± 72.42 μm) and LASER (611.11 ± 25.94 μm) groups (MU P < 0.01). In addition, collagen content decreased by 27% in the SUI group, compared to the CTRL group (Fig. 5G; CTRL = 35.8 ± 0.2% and SUI = 26.1 ± 0.2%; MU P < 0.001). In turn, there was significant decrease in not only the skeletal muscle thickness but also urethral muscle distribution in SUI group, compared to all the groups (CTRL and LASER). On the other hand, the SUI+LASER group showed significantly increased urethral skeletal muscle thickness (611.1 ± 25.9 μm; 33% higher than SUI; MU P < 0.01) and urethral muscle distribution (34.0 ± 0.3%; MU P < 0.05), compared to the SUI group. It was evident that the proposed laser treatment enhanced the urethral skeletal muscle as well as the urethral collagen distribution. In Fig. S1, vaginal collagen distribution was higher in the SUI+LASER group (54.24 ± 0.65%), compared to CTRL (52.8 ± 4.0%) and SUI (41.8 ± 0.8%) groups (Fig. S1A, B). Conversely, vaginal mucosa thickness significantly increased in the SUI+LASER group (192.2 ± 8.7 μm), compared to all the groups (CTRL = 55.6 ± 4.8 μm and SUI = 61.64 ± 2.5 μm; MU P < 0.05 vs. CTRL; MU P < 0.005 vs. SUI; Fig. S1A, C). These findings suggest that the potential of transurethral 980 nm laser treatment be a means of inducing both collagen synthesis and muscle hypertrophy.

Identification of mRNA expression in guinea pig urethra

To confirm the potential therapeutic effect of the proposed transurethral laser treatment, qPCR analysis was performed. Figure 6 presents the mRNA expression levels of collagen synthesis, muscle hypertrophy, and inflammation-associated cell proliferation and inflammatory response (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, SUI+LASER group exhibited significant upregulation of TGF- β, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD4, and COL1A1 expression (85-, 288-, 3-, 28-, and 15-fold, respectively), compared to SUI group (MU P < 0.05). Conversely, COL3A1 and fibronectin expression levels were lower in the SUI+LASER group than in the SUI group but were comparable to or slightly increased, relative to the CTRL group. These molecular analyses indicate that collagen synthesis may be enhanced through upregulation of genes associated with the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway in the SUI+LASER group, although direct pathway activation was not confirmed. Additionally, genes associated with PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which promoted protein synthesis and facilitates new protein translation within muscle cells, were examined (Fig. 6C). In the SUI+LASER group, IGFR, PI3K, AKT, and mTOR expression levels were significantly upregulated (6.5, 4.3, 7.3, and 74-fold, respectively), compared to the SUI group (MU P < 0.05), suggesting possible involvement of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in muscle hypertrophy; however, activation at the protein or phosphorylation level was not measured. To further investigate the proliferative effects of the proposed laser treatment, the EGFR pathway, which regulates cell proliferation, was examined by measuring ERK and STAT3 expression (Fig. 6D). The SUI+LASER group exhibited significant upregulation of EGFR, ERK and STAT3 (5.1, 5.6, and 2.2-fold, respectively), compared to the SUI group (MU P < 0.05). According to Fig. 6E, TNF-α signaling pathway, which plays a key role in acute and chronic inflammation, was analyzed. The pro-inflammatory factor IL-6 was significantly lower (8.5-fold) in the SUI+LASER group than in the SUI group (P < 0.05), whereas the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 was significantly upregulated (2.2-fold; P < 0.05). As a result, the qPCR analysis revealed that the SUI+LASER group effectively stimulated the gene expression in the urethra while mitigating inflammation, demonstrating its potential for minimally invasive treatment of stress urinary incontinence.

Discussion

The current study demonstrated that a balloon-integrated optical device (BOD) exhibited a highly uniform spatial light distribution to ensure consistent laser energy delivery for therapeutic applications. In Fig. 3D, the polar emission profile of the BOD was measured at 0.96 ± 0.03, surpassing previously reported normalized angular intensities (i.e., 0.86 ± 0.0926, 0.95 ± 0.0333, 0.94 ± 0.0334, and 0.9 ± 0.0535. According to the 1/e2 (∼0.135) intensity criterion29,36, the longitudinal width of the output beam from the BOD was approximately 8 mm (Fig. 3E). Due to the light divergence from the diffusing fiber (DF)25, the longitudinal width of the output beam was almost 2.5 times larger than the cutting length of the DF at the balloon surface. Based on the effect of heat flow on the temperature distribution29,37, the two intensity peaks observed from the DF (Fig. 3E) are expected to gradually merge into a single highest peak after propagation through various tissue depths. Notably, these dual peaks were hardly observed in the in vivo guinea pig model and histological analysis, implicating that tissue scattering and absorption effectively smoothed out the beam profile. Thus, the fabricated BOD (Fig. 3A) successfully achieved homogeneous emission in both polar and longitudinal directions. Due to the consistent radial emission from the proximal-to-distal (P-D) ends (Fig. 3C), the circumferential laser treatment effect was produced within the urethral tissue. Nevertheless, it is crucial to monitor the beam profile in real-time during in vivo laser treatment in order to optimize therapeutic outcomes and to minimize the risk of adverse thermal effects.

Transurethral 980 nm laser treatment showed distinct advantages over prior vaginal laser approaches (Er: YAG and CO₂ lasers) in efficacy and targeting. The traditional vaginal lasers deliver energy to the vaginal mucosa instead, inducing collagen contraction and remodeling through controlled thermal injury​6,10,38–40. Er: YAG laser (wavelength = 2.94 μm) causes hyperthermia of superficial vaginal collagen (penetration ~ 5–20 μm), leading to tissue tightening. In contrast, fractional CO₂ laser (wavelength = 10.6 μm) creates micro-ablative columns (~ 50–125 μm depth) that trigger healing responses​6,38–41. Although these methods can improve vaginal wall support modestly, their limited penetration hardly achieves the direct SUI treatment. The shallow depth confines thermal effects largely to the epithelium and subepithelial vaginal tissue, with minimal impact on the deeper urethral sphincter complex​42. Clinical studies have reported that the vaginal laser treatment carries risks (vaginal burn, scarring) due to imprecise energy delivery11. In fact, a FDA safety communication in 2018 warned against the vaginal rejuvenation lasers for the SUI treatment due to a lack of evidence and potential complications​11,12. On the other hand, the proposed method delivered 980 nm transurethral laser light to the urethra through a circumferential optical device, directly targeting the urethral submucosa and muscle layers​. The non-ablative 980 nm laser achieved a far greater penetration (penetration depth = 2 ~ 3 mm​) into the urethral wall, effectively reaching the striated sphincter and the surrounding connective tissue22. In Figs. 4 and 5, LPP increased by approximately 65%, and histological analysis showed that the 980 nm laser treatment applied immediately after VD preserved urethral skeletal muscle thickness, resulting in a 33% greater value at 14 days compared with the untreated SUI group. Notably, histological analysis confirmed that laser energy preserved the urethral mucosa (no coagulative damage or thinning; Fig. 5D) while remodeling the urethral submucosa and muscle. Therefore, the transurethral 980 nm laser demonstrated a novel focused modality that can overcome the limited depth and safety of the vaginal laser treatments.

Photobiomodulation (PBM), also referred to as low-level laser therapy (LLLT), has been widely reported to induce cellular proliferation, enhance stem cell differentiation, and modulate inflammation through non-thermal photochemical mechanisms43,44. In PBM, low-irradiance light in the visible to near-infrared range is primarily absorbed by mitochondrial chromophores such as cytochrome c oxidase, leading to increased mitochondrial respiration, ATP production, and secondary signaling cascades involving reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide release, and transcriptional regulation45,46. These processes collectively contribute to enhanced tissue repair and anti-inflammatory effects without a measurable rise in tissue temperature44. In contrast, the therapeutic mechanism explored in the present study is not based on classical PBM, but rather on controlled non-ablative photothermal hyperthermia (Fig. 2). The transurethral 980 nm laser irradiation was designed to transiently elevate tissue temperature into a mild hyperthermic range, thereby promoting collagen remodeling and skeletal muscle adaptation through heat-responsive pathways, while preserving mucosal integrity (Figs. 5 and 6). Although both PBM and photothermal hyperthermia can ultimately facilitate tissue repair, they differ fundamentally in their primary photoreceptors, energy deposition profiles, and dominant biological signaling mechanisms. Clarifying this distinction is important for accurate interpretation of the observed molecular, histological, and functional outcomes.

A vaginal distension (VD)-induced SUI model hardly emulates chronic SUI pathology in the current study. In rodents, a single simulated childbirth injury (prolonged vaginal distension) causes acute pelvic floor trauma, leading to transient SUI that often spontaneously recover within a few weeks30,31​. Recent studies have reported that mice subjected to VD had significantly reduced LPP for up to 10 days but recovered normal continence by 20 days as the urethral sphincter muscle and nerves repaired themselves30,47​. It was notable that the VD model primarily reflected an acute, reversible injury response. In contrast, human SUI usually develops over years of repetitive stress and incomplete healing47,48. Clinical SUI is often associated with chronic degeneration, cumulative damage from vaginal deliveries, aging, hypoestrogenism, and long-term elevation of intra-abdominal pressure (cough, obesity, etc.)2​. The young and healthy animals can heal the induced injury quickly, which may overestimate treatment effects or fail to capture long-term failure modes47. In the present study, the 980 nm laser was applied soon after an acute injury, and tissue analysis at 14 days likely coincided with the natural healing phase. The implication is that while the 980 nm transurethral laser treatment showed encouraging tissue effects in a short-term injury model, its efficacy in a persistent SUI condition remains unproven. To increase clinical translatability, future studies should test chronic SUI models that more closely resemble human pathophysiology. These may include aged or multiparous animals, models involving repeated VD to simulate cumulative injury, or surgical/neuromuscular interventions that induce long-term urethral sphincter dysfunction. These SUI models would allow evaluation of whether the proposed treatment can reverse established fibrosis, muscle atrophy, or neurogenic dysfunction, rather than merely enhancing normal healing. Incorporating functional endpoints (e.g., LPP, electromyography) over extended observation periods will also be essential. Both Chronic models and long-term in vivo studies will be crucial to determine whether the proposed 980 nm laser treatment can provide functional improvements in SUI.

In the current study, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that treatment with 980 nm laser light was associated with gene expression changes related to TGF-β1/SMAD and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways, compared to untreated SUI group (Fig. 6B, C). These gene expression profiles are consistent with known roles of these pathway in extracellular matrix remodeling and muscle growth, respectively49,50. Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) is a master cytokine in wound healing that stimulates fibroblasts to synthesize collagen and other matrix components51. The elevated TGF-β/SMAD signal in the SUI+LASER group is consistent with the histological findings of enhanced collagen content in the urethra (Fig. 5E), compared to the control group​. Concurrently, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a central mediator of cell growth and protein synthesis and upregulation of genes within this pathway has been linked to skeletal muscle hypertrophy in other models49,50,52. The gene expression changes related to PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is a represented trigger for skeletal muscle hypertrophy, as it upregulates protein synthesis in muscle fibers and inhibits atrophic processes53. The SUI+LASER group translated to the growth of the sphincter muscle fibers (Fig. 5F, G). The combination of collagen synthesis and muscle hypertrophy suggests that the transurethral 980 nm laser rebuilds both the structural scaffold and contractile apparatus of the urethra. In contrast, the SUI group exhibited molecular signs of a more pathological healing response (Fig. 5E). Notably, fibronectin expression was elevated in the SUI group (Fig. 6B; VD animal model without laser). Fibronectin is an ECM glycoprotein that is rapidly laid down after tissue injury to provide a provisional matrix54,55. However, high fibronectin is associated with fibrogenesis and scar stabilization​, which forms a template for excessive collagen deposition and myofibroblast activity, leading to stiff, non-compliant tissue (a fibrotic scar). The laser-treated tissue had lower fibronectin levels relative to the SUI group, suggesting that the laser treatment mitigated the pro-fibrotic response (Fig. 6B), which is crucial because uncontrolled fibrosis can affect function54. The LASER promotes collagen synthesis and muscle hypertrophy while restraining fibrotic, thereby providing a mechanism for functional recovery. These molecular insights align with the functional goal of SUI treatment to restore a supple yet strong urethra. The upregulation of the TGF-β/SMAD and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways in the SUI+LASER group correlated with enhanced collagen and muscle distribution, along with a relative reduction in fibronectin. These findings demonstrate that the transurethral laser engaged regenerative pathways to rebuild the SUI mechanism. Future studies should quantify molecular changes over time and confirm whether the newly deposited collagen matures without progressing into harmful fibrosis.

Despite the SUI treatment effect of transurethral 980 nm laser, the current study has several limitations for clinical translation. The current treatment follow-up duration is relatively short (14 days) capturing only the early phase of wound healing. Tissue remodeling, especially collagen maturation and skeletal muscle adaptation, can evolve over several months56,57. Consequently, the increase in urethral collagen content and muscle thickness observed at 14 days should be interpreted as early reparative changes rather than definitive evidence of long-term regeneration58,59. Notably, these early changes were observed in comparison with the VD-only (SUI) group evaluated at the same time point, which did not show comparable functional or histological improvement, supporting an association with laser treatment rather than spontaneous recovery alone. Therefore, the molecular findings and histological alterations reported here are best considered as associations with the observed functional improvements, rather than proof of sustained pathway activation or mature tissue remodeling. Further investigations will evaluate the long-term urethral collagen synthesis and muscle changes as 3, 6, and 12 months post-treatment to determine the persistence and quality of these changes. In the present study, the evaluation was restricted to a single laser wavelength and energy-delivery strategy. Specifically, this study focused on non-ablative transurethral irradiating using a 980 nm diode laser. Other laser modalities, including Er: YAG systems operated in smooth mode, may exhibit distinct tissue-laser interaction characteristics and could potentially be adapted for transurethral applications60,61. Accordingly, direct comparisons across laser wavelengths and delivery mechanisms will be important in future studies to better delineate differences in efficacy, tissue penetration, and safety profiles. Numerical simulation (Fig. 2) was analyzed to determine the laser treatment condition. However, the real-time thermal monitoring during in vivo tests is needed to confirm that the predicted temperature from the numerical simulation is accurately achieved in the urethral of the animal model. The lack of animal temperature monitoring data implicates uncertainties in the thermal dose delivered even though histology showed no mucosal thermal injury post-treatment (Fig. 5D). Therefore, in further studies, the real-time temperature monitoring (e.g., Fiber Bragg Grating sensors or thermocouples) will be performed to elucidate the urethral temperature during the laser treatment. In current study, the observed non-ablative transurethral 980 nm laser treatment could induce collagen and muscle regeneration in the urethra. However, it is crucial to compare the proposed laser treatment with the current standard mid-urethral sling surgery. Therefore, for clinical applications, future investigations will pursue comparative trials with larger sample sizes, chronic SUI models and extended follow-up periods to fully assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles of the proposed laser treatment, compared to mid-urethral sling surgery. The current study relates to the vaginal distension (VD) model employed. While prolonged VD protocols with extended distension durations and delayed functional assessment are widely used to establish chronic stress urinary incontinence models, the VD procedure in this study was intentionally designed to induce acute external urethral sphincter injury rather than chronic SUI. Accordingly, distension was maintained for a shorter duration, and functional assessment was performed immediately to capture acute impairment and early therapeutic responses. As a result, this model does not fully recapitulate the pathophysiological features of chronic SUI or long-term disease progression. Although the acute injury model is appropriate for evaluating early intervention strategies and short-term functional recovery, future studies employing chronic VD models with extended observation periods will be necessary to validate long-term treatment efficacy and disease-modifying effects. Another limitation of the current study is that the estrous cycle stage of guinea pigs was not determined at the time of intervention or tissue collection. Because estrogen status can influence epithelial and stromal morphology, the lack of staging may have contributed to variability in histological appearance62,63. However, animals were randomly divided into experimental groups, which is expected to have balanced any cycle-related differences across groups and minimized systematic bias. Moreover, the primary focus of the present study was on laser-induced tissue remodeling and functional recovery, rather than on hormonal modulation. Future studies may incorporate estrous cycle monitoring or aged animal models to further clarify potential endocrine influences on urethral remodeling. In this study, only mRNA expression was analyzed to investigate potential molecular pathways associated with the therapeutic effects of transurethral laser treatment. While these results suggest possible involvement of TGF-β/SMAD, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, EGFR, and TNF-α signaling, protein expression and phosphorylation analyses are necessary to confirm pathway activation. Future studies will incorporate western blotting or immunohistochemistry to validate these molecular mechanisms.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrated the efficacy profile of new transurethral 980 nm laser treatment in an in vivo stress urinary incontinence animal model. The proposed laser treatment could provide a new treatment option for SUI by promoting collagen synthesis and muscle hypertrophy. Further investigations will validate the long-term safety and efficacy in in vivo porcine models and analyze the entire genomes and targeted regions of DNA and RNA using next-generation sequencing.
