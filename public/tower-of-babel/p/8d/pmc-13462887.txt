Papillary thyroid carcinoma with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stromal component is a rare subtype of papillary carcinoma with moderate aggressiveness: a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study of 725 patients
Authors: Zuhao Yang, Shanshan Zhang, Donglai Fan, Chen Qian, Yingjie Xu, Chengguang Yang, Weiqi Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPapillary thyroid carcinoma with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (PTC/WF/FLS) is a rare subtype with poorly defined clinical and biological behavior. We observed an increasing number of cases and a relatively high rate of capsular invasion, suggesting potential local recurrence and postoperative relapse, which warrant further investigation. The objective of this study is to delineate the invasive and metastatic capabilities of this subtype through the investigation of its clinicobiological parameters.MethodsAccording to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Thyroid Tumors, 725 patients with thyroid cancer collected between 2017 and 2021 were classified into a PTC/WF/FLS observation group, high-risk group (HRG), and low-risk group (LRG). Age, sex, tumor location, lesion number, tumor size, family history of cancer, genetic mutations, and tumor microenvironment were compared among the three groups, and their effects on lymph node metastasis and local invasion of the capsule, blood vessels, and nerves were analyzed.ResultsThe PTC/WF/FLS, HRG, and LRG showed no significant differences in age, sex, tumor location, lesion number, genetic mutations, or tumor microenvironment. In early-stage disease (T1), the PTC/WF/FLS group had the highest capsular invasion rate compared with the HRG and LRG (16.28%, 5.53%, and 7.51%, respectively; P=0.003). The association with a family history of cancer was also higher in the PTC/WF/FLS group than in the HRG and LRG (9.24%, 4.91%, and 3.44%, respectively; P=0.04). Propensity-matched and stratified analyses showed that the VI-region lymph node metastasis rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group was intermediate between those in the HRG and LRG, although the between-group differences were not significant (P=0.10 and P=0.97, respectively).ConclusionsPTC/WF/FLS is a rare, moderately aggressive subtype of papillary carcinoma. It shows a strong tendency toward capsular invasion and lymphatic metastasis, even at an early stage (T1), which may increase the risk of early local recurrence and postoperative relapse.Keywords: Cell pathological subtype, papillary thyroid carcinoma with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (PTC/WF/FLS), family inheritance, gene mutation, tumor microenvironment

Key findings

• Papillary thyroid carcinoma with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (PTC/WF/FLS) is a rare, moderately aggressive subtype of papillary carcinoma. Compared to the high- and low-risk cellular subtypes of papillary carcinoma, it shows a strong tendency toward capsular invasion and lymphatic metastasis, even at an early stage (T1), which may increase the risk of early local recurrence and postoperative relapse.

What is known and what is new?

• PTC/WF/FLS is a relatively rare subtype, with a reported incidence of 0.03–0.5%, relatively high invasiveness, and a propensity for early lymph node metastasis. Published reports suggest that its molecular characteristics reflect a unique bicomponent tumor feature. The epithelial component is primarily characterized by the BRAF-V600E mutation and MAPK pathway activation, and the stromal component is mainly characterized by CTNNB1 mutations and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation.

• PTC/WF/FLS is a moderately aggressive subtype of papillary carcinoma. Compared to the high- and low-risk cellular subtypes of papillary carcinoma, it shows a strong tendency toward capsular invasion and lymphatic metastasis, even at an early stage (T1), which may increase the risk of early local recurrence and postoperative relapse.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Given that this subtype already demonstrates a high incidence of capsular invasion and lymph node metastasis at the early T1 stage, surgeons should prioritize the completeness and adequacy of surgical resection when managing this subtype to prevent local recurrence and metastasis resulting from residual tumor tissue.

Introduction

The incidence of thyroid cancer has increased steadily in recent years (1). Papillary thyroid carcinoma with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (PTC/WF/FLS) is a relatively rare subtype, with a reported incidence of 0.03–0.5%, relatively high invasiveness, and a propensity for early lymph node metastasis (2). The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines (8th edition) do not identify explicit clinical risks for this subtype (3,4). However, our observations indicate an increasing number of PTC/WF/FLS cases, underscoring the need for further investigation. PTC/WF/FLS has poorly defined clinical and biological behavior. Published reports suggest that its molecular characteristics reflect a unique bicomponent tumor feature, in which the malignant epithelial and benign stromal components are driven by two independent and distinct signaling pathways (5,6). The epithelial component is primarily characterized by the BRAF-V600E mutation and MAPK pathway activation, which drive epithelial malignant transformation (5,6). In contrast, the stromal component is mainly characterized by CTNNB1 mutations and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, which promote stromal overgrowth and are associated with local tumor recurrence (6,7). Because PTC/WF/FLS shares BRAF mutations and histopathological features, including ground-glass nuclei and papillary architecture, with high- and low-risk papillary carcinoma subtypes, and because the biological behavior of these comparator subtypes is well established (3,4,8), we compared PTC/WF/FLS with the high-risk group (HRG) and low-risk group (LRG). We investigated the clinical onset and epidemiological characteristics of PTC/WF/FLS to provide a theoretical foundation and practical reference for the diagnosis and treatment of thyroid cancer. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-0190/rc).

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital, and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Clinical data were retrospectively retrieved from the thyroid cancer patient database of Shanghai Tongren Hospital for 725 patients who underwent surgical treatment for thyroid cancer between January 2017 and December 2021. The cohort included 119 patients with PTC/WF/FLS (Figure 1) and 606 patients with non-PTC/WF/FLS subtypes, who were further classified into HRG and LRG. We compared age at onset, sex, lesion location, lesion size, lesion number, capsular invasion, lymph node metastasis, tumor microenvironment, familial genetic predisposition to tumors, and disease mutations among the PTC/WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG. Patients were informed of all preoperative risks, and written informed consent was obtained before surgery. Cytopathological subtype classifications were determined by two pathologists with more than 10 years of experience in slide interpretation. Disease-related gene mutations, including BRAF, NRAS, KRAS, HRAS, TERT, CCDC6-RET, PAX8-PPAR, and ETV6-NTRK3, were detected in paraffin-embedded tissue sections using a PCR-based thyroid cancer gene detection kit (Shanghai Rightongene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Patient screening flowchart. WF/FLS, with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients undergoing open, laparoscopic-assisted, or total laparoscopic thyroid lobectomy with isthmus resection, with total thyroidectomy performed for bilateral cancer, with or without lymph node dissection. The extent of thyroidectomy included unilateral lobectomy, bilateral thyroidectomy, and total thyroidectomy with resection of surrounding invaded tissues.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: inability to undergo surgery because of significant dysfunction of the heart, lungs, liver, or kidneys on preoperative examination or inability to tolerate surgery; abnormal coagulation function, use of aspirin, or other anticoagulants without timely discontinuation; refusal of surgery for personal reasons after admission; incomplete clinical data; paraffin sections confirming non-papillary carcinoma; and slides diagnosed as papillary carcinoma that could not be subtyped because of poor slide quality or an excessively small lesion.

Diagnostic methods and criteria for papillary carcinoma subtypes

The diagnosis was confirmed by re-evaluating previous paraffin-embedded tissue diagnoses based on thyroid cancer immunohistochemical markers, molecular testing results, and the thyroid tumor classification criteria outlined in the 5th edition [2022] of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Endocrine Tumors (4). To ensure quality control in pathological diagnosis, at least two board-certified endocrine pathologists with more than 10 years of experience independently reviewed cases in a blinded manner. Cases with diagnostic discrepancies were discussed by the multidisciplinary pathology department until consensus was reached or were sent to an external institution for independent confirmation and review. Thyroid tumor subtyping followed the latest definitions and diagnostic criteria for each subtype in the 5th edition [2022] of the WHO Classification of Endocrine Tumors (4), with additional reference to the 2025 American Thyroid Association guidelines for the management of differentiated thyroid cancer in adults (9), the AJCC guidelines (8th edition) (3), and the 2023 Chinese National Guidelines for Thyroid Disease Diagnosis and Treatment.

HRG and LRG classification

According to the AJCC Thyroid Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines (8th edition) and WHO Classification Criteria for Endocrine Tumors (5th edition, 2022) (3,4), the diffuse sclerosing, tall cell, columnar cell, hobnail, and solid subtypes are associated with relatively high clinical invasion and metastasis capabilities, including a relatively high incidence of local invasion and lymph node metastasis. In our study, these subtypes were classified as the HRG. In contrast, the infiltrative follicular, encapsulated, and oncocytic subtypes exhibit relatively low clinical invasion and metastasis and were therefore classified as the LRG. The other three subtypes, the Warthin-like, spindle cell, and clear cell subtypes, were excluded due to limited and incomplete clinical data and undefined biological behavior.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Origin 8.0 software (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-tests. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Potential multivariable confounders were addressed using propensity score matching and stratified analysis. All analyses were performed using two-sided tests. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Figure 2 presents the incidence of the PTC/WF/FLS subtype and overall PTC cases (including 10 PTC cases with undetermined subtype classification) from 2017 to 2021. A gradually ascending trend was observed for PTC/WF/FLS subtype cases, mirroring the increase in overall PTC cases. We analyzed the clinical data of 725 patients who met the research criteria and underwent surgical treatment for thyroid cancer at our hospital. Specifically, demographic characteristics and related clinical parameters were assessed (Table 1). Statistical analysis showed no significant differences among the WF/FLS, HRG, and LRG groups in terms of gender distribution, age composition, primary tumor site, and mutation rate (P=0.12, P=0.43, P=0.07, and P=0.59, respectively), indicating baseline consistency. However, differences were observed in the number of lesions, tumor size distribution, and family history of thyroid cancer (P=0.04, P=0.01, and P=0.04, respectively) (Table 1). The differences in capsular invasion rate and central compartment lymph node metastasis rate between the three groups (P=0.01 and P=0.04, respectively) may be influenced not only by tumor size but also potentially by other confounding factors such as the number of lesions and family history of thyroid cancer. Therefore, subsequent analyses on the association between tumor size and lymph node metastasis or local invasion should be conducted using propensity score-matched stratified analysis.

Sex and age at onset across risk groups

Although the male-to-female ratio in the PTC/WF/FLS group (25/94) was lower than that in the HRG (76/209) and LRG (85/236), no statistically significant differences were observed across groups (P=0.12). Furthermore, no statistically significant differences in the male-to-female ratio were identified when comparing any two groups among the three groups (P=0.23, P=0.24, and P=0.13, respectively), with all groups showing a ratio similar to the overall ratio (185/539). Although the average age at onset in the PTC/WF/FLS group (46.5 years) was marginally lower than that in the HRG and LRG (48.4 and 48.7 years, respectively), no statistical differences in the average age at onset were identified in pairwise comparisons among the groups (P=0.43). Additionally, no statistical differences were identified between the average age at onset in the PTC/WF/FLS group (46.5 years) and overall age at onset (48.2 years).

Anatomical site incidence across groups

The bilateral incidence rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group was 9.24%. Although lower than that of the HRG (15.44%) and LRG (11.53%; Table 2), pairwise comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences (P=0.07). The bilateral incidence rate was highest in the high-risk group; however, no significant disparity was observed across groups (P=0.053). We also reanalyzed unilateral incidences (Table 2). Pairwise comparisons revealed no notable differences in the left side, right side, and isthmus incidence rates (P=0.18, P=0.12, and P=0.88, respectively).

Lymph node metastasis potential across groups

Table 4 shows that most cases in all three groups were stage T1 (98.22%, 95.09%, and 94.39%, respectively). Because the T2 and T3 sample sizes were small and became even smaller after exclusion of confounding factors, such as multifocality, they were insufficient for statistical analysis. Therefore, we performed a stratified analysis of T1-stage patients in the WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG. To minimize the influence of potential confounders on baseline characteristics among strata, and based on the baseline statistics in Table 1, we excluded multifocal cases and cases with a family history. We retained only unifocal or bilateral unifocal cases with relatively consistent baseline characteristics, including age distribution, sex ratio, and genetic mutations, as shown in Table 1. Unifocal T1 cases were further divided into T1a and T1b subgroups, and the WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG were compared in terms of the number and positivity rate of lymph node metastases in the VI and II–V regions and capsular invasion within these subgroups (Table 5).

In T1-stage disease, the VI-region lymph node metastasis rates in the WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG were 20.93%, 30.41%, and 22.13%, respectively. No significant differences were observed between the WF/FLS group and the HRG or LRG (P=0.10 and P=0.97, respectively; Table 5). Subgroup analysis showed no significant differences between the WF/FLS group and the HRG or LRG at T1a (P=0.13 and P=0.99, respectively) or T1b (P=0.63 and P=0.86, respectively). In T1-stage disease, the II–V-region lymph node metastasis rates in the WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG were 4.65%, 4.61%, and 2.37%, respectively. No significant differences were found between the WF/FLS group and the HRG or LRG (P=0.99 and P=0.28), and no significant differences were observed at T1a or T1b (Table 5). In T1-stage disease, the capsular invasion rates in the WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG were 16.28%, 5.53%, and 7.51%, respectively. The WF/FLS group differed significantly from both the HRG and LRG (P=0.003 and P<0.001, respectively; Table 5). Subgroup analysis showed a significant difference in capsular invasion between the WF/FLS group and HRG at T1a (P=0.01), but not between the WF/FLS group and LRG (P=0.10). At T1b, no significant difference was observed between the WF/FLS group and HRG (P=0.11), whereas a significant difference was found between the WF/FLS group and LRG (P=0.04; Table 5).

Thyroid capsule, nerve, and vascular invasion rates across groups

The thyroid capsule invasion rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group (21/129, 16.28%) was significantly higher than that in the HRG (23/327, 7.03%) and LRG (32/346, 9.25%; P=0.003 and P=0.03, respectively; Table 6), indicating that the PTC/WF/FLS subtype was more susceptible to local direct invasion. The perilesional microvascular invasion rates across the groups were relatively low (PTC/WF/FLS: 1.55%; high-risk: 1.83%; low-risk: 1.16%, respectively), with no statistically significant differences (P=0.84). Furthermore, the perilesional nerve invasion rates among the groups were also relatively low (PTC/WF/FLS: 2.33%; high-risk: 0.16%; low-risk: 0.58%), with no statistically significant differences (P=0.28, P=0.66, and P=0.25, respectively).

Furthermore, the epithelial dysplasia incidence in the PTC/WF/FLS group was 24.81%, significantly higher than that in the HRG (1.53%) and LRG (4.05%) (P=0.03 and P=0.04, respectively).

Family disease history across groups

Statistical analysis of familial incidence across groups revealed a genetic onset rate of 9.24% (11/119), 4.91% (14/285), and 4.91% (14/285) in the PTC/WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG, respectively. Notably, no statistically significant difference in the incidence rates were observed between the PTC/WF/FLS group and HRG (P=0.10); however, a significant difference was observed between the PTC/WF/FLS group and LRG (P=0.04). Furthermore, no statistically significant differences were observed between the HRG and LRG (P=0.36), indicating stronger associations with genetic factors in the PTC/WF/FLS subtype compared with the HRG and LRG.

Gene mutations in the PTC/WF/FLS subtype compared with HRG and LRG

We also conducted a gene mutation investigation using random sampling and sample testing for each subtype. The gene mutation rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group was 89.66% (26/29), with all 26 mutations identified as single-gene BRAF-V600E variants. The gene mutation rate in the HRG was 89.13% (41/46), comprising 37 single-gene BRAF-V600E mutations, 2 BRAF-V600E and TERTC228T combined mutations, 1 NRASQ61R point mutation, and 1 CCDC6-RET rearrangement. The mutation rate in the LRG was 82.98% (39/47), comprising 37 single BRAF-V600E locus mutations, 1 BRAF-V600E and TERTC228T combined mutation, and 1 KRASQ61R point mutation. All groups exhibited relatively high gene mutation rates, with no statistically significant differences (P=0.59).

Discussion

PTC constitutes the majority of differentiated thyroid carcinomas and is generally associated with favorable prognosis outcomes (3). Surgical resection is the primary treatment approach, primarily involving lobectomy or total thyroidectomy and regional lymph node resection (9,10). While these treatment modalities are largely standardized, the decision to perform surgical resection and the surgical extent is determined based on the surgeon’s experience and the patient’s condition (10-13). Certain highly invasive cytopathological subtypes present with multiple lesions, increased lymph node metastases, single gene or combined mutations, and bilateral or unilateral multifocal carcinomas, which may result in poor postoperative prognosis and high recurrence risk. Additionally, some cytopathological subtypes with unfavorable prognoses, specifically those with single or combined gene mutations, are resistant to iodine therapy and are associated with even worse prognoses (14,15). Clinical evidence also indicates that some PTCs display familial genetic predispositions, often accompanied by higher rates of lymph node metastasis and disease recurrence (16).

PTC/WF/FLS is a rare subtype of PTC (2), and there remains no consensus regarding its genetic mutation profile. Literature reports indicate that the BRAF-V600E mutation is the most common molecular event (2), occurring in 22–100% of cases (8). The BRAF-V600E mutation is almost exclusively present in the epithelial component of the tumor, whereas CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutations are found in the stromal component (5), with a mutation rate ranging from 75% to 86% (6). NRAS mutations have also been reported in some cases (8). In our study, we observed a high frequency of BRAF-V600E mutation—up to 86.88%—in this subtype, consistent with previous literature findings (8). Therefore, this subtype exhibits biological characteristics more akin to BRAF-type tumors, with the tumor parenchyma displaying typical features such as ground-glass nuclei and papillary architecture (8). The molecular characteristics of PTC/WF/FLS reflect a unique “bipartite tumor” model: the malignant epithelial component and the benign stromal component are driven by two distinct and separate signaling pathways (6-8). The epithelial component is primarily characterized by BRAF-V600E mutations, which predominantly activate the MAPK signaling pathway and drive epithelial neoplastic transformation (7,8). In contrast, the stromal component is mainly associated with CTNNB1 gene mutations, particularly hotspot mutations at codons T41A and S45P in exon 3, involving the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which promotes stromal overgrowth and is linked to a higher tendency for local tumor recurrence (6,7). Our study further reveals that this subtype frequently demonstrates capsular invasion, which may be related to the signaling pathways driven by the tumor stroma.

In our study, the incidence of the PTC/WF/FLS subtype was 15.69%—higher than that previously reported (2). Women were predominantly affected by this subtype, accounting for 78.99% of cases, with no statistical differences compared with the HRG (73.33%) and LRG (73.52%). The average age at onset was approximately 46.5 years, with no statistical differences compared with the HRG (48.4 years) and LRG (48.7 years). Previous studies have reported an onset age range of 19–82 years for the PTC/WF/FLS subtype, with an average of 44.8 years (2), aligning with that reported in our study (46.5 years). The bilateral incidence rate of the PTC/WF/FLS group was 9.24%, lower than that in the HRG (15.44%) and LRG (11.53%); however, pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences. Furthermore, no significant differences in the left, right, or isthmus involvement were observed across the groups when comparing unilateral incidences. These findings indicate that the PTC/WF/FLS subtype shares similar onset characteristics with the HRG and LRG in terms of sex, age, and anatomical location. Overall, women were the predominant group affected, average onset ages were comparable, and unilateral and bilateral incidence rates were similarly distributed.

Multifocality is a typical feature of papillary carcinoma and is considered a major risk factor for postoperative recurrence (17). In our study, nearly all subtypes exhibited some degree of multifocality. The maximum number of macroscopic lesions/foci (>2 mm) observed was four, whereas microscopic lesions (≤2 mm) identified under a microscope were described as intrathyroid dissemination. No significant differences in the incidence of single and multiple foci were observed among the groups, although a significant difference was noted between the HRG and LRG. This suggests that the incidence of single and multiple foci in the PTC/WF/FLS group was between the HRG and LRG; however, the difference was not significant. Similarly, although we identified a difference in the incidence of intrathyroid dissemination between the groups, it was not significant. Overall, disease characteristics in the PTC/WF/FLS group were largely comparable to those of the HRG and LRG, with similar multifocality and intrathyroid dissemination incidences.

Previous studies have reported the metastasis rates of 24.1–64.1% in the central lymph nodes in papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (18), whereas it is 48% for the PTC/WF/FLS subtype. This relatively high rate among differentiated thyroid carcinoma subtypes suggests that PTC/WF/FLS may represent a more aggressive variant (2). In this study, although the PTC/WF/FLS group exhibited a positive metastasis rate of 24.81% compared with the HRG (34.25%) and LRG (26.01%), no statistically significant differences were identified. However, a statistically significant difference was observed between the HRG (34.25%) and LRG (26.01%). Further analyses showed that tumor stages reported in the literature were predominantly T3b and T4 in thyroid cancer samples (2), whereas most cases in our study ranged from T1a to T3a. These disparities in lymph node metastasis rates may be explained by differences in tumor size. In our study, the tumor size in the PTC/WF/FLS group was generally smaller than that in the LRG. Given the known influence of tumor size on metastasis, the PTC/WF/FLS subtype still exhibited a relatively high lymph node metastasis rate—between the HRG and LRG. Propensity-matched and stratified analyses indicated that the PTC/WF/FLS group had a lymph node metastasis rate in the VI region intermediate between the HRG and LRG, although there were no significant differences among the groups (P=0.10 and P=0.97, respectively).

Capsular invasion of the thyroid gland is used to assess disease malignancy (10,19), which may occur in some small-sized microcarcinomas. Such invasion may progress to locally advanced thyroid cancer, invading the surrounding adipose and skeletal muscle tissues (20), thereby increasing the surgical complexity. In our study, the thyroid capsule invasion rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group (16.28%) was significantly higher than that in the HRG (7.03%) and LRG (9.25%). At an early stage (T1), the PTC/WF/FLS group demonstrated the highest rate of capsule invasion compared to the HRG and LRG (16.28%, 5.53%, and 7.51%, respectively; P=0.003). This indicated that the PTC/WF/FLS subtype is more prone to local direct invasion and metastasis than the other subtypes, potentially leading to earlier lymph node metastasis and invasion of the surrounding tissues.

The tumor microenvironment is associated with tumor occurrence and development (21). Statistical analyses on tissue morphologies across groups revealed a 0.78% incidence of psammoma body/calcification in the PTC/WF/FLS group, significantly lower than that in the HRG (10.09%) and LRG (8.67%). Furthermore, the incidence of epithelial dysplasia in the PTC/WF/FLS group was 24.81%, higher than that in the HRG (1.53%) and LRG (4.05%). We hypothesized that the tissue cell microenvironment, characterized by low psammoma bodies/calcification and high epithelial dysplasia, was possibly more conducive to PTC/WF/FLS subtype occurrence and development; however, these intrinsic mechanisms require further investigation.

Patients with a history of hereditary familial disease are prone to lymph node metastasis and higher risks of recurrence (16). In our study, the genetic incidence rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG was 9.24%, 4.91%, and 3.43%, respectively. Although the incidence rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group was higher than that in the high-risk group, no significant difference was observed. However, a significant difference was observed between the PTC/WF/FLS group and LRG, suggesting a relatively strong genetic association in the PTC/WF/FLS subtype. However, further studies are warranted to confirm these observations.

Previous studies have reported a 25–82.3% incidence of single BRAF-V600E gene mutation in papillary carcinoma (22). In the present study, the gene mutation rate in the PTC/WF/FLS group, HRG, and LRG was 89.66%, 89.13%, and 82.98%, respectively. All groups exhibited a relatively high BRAF-V600E locus mutation rate, whereas mutation rates for other genes were extremely low, with no significant differences in rates across groups. These results indicate that the PTC/WF/FLS subtype, similar to other papillary carcinomas, exhibits a relatively high rate of BRAF-V600E mutations.

This study focused on comparing the clinical biological behaviors of the PTC/WF/FLS subtype based on cell subtypes, thereby characterizing its features. Nevertheless, these findings are not intended to prompt overtreatment of this subtype. Clinical decision-making must remain comprehensive, considering several factors, including the tumor size, capsular invasion, lymph node metastases or distant metastases as indicated by imaging, multifocality, cell subtype of the lesion (specifically, whether it is a mixed cell subtype), gene mutation, genetic background, and molecular phenotype.

The development, progression, and prognosis of thyroid cancer are influenced by multiple factors; therefore, cell subtype alone should not dictate treatment decisions. Whether patients with PTC/WF/FLS should undergo surgery or be considered for active surveillance requires comprehensive careful clinical judgment. Active surveillance may benefit selected patients with thyroid cancer (23,24). However, based on the findings of our study, for patients with PTC/WF/FLS—particularly those with a relatively high lymph node metastasis rate, with a significant tumor size, or suspected lymph node metastasis or capsular invasion—aggressive surgical intervention may be more advantageous than active surveillance.

This study was a single-center retrospective investigation, and the possibility of information bias between the study samples and the broader population cannot be ruled out. Larger, multi-center studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

PTC/WF/FLS is a rare subtype of papillary carcinoma with moderate aggressiveness, characterized by a high propensity for lymphatic metastasis even at early stages (T1), particularly due to its strong tendency for capsular invasion, which increases the risk of early local recurrence and postoperative relapse. Surgeons should pay close attention to this feature when making surgical decisions and managing postoperative follow-up.
