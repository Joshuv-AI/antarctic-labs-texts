Cost effectiveness of female HPV vaccines for routine childhood immunisation schedule in Türkiye
Authors: Çağrı Emin Şahin, Selma Karabey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the cost-effectiveness of introducing Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination into Türkiye’s National Immunization Program.MethodsThe analysis employed the WHO-endorsed PRIME model in Microsoft Excel. Two vaccination strategies were simulated for 12-year-old girls: Scenario 1 (bivalent or quadrivalent vaccine) and Scenario 2 (nonavalent vaccine). Each scenario was assessed from both the Social Security Institution (SGK) and societal perspectives. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated per disability-adjusted life year (DALY) averted, with sensitivity analyses conducted to test model robustness.ResultsScenario 1 yielded an ICER of USD 2,231 per DALY averted, while Scenario 2 produced an ICER of USD 2,342. The incremental ICER of Scenario 2 versus Scenario 1 was USD 2,592. According to WHO cost-effectiveness thresholds, both scenarios were classified as “very cost-effective.” Sensitivity analyses identified the discount rate as the most influential factor, followed by vaccine efficacy, HPV subtype distribution, and vaccine price. The annual program cost to SGK was estimated at USD 15.7 million for Scenario 1 and USD 23.6 million for Scenario 2. Vaccination remained highly cost-effective at prices up to USD 54 (Scenario 1) and USD 78 (Scenario 2), but not cost-effective above USD 167 and USD 242, respectively.ConclusionsHPV vaccination is highly cost-effective in Türkiye, with both strategies offering substantial health gains and favorable ICERs well below national GDP per capita. Adoption of a national HPV vaccination program would likely reduce cervical cancer morbidity and mortality while representing an efficient use of healthcare resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24878-4.

Introduction

Since Edward Jenner’s first vaccine in 1798, immunization has dramatically reduced the global burden of infectious diseases. Smallpox has been eradicated, polio cases have declined by 99%, and vaccines have brought many other diseases—including diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae type b, measles, mumps, rubella, rotavirus, and hepatitis B—under control [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Expanded Program on Immunization to ensure universal access to essential vaccines, progressively extending its scope to adolescents and adults, and emphasizing equity for underserved populations [2].

Over the past decade, HPV vaccination has gained increasing prominence. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infections, while often benign and self-limiting, can persist and lead to epithelial malignancies, most notably cervical cancer [3, 4].

More than 200 HPV types have been identified; over 40 are transmitted through sexual contact. Types 6 and 11 cause the majority of genital warts, while oncogenic types—including 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58—account for the majority of cervical and other anogenital cancers, as well as a growing proportion of oropharyngeal cancers [5].

Three vaccines are currently licensed worldwide: Cervarix® (bivalent), Gardasil® (quadrivalent), and Gardasil 9® (nonavalent). All protect against HPV 16 and 18, which cause about 70% of cervical cancers [6]. Gardasil additionally prevents types 6 and 11, which cause 90% of genital warts. Gardasil 9 extends coverage to five more oncogenic types, together responsible for an additional 10–20% of cervical cancers [7].

As of 2019, 120 UN member states (53%) had integrated HPV vaccination into their national immunization schedules. Globally, about 60% of HPV vaccine use is quadrivalent, 30% nonavalent, and 10% bivalent. Although HPV vaccines represent only ~ 2% of the global vaccine market by volume, they account for 15% of its value. Uptake remains uneven: while high-income countries have achieved broad coverage, many low- and middle-income countries lag behind. In 2018, WHO called for global action to eliminate cervical cancer, projecting annual demand for HPV vaccines could reach 120 million doses by 2025. Concerns over supply shortages prompted the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) to recommend prioritizing younger cohorts and deferring vaccination of older age groups until supply stabilizes [8]. Clinical evidence supports two-dose schedules for adolescents aged 9–14 years [9, 10].

Beyond the currently available products, additional bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines are in late-stage clinical development by manufacturers in China and India, all formulated with aluminum-based adjuvants and targeting girls aged 9–14 [11].

Pricing remains an important determinant of vaccine adoption. In the European Union, the average HPV vaccine price declined from €101.8 in 2007 to €28.4 in 2017, with nonavalent formulations priced higher than bivalent ones. Factors influencing price include procurement volume, contract length, GDP per capita, and tender competition [12]. By 2018, international procurement agencies such as UNICEF and GAVI were able to secure the lowest prices (~ USD 4.50 per dose), while middle-income countries typically paid USD 13 or more [13].

In Türkiye, cervical cancer continues to pose a significant public health challenge. According to the Ministry of Health Cancer Registry, the age-standardized incidence is approximately 5.2 per 100,000 women, with a mortality rate of 2.0 per 100,000. This corresponds to an estimated 1,500–1,700 new cases and about 550 deaths annually [14]. Despite the global adoption of HPV vaccination since 2006, Türkiye does not currently include HPV vaccination in its publicly funded national immunization program. Previous pilot projects and policy discussions have been limited in scope and have not resulted in sustained national implementation [15]. This leaves policymakers with a pressing question: should HPV vaccination be introduced into Türkiye’s national program, and if so, under what conditions would it be cost-effective and equitable?

The objective of this study is to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of HPV vaccination in Türkiye’s National Immunization Program. By using national data within the WHO-endorsed PRIME model, we aim to generate evidence to inform health policy decisions and support the implementation of an effective and equitable HPV vaccination strategy.

Methods

Model

We conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), a widely applied economic evaluation method that compares the costs and health outcomes of alternative health interventions. Although CEA does not directly address affordability or long-term fiscal sustainability, it provides a structured framework for quantifying the value of health gains, making it particularly suitable for preventive strategies such as HPV vaccination. By comparing both costs and health outcomes, CEA helps decision-makers determine which intervention yields the greatest health benefit for the resources invested.

To standardize such evaluations, WHO developed the PRIME model (Papillomavirus Rapid Interface for Modeling and Economics) [16]. The PRIME model estimates the effectiveness of HPV vaccination at the population level. It is a static, Excel-based tool consisting of four modules: country selection, demographic inputs, output generation, and customization. While simple in design, it enables the use of country-specific data to support evidence-based policy decisions at the national level.

Scenarios

Following WHO recommendations, HPV vaccination is modeled as two doses administered 6–12 months apart in girls aged 9–14 years, prior to sexual debut [17]. Since different vaccines vary in their effectiveness against cervical cancer, we evaluated two primary vaccination strategies:

Scenario 1: Bivalent or quadrivalent HPV vaccination of 12-year-old girls, two doses 6–12 months apart, delivered mainly through school-based programs with supplemental delivery in health facilities. Both scenarios were compared to a null baseline (no vaccination). Scenario 2 was also compared incrementally against Scenario 1. For each scenario, the model generated outputs including: vaccination costs, treatment costs averted, cervical cancer cases prevented, deaths prevented, years of life saved, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) averted. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated per case prevented, per death prevented, per life-year saved, and per DALY averted.

Sensitivity analysis

One-way sensitivity analysis was performed from the government perspective. Model inputs were varied by ± 10% of their base values, with additional variation in the discount rate (1–5%) consistent with WHO guidance for health economic evaluations [18, 19]. Tornado diagrams were generated to illustrate parameter uncertainty and to identify which variables most strongly influenced ICER estimates. In addition to identifying the most influential inputs, the sensitivity analysis tested the plausibility and robustness of the model assumptions.

Inputs

Common inputs accross scenarios

The target cohort comprised 642,968 girls aged 12 years in 2018, based on data from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TUIK). After excluding girls with immunodeficiency or hypersensitivity to vaccine components, the eligible vaccination cohort was estimated at 610,820 [20–22].

Vaccine coverage was modeled assuming a health facility–based delivery strategy. In Türkiye, all adolescents are scheduled to receive a Td booster dose at the age of 12 years in family health centers, with a reported coverage of 93%. Furthermore, routine childhood immunisation coverage for the first five years of life exceeds 95% nationally. Drawing on these benchmarks and informed by the 2018 Türkiye Health Statistics Report, we applied the average coverage rates observed for adolescent-targeted vaccines delivered through health facilities. Based on this assumption, the overall HPV vaccine coverage was estimated at 90%, in line with other vaccines included in the national immunisation schedule.

DALY weights were obtained from international sources [23]: 0.08 for cervical cancer diagnosis, 0.11 for non-terminal sequelae, and 0.78 for terminal cancer. A 3% discount rate was applied in line with WHO recommendations [24]. Türkiye’s 2018 mid-year GDP per capita was USD 9,693.

Whenever possible, country-specific demographic and epidemiological data were used rather than regional estimates. Cervical cancer incidence and mortality were derived from GLOBOCAN 2018, which integrates nine WHO-accredited provincial cancer registries in Türkiye. HPV subtype distribution was based on Usubütün et al. [25]. Costing data were drawn from TUIK, WHO databases, and national health expenditure reports. Inputs are summarized in Table 1.

Scenario-spesific inputs

In Scenario 1, vaccination with bivalent or quadrivalent HPV vaccines was modeled, which together prevent approximately 75% of cervical cancer cases [26]. CDC reports indicate 100% efficacy in preventing cervical cancer among fully vaccinated girls [27–30]. Vaccine prices were set at USD 8.5 (bivalent) and USD 9.6 (quadrivalent), based on PAHO procurement data [31, 32].

In Scenario 2, the nonavalent vaccine was modeled, preventing up to 90% of cervical cancer cases [33]. Phase III trials report efficacy exceeding 90%, with literature estimates ranging from 88 to 93% [34]. Vaccine cost was set at USD 16.81, reflecting CDC and WHO guidance [31, 32]. Cost-effectiveness threshold

In addition to the WHO-CHOICE rule (1–3× GDP per capita per DALY averted), we applied opportunity cost–based thresholds following Ochalek et al. (2018) [33]. For Türkiye, this range was 0.30–0.60× GDP per capita (USD 2,908–5,816). ICER values below this threshold were interpreted as cost-effective from an opportunity-cost perspective, in addition to the traditional GDP-based criteria.

The study adhered to the CHEERS 2022 reporting checklist (see Supplementary Table 1).

Results

The total cost of the vaccination program was estimated at USD 15.7 million for Scenario 1 and USD 23.6 million for Scenario 2. Treatment costs averted amounted to USD 5.3 million in Scenario 1 and USD 7.7 million in Scenario 2. After accounting for these savings and applying a 3% discount rate, the net program costs were USD 14.3 million and USD 21.7 million, respectively. Scenario 1 was projected to avert 6,429 DALYs, while Scenario 2 averted 9,258 DALYs.

Compared with no intervention, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) per DALY averted was USD 2,231 for Scenario 1 and USD 2,342 for Scenario 2. Overall, both interventions were classified as very cost-effective according to WHO thresholds, given Türkiye’s 2018 GDP per capita of USD 9,693. While Scenario 2 required a higher investment, it produced greater health benefits, preventing more cervical cancer cases and deaths, saving more life-years, and averting more DALYs.

Direct comparison of Scenario 2 versus Scenario 1 indicated an incremental cost of USD 7.3 million and an incremental health gain of 2,829 DALYs averted, yielding an ICER of USD 2,592 per DALY averted.

Threshold analyses showed that Scenario 1 remained highly cost-effective at vaccine prices up to USD 54 but became not cost-effective above USD 167. For Scenario 2, vaccination was highly cost-effective up to USD 78, but not cost-effective above USD 242. The annual budgetary impact to SGK was estimated at USD 15.7 million for Scenario 1 and USD 23.6 million for Scenario 2.

Discussion

For both scenarios, HPV vaccination services should be delivered through family medicine units and supervised by family physicians, as is the case for other vaccines in the routine childhood immunization schedule. Although national childhood vaccination rates are high (approximately 98%), some children may still remain unvaccinated due to barriers in access or vaccine hesitancy among certain families [22].

In Türkiye, the introduction of a national HPV vaccination program faces several potential implementation challenges. First, logistical issues—such as maintaining a continuous vaccine supply, ensuring the cold chain, and integrating procurement into existing tender systems—may affect program sustainability. Second, the delivery strategy, whether school-based, health facility–based, or a hybrid model, will influence achievable coverage rates. Evidence from other middle-income countries suggests that school-based programs can achieve higher uptake, though they require strong coordination between the health and education sectors. Third, vaccine hesitancy remains a barrier, often driven by misinformation and sociocultural sensitivities related to sexually transmitted infections. Tailored communication strategies, public awareness campaigns, and engagement with community and religious leaders will be essential to build public trust. Fourth, adequate training of healthcare providers is critical to ensure consistent counseling and vaccine delivery. Leveraging Türkiye’s well-established primary care and family medicine infrastructure, combined with outreach in underserved areas, may optimize both coverage and equity [14, 19, 21].

Equity considerations are especially important when assessing the public health impact of HPV vaccination. In Türkiye, disparities in access to preventive services are evident between urban and rural populations, as well as between higher- and lower-income groups. Women in rural areas tend to participate less in cervical cancer screening, reflecting structural barriers such as limited health infrastructure, transportation difficulties, and lower health literacy. Similarly, socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with higher prevalence of risk factors but reduced access to vaccination. Although the PRIME model does not provide subgroup-specific outputs, international evidence indicates that targeted strategies—such as mobile vaccination units, integration with community-based health programs, and prioritization of high-incidence regions—can help reduce inequities in uptake. Ensuring equitable access will be crucial to maximizing the overall health benefits of a national HPV vaccination program [14, 19, 26].

In the Turkish context, the cost-effectiveness findings of this study are particularly relevant given the current burden of cervical cancer—approximately 2,500 new cases and over 1,200 deaths annually. Both vaccination scenarios produced ICER values well below national GDP per capita, thus meeting WHO’s definition of “very cost-effective.” From a fiscal standpoint, incorporating HPV vaccination into the national immunization program would require only a 5–8% increase in the current annual vaccine budget—a relatively modest investment compared with the long-term savings from prevented cancer treatment. These results are consistent with earlier Turkish modeling studies that also supported the inclusion of HPV vaccination, but our analysis incorporates more recent local data and evaluates multiple vaccine options. Together, the findings underscore that timely policy adoption could substantially reduce cervical cancer morbidity and mortality, especially if implemented alongside existing screening programs.

Sensitivity analysis

Our one-way sensitivity analysis demonstrated that thediscount rate is the most influential factor affecting the ICER, with variation between 1 and 5% causing the greatest swings. Vaccine efficacy and the proportion of cervical cancer attributable to vaccine-covered HPV subtypes followed as the second most impactful variables. Vaccine price also meaningfully influences ICER, reflecting procurement and market dynamics. The tornado diagrams for both scenarios (Figs. 1 and 2) clearly illustrate these patterns, affirming the robustness of our findings. Budget effect and threshold analysis

From a budgetary standpoint, Scenario 2 (nonavalent vaccine) imposes an additional annual cost of USD 7.9 million compared with Scenario 1 (bivalent/quadrivalent vaccine). Thus, projected annual costs to SGK are USD 16.9 million for Scenario 1 and USD 25.4 million for Scenario 2. These figures represent a modest increase in the national immunization budget, especially when offset by long-term savings in cancer treatment and wider societal benefits.

Moreover, projected declines in unit costs—driven by the shrinking birth cohort and advances in vaccine technology—may further improve cost-effectiveness. Threshold analyses confirm that both strategies remain very cost-effective, with ICERs well below GDP per capita per DALY averted [33]. This economic justification supports the timely inclusion of HPV vaccination in Turkey’s national schedule, particularly given competing health priorities and budget constraints.

Other Turkish literature comparison

A recent Turkish literature review by Özdemir et al. [15] reported that HPV vaccination in Türkiye is highly cost-effective, particularly when implemented as part of a broader strategy that includes effective cervical cancer screening. The authors emphasized that while vaccination alone may not achieve optimal cost-effectiveness in the absence of expanded screening coverage, combining vaccination with screening would substantially improve outcomes and remain cost-effective [15].

Another multi-country modeling study published in The Lancet in 2014 [16], which analyzed data from 179 countries under bivalent or quadrivalent vaccination scenarios, also concluded that HPV vaccination in Türkiye would be highly cost-effective. This analysis highlighted that treatment costs significantly exceeded vaccination program costs. Although data quality for parameters such as HPV-type distribution was considered satisfactory, the study noted that cervical cancer incidence and mortality estimates for Türkiye were suboptimal.

Comparison with global literature

A 2019 Chinese study using the PRIME model evaluated the cost-effectiveness of the nonavalent HPV vaccine and reported that two doses were cost-effective at a threshold between USD 450 and 550. In our analysis, the cost-effectiveness threshold for two doses of the nonavalent vaccine in Scenario-2 was estimated at USD 484, which aligns with these findings. The ICER of the nonavalent vaccine in our model was USD 2,342, compared to USD 23,012 reported in the Chinese study. When compared with other vaccine types, the Chinese study found that the ICER of the nonavalent vaccine was USD 35,000 versus the quadrivalent vaccine and USD 50,455 versus the bivalent vaccine, indicating that the nonavalent vaccine was not cost-effective relative to the alternatives [35].

In Vietnam (2017) and Bangladesh (2020), the PRIME model was also applied to evaluate bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines. The Vietnamese study assessed three-dose regimens across five scenarios, including stage-specific cancer treatment costs and average treatment costs. In all scenarios, the bivalent vaccine yielded more favorable ICERs than the quadrivalent vaccine [38]. In Bangladesh, two-dose regimens were compared across three implementation strategies (school-based, facility-based, and community-based) [39]. Both studies adopted a 3% discount rate, with vaccine efficacy assumed as 100% in Vietnam and 95% in Bangladesh. ICER estimates ranged between USD 780 and 1,200 in Vietnam (highly cost-effective) and between USD 356 and 3,250 in Bangladesh, where strategies exceeding USD 854 were not considered cost-effective. At GAVI-subsidized prices, the bivalent vaccine was found to be cost-effective, while other strategies were not [35–39].

These comparative results reinforce the robustness of our findings, as our ICER values fall within or below the ranges reported in other LMIC settings. The analysis was revised in accordance with the CHEERS 2022 reporting checklist to ensure transparent and comprehensive presentation of all economic evaluation elements.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, genital warts and precancerous lesions were not included in the analysis, despite their substantial clinical and economic burden. Quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines provide protection against HPV types 6 and 11, which cause approximately 90% of genital warts and are associated with significant treatment costs and psychosocial consequences. The exclusion of these outcomes may have underestimated the cost-effectiveness of the quadrivalent and nonavalent vaccines. While the PRIME model does not directly incorporate genital warts, the absence of reliable national epidemiological data from Türkiye also limited the feasibility of robust modelling. Future research incorporating these outcomes would likely yield even more favorable cost-effectiveness ratios [35, 40].

Second, although the nonavalent vaccine represents the most recent advancement in HPV prevention, the relative scarcity of long-term real-world data compared with bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines may represent a disadvantage when evaluating its implementation strategy.

Third, the analysis relied on a static rather than a dynamic transmission model. As a result, herd immunity and indirect effects could not be fully captured, potentially leading to conservative estimates of vaccine benefits. Additionally, outcomes in individuals with pre-existing HPV infection were not modeled.

Finally, although WHO-approved PRIME methodology allows for comparability across countries, it inevitably simplifies complex epidemiological realities. Nevertheless, the use of current, country-specific data from Türkiye has strengthened the validity of our findings and enhanced their policy relevance [41].

Conclusion

From the perspective of the Turkish Social Security Institution (SGK), the vaccination program was estimated to cost USD 15,667,533 in Scenario 1 and USD 23,583,760 in Scenario 2. Scenario 1 was associated with 6,429 DALYs averted and an ICER of USD 2,231, whereas Scenario 2 averted 9,258 DALYs with an ICER of USD 2,342. When assessed from a societal perspective, the ICERs were USD 2,175 for Scenario 1 and USD 2,285 for Scenario 2. According to both thresholds commonly used in health economics, these values indicate that HPV vaccination is highly cost-effective.

This study represents the first national cost-effectiveness analysis of HPV vaccination in Türkiye based on local epidemiological and economic data. The findings provide robust evidence for policymakers, not only confirming the cost-effectiveness of HPV vaccination but also clarifying its budgetary impact. With Türkiye’s annual vaccine budget of approximately USD 300 million, the introduction of HPV vaccination would correspond to an additional burden of 5.2% (Scenario 1) to 7.9% (Scenario 2).

Beyond the model’s scope, inclusion of precancerous lesions and genital warts would likely further strengthen the cost-effectiveness profile, given their substantial clinical and economic burden. Taken together, these results strongly support the introduction of HPV vaccination into the national immunization schedule, in alignment with global public health priorities and the practices of more than 100 countries worldwide.
