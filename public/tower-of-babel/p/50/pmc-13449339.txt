Bacterial ghost cells as tools for the stimulation of PRR-mediated immune-transcription in zebrafish larvae
Authors: Aiswarya Pradeep, Amritha Nair, Bindiya Ellathuparambil Saidumohamed, Adheena Panangattu Baburajan, Sarita Ganapathy Bhat, Sreeja Narayanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The potential of bacterial ghosts as cost-effective prophylactic immunostimulants with immersion exposure as a simple, non-invasive and relevant delivery method of administration for aquaculture applications.•Investigates Gram-positive bacterial ghosts as alternatives to conventional immune stimulants.•GCs induce systemic immune responses in zebrafish larvae without major developmental defects or lethality.•Provides mechanistic insights into GCs-elicited PRR-mediated innate immune activation and ROS-associated responses.Keywords: Bacterial ghost, Immunostimulant, Zebrafish larvae, Gene expression, Innate immunity

Introduction

The aquaculture industry is rapidly expanding to meet the increasing global demand for protein driven by population growth. Maintaining aquatic animal health is essential for ensuring high productivity, as infectious diseases remain a major challenge causing significant economic losses and reduced product quality [1]. To prevent disease outbreaks, antibiotics have been extensively used as prophylactic agents; however, their overuse in aquatic systems has led to environmental accumulation and raised serious concerns regarding food safety, antimicrobial resistance, and consumer health. Although vaccines are considered one of the most effective preventive strategies against several aquatic diseases, vaccination alone is insufficient to control all infections [2].

In this context, immunostimulants have gained attention as alternative prophylactic agents due to their ability to enhance immune responses and improve disease resistance in aquatic animals. Various immunostimulants have been reported, including biologically derived compounds such as polysaccharides, bacterial components including lipopolysaccharides (LPS), peptidoglycans, yeast derivatives such as glucans and algal derivatives such as laminarins, as well as hormones, vitamins, and synthetic compounds [3]. In case of Gram-positive bacterial cells, they predominantly activate cells through tlr2, tlr21 or tlr6 receptors. This activation is followed by myd88-dependent pathway, releasing cytokines and bringing down the bacterial infection. Both myd88-dependent and independent pathways activate similar downstream activation cascade stimulating ifn and nfkb and eventually results in secretion of il1b, and tnfa [4]. While, bacterial LPS is suitable for inducing inflammatory response they serve as an endotoxin as well. Therefore, a better alternative for inducing inflammatory responses is important. Given that BGs retain native surface-associated PAMPs while lacking cytoplasmic content, they represent a biologically relevant alternative to purified endotoxins for immune stimulation. Unlike LPS or peptidoglycan alone, BGs provide a complex antigenic architecture capable of engaging multiple pattern recognition receptors, potentially eliciting a more integrated and sustained immune response [5,6]. BGs retain an intact cellular architecture that enables the gradual release of surface-associated components upon intake [7].

The BGs were widely studied as vaccine candidates and drug delivery systems in cell-based and animal models in previous studies [4,8,9]. BGs exposure has also been reported to increase the production of inflammatory mediators [10]. As a prophylactic immunostimulant, BGs can enhance non-specific immune responses through activation of innate immunity [11,12]. Therefore, BGs can be conveniently administered to larvae via immersion exposure in a cost-effective manner. Given their established pro-inflammatory activity in vitro [10], BGs were investigated for their ability to induce an immunomodulatory environment under in vivo conditions. However, studies addressing the basic immunomodulatory mechanisms underlying their protective effects, remain limited.

As an animal model, zebrafishes are widely used in biomedical research, and their conserved innate immune system makes them particularly relevant for investigating human health and disease [13]. Zebrafish embryos and larvae are highly sensitive to chemicals that can disrupt normal growth and development [14], making them important models for studying toxicity. Compared to primary or permanent fish cell cultures, the zebrafish embryo provides a higher level of biological complexity and more closely reflects organismal responses [15]. It is therefore a suitable model for studying the pro-inflammatory responses associated with drugs, chemicals, vaccines and so on [16,17]. Zebrafish embryo models also provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of action of these substances [18].

Therefore, in the present study, Gram-positive BGs derived from Staphylococcus aureus, referred to as GCs, were employed as the immunomodulatory agent, for which peptidoglycan is the major cell wall component. Additionally, the mechanisms of immune activation by Gram-positive pathogenic BGs, especially through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and nfkb-mediated signalling in zebrafish embryos, were not studied previously. Hence, the present study was designed to assess the modulation of inflammatory mediators and the underlying mechanisms of action in zebrafish embryos exposed to GCs.

Materials and methods

Bacterial ghost cell production

GCs were generated based on our previously validated procedures reported in [10]. Briefly, Staphylococcus aureus strain NCIM 2127 was obtained from the National Collection of Industrial Microorganisms (NCIM), India. The bacteria were cultured in nutrient broth at 37 °C in a shaking incubator set at 200 rpm ensuring adequate aeration, untill it reaches 1 OD (absorbance measured at 600 nm). This fresh bacterial culture was pelleted and resuspended in 0.25% Lugol’s iodine, vortexed, and incubated at ambient room temperature for 10 min. Following treatment, the sample was pelleted by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min and washed five times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under similar centrifuge conditions. The resulting GCs were stored under refrigerated storage conditions without losing sterility and utilised for further experiments.

Zebrafish maintenance and embryo collection

The male and female adult wild-type zebrafish were fed twice daily and maintained at 28 ± 0.5 °C and 14:10 h light–dark cycle in an aerated condition. These zebrafish once they are sexually mature were separated before breeding [19]. Acclimatized fish were randomly selected in a 2 male:1 female ratio and kept for breeding in a spawning tank without any disturbances. Spawning was induced by the onset of light under undisturbed conditions. Fertilized embryos were collected using a suction pipette or strainer, washed with 0.1% iodine solution and immediately washed thrice with pH adjusted sterile water, within 30–60 min post-spawning. Then embryos were maintained at 28 ± 0.5 °C and in sterile, pH-adjusted E3 medium with Methylene blue as antifungal agent. Healthy, normally developing, fertilized eggs were separated from unfertilized ones and used for further studies. Unfertilized or dead embryos if any, were removed from the medium periodically and the media was changed every 24 h. Healthy embryos were maintained in a 14:10 h light–dark cycle during normal development and experimental conditions alike. All the experiments were performed in triplicates and treatments were given together. Experiments were conducted in strict accordance with national and institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. For conducting the present study approval was obtained from institutional animal ethics committee (IAEC approval number: 363/GO/Re/S/01/CPCSEA/72).

Experimental design

Fertilized eggs 24 h post fertilisation, at their blastula stage were selected and transferred to glass containers containing 5 mL of fresh E3 embryo medium. A total of 60 fertilized eggs were randomly selected and subjected to treatment with GCs at concentrations 106, 107, and 108 units/mL for a period of 6 h, 12 h or 24 h by immersion. Embryos under similar conditions, devoid of any treatments, served as the untreated control group. Upon completion of the treatment, 20 embryos for each group were picked, washed with fresh E3 medium and allowed to grow until 5 dpf. The experiments were performed in triplicates under similar conditions.

Analysis of ROS generation

Experiments were performed according to previously reported protocol with some modifications [20]. Briefly, the embryos post-treatment were transferred to fresh well plates with sterile E3 buffer and allowed to hatch to larvae. The larvae on 5 dpf were randomly selected and incubated in DCFHDA at room temperature for 2 h. The larvae were washed four times with E3 buffer to stop further ROS induction. The larvae were placed in cavity slides individually and imaged under 4X magnification through a fluorescent microscope (Nikon eclipse Ti 2).

qRT-PCR analysis of pathway activation

Total RNA was extracted from 5 dpf zebrafish larvae using trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Germany). For this, 20 embryos per group were taken in triplicates and homogenised in trizol reagent. Following this the tubes were centrifuged to collect the supernatant and RNA was isolated. The isolated RNA was then quantitatively and qualitatively evaluated using nanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop ND-1000, Thermo Scientific, USA) and by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the SuperScript III reverse transcriptase cDNA synthesis kit with random hexamers (Takara, USA). Target genes were amplified from cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit using qRT-PCR system (Biorad, USA). The primers used in the study were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, USA. The primer sequences used in the study were taken from the following literature [[21], [22], [23]] are shown in Table 1. Each reaction was performed in 25 μl volume with an annealing temperature of 60 °C. The experiments were performed in triplicates.

Survival and hatchability assessments

The experiments were conducted similarly as mentioned in experimental design. Each fertilized egg was monitored for up to 5 dpf to assess cytotoxic effects, hatchability and survival. The embryos after the treatment period (6 h, 12 h, or 24 h) were carefully transferred to fresh plates with pH adjusted E3 buffer and observed microscopically for any phenotypic abnormalities using an inverted microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed at least in triplicates and therefore presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05. Comparison between the untreated and treated groups were represented as graphs were analysed by analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc Tukey's test and plotted using GraphPad Prism v8 software (CA).

Results and discussion

Immunomodulators are widely used in aquaculture to improve disease resistance and minimize the use of antibiotics. Therefore, potential immunomodulators inducing controlled and sustained immune activation are increasingly being explored as promising alternatives to conventional antibiotic treatments. Many of these agents have antimicrobial and immune-stimulating properties. Since BGs have been shown to elicit pro-inflammatory responses in vitro [10], we sought to examine their impact in vivo as an immunostimulant. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the modulation of innate immune and inflammatory responses and underlying mechanisms in zebrafish embryos exposed to GCs. Embryos were exposed to three concentrations of GCs across three time points and monitored for abnormalities and developmental defects up to 5 dpf (Fig. 1A and B). No visible morphological defects or treatment-related lethality were observed in any group irrespective of treatment doses or duration.

Stereomicroscopic images of A) larvae visualised on the same day after treatment and B) larvae visualised on the 5 dpf, under 10X or 20X magnification are shown. There were no visible teratogenic changes observed in any of the treatments.

Throughout the study embryos were monitored for their ability to hatch into a larva. The zebrafish embryonic developmental stages are completed in 72 h and all the embryos hatch to larval stages [24]. At 3 dpf, approximately 60% hatchability was recorded across most treatment groups, comparable to the control, except at the highest GC concentration. However, hatchability increased with exposure duration: embryos exposed for 12 h showed ∼80% hatchability at 3 dpf (Fig. S1 B), while those exposed for 24 h (Fig. S1 C) reached nearly 100% hatchability by 3 dpf. In contrast, the control and shorter exposure groups (6 h and 12 h) achieved 100% hatchability only by 4 dpf (Fig. S1A and B). Overall, GC concentration had no significant effect on hatchability, whereas longer exposure duration accelerated the hatching process. Additionally, all the larvae survived untill 5 dpf without any lethality, malformations, or teratogenic effects, indicating safety at all tested doses.

As GCs were evaluated as inducers of inflammatory responses, ROS generation was examined. ROS are primarily produced by mitochondria or through oxidative burst mediated by the NADPH oxidase complex. They play essential roles in host defense, metabolism, and signal transduction. While ROS are tightly regulated under physiological conditions, excessive accumulation can disrupt cellular functions, leading to oxidative stress, and ultimately contribute to neurological and cardiovascular diseases and cancers [25]. Rapid ROS generation upon recognition of bacterial and fungal infection is essential for host defense. As an antimicrobial defense mechanism which prevents excessive inflammation and injury-controlled generation of ROS plays is vital in innate immunity [26]. Fluorescence microscopy revealed systemic ROS generation in GC-treated embryos, using a DCFH-DA assay. Overall ROS intensity was comparable between control and GC-treated groups, with mild increase observed upon quantifying the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the 6 h treatment set (Fig. 2A and B). But in the case 12 h (Fig. 2C and D) and 24 h (Fig. 2E and F) treatment sets the ROS production is significantly upregulated for 106 and 107, while for 108, ROS is comparable to the control. In control embryos, fluorescence was predominantly localized around the yolk sac, likely reflecting high metabolic activity. In contrast, GC-treated embryos exhibited a more dispersed ROS distribution, with green fluorescence extending from head to tail and appearing as distinct punctate signals throughout the body, particularly around the yolk and tail regions. This altered spatial pattern suggests systemic ROS generation, possibly due to widespread uptake of GCs and their interaction with host immune pathways. Interestingly the lower GC concentration elicited a similar or faster response than the highest concentration tested. Similar non-linear responses as a function of the dose have been reported for several immunostimulatory agents where excessive stimulation may induce regulatory feedback mechanisms or reduce effective particle-cell interactions [27]. Aggregation at higher concentrations may also limit accessibility of surface-associated PAMPs and slow down cellular recognition in particulate systems such as BGs, resulting in a slower response despite the higher particle load.

Additionally, ROS function as secondary messengers in immune signalling pathways [28]. Therefore, we investigated the role of ROS in the production of pro-inflammatory mediators in zebrafish following GC-induced inflammation. The innate immune system recognises the PAMPS through their pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to generate a pro-inflammatory response against an invading pathogen. The microbes in the extracellular environment are recognised by the cell surface PRRs, while cytosolic PRRs recognise microbes or their components internalised by the cell. PAMP recognition leads to inflammatory responses and release of cytokines and antimicrobial peptides through activated intracellular signalling [29]. As GCs derived from Gram-positive bacteria are rich in peptidoglycan, the expression of tlr2 was evaluated as a key PRR involved in their recognition (Fig. 3A). The expression pattern was dose-dependent; at the highest dose (10⁸), tlr2 expression increased rapidly within 6 h, remained elevated at 12 h, and then declined over time. At 10⁷, expression increased more gradually, peaked at 12 h, and subsequently decreased. In contrast, the lowest dose (10⁶) showed a slower but steady increase, reaching maximum expression at 24 h. Notably, at 12 h, all doses exhibited comparable tlr2 expression levels.

qRT-PCR analysis of gene expression of (A) tlr2, (B) nod2, (C) nfkb (D) tnfa and (E) il1b. The data was analyzed by ordinary two-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.

A corresponding expression pattern was observed for nod2, a cytosolic PRR with broad range of pathogen detection. They can recognise muramyl dipeptide, a common bacterial cell wall component. The PAMP recognition by nod2 receptors can also activate nfkb, which can synergistically act along with tlr to induce cytokine expression [29]. At 10⁶, nod2 expression increased gradually and peaked at 24 h, whereas at higher doses, peak expression occurred at 12 h, similar to tlr2 but at higher levels (Fig. 3B). The elevated nod2 response suggests that GCs interacted with the receptors in 12 h, leading to peak expression at this time point through recognition by both cell surface and cytoplasmic receptors.

Since BGs creates a pro-inflammatory response in vitro, and as ROS is thought to be a redox based activator of inflammatory gene expression their involvement in the downstream activation of the nfkb signalling pathway was further investigated. Results suggest that GC-stimulated zebrafish embryos displayed increased mRNA expression of nfkb than the control group with more evident expression for 108 units/mL GC treated for 12 h (Fig. 3C). Further, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tnfa and il1b were evaluated. The expression of tnfa increased at 24 h in all treatments, indicating dependence on nfkb activation (Fig. 3D). In contrast, il1b expression decreased after 12 h across all doses, with the highest levels observed at 107. This suggests alternative activation pathways, possibly via ROS-mediated neutrophil activation [30]. The cytokine il1b is also important for B cell activation and antibody responses, indicating its potential role in enhancing vaccine efficacy [31]. Therefore, BGs have the potential to elicit effective immune activation while reducing the risk of excessive inflammation or septic shock typically associated with high-dose endotoxins used in aquaculture applications.

Conclusion and future perspective

In conclusion, GCs induce a balanced, non-toxic immune activation, supporting their potential as next-generation immunomodulatory agents. No significant developmental toxicity was observed at safe doses. GCs stimulate ROS production during the transition from embryo to larval stages and elicit pro-inflammatory responses in early-stage larvae. The punctate ROS signals observed following GC exposure may be indicative of localized oxidative activity, potentially associated with cellular uptake or localised responses. Nonetheless, this interpretation should be considered preliminary and requires further validation. Despite this immune activation, no morphological abnormalities were detected. These findings indicate that GCs can trigger immune responses even at early developmental stages, highlighting their potential as promising candidates for aquatic vaccines and adjuvants, warranting further investigation.
