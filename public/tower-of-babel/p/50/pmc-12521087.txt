Elucidating the causal triad of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and lipid traits: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study with genetic mediation analysis
Authors: Weijie Wu, Aiming Zeng, Yunzhe Yu, Liqun Liao, Yuheng Jiao, Jiaqi Hu, Jiansheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have indicated the association between cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer and lipid, while the causality remain unclear. We aim to elucidate the causality among them and potential mediating factors.MethodsWe extracted datasets on CVD, cancer and lipid from the Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database. Univariate and multivariate Mendelian Randomization (MR) analyses were employed to assess the causal relationships among them. Potential interactions were explored through mediation analysis. Sensitivity analysis was performed to ensure the robustness.ResultsIn the univariate MR analysis, all CVDs demonstrated a negative causal relationship with at least one type of cancer (all P < 0.05). The reverse MR analysis indicated that other cancers exhibited a negative causal relationship with at least one CVD, except for lung, prostate or thyroid cancer (all P < 0.05). Regarding the effect of lipids on CVDs, high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) were found to be negatively correlated with CVDs (all P < 0.05), while other lipids showed a positive relationship (all P < 0.05). Notably, most lipids are negatively causally associated with cancers (all P < 0.05). Mediation analysis confirmed that CVD mediates the causal relationship between lipids and cancers.ConclusionThis study explored the pairwise causal relationships among lipid, CVD, and cancer from a genetic perspective. However, the identified inverse causal associations should be interpreted as effects at the genetic level, rather than as evidence supporting CVD as a direct preventive strategy against cancer in clinical practice. Nonetheless, these findings inspire further investigation into novel biological mechanisms linking CVD and cancer, with the potential to identify new anticancer strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03736-0.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death globally [1]. Despite a notable decline in CVD mortality rates from 358.4/100,000 in 1990 to 233.2/100,000 in 2022 attributed to medical advancements and economic progress, the expanding population affected by CVD and the aging demographic have resulted in an annual increase in cardiovascular-related fatalities, posing a substantial public health challenge [2]. Cancer, which shares common risk factors such as obesity and smoking with CVD, represents another serious global public health concern [3, 4]. Cancer currently accounts for 61.2% of deaths among individuals under 70 years old in many countries [5]. According to epidemiological reports, a total of 19,292,789 incident cases of cancer and 9,958,133 cancer-related deaths were observed globally in 2020 [6], and these numbers are expected to continue increasing with aging populations. Despite CVD currently being the leading cause of death globally, Dagenais' study indicated that in developed countries, cancer has surpassed CVD as the primary cause of death, with mortality rates even doubling those of CVD [7]. Hence, the prevention and management of both cancer and CVDs constitute crucial global public health priorities.

It is noteworthy that despite CVD and cancer having long been regarded as two separate disease entities, both are major causes of global mortality and often coexist [8]. Evidence suggests that certain CVD risk factors may be associated with an increased risk of cancer incidence [9, 10]. Patients with CVD have a higher risk of developing cancer compared to those without CVD [11]. Furthermore, reports have indicated that CVD is a major cause of non-cancer-related deaths among cancer survivors [12]. Extensive statistical data showed that CVD accounts for one-third of deaths in cancer patients, potentially surpassing the mortality rate from cancer itself [13], which suggests a potential association between CVD and cancer, though the causal relationship between them remains unclear.

The occurrence, invasion, and metastasis of cancer are significantly influenced by lipid metabolism [14, 15]. Moreover, Lipid metabolism has also been confirmed to be closely associated with the occurrence and development of CVD [16]. For instance, in coronary artery disease, lipid function plays a crucial role throughout the entire process-from the initial endothelial damage that causes atherosclerosis, to coronary artery spasm or occlusive ischemia resulting in angina or myocardial infarction, and ultimately to myocardial remodeling and heart failure post-myocardial infarction [17]. Regrettably, most studies on lipid metabolism typically focus on individual diseases, with little attention given to how abnormal lipid metabolism may impact the interaction between different diseases. Research has demonstrated striking similarities in lipid metabolism between CVD and cancer, particularly in processes such as fatty acid oxidation and synthesis, and glutamine breakdown [18–20], further indicating a connection in the etiology and progression of both CVD and cancer, where lipids play a crucial role [21].

Currently, academic views on CVD and cancer are based on observational studies, which can reveal associations between these conditions. However, because of unknown confounding factors and the potential reverse causation, these conclusions do not provide direct evidence of causation [22]. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method that uses genetic variants of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to analyze causal relationships between exposure and outcomes. The genotypes studied in MR are randomly allocated from parents to offspring and remain unaffected with age or lifestyle, thus effectively compensating for some limitations of observational studies [23]. It is noteworthy that the genetic determinants of CVD and cancer have also been well confirmed in recent years [24–27].

As far as we know, there are currently no randomized controlled trials exploring the causality between CVD and cancer. Therefore, in this study, we employed a two-sample MR analysis method to preliminarily investigate the bidirectional causal relationships between CVDs and common cancers, as well as the causal relationships involving lipids with both conditions. We aim to further explore the potential interactions between CVD, cancer, and lipid through mediation analysis. We hope that this study will provide a clearer understanding of the pathogenic and interactions between CVD and cancer, and offer new insights and approaches for the prevention and treatment of cancer in the future.

Method

Study design

This study utilized data from United Kingdom Biobank (UK BioBank) (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/), FinnGen (https://www.finngen.fi/en), and GWAS datasets from reputable research institutions, universities, or government agencies, prioritizing datasets published in the last five years (Supplementary Table S1). All participants were derived from European populations. All data can be analyzed through the publicly available Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).

This study was divided into four main parts. First part, it investigated the bidirectional causal relationship between CVDs and cancers. Second part, it analyzed whether this causal relationship persists after excluding confounding factors affecting cancer occurrence. Third part, it separately explored the causal relationships of lipids with CVDs and cancers. Finally, it employed mediation analysis to verify the potential interactions between CVDs, cancers, and lipids. Cardiovascular disease data source

The SNP data for stable angina pectoris (SAP) (sample size: 17,894), unstable angina pectoris (UAP) (sample size: 9481), myocardial infarction (MI) (sample size: 20,917), heart failure (HF) (sample size: 47,309), and coronary atherosclerosis (CA) (sample size: 23,363) were sourced from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS database (Supplementary Table S1).

Cancer data source

The study selected the top 10 cancers worldwide for analysis, with data from the UK Biobank and FinnGen studies. The cases for each cancer type were as follows: breast cancer (sample size: 17,398), lung cancer (sample size: 3791), colorectal cancer (sample size: 6581), prostate cancer (sample size: 11,599), stomach cancer (sample size: 1029), hepatic cancer (sample size: 379), cervical cancer (sample size: 909), esophageal cancer (sample size: 232), thyroid cancer (sample size: 1054), bladder cancer (sample size: 1115) (Supplementary Table S1).

Lipids data source

Lipids were selected based on several common clinical indicators for assessing cardiovascular risk. Triglycerides (TG) (sample size: 78,700), total cholesterol (TC) (sample size: 437,878), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (sample size: 315,133), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (sample size: 343,621), very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C) (sample size: 115,078), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) (sample size: 115,078), apolipoprotein B (Apo B) (sample size: 115,078), ratio of ApoB to ApoA1 (sample size: 115,078) (Supplementary Table S1).

Anti-tumor drug data source

Literature review identified that the anti-tumor drugs 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, bleomycin, bevacizumab, and nivolumab have been shown to damage vascular endothelium, induce vasospasm, and accelerate coronary atherosclerosis progression [28–32]. Additionally, target genes of these drugs like dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPYD), deoxyribonucleic acid-3-methyladenine glycosylase (MPG), alpha-2-macroglobulin (A2M), serotransferrin (TF), sntioxidant 1 copper chaperone (ATOX1), deoxyribonucleic acid ligase 1 (LIG1), deoxyribonucleic acid ligase 3 (LIG3), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), complement C1q subcomponent subunit A (C1QA), complement C1q subcomponent subunit B (C1QB), complement C1q subcomponent subunit C (C1QC), high affinity immunoglobulin gamma Fc receptor I (FCGR1A), low affinity immunoglobulin gamma Fc region receptor II-a (FCGR2A), low affinity immunoglobulin gamma Fc region receptor III-A (FCGR3A), low affinity immunoglobulin gamma Fc region receptor II-b (FCGR2B), programmed cell death protein 1 (PDCD1), programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (CD274) were retrieved from the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/) (Supplementary Table S2). To identify cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) associated with the expression of drug target genes in blood, we utilized the expression quantitative trait loci generation consortium (eQTLGen Consortium) database (Supplementary Table S3).

Instrumental variables selection

The selection of IVs was based on the three main hypotheses of MR. SNPs significantly associated with exposure factors were selected using a threshold of P < 5 × 10–8. To ensure independence among SNPs, a linkage disequilibrium coefficient (r2 < 0.001) was set. To limit the scope of the analysis, a region width of 10,000 kilobase (kb) was set to reduce the effect of pleiotropy on the results, thus obtaining pertinent IVs [33]. In cases where SNPs were missing in the outcome data, they were substituted with proxy SNPs exhibiting high linkage disequilibrium (LD > 0.8); if no suitable proxy SNP was available, the SNP was excluded from further analyses. Furthermore, SNPs with palindromic variants were excluded from the analysis. SNPs were excluded if they were identified as weak IV with an F-statistic < 10, as they could cause bias in MR analysis due to weak instruments [34]. The final set of SNPs for analysis were determined through rigorous screening procedures.

Two-sample MR analysis

This study intended to perform two-sample MR analysis using methods including inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median (WM), MR-Egger, and Mendelian Randomization-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO). The outcomes of this study were primarily interpreted using random effects IVW to assess the causal relationship between exposure and outcome, as this method assumes that MR estimates of different SNPs follow a normal distribution, providing stronger robustness and some tolerance for heterogeneity. The WM method requires that more than 50% of SNPs satisfy the three major assumptions of MR to obtain unbiased results, but statistical power is lower compared to IVW. The main difference between MR-Egger and IVW is that MR-Egger regression analysis considers the presence of an intercept term, even in the presence of genetic pleiotropy among included SNPs. The slope of the regression represents the estimated causal effect of exposure on the outcome. However, owing to its lower statistical power and wider confidence intervals, the intercept value in MR-Egger analysis is typically used as a sensitivity analysis to assess the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. Moreover, MR-PRESSO can also detect the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, it performs outlier tests to identify and remove SNPs that affect horizontal pleiotropy, enabling the final results of this study to be obtained through MR analysis [35–37]. Heterogeneity testing was mainly conducted using Cochran's Q statistic. The analysis above suggests statistical differences when P < 0.05 [38]. In IVW analysis, we further applied Bonferroni correction to adjust the significance threshold for multiple tests. Evidence of significant causal relationships is considered when P < 0.05/n (where n is the number of outcomes), and evidence with 0.05/n ≤ P < 0.05 is considered suggestive [39]. Additionally, leave-one-out analysis was performed to determine whether MR results are decisively influenced by any single SNP.

Multivariate MR analysis

Given the numerous shared risk factors between CVD and cancer, such as obesity, alcohol consumption, smoking, exercise, fasting glucose, etc. We used MVMR-IVW method to adjust for these confounding factors. This allowed us to assess the robustness of the causal relationship between CVD and cancer. Additionally, the target genes of chemotherapy and other anti-tumor drugs were incorporated as confounding factors in a MVMR-IVW method to assess the robustness of the causal relationship with cancer as the exposure factor and CVD as the outcome. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05.

Mediation analysis

To examine the associations among CVDs, cancer, and lipids, we conducted a mediation analysis using the Prodclin method implemented in the RMediation package (version 1.2.2) in R [40]. Initially, the effect of lipids on cancer (beta1) was evaluated using univariable Mendelian randomization (UVMR). Subsequently, UVMR examined the effect of lipid levels on CVDs (beta2). Finally, UVMR evaluated the influence of CVDs on cancers (beta3). The mediation ratio was calculated as [(beta2 × beta3) / beta1]. The detailed analysis code is available in the Supplementary materials.

All MR analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.2.2) (http://www.r-project.org), primarily using the “Two Sample MR” package (version 0.5.8) for UVMR and MVMR analyses and the “RMediation” package (version 1.2.2) for mediation Mendelian analyses. Other R packages, such as “cowplot,” “ggplot2,” “forestploter,” “ComplexHeatmap,” and “circlize,” were used to assist with supplementary analyses and figure generation [41].

The causal effect of CVDs on cancers

We used IVW, WM, MR-Egger, and MR PRESSO to evaluate the causal relationship between CVDs and cancers. In the IVW analysis model, The following inverse causal associations between CVDs and cancers were observed (all P < 0.05): stable angina pectoris with breast, lung, colorectal, prostate, gastric, hepatic, and esophageal cancers; unstable angina pectoris with colorectal and hepatic cancers; myocardial infarction with lung, colorectal, gastric, and hepatic cancers; heart failure with hepatic cancer; and coronary atherosclerosis with lung, colorectal, and hepatic cancers, while no causal relationship was found between CVD and cervical cancer, thyroid cancer, and bladder cancer (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S11). After Bonferroni correction, the following CVDs and cancers still exhibited negative causal relationships (all P < 0.005): stable angina pectoris with lung, prostate, gastric, and hepatic cancers; unstable angina pectoris with colorectal and hepatic cancers; myocardial infarction with lung, colorectal, gastric, and hepatic cancers; and coronary atherosclerosis with hepatic cancer. Additionally, the causal relationships calculated using the WM and MR-Egger methods did not yield significant results for all the above outcomes. Except in the MR-Egger analysis, where the direction of the relationship between unstable angina pectoris and colorectal cancer was inconsistent with the directions found by the other two methods, the directions of the remaining results were consistent across the three methods (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S11). All three methods support a negative causal relationship between the aforementioned CVDs and cancers. Heterogeneity tests indicated heterogeneity between heart failure and thyroid cancer, and between coronary atherosclerosis and gastric cancer. MR-Egger intercept test revealed horizontal pleiotropy between stable angina pectoris and colorectal cancer, unstable angina pectoris and prostate cancer, myocardial infarction and colorectal cancer and prostate cancer, coronary atherosclerosis and bladder cancer (P < 0.05). After conducting MR-PRESSO analysis and removing the corresponding outliers for reanalysis, the horizontal pleiotropy effect between coronary atherosclerosis and gastric cancer persists (Supplementary Table S11, S12). The causal effect of cancers on CVDs

The IVW genetic prediction analysis evaluating the causal effects of cancers on CVDs showed that the following inverse causal relationships were observed between cancers and CVDs (all P < 0.05): breast cancer with unstable angina pectoris; colorectal cancer with stable angina pectoris and myocardial infarction; gastric cancer with stable angina pectoris, unstable angina pectoris, and myocardial infarction; hepatic cancer with stable angina pectoris, unstable angina pectoris, and coronary atherosclerosis; and cervical cancer with stable angina pectoris, unstable angina pectoris, and myocardial infarction, whereas there was no causal relationship between each cancer and heart failure (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S14). After Bonferroni correction, there were significant negative causal relationship between the following CVDs and cancer types (all P < 0.01): colon cancer and stable angina pectoris, gastric cancer and stable angina pectoris, gastric cancer and myocardial infarction, hepatic cancer and unstable angina pectoris, cervical cancer and stable angina pectoris, and cervical cancer and unstable angina pectoris, the significant causal relationships identified after Bonferroni correction are consistent with the results from the WM analysis (P < 0.05). MR-Egger analysis did not indicate significant causal relationships for the above meaningful results, but the directional consistency among the three methods remains generally consistent. However, in the MR-Egger analysis, the directionality of gastric cancer with stable angina pectoris and with myocardial infarction differs from the direction observed in the other two methods, indicating a bias in these two results and suggesting that the causal relationships are invalid (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 3). MR-PRESSO analysis did not detect horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S14, S15). The causal effect of lipids on CVDs

MR-based genetic prediction analysis revealed a significant causal relationship between lipids and CVDs. In the IVW analysis, anti-atherogenic lipids HDLC and ApoA1 showed negative causal associations with CVDs, whereas atherogenic lipids, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and apolipoprotein B/apolipoprotein A1 ratio exhibited positive causal associations with CVD (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table S19, 20). After Bonferroni correction, the negative causal relationships of apolipoprotein A1 with heart failure and coronary atherosclerosis were considered weakened (P < 0.05). In either the WM or MR-Egger analysis, there was at least one analysis indicating a significant causal relationship between lipids and CVDs. In the sensitivity analysis, some lipids exhibited heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy with CVDs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table S21). Mediation Mendelian analysis

Based on MVMR analyses, the study further identified triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol—three lipids with significant causal associations with cancer after Bonferroni correction—as exposures. As outcomes, we selected the cancer types (breast, colorectal, gastric, and hepatic cancer) that showed overlapping positive associations in both lipid–cancer and CVD–cancer causal relationships. Mediation analysis was conducted using CVD as the mediator to assess its potential mediating effect in the relationship between lipids and cancer. It revealed that stable angina pectoris, unstable angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, coronary atherosclerosis, and heart failure all exert significant mediating effects in the relationship between lipids and cancer development. Myocardial infarction was found to nearly fully mediate the relationship between LDL-C and hepatic cancer, accounting for 98.4% of the effect (OR = 0.852, 95% CI 0.792–0.908; P = 4.18E-6). Similarly, myocardial infarction mediated 93.7% of the effect of triglycerides on gastric cancer (OR = 0.948, 95% CI 0.919–0.973; P = 3.04E-4) and 88.7% of the effect of total cholesterol on hepatic cancer (OR = 0.883, 95% CI 0.830–0.930; P = 1.72E-05). Moreover, in the positive association between triglycerides and hepatic cancer, CVD–related mediators significantly attenuated the risk effect. Among them, unstable angina pectoris exerted the strongest mediating effect, with a mediation proportion of –25.10% (OR = 0.909, 95% CI 0.845–0.966; P = 4.86E − 03) (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 26).Fig.7Forest plot shows Mediation analysis of lipid traits on cancer risk through cardiovascular mediators. Discussion

CVDs and cancers are prevalent worldwide, characterized by high incidence and mortality rates, understanding their causal relationship is of crucial importance for changing prevention and treatment strategies. As far as we know, this study represents the first comprehensive exploration of causal relationships between CVDs and cancers using MR analysis, while establishing a valid link with lipids, revealing the mediating role of CVDs in the effect of lipids on cancer development. Pairwise analysis of common CVDs and cancers consistently suggests a causal relationship between the onset of CVD and cancer. Interestingly, they exhibited a negative correlation. Forward MR analysis revealed that each type of CVD was inverse causally associated with at least one type of cancer. Specifically, all CVD types showed negative causal relationships with hepatic cancer, stable angina showed the richest causal relationships with cancers, heart failure was solely associated with hepatic cancer, and there was no causal relationship between CVD and cervical cancer, thyroid cancer, or bladder cancer. Contrary to prevailing stereotypes, research findings significantly diverge from the widely accepted notion that CVD increase the risk of cancer.

According to Bell and colleagues' analysis of a population sample exceeding 20 million individuals, patients with CVD face a significantly higher risk of cancer compared to residents without CVD. Although the study included a sufficient sample size and considered the impact of potential common risk factors, it remains an observational analysis that can only demonstrate an association between CVD and cancer, not establish a causal relationship. Moreover, they merely classified CVD broadly based on their association with atherosclerosis, without specifying specific types of CVD. MR analysis method approached the study from the perspective of genetic variation, aiming to minimize confounding factors and effectively demonstrate causal relationships. Simultaneously, study analyzed disease subtypes within CVD, encompassing the complete disease progression from atherosclerosis to heart failure [11]. Additionally, Kaneko et al. found through retrospective studies that for each additional susceptible risk factor in cancer patients, such as smoking, dyslipidemia, etc., increased the incidence of CVD by 12% [43]. This suggests that cancer patients experience higher incidences and mortality rates of CVD compared to non-cancer patients. However, due to the study's lack of a healthy control group and its inability to effectively assess the proportion of CVD caused by medications or radiation toxicity during cancer treatment, the general applicability of this findings is limited [43, 44]. Researchers have further demonstrated through cachexia mouse models of cancers that cancers indeed induce cardiac fibrosis and decrease myocardial contractile protein levels, resulting in impaired cardiac function. However, it is important to note that pure malnutrition can also lead to myocardial damage, and the study did not establish corresponding control groups [45, 46]. This study identified inverse associations between CVD and cancer using MR analysis, which appears to contradict findings from traditional epidemiological studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to fundamental methodological differences: observational studies capture the cumulative effects of lifelong pathological conditions and various environmental exposures, whereas MR analysis reveals the effect of genetically predicted lifelong CVD risk, independent of confounding factors. MR estimates reflect lifelong genetic predispositions rather than transient environmental exposures [26, 47, 48]. Therefore, our findings suggest that the genetic liability to certain CVDs may be inversely associated with cancer risk, which may not necessarily translate into real-world scenarios where behavioral, environmental, and treatment-related factors play dominant roles. Moreover, shared risk factors such as obesity and smoking may account for the comorbid patterns observed between CVD and cancer in clinical and epidemiological settings, potentially exaggerating their apparent positive association [49, 50].

It is noteworthy that evidence have revealed an intriguing finding that the strongest genetic susceptibility locus associated with atherosclerosis is located at a well-known cancer gene locus on chromosome 9p21. Notably, this locus also harbors several cancer suppressor genes, including methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A). Genetic variations at the 9p21 locus related to both conditions may affect the expression of long non-coding RNA ANRL (Antisense Non-Coding RNA in the INK4 Locus), which in turn regulates the expression level of CDKN2A, influencing cancer development [51]. Park et al. discovered that there are common pathways in lipid metabolism between CVD and cancer. Both cancer and many CVDs exhibit changes in glutamine metabolism levels. In different stages of CVD, downregulation of glutamine metabolism levels can inhibit cancer cell proliferation and metastasis. These potential mechanisms may explain the influence of CVD on cancer. It is possible that the pathogenesis of CVDs may activate certain tumor-suppressive pathways, although further mechanistic studies are needed to validate our findings [19–21]. When discussing cancer and CVD, it is inevitable to consider shared risk factors such as obesity, smoking, diabetes, and alcohol consumption. To account for potential confounding in the UVMR results, we performed MVMR analysis on the significant outcomes they identified. After sequentially controlling for confounding factors, we found that the negative correlation between CVD and cancer remained largely unchanged, thereby further strengthening the credibility of our findings. Considering that cancer and CVDs typically share common risk factors, we further performed MVMR analysis to control for confounders. After adjustments, the overall negative correlation between CVDs and cancer remained robust, further strengthening the reliability of our findings. After adjusting for genetic variations related to glycosylated hemoglobin and obesity, the genetic association between stable angina pectoris and prostate cancer was no longer statistically significant. This may be because these two variations play a dominant role in the development of prostate cancer. Gudmundsson et al. reported that diabetic patients have a lower risk of prostate cancer [52, 53]. Obesity and diabetes may reduce insulin and testosterone levels, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of prostate cancer cells [54–59]. This plausibly explains the changes observed in the adjusted results. Furthermore, we found that most cancers have a negative causal relationship with at least one CVD through reverse MR analysis. This contrasts with the observed phenomenon that cancer patients generally have a higher incidence of CVD compared to non-cancer patients [42]. We believe this discrepancy may be partly due to the fact that both cancer and CVD predominantly affect older populations, and these conditions often coexist, leading to a higher incidence of CVD among cancer patients.

Lipids are closely associated with cardiovascular, endocrine, and tumor diseases [16, 60, 61]. Lipids were categorized into atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipids for MR analysis. Most atherogenic lipids were negatively associated with the risk of various cancers. The mediation analysis suggests that CVD may play a mediating role in the pathway through which lipids influence cancer development, but further validation is needed. Notably, triglycerides were an exception among atherogenic lipids, exhibiting a significant positive association with hepatic cancer. A large prospective cohort study based on the Swedish AMORIS cohort by Nderitu et al. also indicated that elevated triglyceride levels are associated with an increased risk of primary liver cancer [62]. Mechanistically, hepatocellular carcinoma cells exhibit lipid remodeling compared with normal hepatocytes. Tumor cells can enhance the utilization efficiency of triglycerides through lipid metabolic reprogramming. This leads to the production of sufficient free fatty acids and glycerol, which serve as precursors for phospholipid synthesis and energy supply, thereby supporting energy production, membrane biosynthesis, and proliferation in cancer cells. In the context of liver cirrhosis, hepatic stellate cell activation results in triglycerides accumulation, potentially facilitating hepatocarcinogenesis through the PPARγ signaling pathway [63, 64]. Additionally, the mediation analysis suggests that lipids may influence the development of liver cancer via CVDs. Altayebet al. found that when hepatic fat accumulation results from impaired triglyceride export, it leads to a blood profile characterized by low circulating triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein. This condition not only lowers CVD risk but also indirectly reduces liver cancer risk by decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators. Therefore, the association between hepatic fat and CVD is determined by the underlying mechanisms of hepatic fat accumulation, and may be largely modulated by circulating lipids [65]. However, whether dyslipidemia is a definitive risk factor for cancer remains inconclusive across different studies. Atherogenic lipid low-density lipoprotein cholesterol can activate the p53/p16 pathway, triggering cancer cell senescence, and anti-atherogenic lipids such as high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1 inhibit tumor cell proliferation by mediating reverse cholesterol transport via the SR-B1 receptor [66, 67]. Moreover, 27- hydroxycholesterol, a cholesterol-derived atherogenic lipid, exhibits contrasting effects on cancer cell proliferation in specific cancer types such as breast and prostate cancer. Upregulation of cholesterol 27-hydroxylase can clear cholesterol, thereby protecting against atherosclerosis while also influencing cancer cell proliferation [68, 69]. Fatty acid β-oxidation, as a key metabolic process in the tumor microenvironment, plays a vital role in regulating oxidative stress, immune responses, and signaling pathways. Fatty acid β-oxidation also plays an important role in the development of CVDs, and the competition for biological enzymes or proteins required by this metabolic process may influence cancer [21]. These mechanisms partly explain the inverse lipid associations mediated by certain CVDs. When exploring the causal effect of cancer on CVD, it is essential to account for the potential of anti-tumor drugs to induce CVD in patients during treatment [70]. Accordingly, this study incorporated the target genes of anti-tumor drugs as confounding factors in a MVMR analysis. After adjusting for anti-tumor drugs, the causal effects of certain cancers on CVDs were weakened, indicating that anti-tumor drugs may play a role in the occurrence of CVD in certain cancer patients. Traditional statistical tests often consider a P-value below 0.05 as statistically significant, but repeated tests may increase the risk of false positives, requiring multiple comparison corrections [39, 71–73]. After applying Bonferroni correction, the results of this study remain largely consistent with the uncorrected results. However, although multiple comparison correction methods enhance the reliability of statistical results, they may also over-exclude the actually meaningful results. Therefore, scholars argue that multiple correction method, as failing to pass it does not necessarily imply a lack of practical significance, but rather enhances statistical credibility [74].

According to our current knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive MR investigation of the causal relationship between lipid, CVDs, and common cancers. These findings have not been previously reported, and the study employed multiple statistical methods to enhance the robustness and reliability of the results. An important strength of this study is the use of the largest publicly available GWAS dataset. MR analysis leverages the random assignment of genotypes from parents to offspring, minimizing confounding and reverse causality bias, addressing the limitation of observational studies that can only identify correlations, and effectively validating causal relationships. Given that the GWAS database for European populations is currently more comprehensive than those for other populations, this study focused on Europeans. Further validation is warranted in diverse populations.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. Due to missing SNPs, a reverse analysis could not be performed for all cancers included in the forward MR analysis. Although multiple analytical methods were employed to mitigate horizontal pleiotropy, genetic variations may still independently influence multiple traits and create potential biases in the inference. Furthermore, population stratification bias may exist among datasets due to differences in geographic regions and socioeconomic backgrounds. However, due to database limitations, subgroup analysis based on characteristics like age, or ethnicity could not be conducted. In the analysis of the effect of anti-tumor drugs on the relationship between cancers and CVDs, we used drug target genes as proxies to represent the influence of the drugs on this relationship. However, target genes cannot fully represent the drugs themselves and may not entirely reflect the drugs' impact on the cancer-CVD relationship. It is important to consider that, while lipid levels are typically maintained within a reasonable range to prevent CVDs, the potential impact of such control on other diseases is often overlooked. Whether lower lipid levels within the normal range are always better, and how to balance the reduction of CVD risk with cancer risk, still requires further exploration [75]. It is important to emphasize that this study does not negate the epidemiological associations between CVD and cancer, but rather highlights, from a genetic perspective, that certain biological processes underlying CVD may inadvertently suppress tumorigenesis after removing the influence of confounding factors. Epidemiological evidence and MR serve complementary rather than contradictory roles, with the former offering insights into comorbidity prevention by targeting conventional risk factors, while MR uncovers complex interactions among CVDs, cancer, and lipid traits, which encourages further exploration of potential biological mechanisms, including identifying lipid levels or CVD biomarkers that influence cancer development, and developing novel anticancer therapies by targeting CVD-related markers or lipid metabolic pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, this study revealed pairwise causal relationships among lipid, CVDs, and cancers, but the inverse causal associations identified through genetically predicted MR analyses should be interpreted as effects rooted in genetic predisposition, rather than as evidence that CVD can directly “prevent” cancer or act as a protective factor in real-world clinical settings. From a genetic perspective, our findings suggest that certain unknown biological processes associated with CVD may suppress tumorigenesis, providing new insights into the complex interplay between CVDs and cancer, and potentially guiding the discovery of novel anticancer strategies.
