Oral Microbiota and Alzheimer's Disease: A Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization Study Based on East Asian Ethnicity
Authors: Jieyi Li, Qingqing Zhu, Jie Qin, Honglei Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe “oral–microbiota–brain axis” has been hypothesized to contribute to Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, but causal evidence remains limited. We performed a bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate potential genetic causality between oral microbiota and AD in East Asians.MethodsTwo‐sample MR integrated genome‐wide data from 2984 Chinese participants (saliva/tongue dorsum microbiomes) and 7036 Japanese participants (3962 AD cases/4074 controls). Genetic instruments were selected using pragmatically moderated significance thresholds (forward: p < 5 × 10−4; reverse: p < 5 × 10−5), with causality assessed via inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) regression and four Supporting methods. Sensitivity analyses validated robustness. We examined 3117 microbial taxa‐AD pairs, followed by exploratory Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis.ResultsForward MR identified 12 candidate causal taxa: five with risk‐increasing associations (e.g., Prevotella sp. MGS2526, OR = 1.32 [1.14–1.52], p = 1.4 × 10−4) and seven with protective associations (e.g., Streptococcus mitis MGS519, OR = 0.81 [0.69–0.94], p = 7.9 × 10−3). Reverse MR suggested AD may influence the abundance of three microbial species (e.g., Streptococcus sanguinis MGS515, OR = 1.04 [1.01–1.07], p = 3.6 × 10−3). Exploratory GO analysis highlighted enrichment in pathways related to synaptic transmission and nutrient metabolism.ConclusionThis study provides genetic evidence consistent with a bidirectional relationship between oral microbiota and AD in East Asians, nominating 15 microbial taxa as candidates for further investigation. The implicated biological pathways offer hypotheses for mechanistic links via the oral–microbiota–brain axis. These findings advance the etiological understanding of AD and highlight priority targets for future experimental and clinical validation.

Introduction

Alarmingly, the global elderly population is expanding at an unprecedented rate, with population aging being paralleled by an increasingly prevalent Alzheimer's disease (AD) pandemic. According to joint assessments by the World Health Organization and Alzheimer's Disease International, the current global dementia population has surpassed 55 million and is projected to double by 2050 [1, 2]. The progressive memory decline, cognitive dysfunction, and even basic behavioral abnormalities [3] associated with AD impose substantial burdens on families and society, with estimated healthcare and social care costs expected to reach trillions of dollars [4, 5]. While β‐amyloid deposition and tau protein abnormalities remain established core pathological hallmarks of AD [6], emerging evidence implicates multiple contributing factors, including neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, genetic predisposition, gut microbiome dysbiosis, and cerebrovascular dysfunction in AD pathogenesis [7]. Nevertheless, the precise etiology of AD remains elusive. Current FDA‐approved therapies primarily provide symptomatic management with limited disease‐modifying effects and notable safety concerns [8]. This therapeutic impasse underscores the urgent need to identify additional observable and modifiable risk factors.

Emerging evidence has revealed a striking association between the oral environment and AD [9, 10]. Older adults with dementia demonstrate significantly higher risks of oral pathologies such as periodontal disease and mucosal lesions compared to cognitively intact counterparts [11]. Conversely, periodontal disease and oral malignancies have been associated with increased AD incidence [12, 13], with epidemiological studies further revealing mortality correlations between periodontitis and AD [14]. The potential mechanisms underlying this oral–brain axis remain debated, with proposed hypotheses encompassing chronic infection/systemic inflammation [15], nutritional modulation [16], behavioral factors [10], and notably, the emerging role of oral microbiota [17]. As the second largest microbial reservoir after the gut microbiome, the oral microbiota exerts profound ecological regulation effects and has been increasingly linked to various systemic disorders, including neurological diseases [18]. 16S rRNA sequencing analyses have identified significant oral microbiome alterations in AD patients [19, 20], with specific microbial taxa potentially driving AD pathogenesis [21]. Promisingly, oral microbiome‐targeted interventions have shown therapeutic potential for AD management [22, 23]. More directly, typical oral pathogens have been detected in AD brains [24], suggesting possible hematogenous dissemination either via direct vascular penetration or indirect gut‐mediated pathways [25], potentially triggering amyloidogenesis or neuroinflammation [26]. However, current evidence predominantly derives from observational studies lacking randomized intervention designs. A 2022 Periodontology 2000 review critically questioned [27]: “Periodontal microbiota and Alzheimer's disease – a causative relationship?” Current research remains insufficient to establish direct causality, particularly regarding specific oral microbiome subpopulations.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to infer causal relationships through genetic instrumental variable approaches. This methodology leverages genome‐wide association study (GWAS) data [28], utilizing single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as genetic proxies for exposures while adhering to Mendelian inheritance principles, thereby enabling causal effect estimation through validity (p‐value) and directionality (odds ratio) assessments [29]. MR analysis has become increasingly valuable for investigating exposures where randomized clinical trials are impractical. Using East Asian‐specific GWAS datasets for oral microbiota and AD, our MR study aims to: (1) elucidate potential causal links between oral microbiome composition and AD risk; (2) identify specific oral microbial taxa exerting protective or detrimental effects; and (3) provide novel insights into establishing the oral–brain–microbial axis for AD prevention, detection, and therapeutic development. Additionally, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of lead SNPs to explore biological pathways through which oral microbiota may influence AD pathogenesis and vice versa. This multidimensional approach may reveal potential intermediate mechanisms underlying the oral microbiome‐AD interaction.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

Figure 1 illustrates the analytical framework. We employed a bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) design to investigate potential causal relationships between oral microbiota and Alzheimer's disease (AD). This approach adheres to three core MR assumptions: (1) genetic instruments (SNPs) must strongly associate with exposure; (2) SNPs should not directly influence outcome; and (3) SNPs affect outcome exclusively through the exposure pathway [30].

Study design of the Mendelian randomization between oral microbiota and Alzheimer's disease.

Both exposure and outcome genome‐wide association study (GWAS) data were derived from East Asian populations to minimize population stratification bias. The oral microbiota GWAS dataset (exposure) originated from the largest pioneering oral microbiome genome‐wide study to date [31], comprising 2017 tongue dorsum samples and 1915 saliva samples from 2984 healthy Chinese participants. The AD GWAS dataset (outcome) was obtained from a Japanese mega‐cohort study [32] including 3962 clinically diagnosed AD cases (per 2011 NIA‐AA criteria) and 4074 cognitively normal controls from the NBDC Human Database. To ensure compatibility for MR analysis, we performed a comprehensive data harmonization process. Specifically, we: (1) aligned all genetic variants to the same genome build (GRCh37/hg19) using the liftOver tool where necessary; (2) standardized the effect allele and effect size (beta) orientation across datasets based on reported allele frequency and strand information; and (3) excluded palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies (e.g., > 0.42) to avoid ambiguous strand alignment during harmonization. All data were accessed through authorized public repositories (provide repository names/accession codes if permissible), with raw data availability restricted per original study protocols. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE‐MR) guidelines [33]. A completed STROBE‐MR checklist mapping each requirement to the corresponding section of our manuscript is provided in Supporting Information S14.

Instrumental Variable Selection

To fulfill the core assumptions of MR analysis and ensure robust causal inference, we established specific genetic instrument selection criteria. Acknowledging the inherent limitations of current microbiome GWAS, which often lack the statistical power to yield a sufficient number of genome‐wide significant variants, we employed pragmatically moderated significance thresholds to secure an analytically viable set of instrumental variables. Specifically, for the forward analysis (oral microbiota as exposure), the significance threshold was set at p < 5 × 10−4, while a stricter threshold of p < 5 × 10−5 was applied for the reverse analysis (AD as exposure). To stringently compensate for this initial liberal selection and to rigorously ensure the quality and validity of the instruments, we implemented a multi‐layered validation and filtering protocol. First, to mitigate linkage disequilibrium (LD) effects, SNPs were clumped using an exceptionally stringent r 2 threshold of < 0.001 and a window size of 10,000 kb. Subsequently, genetic instrument strength was critically assessed by calculating the variance explained (R 2 = 2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × β2) and the corresponding F‐statistics (F = [R 2 × (N − 2)]/(1 − R 2)). All SNPs with an F‐statistic < 10 were decisively excluded to eliminate weak instrument bias. Furthermore, we performed a manual curation of the retained SNPs against major genomic databases (e.g., GWAS Catalog) to identify and exclude those with known associations to potential confounders or pleiotropic pathways, thereby proactively reducing the risk of horizontal pleiotropy. This comprehensive strategy was designed to balance the practical constraints of the data with the methodological rigor required for valid causal inference.

Statistical Analysis

Five MR methods were employed to leverage their complementary strengths and assess the robustness of findings: random‐effects inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) served as the primary method, supplemented by MR‐Egger regression (to detect and adjust for directional pleiotropy), weighted median (which provides consistent estimates if up to 50% of the instruments are invalid), simple mode, and weighted mode. The analytical workflow proceeded as follows: after instrumental variable selection and data harmonization, each MR method was applied independently to the harmonized dataset. The IVW method was implemented using a multiplicative random‐effects model to account for potential residual heterogeneity. A causal relationship from the primary analysis was considered statistically significant at a threshold of p < 0.01, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) quantifying the association between oral microbiota and AD risk. Sensitivity analyses were then systematically conducted to validate the assumptions: heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q‐statistics for the IVW and MR‐Egger methods; horizontal pleiotropy was assessed via the MR‐Egger intercept test (reporting intercept value, standard error, and p‐value); leave‐one‐out analysis was performed to identify any single SNP exerting disproportionate influence on the pooled estimate; and single‐SNP analysis (Wald ratio method) was conducted to verify the directional consistency of effect estimates from individual instruments. Robust causal inference required the simultaneous satisfaction of six pre‐specified criteria: (1) statistically significant IVW results (p < 0.01); (2) concordant OR directions across IVW, MR‐Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods; (3) absence of significant horizontal pleiotropy (MR‐Egger intercept p > 0.05); (4) no significant residual heterogeneity (IVW Q‐statistic p > 0.05); (5) stable effect estimates in leave‐one‐out analysis (i.e., no single SNP removal drastically altered the conclusion); and (6) majority directional consistency in single‐SNP analysis. All analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.1) with publicly available packages, primarily the TwoSampleMR (v0.5.6) and MRPRESSO (v1.0) packages.

GO Enrichment Analysis

Lead SNPs (the SNP with the lowest p‐value per independent locus) identified in significant MR analyses underwent gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis as an exploratory step to generate hypotheses regarding biological mechanisms linking oral microbiota and AD. The workflow for this analysis was as follows: First, causal SNPs were mapped to their proximal genes (within a 10 kb window upstream and downstream of the SNP) using the bioinformatics tool g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost) [34]. The resulting gene list was then submitted for functional enrichment analysis via the Metascape platform (https://metascape.org) [35]. The analysis was performed against the GO Biological Processes database. Statistical significance was assessed using a hypergeometric test, with multiple testing correction applied via the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. For result interpretation, we retained terms meeting the following thresholds: adjusted p‐value (Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.01, minimum number of genes from the input list (count) > 3, and enrichment factor > 1.5. Finally, significant GO terms were clustered based on semantic similarity, and a network of these enriched terms was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1) to highlight key biological pathways and their interrelationships.

Instrumental Variable Selection

Through sequential filtering (significance thresholds: p < 1 × 10−4 for oral microbiota taxa, p < 1 × 10−5 for AD; LD clumping: r 2 < 0.001, window size=10,000 kb; exclusion of weak instruments with F‐statistic < 10), we identified multiple SNPs as instrumental variables (IVs) for 1549 salivary microbiota taxa, 1568 tongue dorsum microbiota taxa, and AD‐associated loci (Supporting Information S1 and S3).

Discussion

The concept of the “oral–microbiota–brain axis” has gained increasing attention, with the oral environment closely linked to various neurological conditions such as depression, anxiety, and epilepsy. Research on AD is also deepening. Before interpreting these findings, it is crucial to acknowledge the inherent challenge in distinguishing whether observed oral microbiota alterations represent causal contributors to AD pathogenesis or secondary consequences of disease progression. While our Mendelian randomization analyses support potential causal directions for certain taxa, the bidirectional nature of microbiota‐host interactions necessitates cautious interpretation. Notably, while Porphyromonas gingivalis has been frequently implicated in AD pathogenesis [36], our analysis did not detect significant associations—a finding consistent with recent null reports [37]. Potential explanations for this discrepancy include cohort‐specific factors such as ethnic differences in microbiome composition or AD risk alleles, methodological variations in detecting low‐abundance taxa, or temporal dynamics whereby P. gingivalis effects manifest earlier in disease progression than captured here. These conflicting observations highlight the necessity of standardized profiling and longitudinal studies to definitively clarify its role in AD. Collectively, these considerations underscore that our MR findings, while providing valuable genetic evidence consistent with a causal hypothesis, are primarily exploratory and require mechanistic validation.

Five oral microbial taxa (three from saliva and two from the tongue dorsum) act as risk factors in AD pathogenesis. The genus Prevotella lacks species‐level studies due to cultivation limitations and phenotypic classification challenges [38]. Prevotella is abundant in the oral cavity, particularly in saliva (12–17% abundance) [39], making it the second most abundant oral genus after Streptococcus. It participates in biofilm formation [40] and is associated with gingivitis and periodontitis [41]. Additionally, it colonizes the gastrointestinal tract, where it has been observed in colorectal cancer patients [42]. The observed genetic association between Prevotella and AD could be consistent with either: (a) a potential active role in neuroinflammation through hematogenous translocation of live bacteria or LPS [43, 44, 45, 46], or (b) secondary proliferation due to AD‐related oral microenvironment changes. The observed IL‐1β/TNF‐α elevation [44, 45] could represent both cause and effect of neurodegeneration. The family Lachnospiraceae is widely studied in the gut, where it is considered a preventive factor for colorectal cancer [47] and a promoting factor for diabetes [48]. However, it is understudied in the oral cavity. Observational studies on Lachnospiraceae and AD have found higher levels in fecal samples of AD patients, similar to findings in Parkinson's disease [49] and schizophrenia [50]. One of Lachnospiraceae's most valuable metabolic products is butyrate, which plays a significant role in its interactions [51, 52]. Increased oral butyrate could potentially activate local oxidative stress and cell death in the brain, as well as elevate tau protein levels in the hippocampus and cerebellum [53], which offers a plausible mechanistic hypothesis for its association with AD pathology. The genus Streptococcus is a core member of the oral microbiota. Observational studies have shown that Streptococcus is more abundant in the oral cavity of AD patients compared to cognitively normal individuals [20], and its titer is associated with increased mortality risk in AD patients over 65 [14], leading to higher AD risk under greater infectious burden [54]. The genetic predisposition linking Streptococcus to higher AD risk might be explained by its potential to act as a pathogen capable of direct brain invasion [55, 56], which could trigger local immune inflammation [57] and subsequent neurological symptoms [58]. The genus Rothia is frequently reported in cases of oral‐origin infective endocarditis [59]. 16S studies have found reduced Rothia in the saliva of AD patients [60], and we hypothesize that the observed inverse association could potentially involve mechanisms such as pathogen translocation [61]. The family Saccharimonadaceae has rarely been reported in association with AD or the nervous system and remains understudied.

Our findings indicate that seven oral microbial taxa function as protective factors. These taxa belong to five distinct lineages, all of which are understudied and, for the first time, reported as protective against AD. Among them, four taxa exhibit positive roles in inflammatory and immunomodulatory processes, suggesting that they may reduce AD risk by regulating the immune microenvironment.

The genus Peptoanaerobacter has been shown to induce neutrophils to release TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐1ra, and the chemokines CXCL1 and CCL [62] in periodontal environments, indicating its potential role in immune modulation through inflammatory signaling. Similarly, Mogibacterium exhibits a positive correlation with IFN‐γ and TNF‐α [63], suggesting its involvement in immune activation. Additionally, Lancefieldella has been shown to increase IL‐6 and TNF‐α expression [64], further supporting its role in inflammatory regulation. Meanwhile, Saccharimonadaceae‐TM7x is significantly positively correlated with Treg cells [65], implying its involvement in immune tolerance and inflammatory regulation. Given that moderate, controlled inflammation and immune balance help prevent neuroinflammatory dysregulation, reduce neuronal damage, and lower AD risk. These findings propose that these taxa could be candidates involved in maintaining immune homeostasis, meriting further investigation.

The remaining three protective microbes, namely unclassified_mgs_ 2888, unclassified_mgs_1512, and Streptococcus mitis, belong to the Streptococcus genus, with only one having been specifically located. Streptococcus species, particularly S. mitis, are oral commensals known to inhibit pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, a recognized AD‐associated bacterium [66]. Their ability to produce H2O2 may contribute to oral microenvironment regulation by suppressing the overgrowth of pro‐inflammatory microbiota [67]. Additionally, S. mitis has been shown to influence systemic immune responses by modulating dendritic cells (DCs) and Treg cells [68], potentially reducing AD‐related immune abnormalities. By lowering pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels, including TNF‐α and IL‐6 [69], it may help slow AD progression. Interestingly, while Streptococcus‐MGS1983 acts as a risk factor, Streptococcus‐MGS2888, Streptococcus‐MGS1512, and S. mitis exhibit protective effects. This suggests that different Streptococcus strains may possess distinct physiological and immunomodulatory functions, potentially exerting opposing effects depending on their microenvironment. Factors such as strain specificity, microbial niche competition, and metabolite production may underlie this bidirectional role of Streptococcus. This speculative model illustrates how our genetic findings can generate specific, testable biological hypotheses.

Conversely, three oral microbial taxa—Streptococcus sanguinis, Absconditabacterales‐X112‐HOT‐345, and Saccharimonadaceae—were associated with increased activity under AD risk. This is the first report of AD‐driven adaptive changes in oral microbiota, highlighting the need for further studies to elucidate the mediators of this reverse communication.

Additionally, enrichment analysis was conducted to explore potential mechanistic links and generate novel hypotheses. The enrichment analysis indicated that genetic variants associated with oral microbiota‐AD links were overrepresented in GO terms broadly related to neuronal function and synaptic plasticity. These included terms for synaptic transmission (e.g., modulation of chemical synaptic transmission) and neuronal differentiation/cytoskeletal remodeling (e.g., cell morphogenesis involved in neuron differentiation). This indirect genetic evidence raises the possibility that oral microbiota might be linked to AD pathogenesis through processes involving synaptic function, neuroplasticity, and cellular signaling. However, this remains a speculative hypothesis derived from in silico analysis. Similarly, the enrichment of terms related to the extracellular matrix (ECM) and encapsulating structures (e.g., extracellular matrix organization) hints at a potential, albeit indirect, connection to the maintenance of brain microenvironment stability. Taken together, these enrichment patterns allow us to hypothesize that oral microbiota could potentially interact with AD‐related neurodegeneration via mechanisms that may involve glial cells and ECM structure. In contrast, lead SNPs directly associated with AD were enriched in pathways related to synaptic function, cellular morphology, and protein transport/endocytosis, which is consistent with the established understanding of AD neuropathology. Notably, the genetic loci shared between oral microbiota traits and AD implicated similar biological domains (e.g., synaptic plasticity, endocytosis). This overlap generates the hypothesis that oral microbiota might influence or reflect AD progression by engaging with these critical cellular processes. It is crucial to note that the directionality and biological reality of this engagement cannot be inferred from enrichment analysis alone. Therefore, the shared pathways highlighted here serve as a prioritized list for further experimental investigation. Future studies are needed to determine whether and how oral microbiota mechanistically contribute to AD pathogenesis via these pathways, which could eventually inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

If validated by future research, the oral microbial taxa identified in this study could hold promise for public health and clinical applications. In the long term, monitoring specific oral microbiome signatures might contribute to AD risk assessment frameworks. Similarly, strategies aimed at modulating these microbial taxa could be explored as potential avenues for AD prevention or therapy. The functional pathways highlighted here provide a prioritized starting point for mechanistic research. However, it is crucial to emphasize that these are forward‐looking possibilities, and substantial experimental and clinical work is required to determine any true translational utility. This study has several notable strengths. It utilizes the largest and most recent GWAS dataset on oral microbiota, analyzes 3117 taxa, and rigorously identifies differentially abundant taxa, ensuring broad coverage and high reliability. While our findings are derived from East Asian populations, it is important to acknowledge potential variability in genetic and microbiome associations across different ethnic groups. For instance, APOE ε4 allele frequency, a major AD risk factor, varies significantly between East Asians (~10%–14%) and Europeans (~19%–26%). Additionally, environmental factors such as diet and oral hygiene practices can influence microbiome composition, potentially affecting the generalizability of our results. Future studies in diverse populations, including African and European cohorts, will be essential to validate these preliminary associations and assess their broader applicability. This further underscores the exploratory and hypothesis‐generating nature of the current study.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The primary limitation of this study stems from the challenge of multiple testing inherent in our bidirectional MR framework, which involved a large number of microbial features. We performed rigorous sensitivity analyses and applied a conservative nominal significance threshold to enhance robustness. However, no associations survived formal statistical correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, we emphasize that our findings should not be interpreted as providing definitive evidence for causal relationships. Instead, this study is more appropriately viewed as a hypothesis‐generating resource that prioritizes a set of candidate microbial‐AD associations for future targeted investigation. Second, many oral microbial species lack standardized nomenclature or pure culture methods due to cultivation challenges, which limits experimental validation. Third, the current study is based on East Asian populations, and the extent to which these findings apply to other ethnic groups remains to be explored. Genetic background, dietary habits, and oral microbiome composition may differ across populations, influencing the observed microbial‐AD relationships. Finally, as the conclusions are based on algorithmic modeling, further validation through experimental studies is required.

Conclusion

In summary, this bidirectional Mendelian randomization study provides genetic evidence consistent with potential causal links between the oral microbiome and Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk in East Asian populations. Our analysis identified a set of candidate microbial taxa (12 from forward MR, 3 from reverse MR) whose genetically predicted abundance is associated with AD risk, and highlighted biological pathways through exploratory Gene Ontology enrichment analysis. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, given the inherent limitations of the microbiome GWAS data (requiring relaxed instrument selection thresholds) and the exploratory nature of analyzing a large number of microbial features without formal multiple testing correction.

Collectively, this work contributes to the emerging research on the oral–brain axis by nominating priority targets for future research. The implicated taxa and pathways offer a focused starting point for subsequent experimental validation to determine their biological roles and any true causal involvement in AD pathogenesis. Future studies in larger, diverse cohorts, employing stricter genetic instruments and mechanistic models, are needed to confirm these associations and explore their potential relevance for understanding AD etiology.
