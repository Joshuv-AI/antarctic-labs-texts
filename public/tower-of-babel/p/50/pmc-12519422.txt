The Effect of Iron Deficiency Anemia on the Morphology–Voltage–P Wave Duration (MVP) Risk Score
Authors: Göknur Yildiz, Özge Turgay Yildirim, Şeyhmus Kaya, Fatih Alper Ayyildiz, Can Gökay Yildiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIron deficiency anemia (IDA) is a common global health issue affecting over two billion people and is associated with cardiovascular complications. Electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities may reflect cardiac involvement in IDA. The Morphology‐Voltage‐P wave Duration (MVP) score, which assesses P‐wave duration, voltage, and morphology, is linked to atrial fibrillation (AF) risk. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between IDA and MVP score to evaluate its potential in cardiac risk assessment.MethodsA prospective study was conducted between January 1 and June 30, 2024, at a tertiary hospital. It included 241 IDA patients and 204 healthy controls. All participants underwent ECG and echocardiography. MVP scores were calculated, and left atrial (LA) diameter and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were recorded.ResultsMVP scores were significantly higher in the IDA group compared to controls (p < 0.001). P‐wave morphology and voltage were notably altered in IDA patients (p < 0.001). LA diameters were larger, and LVEF values were lower in the IDA group (p < 0.001). MVP scores positively correlated with LA size and negatively with LVEF. Additionally, IDA patients had higher heart rates and lower diastolic blood pressure (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIDA is associated with increased MVP scores, suggesting possible atrial remodeling and higher AF risk. The MVP score may serve as a non‐invasive tool for cardiovascular risk stratification in IDA patients. Further research is needed to assess the impact of IDA treatment on ECG parameters and arrhythmia risk.

Introduction

Anemia is the most prevalent blood disorder globally, affecting over two billion people. If untreated, it can lead to severe complications and even death (Kant and Kant 2020; Kwon et al. 2020). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anemia as a hemoglobin level below 13 g/dL in men (≈39% hematocrit) and below 12 g/dL in women (≈36% hematocrit) (Kant and Kant 2020; Cappellini et al. 2022; Mansour et al. 2021). Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) accounts for nearly half of all anemia cases worldwide (Petry et al. 2016). It results from insufficient iron for hemoglobin production during erythropoiesis, leading to reduced oxygen‐carrying red blood cells. In developed countries, IDA often stems from gynecological bleeding in women and gastrointestinal blood loss in men and postmenopausal women (Kant and Kant 2020; Findikli and Tutak 2018).

Anemia significantly contributes to ischemic cardiovascular diseases and heart failure and is linked to increased cardiovascular mortality (Kaiafa et al. 2015; Stucchi et al. 2018). Compensatory mechanisms—such as sympathetic activation, elevated heart rate, left ventricular hypertrophy, and activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system—attempt to counteract reduced oxygen delivery. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress are also commonly associated with anemia (Zoppini et al. 2010).

Electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities are frequently observed in anemic patients due to myocardial oxygen imbalance (Findikli and Tutak 2018; Coppler et al. 2015). Several P wave parameters, including duration, dispersion, terminal force in lead V1, and interatrial block, are linked to left atrial dysfunction (Ahmad et al. 2019; Aizawa et al. 2019). The Morphology‐Voltage‐P wave duration (MVP) score, which incorporates these features, has emerged as a potential predictor of atrial fibrillation (AF) (Hayıroglu et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2020). Studies suggest that P wave indices can help identify individuals at risk for AF (Alexander et al. 2017; Skov et al. 2018; Xing et al. 2023).

This study aims to investigate the relationship between IDA and the MVP risk score, potentially offering insights into the impact of IDA on cardiac electrical function and AF risk.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital between January 1, 2024, and June 30, 2024, involving the emergency medicine and cardiology departments. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting to the emergency department with various symptoms and subsequently diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia (IDA) based on laboratory results were enrolled. Two experienced emergency medicine specialists, working in rotation, assessed all patients to ensure consistency in evaluation.

An age‐ and sex‐matched control group was selected from individuals visiting the cardiology outpatient clinic for routine assessments. These participants had no history of anemia, electrolyte imbalance, or other exclusionary conditions.

Exclusion criteria included: a prior diagnosis or treatment for anemia, coronary artery disease, hypertension, renal or rheumatologic diseases, valvular heart disease, hematologic disorders other than IDA, arrhythmias, malignancies, antiarrhythmic drug use, active bleeding, pregnancy, diabetes mellitus, electrolyte imbalances, and age under 18. Control participants found to have anemia during screening were also excluded.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval no: ESH/GOEK 2024/75), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and Laboratory Evaluation

Detailed clinical data were collected systematically. Blood samples were obtained from all participants for biochemical and hematological analyses, performed using automated systems: the Cell‐Dyn Ruby Hematology Analyzer for complete blood count (CBC), and the Architect c8000 for biochemical parameters.

IDA diagnosis was made according to established criteria: hemoglobin < 12 g/dL in women and < 13 g/dL in men, along with serum ferritin < 30 μg/L. Additionally, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) below reference values were used to support diagnosis. Normal MCV and MCHC ranges were defined as 80–99 fL and 26–33 g/dL, respectively (Cappellini et al. 2022; Mansour et al. 2021).

Electrocardiography Examination

A standard 12‐lead surface ECG was performed for all participants using a Philips TC20 device. ECGs were recorded while participants were in the supine position, after a 10‐min rest. The cardiologist performing and analyzing the ECGs was blinded to group allocation to eliminate bias. Standard settings were applied: 20 mm/mV amplitude, 50 mm/s paper speed, and consistent electrode placement.

The Morphology‐Voltage‐P wave duration (MVP) score was calculated based on three parameters: P‐wave morphology in inferior leads, P‐wave voltage in lead I, and P‐wave duration. Each parameter was scored between 0 and 2 points (maximum score = 6) (Table 1). Risk categories were defined as: 0–2 (low), 3–4 (intermediate), and 5–6 (high risk of atrial fibrillation) (Alexander et al. 2019).

P‐wave duration was measured according to consensus guidelines (Bayés De Luna et al. 2012). The start of the P wave was defined as the initial deflection from the isoelectric line, and the endpoint as its return. This measurement technique is known for strong inter‐ and intraobserver agreement (Kızılırmak et al. 2016). P‐wave voltage in lead I was measured as the amplitude from the peak of the P wave to the baseline within the T–P segment. Measurement methods are detailed in Figure 1 (Alexander et al. 2019).

Technique for Measuring P‐Wave Indices: The duration of the P‐wave is determined by measuring from the first upward deflection of the P wave to the point where the waveform returns to the baseline in leads II, III, and aVF. P‐wave voltage is quantified by measuring the distance from the peak of the P wave to the baseline within the T–P interval in lead I.

Echocardiography Examination

Echocardiographic evaluations were performed using a Hitachi Arietta V60 system with a 3‐MHz transducer, with participants in the left lateral decubitus position. All examinations were conducted by an experienced echocardiographer blinded to clinical information. Each measurement was repeated three times, and mean values were used for analysis.

M‐mode measurements from the parasternal long‐axis view were used to assess interventricular septal (IVS) and posterior wall (PW) thickness at end‐diastole. Left atrial (LA) size was evaluated in the apical four‐chamber view, including horizontal and vertical diameters and maximum area using the biplane area‐length method, in accordance with current echocardiographic guidelines (Lang et al. 2005; Gavazzoni et al. 2024). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated via Simpson's method.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and as proportions for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Levene's test was used to evaluate homogeneity of variances.

Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 445 patients were included in the study, with a median age of 56 years (IQR: 47–65.5). Of the total cohort, 36% (n = 160) were male. Participants were divided into two groups: 241 patients (54.2%) comprised the anemia group, and 204 patients (45.8%) were assigned to the control group.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of Body Mass Index (BMI), Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), or Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) (p > 0.05). However, Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP) was significantly lower in the anemia group [median: 76 mmHg (IQR: 71–82)] compared to the control group [79 mmHg (IQR: 72–88); p < 0.001]. Heart Rate (HR) was significantly higher in the anemia group [80 bpm (IQR: 71–95)] than in controls [77 bpm (IQR: 68–85); p < 0.001] (Table 2).

Correlation analysis demonstrated that the MVP score was positively associated with horizontal LA diameter (r = 0.291), vertical LA diameter (r = 0.242), LA area (r = 0.198), posterior wall thickness (r = 0.217), and interventricular septum thickness (r = 0.098; p = 0.039 for all). Conversely, it was negatively correlated with Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) (r = −0.284; p < 0.001).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the effect of the Morphology‐Voltage‐P wave Duration (MVP) score, which is an innovative and strongly predictive measure for arrhythmogenicity, in patients diagnosed with anemia. Anemia, especially iron deficiency anemia (IDA), is a widespread health problem globally, affecting more than two billion people around the world. It has a substantial impact on cardiovascular health, and our study highlights how anemia is linked to changes in electrocardiographic (ECG) markers, including alterations in P‐wave morphology. These changes in P‐wave morphology could potentially indicate left atrial (LA) dysfunction and serve as a predictive marker for atrial fibrillation (AF), a commonly occurring form of arrhythmia. Specifically, our study found that the MVP score, which combines several P‐wave parameters, was significantly higher in patients with iron deficiency anemia when compared to the control group. This suggests that the MVP score might be a useful tool for assessing the likelihood of arrhythmic occurrences, particularly AF, in patients with anemia.

Our findings revealed notable differences between the anemia and control groups regarding P‐wave morphology, voltage, and duration. These findings are consistent with earlier studies that have highlighted a link between anemia and changes in ECG patterns, particularly those indicating left atrial remodeling and dysfunction (Cappellini et al. 2022; Sarnak et al. 2002). Changes in the P‐wave, including its duration, morphology, and voltage, are often considered markers for left atrial enlargement and increased atrial pressure, both of which are well‐established predisposing factors for AF onset (Hayıroglu et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2020). Interestingly, our study observed left atrial enlargement in the anemia group, despite the absence of typical arrhythmias or cardiac diseases that are usually associated with left atrial dilation. This finding suggests that left atrial enlargement might be an important contributing factor to the differences seen in the MVP risk score parameters between the two groups. When the left atrium enlarges, it can disturb the electrical pathways in the heart's atria, leading to conditions such as interatrial blocks, which in turn can cause changes in the P‐wave morphology. Additionally, chronic stretching of the left atrium may lead to fibrosis, and this fibrotic tissue can result in reduced voltage in the P‐wave, which might explain the lower P‐wave voltage observed in anemic patients in our study.

Iron deficiency anemia leads to a reduced oxygen‐carrying capacity in the body, activating compensatory mechanisms within the heart (Sarnak et al. 2002). These mechanisms include an increased cardiac output, tachycardia, and consequently a higher volume load imposed on both the left ventricle and the left atrium. Chronic exposure to this volume overload can result in structural changes and dilatation of the left atrium. This situation is associated with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and increased filling pressures, ultimately impairing the mechanical and electrical functions of the left atrium (Cho et al. 2014). Chronic atrial stretch not only affects electrical conduction but also promotes fibrosis, both of which can be reflected as changes in P wave morphology and voltage on the electrocardiogram (Osranek et al. 2006; Modena et al. 1997). In our study, significant increases in left atrial measurements and MVP scores were observed in patients with iron deficiency anemia, suggesting the presence of atrial remodeling.

These findings are also consistent with recent studies investigating the relationship between iron deficiency anemia and cardiac structural/functional alterations. As demonstrated in the literature, iron deficiency anemia leads to marked increases in both the volume and area of the left atrium and exacerbates structural changes, particularly in patients with concomitant left ventricular dysfunction. Sustained volume overload of the left atrium results in atrial stretch, fibrosis, and electrical heterogeneity, thereby triggering P wave changes that are also reflected in elevated MVP scores. This process sets a favorable substrate for arrhythmias, particularly the development of atrial fibrillation (Dereli et al. 2019).

Although no statistically significant difference was found in P‐wave duration between the anemia and control groups, the anemia group did show numerically longer P‐wave durations. This could suggest early left atrial remodeling without significant delays in electrical conduction. However, when looking at the MVP score as a whole, the anemia group had higher scores, which indicate the presence of left atrial enlargement. This enlargement could be caused by mechanical stretching, fibrotic changes, and elevated sympathetic nervous response. The higher MVP score in the anemia group may, therefore, reflect a higher likelihood of developing AF, which further highlights the importance of cardiovascular monitoring in these patients.

Additionally, our study found that anemia was related to a higher heart rate and decreased diastolic blood pressure. These results align with earlier studies, which have shown that anemia is linked to increased sympathetic nervous system activity and altered hemodynamic responses (Gungoren et al. 2020; Yilmaz and Aydin 2021). The increased heart rate observed in anemic patients is likely a compensatory mechanism to make up for the reduced oxygen‐carrying capacity of the blood. This adaptive response may increase the risk of developing arrhythmias, including AF, and helps explain the changes observed in ECG markers in our study population.

Echocardiographic findings further supported the relationship between anemia and alterations in heart structure and function. Specifically, patients with anemia exhibited significantly larger left atrial diameters and area in comparison with the control group. These results indicate that anemia may contribute to left atrial remodeling, which could increase the susceptibility of patients to AF. The strong correlation between the MVP score and the left atrial dimensions strengthens the idea that the changes in ECG markers, as measured by the MVP score, reflect underlying structural alterations in the heart that are associated with anemia.

An interesting observation in our study was the negative correlation between the MVP score and the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). A lower LVEF, which indicates impaired cardiac function, has been connected to more unfavorable outcomes in anemic patients, particularly in those with heart failure or ischemic heart disease (Savarese et al. 2019; Sabah et al. 2020). Anemia may exacerbate pre‐existing cardiac dysfunction or play a role in the onset of heart failure through mechanisms such as increased myocardial oxygen demand, reduced oxygen supply, and aggravated ischemic injury (Sabah et al. 2020). The observed relationship between the MVP score and LVEF in our study suggests that a higher MVP score could be indicative of worsening cardiac function in patients with anemia, which could provide valuable insight for clinical decision‐making regarding the management and monitoring of these patients.

There are several important implications of our study. First, the MVP score could serve as an effective method for identifying anemic patients who are at a higher risk of developing arrhythmic events, including AF. Given the well‐established relationship between anemia and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, identifying high‐risk individuals early could allow for timely interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Second, our findings underscore the need for comprehensive cardiovascular evaluation in patients with anemia, particularly those with iron deficiency. Future research should aim to determine whether interventions aimed at treating anemia, such as iron supplementation or blood transfusions, can lead to improvements in ECG parameters, left atrial remodeling, and overall cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

This study shows that iron deficiency anemia is associated with significant changes in ECG parameters, particularly an increased MVP score. This elevation may indicate left atrial remodeling, which is known to increase the risk of atrial fibrillation (AF). Our findings suggest that patients with anemia should be monitored for potential arrhythmic complications. The MVP score may serve as a practical tool to identify those at higher risk for AF. Future studies are needed to evaluate whether correcting anemia can improve ECG findings, reverse atrial remodeling, and reduce the risk of developing AF in this population.
