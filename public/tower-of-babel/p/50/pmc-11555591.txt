Integration of lung function data: turning snapshots into stories
Authors: Samuel R. Wallbanks, Calvin Apen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Missing or inaccessible lung function measurements, gathered over time, have the potential to stagnate or impair clinical care decisions being made. This jeopardises patient safety and often contributes to excessive resource utilisation. Data integration is fundamental to clinical decision-making and entails amalgamating lung function data from multiple sources in a user-friendly format. Despite this, current systems for recording lung function data are suboptimal, with copious gaps in the clinical picture arising from missing or inaccessible lung function measurements. This article discusses the importance of data integration for lung function, with a call to action for key stakeholders involved in the performance, management and interpretation of such tests.

Introduction

Lung function is a critical test in the diagnosis and management of respiratory disease. Many patients undergo numerous lung function tests in their patient journey, providing clinicians with an understanding of trends in lung function decline that are superior to cross-sectional “snapshots” of lung function at one point in time. Without timely and accessible lung function data measurements, the potential for untimely, spurious or misinformed care decisions is much higher, impacting on patient outcomes and safety. Despite this, at present, there remain considerable defects in the integration and amalgamation of lung function data in hospital systems across the globe. In this viewpoint article we discuss: 1) the importance of longitudinal lung function measurement; 2) the benefits of integrated lung function data to patient care; and 3) the challenges of implementing integrated systems for recording and monitoring lung function data over time. This article is of particular relevance to clinicians and respiratory specialist healthcare professionals (e.g. scientists, physiologists, technologists and nurses), in addition to stakeholders who support data management, service provision and manufacturing of equipment for lung function monitoring.

The importance of longitudinal lung function measurement

Interpretation of lung function measurements at one “snapshot” in time carries several limitations. First, a window of uncertainty exists for normative lung function reference values and disease, meaning many patients with significant respiratory symptoms may be observed within the “normal range” for their age, height, sex and ethnicity when pathology exists [1]. The range of “normal” is also wide, meaning those with spirometry lung function values close to the predicted value may fall by >500 mL and still be considered within the normal range (see figure 1).

Illustration of a fictional 26.8-year-old, 180 cm, Caucasian male, showing 500 mL per year decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1). His initial value was 5.5 L (116.8% predicted) and final value was 4.0 L (86.6% predicted) at the age of 29.8 years. All values fall within the lower limit of normal (red slope) and upper limit of normal (blue slope) for the patients age, height, sex and ethnicity, based on most up to date Global Lung Function Initiative reference equations [7].

A second limitation of cross-sectional measurements is that not all of the variance in lung function is explained by age, height, sex and ethnicity. In fact, these factors only explain ∼60–70% of the variance in spirometric lung function [2]; the rest of the variance is explained by complex factors that include, but are not limited to, genetics, nutrition, in utero and occupational exposures, childhood respiratory infections and environmental conditions that, through evolution, have altered the dimensions of the thorax [3]. The only true way to assess what is “normal” for a given patient is therefore to interpret longitudinal changes in lung function through performing serial measurements and corresponding to changes to clinical history.

Numerous validated thresholds exist to quantify the significance of lung function decline in respiratory diseases [4]. These thresholds not only permit understanding of the levels of annual decline and response to treatment but several parameters (e.g. 95% confidence interval) are measurable to assess the likely impact of technical error on lung function measurements [5]. The error associated with a reproducible spirometry measurement is an absolute number of ∼150–200 mL [6]; a value which far exceeds the expected 30–40 mL per year decline in forced expiratory volume in 1 s or forced vital capacity expected in the healthy population [7]. Therefore, the ability to measure lung function longitudinally assists with making complex care decisions, especially in the context of diagnostic uncertainty (see figure 2).

The initial spirometry in healthcare setting 1 (H1) was 3.8 L (112.4% predicted) and declined by 600 mL to 3.2 L (97.2% predicted) by the time he was seen in healthcare setting 2 (H2). The benefits of integrated lung function data to patient care

Data integration refers to processes that bring together data from diverse sources, providing users with a unified view of the data in a usable format [8]. Effective data integration is desirable for lung function measures for several reasons, all of which have the potential to improve and personalise care for patients treated within healthcare systems: Better clinical decision-making based on a complete lung function clinical picture.Reducing the need for duplicate testing, which improves patient experience and reduces resource waste.Provide opportunities to standardise lung function interpretation across care settings; a factor linked to inaccurate disease management in primary care [9].Clearer record keeping, ensuring compliance with general data protection regulation legislation.Opportunities to understand the progression of lung function in respiratory diseases using big data approaches.Despite these benefits, a daily occurrence in respiratory clinical practice is the question “Where is the previous lung function report?”. Integrated systems for monitoring and recording lung function were discussed as early as the 1990s by those involved in the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) network [10], but, in 2024, such systems remain to exist in practice.

Failure to address the issues of integration and centralisation of lung function measurement has large implications for patients. In a recent feasibility study of domiciliary spirometry, 11% of patients with severe asthma were newly identified with an obstructive spirometry defect during 24 months of follow-up [11], enabling them to be commenced on biologic therapy. In variable conditions such as asthma, where spirometry mostly exists within a normal range and becomes more obstructive with aggravation of symptoms, integrated and efficient data handling are even more important to effective diagnosis and treatment (see figure 3). No cross-site studies have been performed showing the extent of the problem with data integration; however, in disparate care services across regions and countries, it is likely that a large number of lung function measurements available at one site are not available at another. This requires further attention.

Spirometry was collected through workplace surveillance (W1), in an emergency department (H1) and in a specialist tertiary care centre (H2). In the specialist care centre (H2), there was an inability to access forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) data recorded in H1. Objective confirmation of asthma was therefore prevented as spirometry follow-up in H2 mainly showed results within a normal range, expected for healthy persons of the same age, height, sex and ethnicity.

Challenges to implementing data integration approaches for lung function measurements

Numerous challenges exist in the integration of lung function measurements. First, integration of data across multiple sites of testing will be the main challenge. Settings where lung function may be performed include primary, secondary and tertiary care, as well as private healthcare providers and employers performing occupational health surveillance. Coordinating cooperation between each of these healthcare spheres would require a considerable effort and shared interest in collaborating to achieve data integration.

Secondly, system access and data intelligence are fundamental to achieving integrated lung function systems. Global interfaces already exist that integrate patient electronic health records across different healthcare settings [12]; however, caution around third-party use may pose a barrier to implementation of solutions to improve lung function accessibility. While generic information technology (IT) systems are typically in place to record physiological measurements within local hospital providers, the quality and integration of such systems with lung function testing equipment is variable. The manufacturers of lung function equipment also vary by region and lung function results may be stored and communicated through different forms of media in healthcare settings (e.g. lung function reports or written correspondence), making data extraction to a centralised platform more complex. A scalable integrated solution to achieving more accessible lung function data would require a coordinated effort between hospital governance, IT services and equipment manufacturers, and the use of new technology. Artificial intelligence (AI) has gathered increasing attention in the restoration of respiratory physiology services following the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic [13]. It is possible that trained data models within AI could be developed to bring together lung function measurements from the various media, with further ability to quality control and identify measurements that represent errors or outliers.

Ensuring that systems are continually improved and sustainable is a further challenge requiring investment. Lung function incorporates many different tests to evaluate airway dimensions, gas exchange capabilities and aspects such as resistance and reactance; each of which would require personalisation and agreement from multiple professionals on values most relevant to practice. New developments in monitoring occur continuously and it is important that integrated systems have the ability to adapt to changes in practice in real-time.

A further key challenge is the importance of data security and confidentiality. In accordance with the Caldicott principles, “the duty to share information for individual care is as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality” [14]. However, centralised datasets should not be viewed as an excuse to harvest unnecessary data for research purposes. The clinical benefits of a centralised and interoperable management system are of much greater importance to the patient than that for research-derived benefits. Large, centralised datasets offer the challenge of greater requirements for data protection and upkeep to safeguard against data breaches, ensuring anonymity of patient details in the context of multiple users across different healthcare settings.

Appropriate data visualisations also require attention. Historically, the software SPIROLA, developed by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), included many validated tools to assess lung function decline in occupational workplace surveillance but is no longer supported as a software [5]. The recent advances made by the GLI in the development of LUNG TRACKER V1.1 are promising [15], however, this system has no capabilities to store data. Re-entering data into the plotter is therefore required at each clinical visit, reducing the transferability of this tool to clinical practice.

This viewpoint is a call to action for key stakeholders involved in monitoring lung function. There is a humanitarian need to better integrate our lung function data to ensure patient safety and experience, as well as safeguard the resources within respiratory care systems. Further research is required in this area to improve data management practices and identify the scale of the current problem with integration of lung function measurements.
