Adjuvant chemotherapy with or without EGFR-TKIs for resected EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer: a pooled analysis of four randomized controlled trials
Authors: Sui Chen, Feilong Guo, Jiafu Zhu, Jiangbo Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of adding adjuvant epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) to chemotherapy (ETC) among patients with completely resected EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains unclear, particularly with respect to overall survival (OS). A meta-analysis was performed using randomized controlled trials (RCTs), aiming to assess both efficacy and safety profiles of adjuvant chemotherapy plus EGFR-TKIs versus chemotherapy alone.MethodsSix databases were systematically screened to retrieve eligible RCTs evaluating ETC versus placebo plus chemotherapy (PC) as adjuvant therapy. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were defined as the primary endpoints, while disease relapse together with treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) constituted the secondary endpoints.ResultsA total of five RCTs comprising 890 patients were included. Baseline patient characteristics (e.g., age, sex, and disease stage) were comparable between groups. ETC significantly improved OS (HR: 0.56 [0.41, 0.77], P = 0.0003) and DFS (HR: 0.36 [0.23, 0.57], P < 0.0001), with consistent benefits observed across all predefined subgroups. OS rates at 30–60 months and DFS rates at 6–60 months were significantly higher in the ETC group. More tumor recurrences were found in the PC group (RR: 0.37 [0.21, 0.66], P = 0.0007), predominantly due to distant metastases. TRAEs (RR: 1.35 [1.05, 1.73], P = 0.02) and TRAE-related treatment discontinuation or dose reduction (RR: 5.00 [1.12, 22.28], P = 0.03) were more frequent in the ETC group.ConclusionsAdjuvant ETC significantly improves survival and reduces distant recurrence for resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC; however, these benefits should be weighed against a higher but manageable risk of toxicity.Trial registration.PROSPERO ID: CRD420261381478Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16514-0.Keywords: Adjuvant, Chemotherapy, EGFR-TKIs, Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Radical surgical removal is the main curative strategy for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), though prognosis remains limited; however, five-year postoperative relapse incidence remains elevated, and adjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy confers merely an approximate 5% absolute survival benefit at the cost of substantial toxicity [1, 2]. Clinical identification of activating epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations has reshaped treatment paradigms: in advanced disease, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) consistently outperform chemotherapy [3]. This success prompted adjuvant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of first-generation TKIs as monotherapy (e.g., ADJUVANT, EVAN, EVIDENCE, and IMPACT), which uniformly improved disease-free survival (DFS) but failed to demonstrate an overall survival (OS) benefit, fueling debate over whether these agents merely delay recurrence [4–7]. Two recent RCT-based meta-analyses further supported this concern, showing that adjuvant EGFR-TKI monotherapy improved DFS but did not consistently improve OS [8, 9]. A parallel strategy—combining an EGFR-TKI with adjuvant chemotherapy—gained prominence following the ADAURA trial, which demonstrated that osimertinib significantly improved both DFS and OS, leading to guideline endorsement [10]. Nevertheless, the evidence base for combination adjuvant therapy remains incompletely defined.

Several key controversies remain. First, the OS benefit demonstrated with osimertinib has not been consistently replicated with other EGFR-TKIs or combination regimens, raising questions about a class-wide effect [10, 11]. Second, individual trials have been underpowered to precisely estimate treatment effects in subgroups, such as patients with stage IB disease or those harboring uncommon EGFR mutations [12]. Third, adding an EGFR-TKI to chemotherapy predictably increases treatment-related toxicity—predominantly rash, diarrhea, and dose reductions—and the overall risk–benefit profile remains incompletely defined [13]. Fourth, small sample sizes have resulted in imprecise pooled OS estimates, leaving unresolved the critical question of whether DFS gains translate into a consistent and durable OS benefit across the broader eligible population [7].

To overcome these limitations, we systematically conducted a meta-analysis of RCTs comparing adjuvant EGFR-TKI plus chemotherapy (ETC) with placebo plus chemotherapy (PC) in patients with completely resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC. OS and DFS were defined as primary endpoints, whereas recurrence patterns together with treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were assessed as secondary endpoints. By pooling all available data, we aimed to generate robust and precise estimates to inform clinical practice and help resolve the remaining controversies surrounding this strategy.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Literature retrieval was performed using “Lung cancer”, “Adjuvant”, “EGFR”, and “Randomized”. PubMed, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, Scopus, EMBASE, and Web of Science were systematically screened for eligible studies published before March 28, 2026 (Table S1).

Study design (S): RCTs.

Study design (S): RCTs.

Exclusion criteria: (1) publications designed as retrospective analyses, conference abstracts, case reports, reviews, and meta-analyses; (2) experiments conducted in animals; and (3) articles without sufficiently detailed reporting or data appropriate for statistical analysis.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted data on baseline patient characteristics (e.g., disease stage), OS, DFS, disease relapse (local or distant), and TRAEs. Survival probability was calculated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Data extraction employed Engauge Digitizer (version 12.1) [14]. Unreported information was obtained by contacting study authors, and inconsistencies were addressed after repeated review.

Outcome assessments

OS and DFS were evaluated across subgroups defined by age, sex, ECOG performance status (PS), smoking status, disease stage, EGFR mutation status, and EGFR-TKI generation. Intergroup survival rates (OS rates [OSRs] and DFS rates [DFSRs]) were compared at 6-month intervals within 5 years following surgery. In addition, DFSRs for pathological stages II and III were analyzed separately to facilitate direct comparisons between the two groups.

Quality assessment

Evaluation of study rigor used Cochrane Risk of Bias tool together with Jadad scoring system (7-point ceiling); results ranging from 5–7 corresponded to strong methodological rigor [15, 16]. Overall evidence strength was appraised with the GRADE framework, organizing outcomes into four tiers spanning very low to high certainty [17].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis calculations were conducted using RevMan 5.4 and Stata 13.0 software. When multiple datasets or time-point data were available across studies, the most recent dataset was selected for analysis. When newer analyses lacked endpoint values, information was obtained from previous literature sources. Survival endpoints were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs), while binary endpoints were reported as risk ratios (RRs). Inter-study variability was evaluated using the I2 index and Cochran’s Q test. Model selection depended on the magnitude of heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was low (I2 < 50% or P > 0.1). In cases of high heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out method, in which studies were sequentially removed to assess their impact on pooled effect estimates [18].

Patient’s baseline characteristics

Baseline sex, age, smoking status, ECOG PS, EGFR mutation, histologic type, pathological stage, and resection type of patients were comparable between groups (Figure S2).

ETC significantly improved OS compared with the PC group (HR: 0.56 [0.41, 0.77], P = 0.0003) (Fig. 2). Consistent improvements in OS were identified across all prespecified subgroup analyses, as reported in the outcome analyses (Table 2, Figure S3). During follow-up intervals of 30–60 months, overall survival rates were significantly greater in the ETC arm (Fig. 3A, Figure S4).

Disease-free survival

ETC significantly improved DFS compared with the PC group (HR: 0.36 [0.23, 0.57], P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). Consistent improvements in DFS were identified across all prespecified subgroup analyses, as reported in the outcome analyses (Table 2, Figure S5).

Across the entire cohort, DFSRs were notably higher in the ETC group throughout the 6–60-month follow-up period (Fig. 3B, Figure S6). Similarly, the DFSR advantage in the ETC group showed consistent trends in both stage II (Figure S7A) and stage III (Figure S7B).

Disease relapse

The tumor recurrence rate was higher in the PC group (78/445 vs 229/445, RR: 0.37 [0.21, 0.66], P = 0.0007). This difference was primarily driven by distant metastasis (distant metastasis only, 19/191 vs 45/190, RR: 0.42 [0.25, 0.69], P = 0.0007; simultaneous local recurrence and distant metastasis, 5/191 vs 19/190, RR: 0.26 [0.10, 0.69], P = 0.007; Fig. 4).

Safety

Overall, ETC treatment showed a higher incidence of total TRAEs (RR: 1.35 [1.05, 1.73], P = 0.02) and TRAEs-related treatment reduction (RR: 5.00 [1.12, 22.28], P = 0.03). Conversely, comparisons between groups revealed no statistically significant disparities in grade 3–4 TRAEs, serious TRAEs, TRAEs-related treatment interruption, TRAEs-related treatment discontinuation, or TRAEs-related deaths (Table 3, Figure S8).

In the analysis of any-grade TRAEs, increased blood creatine phosphokinase, diarrhea, pruritus, pyrexia, prolonged electrocardiogram QT interval, increased blood lactate dehydrogenase, and mouth ulceration were more frequently observed in the ETC group. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out approach were performed by removing each study in turn to evaluate DFS, DFSR-6 m, total recurrence, and overall TRAEs. The pooled results remained stable without meaningful fluctuation, suggesting that none of the individual studies had an excessive impact on the combined effect estimates (Figure S9).

Discussion

The role of adjuvant ETC for completely resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC remains under ongoing debate [11]. Although Osimertinib demonstrated an OS benefit in ADAURA, this finding has not been consistently replicated with first- or second-generation EGFR-TKIs as monotherapy, raising questions about a class-wide effect. In addition, adding an EGFR-TKI to chemotherapy predictably increases toxicity (e.g., rash, diarrhea, and dose reductions), while small trial sample sizes result in imprecise pooled OS estimates, leaving unresolved whether improved disease-free survival translates into a durable OS benefit [8–13]. To clarify these issues, a meta-analysis of four RCTs was performed. Our pooled analysis showed that ETC significantly improved OS and DFS, with consistent benefits observed across all predefined subgroups. The tumor recurrence rate was higher in the PC group, predominantly due to distant metastasis, and TRAEs were more common in the ETC group.

The improvement of OS with ETC (HR 0.56) represents a substantial improvement over the modest 5% absolute survival gain historically achieved with adjuvant chemotherapy alone. This OS benefit is best explained by the complementary mechanisms of the two modalities: adjuvant chemotherapy eliminates rapidly dividing cells regardless of driver mutations, whereas EGFR-TKIs induce apoptosis in cells dependent on oncogenic EGFR signaling [27]. Micrometastatic disease after surgery is biologically heterogeneous, comprising clones that are chemosensitive, TKI-sensitive, or intrinsically resistant to either agent when used alone [28]. By combining both approaches, ETC eradicates a broader fraction of residual disease, thereby limiting the early establishment of distant metastases that ultimately lead to death. Two patterns in our data support this mechanistic explanation. First, the OS curves diverged only after approximately 30 months, consistent with the gradual progression of microscopic residual disease to lethal recurrence; the persistence of this separation through 60 months suggests that ETC is not merely delaying events but may prevent irreversible, life-threatening outcomes [29]. Second, the DFS benefit (HR 0.30, corresponding to a 70% risk reduction) exceeded the OS benefit, a biologically expected pattern, as effective post-recurrence salvage with EGFR-TKIs in the control arm can prolong survival after relapse [30]. However, salvage therapy cannot fully compensate, as cure is rare once distant metastases are established. The remaining significant OS advantage suggests that the recurrences prevented by ETC are disproportionately those with lethal potential. This interpretation is further supported by prior adjuvant EGFR-TKI monotherapy trials, which consistently improved DFS but failed to demonstrate an OS benefit; without the complementary effect of chemotherapy, TKI-resistant clones may persist and ultimately drive fatal recurrence [31]. By addressing this mechanistic limitation, ETC may transform a delay in recurrence into a meaningful survival benefit [32]. The consistent OS benefit across disease stages, and mutation types further supports this conclusion, suggesting a biology-driven, broadly applicable effect rather than one confined to specific subgroups. Importantly, subgroup analyses by EGFR-TKI generation showed that the OS benefit was observed with both third-generation and first-generation EGFR-TKIs, indicating that the OS advantage of ETC was not driven solely by third-generation agents.

The significant reduction in disease recurrence with ETC (RR 0.38) provides a mechanistic link between the marked DFS improvement and the durable OS benefit. Notably, this reduction was driven predominantly by a decrease in distant metastases, the recurrence pattern most directly associated with cancer-related death. This observation aligns with the complementary-mechanism hypothesis: micrometastatic clones that are chemosensitive but TKI-resistant, or vice versa, can each seed distant failure when only one modality is used [33]. By concurrently applying both cytotoxic and targeted therapies, ETC reduces the reservoir of residual cells capable of establishing lethal distant deposits [34]. The timing of recurrence events further supports this interpretation. Although our pooled analysis lacked patient-level data to construct competing-risk curves, the emergence of OS curve separation after approximately 30 months suggests that early distant recurrences were preferentially prevented; patients in the control arm who experienced early distant relapse likely contributed to early mortality, whereas the ETC arm was relatively spared, allowing the survival curves to diverge over time [8]. The pattern of relapse also helps explain the difference between the DFS HR of 0.30 and the OS HR of 0.56. When recurrences occur in the ETC arm, they may be enriched for clones resistant to both chemotherapy and EGFR-TKIs, or may present as isolated local or central nervous system relapses that are more amenable to salvage local therapy—consistent with trial data showing a higher proportion of brain-only recurrences in TKI-treated patients [35]. Nevertheless, the overall reduction in distant, multisite recurrence likely contributes to the observed OS benefit. In contrast to adjuvant EGFR-TKI monotherapy, where DFS gains did not translate into OS improvement, the addition of chemotherapy appears to target TKI-indifferent clones that would otherwise lead to fatal distant metastases [11]. This mechanistic insight, together with the consistent reduction in recurrence across disease stages, supports the conclusion that ETC may alter the natural history of resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC by limiting distant seeding events that ultimately determine survival.

The safety profile of ETC reflects the predictable superimposition of two distinct toxicity spectra, and the observed patterns reinforce rather than undermine the clinical value of the combination. Treatment-related adverse events were more frequent with ETC (RR 1.35), and the fivefold increase in TRAEs leading to dose reduction (RR 5.00) indicates a meaningful additional burden. Importantly, this excess toxicity was primarily driven by known EGFR-TKI–related effects—rash, diarrhea, and paronychia—rather than by a synergistic exacerbation of chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression or organ toxicity [36]. This is biologically consistent, as the two modalities have largely non-overlapping toxicity pathways, resulting in an additive rather than synergistic safety burden [37]. Despite this increase, no unexpected safety signals were identified, and grade ≥ 3 events remained consistent with the established profiles of each drug class. The observed increase in dose reductions, while notable, should be interpreted in clinical context: the persistence of statistically significant DFS and OS benefits suggests that toxicity-related dose adjustments did not meaningfully compromise efficacy [38]. This supports a favorable therapeutic window, in which toxicity is increased but manageable, and necessary dose modifications do not negate the survival benefit [39]. When considered alongside the survival and recurrence data, the overall risk–benefit profile appears to favor ETC. In clinical practice, proactive management of EGFR-TKI–related toxicities, patient education, and adherence to established dose-modification strategies can help preserve both safety and efficacy [40]. Compared with adjuvant EGFR-TKI monotherapy, the addition of chemotherapy introduces an additional layer of toxicity; however, given that monotherapy did not improve OS, this incremental risk appears justified by the survival benefit uniquely achieved with the combination [41]. These safety data therefore do not detract from the use of ETC but instead highlight the supportive care measures required to deliver this regimen safely.

Certain limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, involving merely 4 RCTs, statistical power was inadequate to precisely estimate treatment effects in smaller subgroups, such as stage IB disease or uncommon mutations, and the apparent consistency across subgroups should be interpreted with caution. Second, reliance on published summary data rather than individual patient data precluded more detailed time-to-event and competing-risk analyses that could further evaluate our mechanistic hypotheses. Third, the included trials varied in TKI generation, chemotherapy backbone, and sequencing, introducing clinical heterogeneity; however, the consistently favorable direction of effect and the stability of findings in sensitivity analyses support the possibility of a class-wide phenomenon. Fourth, follow-up remains relatively short in the adjuvant setting, and longer-term data are required to confirm the durability of the OS benefit and to better characterize late toxicities. Fifth, formal assessment of publication bias was not feasible given the small number of studies; however, the magnitude of the observed effect makes it less likely that unpublished negative trials would qualitatively alter our conclusions.

Conclusion

Compared with adjuvant chemotherapy alone, the addition of an EGFR-TKI is associated with significant improvements in both OS and DFS, as well as a reduction in distant recurrence, in patients with completely resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC. These benefits are consistently observed across major clinical subgroups. The additional toxicity, predominantly EGFR-TKI–related and clinically manageable, does not appear to offset the substantial survival benefit. Future large-scale real-world studies are warranted to validate the present RCT-based findings, assess their generalizability in broader clinical populations, and further clarify treatment adherence, toxicity management, and long-term outcomes in routine practice.
