Aortic Sinus Dissection Causing STEMI
Authors: Jiabao Liu, Haoyu Meng, Hao Wang, Xiaomei Zhu, Liansheng Wang, Qiming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocalized aortic sinus dissection is a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome that is often undetected on initial imaging.Case SummaryA 72-year-old man with aortic root aneurysm presented with acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Initial computed tomography was negative for aortic dissection, however angiography revealed total occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA) owing to coronary artery dissection. A heart team approach is vital for staged management.Key words: acute coronary syndrome, dissection, percutaneous coronary intervention

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man presented to the emergency department with sudden onset of chest pain and discomfort 1 hour before. On arrival, a 12-lead electrocardiogram showed significant ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and AVF. Based on the patient's symptoms and electrocardiographic findings, he was diagnosed with an acute inferior wall myocardial infarction (Figure 1).

Emergency Electrocardiogram Indicates Acute Inferior Wall STEMI

The first electrocardiogram obtained in the emergency department showed sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF (red boxes) and ST-segment depression in leads V2 to V5. These findings are consistent with a typical acute inferior wall ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

Past Medical History

The patient had been diagnosed with hypertension at the age of 41 and was currently taking bisoprolol and valsartan/amlodipine. Aortic dilation was identified 20 years ago. Surgical intervention was recommended 6 years ago, but the patient declined.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential included ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), dissection of the aorta, pulmonary embolism, and myocarditis.

Investigations

Upon arrival to the hospital, the patient's vital signs were notable for blood pressure 106/62 mm Hg, pulse 92 beats/min, temperature 36.4 °C, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. A grade 2/6 diastolic murmur was heard in the aortic valve auscultation area. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin T level was mildly elevated at 25.8 ng/L (reference: <14 ng/L). Because he had a history of thoracic aortic dilation, we decided to perform enhanced computed tomography (CT) immediately to rule out an aortic dissection before transferring the patient to the catheterization laboratory for urgent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The CT showed dilation of the aortic sinus and ascending aorta, with no evidence of aortic dissection.

Management

After ruling out an aortic dissection, the patient underwent emergency coronary angiography (CAG) (Figure 2). No significant stenosis was seen in the left main, left anterior descending, or left circumflex arteries. Given the severe dilation of the aortic sinus, a SAL 1.0 catheter was used to engage the right coronary artery (RCA) ostium. Emergency CAG showed total occlusion of the RCA (Video 1), and primary PCI was performed. Puzzlingly, during the primary PCI, the guidewire failed to cross the RCA occlusion despite our repeated attempts. We then withdrew the SAL 1.0 catheter from the RCA ostium and performed an aortogram at the aortic root (Video 2). It was discovered that the site initially cannulated by the SAL 1.0 catheter was not the true anatomical ostium of the RCA; the SAL catheter could not be advanced to the true RCA ostium. It was substituted with an Amplatz left 1.0 catheter, which was then successfully engaged. CAG revealed total occlusion of the RCA owing to coronary artery dissection. Subsequently, primary PCI was performed successfully (Figure 3). For details of the PCI procedure, see Video 3. The patient's chest pain significantly improved.

Transthoracic echocardiography upon admission revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50.7%, moderate-severe aortic regurgitation, aortic sinus and ascending aorta dilation, and left ventricular inferior basal wall motion abnormality. Because of the anomalous RCA ostium found during PCI, we suspected that the cause of this patient's myocardial infarction was uncommon. To elucidate the etiology of the myocardial infarction, coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed using a photon-counting CT scanner 5 days post-MI. The CTA showed multiple intimal tears in the right aortic sinus, extending into the RCA ostium (Figure 4). Importantly, the preoperative aortic CTA (Figure 5) was subsequently re-evaluated in detail by an experienced cardiovascular radiologist after the PCI procedure. This dedicated retrospective review definitively confirmed the presence of a localized dissection at the right coronary sinus with direct extension into the RCA ostium. This post hoc analysis established that the aortic sinus dissection was present before any coronary intervention, thereby providing definitive imaging evidence that it was the direct cause of the RCA occlusion and the consequent acute myocardial infarction. Multidisciplinary discussions involving the cardiac surgery and radiology teams concluded that the myocardial infarction was caused by a localized dissection of the right aortic sinus. Given the persistent severe aortic root dilation, significant aortic regurgitation, and the high risk of recurrent dissection, rupture, or sudden cardiac death, surgical intervention was strongly recommended.

The patient initially declined surgery and was discharged on medical therapy. However, he was readmitted approximately 2 months later because of recurrent palpitations and dyspnea. After re-evaluation and thorough discussion, he consented to surgery. On November 3, 2025, he underwent a Bentall procedure (aortic valve and ascending aorta replacement with a 27-mm Edwards Magna Ease bioprosthetic valve and a 34-mm Dacron graft) combined with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG; saphenous vein graft to RCA). Intraoperative exploration confirmed a transverse chronic dissection at the right coronary sinus extending to involve the RCA ostium, classified as Neri type B (Figure 6). The RCA ostium was found to contain a previously implanted coronary stent. Given the findings of aortic valve leaflet retraction (effective height: approximately 1.0 cm) and the dissected sinus involving the coronary ostium, a valve-sparing (David) procedure was deemed unsuitable. Therefore, the decision was made to proceed with the Bentall procedure combined with CABG. The surgery was successful, and the patient recovered well, with postoperative echocardiography showing satisfactory cardiac function. He was discharged on a regimen including antiplatelet therapy, statins, beta-blockers, diuretics, and warfarin (for bioprosthetic valve anticoagulation), with instructions for close outpatient follow-up.

Intraoperative Exploration Revealed Neri Type B Dissection

Neri classification of Stanford type A aortic dissection involving the coronary arteries: (A) Type A: luminal dissection confined to the coronary artery ostium. (B) Type B: luminal dissection extending into the coronary artery with formation of a coronary false lumen. (C) Type C: sleeve-like dissection and rupture of the coronary intima. In this patient, intraoperative exploration revealed Neri type B dissection.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was followed up 1 month after surgery. He reported feeling well, with no reports of chest pain, chest tightness, or dyspnea. His surgical wound had healed satisfactorily, without signs of infection or complications.

Follow-up investigations included transthoracic echocardiography, which showed preserved left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 60.9%, no significant paravalvular leakage, and normal function of the bioprosthetic aortic valve. A 24-hour Holter monitor revealed normal sinus rhythm without significant arrhythmias. Chest CT demonstrated good postoperative anatomy, with the aortic graft in situ, no evidence of dissection recurrence, and no complications such as hematoma or pseudoaneurysm.

Overall, the patient recovered well from the combined Bentall and CABG procedure. He was advised to continue his current medication regimen, adhere to regular outpatient follow-up, and report any new symptoms promptly. Long-term monitoring will focus on valve function, graft integrity, and cardiovascular risk management.

Discussion

This report describes a rare case of acute inferior wall STEMI resulting from a localized dissection of the right aortic sinus, complicating a long-standing aortic root aneurysm. The case underscores diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in patients with aortic pathology who present with acute coronary syndrome.

Clinically, the patient's presentation was typical for acute myocardial infarction, prompting emergent CAG. However, difficulty in engaging the RCA ostium during PCI raised suspicion of an underlying anatomic abnormality or extrinsic compression. Subsequent imaging and catheter adjustment revealed a dissection-induced RCA occlusion, highlighting the need to consider atypical etiologies of coronary obstruction, particularly in individuals with known aortic root dilation.

Notably, the initial CT scan failed to demonstrate a classic aortic dissection, illustrating a key diagnostic pitfall. It was only through delayed, high-resolution, electrocardiogram-gated coronary CTA that multiple intimal tears within the right aortic sinus, extending into the RCA ostium, were clearly visualized. This emphasizes the limited sensitivity of standard aortic CTA for detecting localized sinus dissections and affirms the value of dedicated coronary CTA in equivocal or complex presentations.1

Pathophysiologically, this case likely represents direct extension of an aortic sinus dissection into the coronary ostium, leading to vessel occlusion and myocardial infarction—a mechanism distinct from classic type A aortic dissection with coronary involvement.2 Such scenarios necessitate a high index of suspicion and a multidisciplinary approach involving interventional cardiology, cardiac imaging, and cardiac surgery.

Despite successful PCI, the patient's underlying aortic root dilation and significant aortic regurgitation persisted, conferring a high risk of recurrent dissection, rupture, or sudden cardiac death. The initial refusal of surgery reflects common challenges in counseling high-risk patients, underscoring the importance of clear risk-benefit communication and shared decision-making. Ultimately, the patient underwent successful Bentall procedure with CABG, demonstrating that definitive surgical management remains critical in addressing both the acute coronary event and the underlying aortic pathology.3

Conclusions

Localized aortic sinus dissection can mimic acute STEMI and should be considered in patients with known aortic root disease, even when initial imaging appears unremarkable. A combination of clinical vigilance, timely multimodality imaging, and a collaborative team approach is essential for accurate diagnosis and comprehensive management.
