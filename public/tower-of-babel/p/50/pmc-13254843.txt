Resiquimod-loaded magnetic nanoparticles reprogram macrophages and induce oxidative cytotoxicity against triple-negative breast cancer cells
Authors: Kamonlatth Rodponthukwaji, Ladawan Khowawisetsut, Nathachit Limjunyawong, Sith Sathornsumetee, Chatchawan Srisawat, Numpon Insin, Tam Nguyen, Primana Punnakitikashem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of alternative cancer therapies remains a critical priority to overcome the significant adverse effects associated with conventional chemotherapy. Accordingly, innovative strategies are required to mitigate chemotherapy-induced toxicity. Among these, reprogramming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment into an immunostimulatory state has emerged as a promising approach; however, single-modality approaches often underperform. In this work, we engineered resiquimod (R848)-loaded carboxymethyl dextran-coated magnetic nanoparticles (MD-R848) to repolarize M2 macrophages toward an M1 phenotype and to exert direct cytotoxicity against triple-negative breast cancer cells. Nanoparticle physicochemical properties were characterized by dynamic light scattering, X-ray powder diffraction, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry, and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry. MD-R848 increased the expression of the M1 surface marker CD80 and reduced the expression of the M2 marker CD206 in macrophages, as determined by flow cytometry. This phenotypic shift was accompanied by increased secretion of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, together with upregulation of TNF and IL6 gene expression, supporting M1-like macrophage polarization. MD-R848 also decreased viability of MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells, likely via oxidative stress, as reactive oxygen species and malondialdehyde, a lipid peroxidation marker, increased by 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) fluorescence and lipid peroxidation assays. The elevated reactive oxygen species and malondialdehyde were attributable to the magnetic nanoparticle core. In a transwell co-culture, macrophage reprogramming combined with direct tumor cytotoxicity synergistically intensified cancer cell death. Thus, MD-R848 represents a dual-function nanoplatform that couples immunomodulation with oxidative cytotoxicity, providing a mechanistic basis for improved therapeutic outcomes in cancer treatment.

Introduction

Breast cancer accounts for 12.5% of all new cancers worldwide and is the most common malignancy in U.S. women, representing 30% of new diagnoses (Breastcancer.org, 2023). Breast cancer often leads to brain metastasis, occurring in approximately 10–16% of patients (Leone and Leone, 2015). Surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are standard; however, chemotherapy remains a cornerstone, and its efficacy is constrained by toxicity and limited bioavailability stemming from high-dose regimens (Lewis et al., 2025), off-target effects (Lin et al., 2019), and poor solubility (Tran et al., 2019). Drug-delivery systems are being developed to address these limitations (Kalepu and Nekkanti, 2015; Lorscheider et al., 2021).

Cancer immunotherapy is a promising alternative, as macrophages within the tumor microenvironment orchestrate immune responses in breast cancer (Duan and Luo, 2021). Their marked plasticity allows reversible adaptation to environmental cues, making them attractive therapeutic targets (Orecchioni et al., 2019). Within the tumor microenvironment, macrophages frequently adopt an immunosuppressive M2 state that promotes tumor progression and invasiveness by secreting cytokines (eg, IL-1ra, IL-10) (Qi et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018) and growth factors (eg, TGF-β1, EGF analogs) (Hwang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2018). Because of this plasticity, M2 cells can be reprogrammed toward an immunostimulatory M1 phenotype, whose proinflammatory cytokines (eg, TNF-α, IFN-γ) have anticancer activity (Belgiovine et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2018a). Accordingly, reprogramming macrophages to tumoricidal phenotypes is an appealing therapeutic strategy. Recent studies further support macrophage reprogramming as a strategy to remodel immune responses. For example, thymosin α1 was shown to reverse M2-like TAM polarization and enhance antitumor immunity, while macrophage-derived vesicles and immune signaling pathways have been shown to regulate macrophage-associated immune communication (Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023). Resiquimod (R848), a synthetic toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist, induces repolarization of tumor-associated macrophages from M2 to M1 and enhances antitumor immunity within the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, combining R848 with oxaliplatin, sorafenib, or squamocin has yielded synergistic tumor suppression (He et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024a).

Monotherapy often fails to achieve durable tumor control; therefore, platforms that combine macrophage polarization with complementary mechanisms may improve efficacy (Ren et al., 2022; Rodponthukwaji et al., 2025). Nanomaterials are widely used in nanomedicine as drug carriers (Zhao et al., 2021), diagnostic agents (Efremova et al., 2018), and theranostic agents (Poon et al., 2021). Recent nanomedicine studies increasingly emphasize multifunctional and tumor microenvironment-responsive platforms that combine therapeutic modalities, imaging guidance, and toxicity reduction. For instance, MnO₂-based nanoplatforms have been designed to integrate photodynamic, photothermal, and chemodynamic therapy, while carrier-free natural product nanoparticles have been reported to enhance antitumor efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity through combined synergy and attenuation strategies (Wang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2024b). Among these, iron oxide–based magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) are distinguished by their magnetic properties (Rahimi et al., 2010; Rahimi et al., 2009; Wadajkar et al., 2012). Recent studies also highlight the theranostic flexibility of magnetic nanoparticles, showing that structural engineering of magnetic nanomaterials can improve tumor imaging performance and enable targeted biomedical applications (Liang et al., 2026). MNPs serve as contrast agents and, importantly, function as therapeutics by inducing iron-dependent cell death (Chen and Hou, 2023; Oberdick et al., 2023). Upon cellular internalization of magnetic nanoparticles, the acidic endolysosomal within cancer cells leads to the degradation of MNPs into iron ions, which catalyze Fenton chemistry (Shen et al., 2018b) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Shubhra, 2023). These ROS drive lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death (Wang et al., 2023), highlighting the therapeutic potential of MNPs against cancer.

Accordingly, we developed resiquimod (R848)-loaded carboxymethyl dextran-coated magnetic nanoparticles (MD-R848) to simultaneously reprogram immunosuppressive macrophages and induce cytotoxicity in breast cancer cells. Combining macrophage reprogramming with iron-mediated oxidative cytotoxicity may enhance anticancer activity through complementary mechanisms. R848 was incorporated into magnetic nanoparticles to provide immunomodulatory activity, as R848 can promote an M1-skewed macrophage phenotype. In parallel, the iron oxide magnetic core can exert direct cytotoxic effects on cancer cells by inducing iron-mediated ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-related cell death. This dual-function design is consistent with recent nanomedicine strategies that combine direct tumor-cell killing with immune activation to enhance therapeutic efficacy. For example, nanoparticle-mediated regulated cell death, such as pyroptosis, has been shown to enhance direct tumor killing while stimulating antitumor immune responses (Wang et al., 2025a). Therefore, MD-R848 was designed to couple immune-mediated tumor suppression with direct nanoparticle-mediated cytotoxicity, providing a dual-action approach that may overcome the limited efficacy of single-modality treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials

Carboxymethyl-dextran sodium salt (CM-dextran), iron(II) chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2·4H2O), iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), ammonia solution (NH4OH, 28%–30%), DMSO, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA). Ferrostatin-1 and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) were also from Sigma–Aldrich. R848 (> 98%) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Escherichia coli were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. PBS (10×) was obtained from Vivantis Technologies Sdn Bhd (Darul Ehsan, Malaysia).

The Fc receptor blocking solution used was Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Antibodies included FITC anti-human CD80 (clone 2D10), PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human CD206 (MMR) (clone 15–2), FITC mouse IgG1, κ isotype control (clone MOPC-21), and PerCP/Cyanine5.5 mouse IgG1, κ isotype control (clone MOPC-21), all from BioLegend. Recombinant human interleukin-4 (rhIL-4), interleukin-13 (rhIL-13), and interferon-γ (rhIFN-γ) were obtained from ImmunoTools (Friesoythe, Germany). RPMI 1640 medium, DMEM, fetal bovine serum, penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL), and trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Transwell polyester filter inserts with 3 μm pores were obtained from SPL Life Sciences (Gyeonggi-do, Korea).

Synthesis of MD and MD-R848

Carboxymethyl dextran-coated magnetic nanoparticles (MD) were synthesized by coprecipitation as previously reported with minor modification (Naha et al., 2019). Briefly, FeCl2·4H2O and FeCl3·6H2O (1:2 M ratio) and dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water containing 10 mg of carboxymethyl dextran. The reaction mixture was maintained in an ice bath. Cold NH4OH was slowly added to initial mixture until the pH reaches 9. The mixture was heated to 80 °C under N2 for 1 h. Nanoparticles were collected, and unbound dextran was removed by dialysis using a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff for 48 h. The purified MD were filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter.

For the preparation of R848-loaded MD (MD-R848), R848 was dissolved in DMSO and added to the MD suspension at an initial feed ratio of 5 mg R848 per 3 mg MD. The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. Importantly, R848 was introduced after the synthesis of the magnetic nanoparticle core and therefore was not exposed to the 80 °C heating step used during MD preparation. Unloaded R848 was removed using an Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter with a 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff at 4,000 rpm for 10 min using a Sorvall Legend XT centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The purified MD-R848 nanoparticles were then collected and resuspended in sterile water.

To evaluate reproducibility, MD and MD-R848 were prepared in three independent synthesis batches under the same conditions. Hydrodynamic size, PDI, and zeta potential were measured for each batch, and the data were reported as mean ± SD.

Characterization

Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Crystal structure was analyzed by X-ray powder diffraction (D8 Discover; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (iS50; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Norristown, PA, USA) was used to confirm R848 loading. Iron release was measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (NexION 2000; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

For the determination of R848 loading, the amount of unloaded R848 in the filtrate was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 320 nm using ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry (Synergy HT; Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). R848 concentration was calculated from a standard calibration curve prepared using known concentrations of R848. R848 loading was further expressed as the amount of R848 loaded per milligram of MD nanoparticles. The amount of loaded R848 was calculated by subtracting the amount of unloaded R848 detected in the filtrate from the initial amount of R848 added. R848 loading was calculated using the following equation:

R848 loading(mg/mg) = Amount of loadedR848mgAmount ofMDnanoparticlesmg.

Encapsulation efficiency (%EE) was calculated using the following equation:

%EE = Amount of loadedR848Amount of addedR848x100

The in vitro release profile of R848 from MD-R848 was evaluated using a dialysis method. Briefly, MD-R848 suspension was placed into a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 kDa and immersed in PBS at pH 7.4. The release system was maintained at 37 °C with stirring for 72 h. At predetermined time points, an aliquot of the external release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain a constant release volume. The amount of released R848 in the collected medium was quantified by measuring absorbance at 320 nm using UV–Vis spectrophotometry with a Synergy HT multi-detection microplate reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). The cumulative release percentage was calculated relative to the total amount of R848 loaded in MD-R848.

The colloidal stability of MD-R848 in biological medium was evaluated by dispersing MD-R848 in serum-containing culture medium and incubating the suspension at 37 °C with shaking for 72 h. At predetermined time points, samples were collected, and the hydrodynamic size was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Stability was assessed based on changes in hydrodynamic size over time and expressed as percentage stability relative to the initial size at 0 h.

THP-1 macrophage differentiation and polarization

THP-1 monocytes were seeded in 24-well plates at 500,000 cells per well in complete RPMI 1640 containing 100 ng/mL PMA and allowed to adhere for 48 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh complete medium for a 24-h rest, yielding M0 macrophages. M2 and M1 macrophages were generated by culturing M0 with rhIL-4 and rhIL-13 (20 ng/mL each for 72 h) or with LPS and rhIFN-γ (20 ng/mL each for 24 h), respectively.

Cytotoxicity toward macrophages

THP-1-derived M0 were seeded in 96-well plates. After the 24-h rest, cells were treated with free R848 at concentrations of 3.75, 7.5, and 15 μg/mL, or with MD and MD-R848 at nanoparticle concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, and 125 μg Fe/mL for 24, 48, and 72 h. Viability was quantified with the CellTiter-Blue Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Fluorescence was read at 545/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc).

Flow cytometric analysis of surface markers

Macrophage repolarization was quantified by flow cytometry. THP-1-derived M2 macrophages were treated with LPS and rhIFN-γ (20 ng/mL) as positive control, free R848 at 15 μg/mL or with MD and MD-R848 at a nanoparticle concentration of 62.5 μg Fe/mL for 24 h. After nanoparticle incubation, M2 cells were washed with cold PBS to remove residual particles, detached with 2 mM EDTA for 5 min, and collected by centrifugation. Viability was assessed with the Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend). Fc receptors were blocked with Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were stained with FITC anti-human CD80 and PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human CD206 for 30 min on ice. Mouse IgG1, κ isotype control antibodies that were conjugated to matching fluorochromes were used. Stained cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS, 5% fetal bovine serum, 1 mM EDTA) and acquired on a BD FACSCelesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed in FlowJo v10 (BD Biosciences).

Cytokine assessment by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Cytokines secreted by nanoparticle-treated macrophages were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from BioLegend. THP-1-derived M2 macrophages were treated with LPS and rhIFN-γ (20 ng/mL) as positive control, free R848 at 15 μg/mL or with MD and MD-R848 at a nanoparticle concentration of 62.5 μg Fe/mL for 48 h. After treatment, culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove cell debris and residual particles. The levels of cytokines were then measured according to the manufacturer's instructions. IL-6 and TNF-α were selected as representative proinflammatory cytokines associated with M1-like macrophage polarization while TGF-β was selected as a representative anti-inflammatory cytokine associated with M2-like macrophage polarization.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

THP-1-derived M2 macrophages were treated with LPS and rhIFN-γ (20 ng/mL) as positive control, free R848 at 15 μg/mL or with MD and MD-R848 at a nanoparticle concentration of 62.5 μg Fe/mL for 24 h. Then, total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen™, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting cDNA was used as a template for quantitative PCR amplification using KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Kits (KAPA Biosystems, Boston, MA, USA). Quantitative PCR was performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to ACTB as the housekeeping reference gene and analyzed using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Cytotoxicity toward MDA-MB-231 cells

MDA-MB-231 viability was measured with the CellTiter-Blue Cell Viability Assay (Promega) per the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5,000 cells per well, allowed to adhere overnight, and then exposed to free R848 at concentrations of 3.75, 7.5, and 15 μg/mL, or with MD and MD-R848 at nanoparticle concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, and 125 μg Fe/mL for 24, 48, and 72 h. Fluorescence was recorded at 545/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc).

Intracellular ROS quantification

Intracellular ROS was quantified with H2DCFDA. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h before nanoparticle exposure. After 24 and 48 h of exposure, media were removed and cells were washed with serum-free medium. Cells were incubated with 20 μM H2DCFDA in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence was measured at 485/530 nm (excitation/emission). H2O2 1 mM served as a positive control.

Ferroptosis assessment by viability assay

Ferroptosis was assessed indirectly by CellTiter-Blue Cell Viability Assay in the presence of ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at 5,000 cells per well, allowed to adhere, and then co-treated with magnetic nanocomposites and ferrostatin-1 for 24, 48, and 72 h. Fluorescence was read at 545/590 nm (excitation/emission) on a Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments).

MDA quantification

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated with magnetic nanocomposites for 24 h. MDA content was measured using the Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Sigma–Aldrich) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Transwell co-culture system

M2 macrophages and MDA-MB-231 cells were co-cultured in transwells. THP-1 monocytes were seeded in the lower chamber at 500,000 cells per well, differentiated, and polarized to M2. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in the inserts at 5,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Both cell types were treated with MD-R848 as specified by each experimental condition. Cancer-cell viability was measured with CellTiter-Blue Cell Viability Assay, and fluorescence was read on a Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean with standard deviation. Statistical comparisons used 1- or 2-way analysis of variance in GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Result and discussion

Characterization of nanomaterials

MD and MD-R848 were successfully synthesized. Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS), and results are summarized in Table 2. Unloaded MD had a hydrodynamic diameter of 148.55 ± 22.21 nm and a zeta potential of −47.94 ± 1.23 mV, attributable to carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on dextran (Vasić et al., 2020). After R848 loading, the hydrodynamic diameter significantly increased to 183.92 ± 8.20 nm compared with unloaded MD, indicating successful incorporation of R848 into the nanoparticle system. The zeta potential also significantly shifted toward a less negative value, from −47.94 ± 1.23 mV to −37.94 ± 5.21 mV, suggesting partial masking of the negatively charged surface after R848 loading. This change is consistent with the masking effect previously observed in dextran-coated magnetic nanoparticles loaded with protocatechuic acid (Anghelache et al., 2021). Nevertheless, both MD and MD-R848 exhibited relatively narrow size distributions, as indicated by their low PDI values of 0.29 ± 0.05 and 0.33 ± 0.06, respectively. Encapsulation of R848 was quantified by ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry; the loading was 0.30 ± 0.13 mg R848 per mg of nanoparticles, corresponding to an encapsulation efficiency of approximately 20.02 ± 11.65%. As shown in Fig. 1B, ultraviolet-visible spectra of MD-R848 displayed absorption maxima at approximately 305 nm and 320 nm, consistent with R848 in aqueous solution (Sorgi et al., 2024). These results confirm the successful incorporation of R848 into the nanoparticle formulation.

Crystalline phases of the magnetic cores were examined by X-ray powder diffraction. As shown in Fig. 1C, uncoated magnetic nanoparticles exhibited mixed-phase diffraction pattern. Hematite-associated reflections were observed at 2θ values of 23.0°, 32.8°, and 40.5°, corresponding to the (011), (112), and (012) crystalline planes, respectively, in accordance with JCPDS 01–079-0007 (Ahmad et al., 2025; Bouafia et al., 2023). Magnetite was identified as the predominant phase, with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 30.4°, 35.7°, 43.6°, 57.3°, and 62.9°, corresponding to the (022), (113), (004), (115), and (044) crystalline planes, respectively, consistent with JCPDS 19–0629 (He et al., 2015; Rodponthukwaji et al., 2021). Upon coating with CM-dextran, the diffraction peaks corresponding to hematite disappeared, yielding a magnetite-only pattern at 2θ values at 30.4°, 35.7°, 43.2°, 53.7°, 57.4°, and 62.9°, corresponding to the (022), (113), (004), (224), (115), and (044) crystalline planes, respectively. This transformation is likely attributed to the adsorption of carboxylate and hydroxyl functional groups onto the nanoparticle surface, which may help stabilize the magnetite phase. The MD-R848 diffraction pattern retained the characteristic magnetite peaks, indicating that R848 loading did not alter the crystalline structure of the magnetic nanoparticle core.

The stability of MD-R848 in biological medium was further assessed by monitoring changes in hydrodynamic size after incubation in serum-containing medium at 37 °C for 72 h under shaking conditions. As shown in Fig. 1D, MD-R848 maintained a relatively constant hydrodynamic size throughout the incubation period, with no marked decrease in percentage stability compared with the initial value at 0 h. These results indicate that MD-R848 exhibits good serum stability and minimal aggregation behavior under biologically relevant conditions.

As magnetic nanoparticles were employed as therapeutic carriers, drug release profile was also evaluated under physiological conditions in PBS at pH 7.4 and 37 °C for 72 h. MD-R848 displayed a burst release during the first 12 h, liberating 35.00% ± 1.10% of R848, followed by the sustained release manner for the remaining time (Fig. 1E). This biphasic release profile suggests that a fraction of R848 was rapidly released from the nanoparticle surface, whereas the remaining R848 was released more gradually from the nanoparticle matrix. These findings support the potential of MD-R848 as a drug-delivery platform capable of providing both early drug availability and sustained release under physiological conditions.

Moreover, the ability of the magnetic nanoparticles in transforming into iron ions in acidic environment was also investigated by using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry. We examined Fe-ion liberation under acidic conditions over 72 h. According to the result in Fig. 1F, MD-R848 released Fe ions faster than MNP, a difference possibly related to hematite impurities reported previously (Gu et al., 2019). However, this result suggested that our constructed nanosystem could show the potential in releasing Fe ions which would subsequently be beneficial in causing ROS generation.

Surface marker analysis from activated macrophages

The capacity of the synthesized MD-R848 to polarize macrophages was assessed via flow cytometry. Fig. 3A illustrates the gating strategy employed to identify single, viable cells, followed by the classification of M1 (CD80+) and M2 (CD206+) macrophage subsets. Surface marker expression analysis, shown in Fig. 3B, reveals a representative histogram indicating increased expression of CD80, an M1-associated marker, upon treatment with MD-R848. In contrast, expression of CD206, an M2-associated marker, was reduced following treatment with MD-R848 at a concentration of 62.5 μg Fe/mL for 24 h. Quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), presented in Fig. 3C, further confirms the upregulation of CD80, comparable to the response elicited by the positive control (LPS&IFN-γ). Concurrently, CD206 expression was downregulated, supporting the role of MD-R848 in promoting M2-to-M1 repolarization. Cytokine release from polarized macrophages

We quantified cytokines released by nanoparticle-treated macrophages to validate macrophage polarization at the protein level. THP-1-derived M2 macrophages were treated with free R848 at 15 μg/mL or with MD and MD-R848 at a nanoparticle concentration of 62.5 μg Fe/mL for 48 h. Untreated M2 macrophages served as the negative control, free R848 served as the immunostimulatory control, and unloaded MD served as the nanoparticle carrier control. IL-6 and TNF-α were measured as representative M1-associated proinflammatory cytokines, while TGF-β was measured as a representative M2-associated anti-inflammatory cytokine. As shown in Fig. 3D, quantitative ELISA analysis demonstrated that MD-R848 markedly increased IL-6 secretion to 9697.25 ± 457.91 pg/mL and TNF-α secretion to 16,218.39 ± 1430.65 pg/mL. In contrast, untreated M2 macrophages secreted only 16.73 ± 0.58 pg/mL IL-6 and 41.61 ± 1.77 pg/mL TNF-α, while unloaded MD induced minimal cytokine secretion, with 22.96 ± 1.65 pg/mL IL-6 and 46.64 ± 0.40 pg/mL TNF-α. Free R848 also significantly increased IL-6 and TNF-α secretion, confirming the immunostimulatory activity of R848. By contrast, unloaded MD did not significantly elevate these cytokines, indicating that the proinflammatory cytokine response was mainly driven by R848 loading rather than the MD carrier itself. In contrast, TGF-β secretion showed no significant difference among the treatment groups. These findings suggest that MD-R848 promoted an M1-skewed proinflammatory cytokine profile without markedly altering TGF-β secretion under the tested conditions. The absence of a significant change in TGF-β may reflect partial macrophage repolarization or the presence of mixed M1/M2-like macrophage populations rather than complete conversion to a purely M1 phenotype. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that macrophage activation can involve overlapping M1- and M2-associated features. For example, R848-containing nanoparticle systems have been reported to partially upregulate M1 markers while residual M2-like populations remain detectable by flow cytometry (Rodponthukwaji et al., 2025). Similarly, melanoma-derived exosomes have been shown to induce both M1- and M2-associated cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β, supporting the concept that macrophage polarization occurs along a functional spectrum rather than as a strict binary M1/M2 state (Bardi et al., 2018).

By contrast, soluble R848 can elicit high cytokine levels and risk a cytokine storm with deleterious cellular effects (Karki et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). Overall, the cytokine profiles align with the flow-cytometric data and support MD-R848 as an effective macrophage-reprogramming platform that yields an antitumor phenotype.

Gene expression analysis of treated macrophages

To further strengthen the validation of macrophage repolarization after treatment for 24 h, we evaluated the mRNA expression of macrophage polarization-associated genes, including IL6 and TNF, by qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 3E, free R848 and MD-R848 significantly upregulated IL6 and TNF expression, whereas unloaded MD did not markedly induce these proinflammatory genes. These findings suggest that the transcriptional activation of proinflammatory mediators was primarily driven by the presence of R848. The qRT-PCR results are consistent with the ELISA data showing increased IL-6 and TNF-α secretion and further support the flow cytometry results demonstrating increased CD80 expression and reduced CD206 expression after MD-R848 treatment.

Taken together, the flow cytometry, ELISA, and qRT-PCR results provide complementary evidence that MD-R848 promotes an M1-skewed macrophage phenotype. This phenotype was characterized by increased CD80 expression, reduced CD206 expression, and enhanced TNF-α/IL-6 production at both transcriptional and protein levels. Nevertheless, macrophage polarization is a dynamic and heterogeneous process rather than a strict binary transition between M1 and M2 states. Therefore, future studies using a broader panel of macrophage polarization markers would provide deeper insight into the full phenotypic profile induced by MD-R848.

Although the macrophage-reprogramming effect of MD-R848 is consistent with the known immunostimulatory activity of R848 as a TLR7/8 agonist, upstream TLR/NF-κB pathway activation was not directly confirmed in the present study. The increased CD80 expression, reduced CD206 expression, elevated TNF-α and IL-6 secretion, and upregulated TNF and IL6 mRNA expression collectively support an R848-associated M1-skewed macrophage response. However, these findings represent downstream phenotypic and functional readouts rather than direct evidence of TLR7/8 or NF-κB activation. Therefore, future studies evaluating pathway markers such as NF-κB p65 phosphorylation or nuclear translocation, IκBα degradation, MyD88 signaling, or TLR7/8 inhibition would be required to confirm the specific upstream signaling mechanism involved in MD-R848-mediated macrophage repolarization.

Cytotoxicity toward cancer cells

To evaluate the therapeutic action of MNPs, we assessed the cytotoxicity of MD and MD-R848 against MDA-MB-231 cells at 24, 48, and 72 h using nanoparticle concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, and 125 μg Fe/mL. To enable direct comparison with the drug-only condition, free R848 was also tested at 3.75, 7.5, and 15 μg/mL. As shown in Fig. 4, both nanoparticle formulations decreased MDA-MB-231 cell viability in a dose- and time-dependent manner, implicating the magnetic nanoparticle core in tumor cell death. In contrast, free R848 showed no significant cytotoxicity under the tested conditions. The differential cytotoxic effect observed in MDA-MB-231 tumor cells and THP-1-derived macrophages, may be partly explained by differences in intracellular iron-handling capacity between these cell types. Macrophages are central regulators of iron metabolism and possess polarization-dependent mechanisms to control intracellular iron homeostasis. M2-like macrophages are generally associated with an iron-export phenotype, whereas M1-like macrophages are characterized by iron retention and ferritin-mediated iron storage. Therefore, after internalization and partial lysosomal degradation of iron oxide-based nanoparticles, macrophages may buffer released iron through coordinated iron storage, sequestration, or export mechanisms, thereby reducing iron-mediated cytotoxicity (DeRosa and Leftin, 2021). In contrast, tumor cells may have less efficient iron-regulatory adaptation and greater susceptibility to iron-mediated ROS generation and lipid peroxidation. However, because iron-regulatory markers were not directly assessed in this study, this explanation remains a possible mechanism underlying the different cytocompatibility profiles observed between macrophages and cancer cells. R848 loading further enhanced cytotoxicity; with MD-R848, viability fell to less than 50% at 48 and 72 h. Because MD and MD-R848 reduced tumor-cell viability, we hypothesized that iron-mediated oxidative stress contributed to cytotoxicity. After cellular uptake, acidic endolysosomal conditions can dissolve the iron oxide core to Fe ions, which catalyze ROS generation via Fenton chemistry and promote downstream lipid peroxidation. At 62.5 μg Fe/mL, both MD and MD-R848 increased intracellular ROS in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 5A–B). Co-treatment with the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC) reduced the signal, confirming nanoparticle-driven ROS accumulation. Because MD-R848 can induce intracellular ROS, these species might mediate cell death. ROS have been associated with lipid peroxidation, which can give rise to ferroptosis (Su et al., 2019). We then assessed MDA-MB-231 viability after 24, 48, and 72 h of exposure to 62.5 μg Fe/mL MD-R848, with or without the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1. As shown in Fig. 5C, MD-R848 significantly decreased viability in a time-dependent manner, whereas co-treatment with ferrostatin-1 preserved viability at approximately 100%, despite MD-R848 exposure. These results implicate ferroptosis as a principal death pathway.

To further test ferroptosis involvement, we quantified malondialdehyde (MDA) content. R848 alone did not increase MDA, whereas MD and MD-R848 significantly raised levels to 37.82 ± 10.32 nmol/mg protein and 34.39 ± 6.93 nmol/mg protein, respectively (Fig. 5D). These data indicate that the magnetic nanoparticle core drives lipid peroxidation, linking iron-mediated ROS to ferroptotic cell death.

Combined effects of macrophage repolarization and nanoparticle-mediated cytotoxicity

MD-R848 not only repolarizes macrophages toward a tumoricidal state but also kills cancer cells by elevating ROS and lipid peroxides, consistent with ferroptosis. To evaluate the combined impact, we used a transwell co-culture system. In this study, the relative contributions of immune-mediated and direct cytotoxic effects were distinguished using separate experimental settings. The immune-mediated effect was evaluated through macrophage phenotype analysis, including CD80/CD206 expression, cytokine secretion, and TNF/IL6 mRNA expression after MD-R848 treatment. The direct cytotoxic effect was assessed in MDA-MB-231 monoculture, where MD and MD-R848 reduced cancer cell viability and induced ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation. The transwell co-culture model was then used to examine whether macrophage reprogramming and direct nanoparticle-mediated cytotoxicity could act together to enhance cancer cell killing.

As shown in Fig. 6, MDA-MB-231 viability was measured after co-incubation with activated M2 for 24 h. Under indirect co-culture conditions, neither conditioned medium from M1 macrophages nor MD-R848 alone produced significant cancer cell death. Although direct MD-R848 treatment modestly reduced viability, this effect was not reproduced in the transwell system. The discrepancy likely reflects pro-tumorigenic cytokines released by M2 macrophages, which support cancer-cell proliferation and survival (Mazan and Marusiak, 2024; Zhang et al., 2012). By contrast, combining macrophage repolarization with direct nanoparticle cytotoxicity significantly reduced viability to 68.68% ± 18.06%, indicating that the two mechanisms contributed cooperatively under co-culture conditions. The cooperative reduction in cancer-cell viability may be partly attributed to TNF-α released from MD-R848-repolarized macrophages. TNF-α can promote tumor-cell death or sensitize cancer cells to additional stress, and recent evidence suggests that M1 macrophage-derived TNF-α can enhance ferroptosis-associated cancer-cell death through TNFR1-associated signaling (Zhang et al., 2026). Therefore, TNF-α secreted by MD-R848-treated macrophages may increase the susceptibility of MDA-MB-231 cells to magnetic nanoparticle-induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. Conclusions

In this study, R848-loaded carboxymethyl dextran-coated magnetic nanoparticles (MD-R848) were developed as a multifunctional nanoplatform with dual in vitro activity. MD-R848 promoted an M1-skewed macrophage phenotype and induced cancer cell death associated with ROS accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-related mechanisms. These findings suggest that MD-R848 can integrate macrophage immunomodulation with nanoparticle-mediated oxidative cytotoxicity in vitro. However, the current study is limited to cell-based models, and further in vivo investigations are required to validate its antitumor efficacy, biodistribution, immune-related effects, and systemic safety. Therefore, MD-R848 provides a mechanistic foundation for further development as a potential cancer immunomodulatory nanoplatform.
