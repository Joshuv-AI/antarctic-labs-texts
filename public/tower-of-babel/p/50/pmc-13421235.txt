Design, Conduct, and Analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials of Chinese Herbal Medicine
Authors: Ling Li, Xiaochao Luo, Yanmei Liu, Qigao Liang, Kelin Deng, Liqin Nie, Yuhu Ma, Danial Aminaei, Shahab Sayfi, Daniel Xie, Amin Meghdadi, Snigdho Talukder, Jiali Liu, Hunong Xiang, Jiayidaer Huan, Minghong Yao, Arthur Yin Fan, Yemeng Chen, Xin Sun, Gordon H. Guyatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow are randomized clinical trials (RCTs) of Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) currently designed, conducted, and analyzed, and are there any differences between RCTs published in English and Chinese?FindingsThis cross-sectional study of 400 CHM RCTs published from 1992 to 2024 identified critical methodological limitations, including unclear study hypothesis, inadequate protocol, failure to register the trial, inadequate allocation concealment, lack of a specified primary outcome and sample size estimation, suboptimal handling of missing participant data, and unsatisfactory conduct of sensitivity analysis or subgroup analysis. The design, conduct, and analysis of CHM RCTs improved over time, and were superior for RCTs published in English-language journals and in English-language journals with higher impact.MeaningThese findings highlight the urgent need for government authorities, universities, and research institutions to take the initiative in improving the planning and conduct of CHM RCTs, as well as for clinicians and methodologists to collaborate in developing standardized methodological guidelines for such RCTs.

Introduction

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are widely considered the optimal design for evaluating the benefits and harms of interventions, including Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), a commonly used therapeutic modality in China.1,2,3,4 However, methodologic limitations in the design, conduct, and analysis of CHM RCTs may undermine their validity, potentially providing misleading evidence that clinicians use in assisting patients in their decision-making.5,6,7

Although researchers have conducted thousands of CHM RCTs, the full picture of their design, conduct, and analysis remains largely unknown.8,9 A 1999 analysis3 identified a limited number of methodological issues (eg, randomization and blinding) in CHM RCTs published in Chinese. However, other important methodological issues, including allocation concealment, sample size estimation, missing participant data (MPD), rationale for using CHM, use of syndrome differentiation, and whether there is prior pharmacological, clinical, or toxicological evidence to support the trial design, remain uncertain.10,11,12 Most CHM RCTs are published in Chinese, and whether there are differences between these RCTs and those published in English remains uninvestigated, as does whether the planning, conduct, and analysis has improved over time.13,14 Therefore, to address these questions, we investigated the design, conduct, and analysis of RCTs of CHM, examining whether these methodological characteristics differed between RCTs published in English and Chinese, between higher-impact and lower-impact English journals, and whether such RCTs have improved over time.

Methods

Overall Study Design

We conducted a cross-sectional study of RCTs of CHM published in English and Chinese. As the unit of analysis consisted of published studies that did not directly use the clinical information of participants, ethical approval was exempted according to the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Participants. We registered this study online at Open Science Framework registries.15 This study followed guidelines for reporting meta-epidemiological methodology research.16 The definitions of terms in this study are shown in eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

Eligibility Criteria

We included RCTs that met all the following criteria: (1) was a parallel RCT enrolling humans; (2) assessed the benefits of a CHM intervention, including Chinese medicine prescription and Chinese patent medicine; (3) was published in journals covered in the journal citation reports (JCR; defined by Web of Science) or Chinese core journals (selected by Peking university); (4) was reported in full; and (5) was published in English or Chinese. We excluded RCTs that were (1) pilot, feasibility, or exploratory trials; (2) included a total sample size of less than 100; or (3) was a subset or secondary analysis of a RCT.

Literature Search

We searched English and Chinese databases from the inception of each database to April 2024. The English databases included PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL); the Chinese databases included Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database (VIP), Wanfang database, and Chinese biomedical literature service system (SinoMed). We combined subject terms and free texts to search for potentially eligible studies. Two experienced investigators (L.L. and X.L.) developed the search strategy with reference to previous related studies17,18,19 (eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1).

Study Process

Teams of paired investigators trained in health research methods used pilot-tested standardized forms, together with detailed written instructions, to independently screen title and/or abstracts and full texts for eligibility and abstract data from eligible RCTs. Reviewers resolved discrepancies through discussion or, if needed, adjudication by a third researcher (L.L. or X.L.).

Sample Size and Random Sampling

We estimated the sample size on the basis of the proportion of RCTs with inadequate allocation concealment, as allocation concealment is a key feature of reliable RCTs, which directly affected study outcomes.10,11 According to our pilot study of a random sample of 100 RCTs, the proportion with inadequate allocation concealment proved 48% for RCTs published in English and 66% for RCTs published in Chinese (eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1). Using a 2-sided α = .05 and 90% power to test our hypothesis that English language RCTs had a lower proportion of inadequate concealment than Chinese language RCTs, we calculated a required sample of 154 RCTs published in English and 154 in Chinese. To ensure adequate precision, we expanded the sample to include 400 RCTs: 200 published in Chinese and 200 published in English.

Of the RCTs published in English, we also randomly sampled those published in higher-impact (ie, JCR quartile [Q] 1 and Q2 journals) and lower-impact English journals (ie, JCR Q3 and Q4 journals), 50 RCTs from each of the Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 categories. We randomly sampled 1000 reports at each iteration, screened these citations for eligibility, and repeated the process until we acquired a total of 400 RCTs with the desired distribution.

Data Collection

We developed a data extraction form to collect information from eligible CHM RCTs. We then piloted the data extraction form by collecting data from 20 RCTs, and after refining the forms, conducted a second pilot of 80 RCTs. Using the revised data extraction form, we abstracted 4 categories of information from the included RCTs. First, we collected general characteristics, including publication year, journal type, study hypothesis (superiority, noninferiority, equivalence, or not specified), countries involved, number of study sites, area of diseases, type of control, cointerventions or background treatments, total number of participants randomized, length of follow-up, trial registration, protocol available, statistical analysis plan available, and funding source. Second, we collected general methodological characteristics, including methods of randomization (simple randomization, blocked randomization, stratified randomization, stratified blocked randomization, adaptive randomization, or not reported), generation of allocation sequences, allocation concealment, blinding (blinding of patients, health care practitioners, and outcome assessors), risk of bias related to blinding, primary outcome clearly specified, category of the primary outcome, patient-important primary outcomes, composite end point as primary outcome, patient-reported outcome as primary outcome, length of follow-up for the primary outcome, sample size estimation conducted, approaches of the investigators used to collect the data, independent data monitoring committee developed, adequate or inadequate generation of allocation sequences, allocation concealment, and blinding and risk of bias related to blinding defined by ROBUST-RCT tool at 4 levels (ie, definitely yes, probably yes, probably no, and definitely no; adequate included definitely yes and probably yes; inadequate included probably no and definitely no) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).20 Third, we abstracted characteristics of tested CHM, including number of the tested CHMs, type of tested CHMs (nonapproved CHM only [Chinese medicine prescriptions] or approved CHM [Chinese patent medicine, approved for marketing by the National Medical Products Administration of China]), reason for prescribing CHM, whether there is prior evidence (clinical, pharmacological, or toxicological) to support the effects of the tested CHM (reported by the authors), traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) syndrome differentiation diagnosis specified, approaches used to make the TCM diagnosis, type of CHM prescriptions, number of herbs included in the tested CHM with fixed prescription, route of administration of CHM, CHM intervention tailoring and modifications stated, and duration of CHM treatment. Fourth, we obtained information on statistical analysis of the primary outcome, including data type of primary outcome, significant effect of the selected primary outcome, MPD reported, proportion of patients with MPD reported, analytical methods used to deal with MPD in the primary analysis, analysis principle used for primary outcome, statistical methods, method used to adjust for multiple comparisons, interim analysis conducted, adjustment of covariates in the primary analysis, sensitivity analysis conducted, subgroup analysis conducted, and detailed information regarding the conduct of adjustment of covariates, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses for the primary outcome.

We selected a primary outcome for each RCT according to the following hierarchy: if the RCT prespecified a primary outcome, we selected it as the primary outcome. If the RCT prespecified more than 1 primary outcome, we selected the first one reported in the methods. If the RCT did not specify a primary outcome, we selected the outcome used for sample size calculation. Finally, if the RCT did not specify a primary outcome and there was no sample size calculation reported, we selected the first reported outcome in the results.

Statistical Analysis

We performed descriptive analyses of general and methodological characteristics of included RCTs. For all descriptive analyses, we used frequencies and percentages for dichotomous variables and mean and SD or median and range for continuous variables. To compare characteristics of included RCTs published in different journals (ie, Chinese vs English; higher-impact vs lower-impact journals), we used χ2 or Fisher exact test for dichotomous variables and t test for continuous variables when the distribution proved normal or Mann-Whitney U test when it did not. All tests were 2-sided, with P ≤ .05 considered statistically significant. To explore whether the design, conduct, and analysis improved over time, we performed an analysis between RCTs published before and after the median publication year of the included RCTs. Data analysis was performed with R statistical software version 4.3.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

We retrieved 80 897 records in English and Chinese. After removing duplicate records and English records not published in JCR included journals, 8000 records were randomly selected for screening. After screening the titles and abstracts, we assessed the full texts of 550 records, leading to 412 candidate RCTs. From these, we randomly selected 400 RCTs, including 200 English and 200 Chinese RCTs (eFigure in Supplement 1). A list of the 400 included RCTs is provided in eAppendix 3 in Supplement 1.

General Characteristics of CHM RCTs

Table 1 shows the general characteristics of the included CHM RCTs. These RCTs enrolled 100 to 4870 patients. Of the 400 RCTs, approximately one-half were published in the journals of traditional, complementary, and alternative medicine, and were government funded. Most RCTs (370 RCTs [92.5%]) did not specify the study hypothesis, were conducted in China only, and had no more than 3 months of follow-up. Half were single-center studies primarily focused on the field of internal medical. No treatment or waiting list was the most common control, followed by Western drugs and placebo. Less than one-third of RCTs (102 RCTs [30.6%]) were registered, and very seldom did they make the protocols (15 RCTs [3.8%]) and statistical analysis plans (4 RCTs [1%]) available.

Methodological Characteristics of CHM RCTs

Table 2 shows the methodological characteristics of included CHM RCTs. More than two-fifths did not mention randomization methods, with simple randomization being the most frequently used method. Approximately two-fifths (159 RCTs [39.8%]) reported inadequate methods of allocation sequences generation, and three-fifths (242 RCTs [60.2%]) described inadequate methods of allocation concealment. For the primary outcome, we identified low risk of bias for blinding participants in 253 RCTs (63.2%), blinding health care practitioners in 194 RCTs (48.5%), and blinding of outcome assessors in 201 RCTs (50.2%).

Of the 400 RCTs, one-third (138 RCTs [34.5%]) explicitly specified a primary outcome. The most common category of primary outcome was symptoms, quality of life, and functional status, followed by surrogate outcomes, which were most commonly mortality and/or survival. More than one-half selected a patient-reported outcome as the primary outcome. The length of follow-up for the primary outcome was most often 1 week to 1 month, followed by 1 to 3 months and 1 week or less. A total of 115 RCTs (28.8%) performed a sample size estimation, most of which based their estimation on the primary outcome (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Most RCTs (365 RCTs [91.2%]) did not report approaches to data collection, and very few (10 RCTs [2.5%]) had an independent data monitoring committee. Compared with CHM RCTs published in Chinese, those published in English were more likely to provide adequate methods of allocation sequences generation and allocation concealment, blind health care practitioners and outcome assessors, clearly specify primary outcome, conduct sample size estimation, and establish an independent data monitoring committee (Table 2).

Characteristics of Tested CHM

Table 3 shows the tested CHM characteristics of the included RCTs. Most tested 1 CHM and used a fixed prescription; 30% contained 8 to 12 herbs. Over one-half tested CHMs that were not approved for marketing by the National Medical Products Administration of China. More than two-thirds (254 RCTs [73.5%]) stated a reason for prescribing CHM, the most commonly mentioned of which was the limited or no effect of Western medicine (215 RCTs [53.8%]), followed by frequent adverse effects of Western medicine (80 RCTs [20.0%]). Over one-half did not mention whether there was prior clinical (279 RCTs [69.8%]), pharmacological (201 RCTs [50.2%]), or toxicological (388 RCTs [97.0%]) evidence to support the effects of the tested CHM. Approximately one-third (146 RCTs [36.5%]) specified the prescription of CHM according to TCM syndrome differentiation diagnosis; almost all failed to mention the approaches used to make this diagnosis. Oral administration was the main route of CHM. Very few stated that CHM intervention was tailored and modified during the treatment period. CHM treatment usually lasted from less than 1 week to 3 months. Compared with CHM RCTs published in Chinese, those published in English were more likely to test approved CHM (ie, Chinese patent medicine), provide prior clinical and pharmacological evidence as a rationale for testing the particular CHM, not mention TCM syndrome differentiation diagnosis, and use fixed prescriptions for CHM (Table 3).

Statistical Analysis of the Primary Outcome

Table 4 shows the statistical analysis characteristics of the primary outcome of included CHM RCTs. The primary outcomes were approximately half binary and 40% continuous; eTable 4 in Supplement 1 presents the detailed effect measures for the primary outcome. Half reported statistical significance at the P ≤ .05 or .01 threshold but did not mention the exact P value; a third reported a statistically significant primary outcome with exact P value, and 80 (20.0%) reported statistical significance at P > .05. Half made no explicit statement whether MPD occurred. Of those that did, most reported less than 10% missing data; very few reported no missing data. Most RCTs with missing data conducted only a complete case analysis (70 RCTs [77.8%] for dichotomous outcomes and 79 RCTs [84.0%] for continuous outcomes).

Methodological Characteristics of CHM RCTs Published in Different JCR Quartile Journals

Compared with CHM RCTs published in lower-impact journals, CHM RCTs published in higher-impact journals were more likely to describe adequate methods of random sequence generation and adequate allocation concealment; blind patients, health care practitioners, and outcome assessors; and clearly specify primary outcomes; and were less likely to select patient-reported outcomes as the primary outcome. Only CHM RCTs published in higher-impact journals had an independent data monitoring committee. RCTs published in higher-impact journals were more likely to conduct sample size estimation, specify prior clinical evidence, report MPD explicitly, conduct sensitivity analysis, and perform subgroup analysis (eTable 8 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of CHM RCTs published in English and Chinese, the methodological rigor of these RCTs—in design, conduct, and analysis—generally was poor. Studies frequently failed to state the hypothesis, provide a protocol, register their trial, conduct adequate allocation concealment, specify a primary outcome or sample size estimation, optimally handle missing data, and perform sensitivity analysis or subgroup analysis. The design, conduct, and analysis of CHM RCTs improved over time and was superior in English-language journals, particularly higher-impact English-language journals.

Previous studies12,13,14,21,22 have addressed methodological characteristics of CHM RCTs. One study21 included 167 CHM RCTs from 11 Cochrane systematic reviews published before July 2005 and found that the overall reporting quality of CHM RCTs was poor. Rather than reporting quality, our study focused on the rigor of RCT conduct. The second study13 included 3159 CHM RCTs from systematic reviews published up to 2009 to assess the risk bias of randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding and found that only 12% of RCTs used adequate randomization methods, 7% used adequate allocation concealment, and 19% used adequate blinding. The third study14 also assessed the risk of bias of Chinese-language CHM RCTs included in CHM systematic reviews from 2021 to 2022 using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool and found that of 2060 RCTs, the majority lacked sufficient information in several areas, resulting in an unclear risk of bias, such as 93.4% of RCTs with unclear risk of allocation concealment. Compared with these 2 studies, our survey included a representative sample of CHM trials published in both Chinese and English journals, and found that these methodological characteristics (eg, 39.8% adequate generation of allocation sequence) have improved, although not to a satisfactory level.

The fourth study22 identified 1096 registered RCTs, including 290 CHM RCTs published before July 2012, and found that registration was associated with low risk of bias (eg, allocation sequence generation and allocation concealment). Consistent with these findings, our results showed that that CHM RCTs published in English were more likely to be registered and demonstrated superior methodological characteristics than those published in Chinese. The fifth study12 searched systematic reviews on CHM treatment published between January 2011 and July 2021, including 1338 CHM RCTs, of which 99.3% were in Chinese, and found that only about 2% had low risk of bias of allocation concealment, and 1.0% used ITT analysis. To ensure a balanced and representative sample, our survey included CHM RCTs from both Chinese and English journals, and our findings showed a significant improvement in the design, conduct, and analysis of CHM RCTs improved over time, with trials published in English-language journals demonstrating superior methodological characteristics.

A positive finding from our study is that the methodological details in CHM trials have improved over time. Furthermore, these details were more rigorously documented in higher-impact English journals compared with lower-impact journals, particularly for fundamental RCT elements such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, and sample size estimation. This trend reflects a gradual enhancement in the clinical research capabilities of TCM investigators. Following the introduction of evidence-based medicine in China, governmental initiatives actively promoted training in clinical research methodologies, which likely contributed to this progress.23,24,25 English-language journals, especially high-impact journals, generally maintain more rigorous editorial oversight and use rigorous peer review, leading to stricter quality control for published RCTs. Consequently, higher-quality trials are more likely to be submitted to such journals,26,27 which could explain why the methodological details were better in English-language journals, particularly those with higher impact.

However, CHM RCTs are still less likely to be published in top journals and are less widely accepted, largely owing to suboptimal study quality and inadequate supporting evidence elucidating CHM’s mechanisms of action.28,29 Given that CHM is often used clinically before full scientific validation—and is likely continue to be applied where officially recognized—there remains an urgent need for high-quality RCTs to support its clinical adoption and international communication.4,9

Our findings highlight the urgent need for government authorities, universities, and research institutions to take initiative to improve the planning and conduct of CHM RCTs.30,31,32,33,34,35,36,37 To gain wider acceptance in the international community, CHM requires carefully designed and rigorously conducted RCTs.9,23,24,25 The complexities inherent in TCM theories, CHM practice patterns, and herbal components suggest the desirability of collaboration between clinicians and methodologists to develop standardized methodological guidelines for CHM trials. TCM researchers should receive enhanced training in clinical research methodology. Establishing multidisciplinary research teams that bring together clinical and methodological experts could improve trial quality. Alongside clinical RCTs, it is crucial to conduct clinical efficacy–oriented mechanistic studies to clarify how CHM exerts its therapeutic effects. Our findings may also extend beyond CHM RCTs, offering valuable insights for enhancing the design, conduct, and analysis of RCTs on herbal medicine globally.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths. Our methods were standardized and rigorous, including preregistration, a systematic literature search, sample size calculation, and literature screening and data extraction conducted in duplicate. To ensure a representative sample, we randomly sampled CHM RCTs in a 1:1 ratio published in English and Chinese, stratified by English journal impact factor, and covered a wide range of disease areas. Finally, our survey not only identified the methodological issues of CHM RCTs, but also systematically determined whether these issues have improved over time and whether differences exist between RCTs published in higher-impact and lower-impact English journals.

Our study has limitations. First, authors might have implemented methodological rigorous approaches that they did not report in the full texts. Second, we only included CHM RCTs reported in Chinese and English, which might be subject to language bias. Third, we collected only prior clinical, pharmacological, and toxicological evidence to support the design of CHM RCTs reported by the authors without conducting our own search for supporting evidence. Fourth, although the search strategy was derived from researchers with very similar research experience,17,18,19 it was not peer reviewed.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, we found that the design, conduct, and analysis of CHM RCTs are generally suboptimal and urgently need improvement. Although improvements occurred over time, even more recent RCTs demonstrated unsatisfactory methodologic characteristics. Government authorities, universities, and research institutions should take the initiative in improving the planning and conduct of CHM RCTs, and clinicians and methodologists need to collaborate in developing standardized methodological guidelines for such RCTs.
