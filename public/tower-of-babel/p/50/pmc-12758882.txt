Potentially Dangerous Incidental Finding on Obstetric Ultrasound – A True Umbilical Cord Knot
Authors: Venkatraman Indiran
Published: 2025

SECTION 2 – ANSWER CASE

A 28-year-old female with no comorbidities came for an antenatal ultrasound scan at 37 weeks of pregnancy for the assessment of amniotic fluid. The fetal biometry was appropriate for age. Amniotic fluid index was normal (~ 10–11 cm). Figure 1 shows an incidental finding. What is the imaging sign shown and diagnosis?

Greyscale ultrasound (a) and color Doppler (b) transverse section in a patient with true umbilical cord knot showing a segment of the umbilical cord (black arrows) surrounded by another loop of umbilical cord (white arrows), representing the hanging noose sign. Color Doppler (b) image showing the knot (white arrows) surrounding the inner loop of the cord (black arrow)

INTERPRETATION

Transverse section of a segment of the umbilical cord surrounded by another loop of umbilical cord seen on ultrasound in patients with true umbilical cord knots (TUCKs) is known as the “hanging noose sign” [Figure 1].[1] “Hanging noose sign” confirmed the diagnosis of “TUCK.” Her fetal Doppler and cardiotocography findings were normal. Cardiotocography showed a fetal heart rate of approximately 140 beats per minute with a category 1 tracing. Cerebroplacental ratio, umbilical artery systolic/diastolic ratio, pulsatility index, and resistance index were normal. A couple of days later, this patient had an uneventful vaginal delivery of a healthy 2.8 kg male baby with Apgar score (8/10). Umbilical cord measured ~60 cm in length and showed a true knot ~20 cm before placental insertion.

DISCUSSION

TUCKs have a prevalence of about 1%–2%.[2] Predisposing factors for TUCK include long cord, small fetus, polyhydramnios, monoamniotic twins, male babies, grand multiparity, and amniocentesis.[34] It is also important to differentiate true and false umbilical cord knots. TUCKs are actual knots in the umbilical cord formed during pregnancy. False umbilical cord knots are due to bulge in umbilical cord due to twisting of the vein around the arteries or due to excessive Wharton’s jelly or just a varicosity or redundancy of an umbilical vein within the cord substance, but none of these findings show an actual knot in the cord.[45] Distinguishing true and false umbilical cord knots on ultrasound is important [Table 1].[6] TUCK usually manifests between 9 and 12 weeks of pregnancy, when sufficient liquor and vigorous fetal movements allow the fetus to pass through a cord loop, but problems usually arise later in pregnancy. Most TUCKs are loose and are detected only after delivery of the baby.[2] Adverse pregnancy and labor outcomes in TUCK include small for gestational age fetus, a low Apgar score at birth, fetal hypoxia, and fetal death (stillbirth).[7] The presence of an umbilical cord knot alone is not an indication for delivery unless associated with fetal compromise which may be manifest as an abnormal fetal heart rate pattern or elevated umbilical artery systolic–diastolic ratio. Expectant management and monitoring of fetal heart rate is the current standard of care in TUCK diagnosed before labor onset. However, TUCK can cause severe fetal distress and fetal loss if it gets tightened during active fetal movements by obstructing the fetal circulation. Increased risk of antepartum fetal death by 4 times has been described in pregnancies complicated by a TUCK.[12]
