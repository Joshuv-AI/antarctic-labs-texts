Prevalence and incidence of chronic kidney diseases in Hong Kong: a 12-year territory-wide retrospective cohort study
Authors: Noel C Yue, Shun-Cheong Ho, Jonathan KL Mak, Franco WT Cheng, Edward YW Tam, Wallis CY Lau, Ruby LC Hoo, Kathryn CB Tan, Desmond YH Yap, Ching-Lung Cheung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundOver the past decades, epidemiology studies on chronic kidney disease (CKD) have been widely conducted globally, but research on its prevalence and incidence in Chinese population remain scarce. This retrospective cohort study utilized all creatinine and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio data obtained from the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), a territory-wide electronic medical database in Hong Kong, to provide the most up-to-date and largest Chinese cohort with long-term follow-up for comprehensive analysis of the trends in CKD prevalence and incidence.MethodsPatients diagnosed with CKD between 2010 and 2021 were identified from CDARS. Prevalence, crude incidence, and age-standardized incidence using the United Nations population in 2018 as a reference were estimated. Subgroups of age and sex were also estimated accordingly. Joinpoint regression was used to estimate the annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC).FindingsA total of 496,796 CKD patients between 2010 and 2021 were identified from CDARS. The prevalence of CKD (per 100,000 persons) increased from 3065.76 (95% confidence interval, CI: 3051.68–3079.88) to 6296.38 (95% CI: 6277.02–6315.78) between 2010 and 2021, with males generally having higher prevalence. The overall prevalence AAPC was 6.69% (95% CI: 6.45–7.03; p < 0.001), with the APC trend decelerating from 13.82% (95% CI: 11.17–16.10; p < 0.001) in 2012 to 4.06% (95% CI: 3.30–4.52; p < 0.001) in 2021. Both crude and age-standardized incidence (per 100,000 persons) showed a decreasing trend, in which the age-standardized incidence decreased from 533.61 (95% CI: 528.4–538.86) to 391.14 (95% CI: 387.2–395.11) between 2010 and 2021, with an AAPC of −3.74% (95% CI: −5.04 to −2.50; p < 0.001). Disparity in trends was observed among sex and age groups.InterpretationThe deceleration in CKD prevalence growth after 2012, along with the declining incidence trends may reflect the impact of the strategic framework launched by the Hong Kong government to prevent and control chronic diseases. Meanwhile, the sex differences in CKD trends support evidence that Asian males generally have a higher prevalence than females, differing from patterns observed in other regions. Further studies in diverse Asian populations are needed to reduce geographical bias and enhance understanding of CKD.FundingNone.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, CKD, Epidemiology, Trends, Prevalence, Incidence

Research in context

Evidence before this study

To obtain information on the current epidemiology and burden of chronic kidney disease, we conducted a literature search on PubMed from the inception of the database to March 1st, 2025, using the keywords: (“chronic kidney disease” OR “ckd”) AND (“prevalence” OR “incidence” OR “epidemiology” OR “trend” OR “Asia” OR “Chinese”). We reviewed the studies’ titles and abstracts to identify relevant studies.

Increasing trends in the prevalence and incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) have been observed in many countries, but the rates vary widely across regions. For example, one study reported that the adjusted prevalence of CKD ranged from 3.31% in Norway to 16.7% in Florida. In addition, another study found that CKD prevalence were higher in males than in females in Asian populations (e.g., Japanese and Singaporean), whereas in many other countries, the prevalence is higher among females than males. Currently, population-based studies focusing on CKD epidemiology in the Chinese population remain limited, and these studies typically have short study periods and/or are survey based, which may not be generalizable to the broader population.

Added value of this study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest epidemiology study on CKD in the Chinese population identified using actual creatinine and urine albumin-creatinine ratio measurement, with the longest study period and complete longitudinal electronic medical records from the public healthcare sector over the past decade, allowing for the most up-to-date and accurate analysis of the epidemiology of CKD in the Chinese population.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study highlights a research gap in the epidemiology of CKD among Chinese patients. Beyond the CKD prevalence and incidence trends over the past decades, we also found that CKD prevalence was higher in males than in females. This finding further supports the evidence that Asian males tend to have a higher CKD prevalence than Asian females, a pattern distinct from that observed in other countries. Additionally, both crude and age-standardized incidence rates showed a notable decline from 2010 to 2015, particularly in individuals aged 80 and above. These trends may reflect the impact of the strategic framework slowly launched by the Hong Kong government in 2008, which could provide insights for other countries in shaping clinical management strategies and action plans. From an epidemiological perspective, this information may help strategize resource allocation, particularly in clinical management and action plans, to address the rising prevalence of chronic diseases and an aging population. Further studies on CKD in diverse Asian populations are needed to limit geographical bias and facilitate a better understanding of CKD among Chinese and other Asian populations.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a chronic disease characterized by kidney damage and a gradual loss of their functions,1 posing a global public health threat.2 CKD is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular morbidity3 and decreased quality of life,4,5 which is currently affecting about 10% of the global population.6 Recently, CKD has emerged as the leading cause of mortality globally.7 In 2019, there were more than 760,000 new cases in Asia, with its prevalence doubled from 202.4 million to 431.2 million between 1990 and 2019.8 While early stages of CKD can be managed with lifestyle changes and medications, treatment options for end-stage CKD are limited to dialysis and kidney transplantation, which impose a significant financial burden. A recent study has revealed that the annual median costs for hemodialysis dialysis and first year of kidney transplantation is approximately US$19,380 and US$26,903 per person, respectively9; thus, it is important to understand the epidemiology of CKD and develop healthcare policies accordingly.

Previous studies suggested a rising global trend in CKD prevalence and incidence,10, 11, 12 however, discrepancies exist largely between regions.8,13,14 For example, the adjusted prevalence of CKD varies from 3.31% in Norway to 16.7% in Florida, United States.13 Such variability could represent the genuine population-specific variation, or the approach used to estimate the epidemiology of CKD. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined CKD prevalence and incidence in the Hong Kong Chinese population. While some studies have explored CKD in other Chinese populations, these studies typically have short study periods and are survey based (i.e., not generalizable to the whole population).15,16 Notably, the latest nationwide cross-sectional survey on the Chinese population was conducted in 2021 encompassing 1-year study period (i.e., 2017–2018),16 whereas the prior study was conducted in 2012 based on a national wide survey in China.15

Hong Kong, a region with the longest life expectancy, may shine valuable insights into CKD epidemiology and disease management.17 In addition, the Hospital Authority (HA) of Hong Kong records and manages longitudinal clinical information on patients from public hospitals, such as patients’ demographic information, diagnoses, procedures, and prescription records. Hence, the HA possesses an up-to-date representative database of the Hong Kong Chinese population. In the current study, we aim to utilize this territory-wide database to characterize the CKD prevalence and incidence trends over the past 12 years in Hong Kong.

Methods

Data source

The Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) is a comprehensive territory-wide electronic medical database managed by the Hospital Authority (HA) of Hong Kong. The HA is responsible for the operation of 43 public hospitals and 123 public outpatient general and specialist clinics in Hong Kong,18 providing CDARS with the capability to encompass the health records for both inpatients and outpatients of more than 80% of the population in Hong Kong,19 which currently stands at approximately 7.5 million people with 92% being ethnically Han Chinese.20 Moreover, the CDARS data have undergone validation across various therapeutic areas and have been widely used in high-quality population-based studies,21, 22, 23 providing a robust foundation for the findings of this study.

Study population

The main aim was to evaluate the recent secular trend and epidemiology of CKD in Hong Kong. The main cohort consisted of all patients who had diagnosed with CKD between 2010 and 2021 (12 years) in the CDARS database. CKD was defined in three different ways to ensure comprehensive identification of cases, the definitions are as follows: (i) if the patient had any two estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) values less than 60 (<60 ml/min/1.73 m2) for more than 90 days within a year (365 days)24 (referred to as “definition 1”), (ii) if the patient had any two urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) values greater than or equal to 3 (≥3 mg/mmol) for more than 90 days within a year24 (definition 2), and (iii) patients who met definition 1 and/or definition 2. Please refer to the supplementary method for more details.

The eGFR value is calculated from serum creatinine using the new Asian modified Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.25 All serum creatinine measurements in CDARS were utilized. Patients with missing date of birth, undefined sex, and did not reach 18 years old within the study period were removed. When calculating prevalence and incidence, additional creatinine data from the year 2008, 2009, and 2022 were retrieved to supplement the main cohort. The years 2008 and 2009 were considered as washout periods, and 2022 was included to capture patients identified in 2021, considering the possibility that their creatinine records may extend into the following year.

The main analysis was conducted using patients identified with definition 3, as this definition provides a more comprehensive assessment of CKD trends using both eGFR and UACR in Hong Kong. Results of patients identified with definitions 1 and 2 were provided in the Supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented in mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The mid-year population was retrieved from the statistics reported by the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department.26 All prevalence and incidence rates were expressed as the number of cases per 100,000 population. Age is calculated by subtracting the year of birth from the given year. For age-specific prevalence and crude incidence, the CKD patients were categorized into four age groups: 18–39, 40–59, 60–79, and 80 or above, while sex-specific analysis was categorized into male and female.

Prevalence calculation

Crude prevalence was calculated yearly between 2010 and 2021. Since CKD is an irreversible disease,1 it does not have a definitive “end date.” Therefore, a prevalent case in a specific year was defined as a CKD patient who had CKD diagnosis records in the given or previous years, still alive at the start of the year, and was at least 18 years old by the end of the year. Patient vital status was determined using recorded dates of death in the database. The crude prevalence in a year was then defined as: the total number of prevalent cases in the year/the mid-year population of adults in Hong Kong in the year.

Incidence calculation

Both crude and age-standardized incidence were calculated yearly between 2010 and 2021. An incident case in a certain year was defined as a CKD patient who was first diagnosed in that year given that he or she was at least 18 years old or above. Given a year, the crude incidence was defined as: the total number of incident cases in the year/the mid-year population of adults in Hong Kong in the year. The age of the patients was defined as the age at first CKD diagnosis.

To enable comparison with other populations, age-standardized incidence was calculated with direct standardization using the United Nations standardized population in 2018 as the reference.27 Patients were categorized into the following age groups: 18–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80 or above.

Trend analysis

Joinpoint regression was used to estimate the annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC), with the software Joinpoint Regression Program (4.0.2).28 Input data include: the prevalence or incidence rates normalized with natural logarithm (dependent variable), the calendar year (independent variable), and sex (by-variable). The best model was selected based on the weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (weighted BIC) and p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. A p < 0.05 indicates that the APC or AAPC is significantly different from zero. Joinpoint analyses were performed on all three trends: prevalence, crude incidence, and age-standardized incidence.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (Reference number: UW 24-346). Informed patient consent is not required as the data used in this study are de-identified.

Role of the funding source

The study was not funded. Supporters of individual authors had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Prevalence trends in CKD patients

Between 2010 and 2021, the number of prevalence CKD cases increased from 181,778 to 405,833 (Table 2). The proportion of male and female cases was nearly equal throughout (difference ±2%) (Table 2). The overall prevalence (per 100,000 persons) also showed an increasing trend, from 3065.76 (95% CI: 3051.68–3079.88) to 6296.38 (95% CI: 6277.02–6315.78), with an AAPC of 6.69% (95% CI: 6.45–7.03; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a, Table 3, Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, there was a significant change in trend during the years 2012 and 2016, where the APC decreased from 13.82% (95% CI: 11.17–16.10; p < 0.001) to 6.58% (95% CI: 5.78–7.40; p < 0.001) and further decreased to 4.06% (95% CI: 3.30–4.52; p < 0.001) (Table 3). Both male and female showed a similar increasing prevalence trend, but the males were constantly higher than the females (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Tables S2, S4).

When stratified by age groups, it was apparent that the number of CKD cases increased with age and notable variations were observed across different age groups (Table 2). In particular, the number of CKD cases for the age group 18–39 remained consistently low from 2010 (1863 cases) to 2021 (3563 cases); while the age group 80+ remained high and doubled from 2010 (77,557 cases) to 2021 (171,679 cases) (Table 2).

Joinpoint analysis showed that all age groups had an overall increasing prevalence trend with positive AAPC (Supplementary Tables S3-S4). In addition, at least one joinpoint was identified within each age groups: the age group 18–39 experienced the change in 2014, the group 40–59 in 2013 and 2017, the group 60–79 in 2012 and 2015, and the group 80+ in 2012 and 2017 (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table S4). Specifically, the APCs were smaller after the change, indicating deceleration in the rate of increase (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table S4).

Patients identified by definition 1 (i.e., based solely on eGFR) and definition 2 (i.e., based solely on UACR) exhibited similar trends (Supplementary Figures S2, S4).

Incidence trend in CKD patients

The number of incidence CKD cases between 2010 and 2021 is provided in Table 2. Overall, there was a decreased in the number of CKD cases from 41,684 in 2010 to 40,723 in 2021, with slightly more cases observed in males than females (Table 2). Likewise, the crude incidence (per 100,000 persons) also fluctuated between 2010 and 2021, with the overall rate decreased from 703.02 (95% CI: 696.28–709.80) to 631.81 (95% CI: 625.68–637.97), resulting in an AAPC of −1.95% (95% CI: −3.33 to −0.62; p = 0.006) (Fig. 2a, Table 3, Supplementary Table S5). Both male and female showed a similar crude incidence trend (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table S7).

When stratified by age groups, the crude incidence (per 100,000 persons) showed an increasing trend in the age groups 18–39 and 40–59, while the age groups 60–79 and 80+ showed decreasing trends (Fig. 2b, Table 2, Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). However, the crude incidence rate was dominated by the age group 80+ with the highest negative AAPC value of −7.36 (95% CI: −8.43 to −6.27; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table S7). Moreover, there was a change in trend identified in the age group 80+ in 2014 (Supplementary Table S7), in which the segment from 2010 to 2014 showed a negative APC value of −10.01% (95% CI: −15.95 to −6.58; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b, (Supplementary Table S7).

Age-standardized incidence showed a similar trend as the crude incidence, in which the age-standardized incidence rate decreased from 533.61 (95% CI: 528.40–538.86) to 391.14 (95% CI: 387.2–395.11) per 100,000 persons, with an AAPC of −3.74% (95% CI: −5.04 to −2.50; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a, Table 3, Supplementary Table S5). Both male and female showed a similar age-standardized incidence rate trend (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table S4).

Patients identified by definition 1 showed similar trends (Supplementary Figure S3). In comparison, those identified by definition 2 exhibited similar trend patterns across sex and age groups, but their data showed greater fluctuation over time (Supplementary Figure S5).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this population-based study represented the largest cohort of Chinese patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) using actual laboratory measurements (both creatinine and urine albumin-to-creatinine) and encompassed the longest study period in the world, providing the most up-to-date and comprehensive evaluation of the epidemiology of CKD in the Chinese population.

The prevalence of CKD had been increasing in Hong Kong between 2010 and 2021. Although similar increasing trends were observed in males and females, males consistently showed a higher prevalence than females. Although it was shown that the prevalence of CKD is higher among females than in males in many countries, such prevalence was indeed higher in males than in females in Asian (e.g., Japanese, Singapore, and Chinese as shown in the current study).29 Such disparity could be due to difference in the prevalence of CKD risk factors between different sexes among different countries as well as contributed by lifestyle and genetic factors. When stratified by age groups, a mixture of trends was observed. In particular, the age group 80 or above showed a more apparent increasing trend, while age group 60–79 showed a second, but more subtle upward trend. These observations could be explained by aging, which is known to be negatively associated with the structural and functional changes in the kidney,30,31 and reflected the onset of the global expansion of the age group 65+.32,33 In the context of Hong Kong, despite the elders aged 80 or above have a higher risk of developing CKD, there was a larger increase in the population of age group 60–79 compared to those 80+ between 2010 and 2021 (age group 60–79: from 14.7% to 22.6%; 80+ from 3.7% to 5.3%),26 resulting in the apparent but more subtle trends.

The incidence of CKD fluctuated between 2010 and 2021, but notably both the crude and age-standardized incidence showed a remarkable decrease from 2011 to 2015, especially in the age group 80+. These observations may reflect the proactive approach of the strategic framework launched by the Government of Hong Kong in 2008, as well as the impact of the Elderly Health Care Voucher Pilot (EHCV) Scheme introduced on January 1st, 2009. These approaches may have heightened health awareness among individuals and increased screening rates between 2008 and 2010, capturing most of the new CKD cases in the early years. In particular, the Government of Hong Kong in 2008 gradually implemented a series of health promotions and actions, such as the EatSmart program, new nutrition labeling scheme, and healthy town planning designs, aimed at preventing and controlling non-communicable diseases,34 while the EHCV Scheme provided Hong Kong citizens aged 70 or above with vouchers to subsidize healthcare costs.35 In fact, the decrease in the overall crude and age-standardized incidence, as well as the decline in the rate of increase in prevalence, can also be explained by these proactive approaches.

These efforts may also facilitate early detection of CKD cases, particularly through increased testing for kidney biomarkers like UACR, which is sensitive for identifying early kidney damage.36 Compared to identification based solely on eGFR, a higher proportion of patients in the age groups of 40–59 and 60–79 were identified using UACR, suggesting that using UACR test could potentially identify subjects with early kidney damage, particularly in the younger population. In other words, the differing incidence trends observed in UACR vs eGFR may indicate an increase in early CKD diagnoses based on UACR compared to eGFR. In addition, since the sample identified using only UACR was much smaller than that identified with eGFR, the incidence trends demonstrated greater fluctuations over time.

Moreover, consistent with the prevalence findings, the males consistently showed a higher crude incidence than females. Although females had a slightly higher age-standardized incidence than males between 2010 and 2011, the differences were negligible. This trend may, at least partially, mirror the decline in type 2 diabetes incidence among females aged 60 or above since 2011, who constitute the major component of CKD patients, while the incidence remained stable in males.37

Despite the scarce number of studies on CKD in recent years, most studies were based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) dataset,14,38,39 which included adjusted data sources from different studies. Thus, comparison with other literature was limited. However, one of these studies showed a consistent prevalence trend with the current study, in which the prevalence of CKD in the Chinese population had increased since 1990 but started to slow down since 2015.38

There are both strengths and limitations in the current study. The primary strength lies in the use of two laboratory measurements, creatinine and UACR, to identified CKD patients, providing a more comprehensive assessment of CKD trends. Prior studies have shown that relying solely on either eGFR or UACR measurement can result in the omission of a significant number of CKD cases (e.g., omitting ∼29% when based solely on eGFR).40,41 Additionally, creatinine testing is routinely performed in patients during hospital visits, which minimizes potential bias in patient identification. On the other hand, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, there may have been patients who were lost to follow-up, resulting in an over-estimation of CKD prevalence. Second, the exclusion of individuals who did not seek medical care under the healthcare system could result in an underestimation of both CKD prevalence and incidence. However, since the CDARS database covers the health records of over 80% of Hong Kong's population,19 the impact on the overall trends is likely to be minimal. Also, the total Hong Kong population was used as the denominator for incidence calculation, which may have led to an underestimation of CKD cases. Next, our cohort consists of non-Chinese patients due to the incomplete ethically sourced data in CDARS. However, given that 92% of the population in Hong Kong is ethnically Chinese, the impact on the overall interpretation should be minimal. Finally, the study did not account for other possible events that may lead to low eGFR, such as taking non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs),42 which could have resulted in an over-estimation of CKD incidence and prevalence.

To conclude, between 2010 and 2021, the prevalence of CKD in Hong Kong increased but the rate of increase had decelerated since 2012; while both the crude and age-standardized incidence fluctuated, the overall trend of both had decreased. This may be due to the effective implementation of the “prevention and control strategic framework” on non-communication disease by the government of Hong Kong. Generally, unlike many western countries, males had a higher prevalence and incidence than females in Hong Kong. When stratified by age, the age group 60–79 and 80+ showed more pronounced trends, which could be explained by the well-known role of advancing age but also the expansion of the age group 60–79. Further studies on CKD in other Asian populations were warranted to limit geographical bias and facilitate a better understanding of the disease across the Chinese populations.
