Association between respiratory-related cortical activation and weaning from mechanical ventilation: a physiological study
Authors: Christophe C. S. Rault, Arnaud W. Thille, Quentin Héraud, Stéphanie Ragot, Rémi Coudroy, Jean-Pierre Frat, Xavier Drouot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn mechanically ventilated patients, extubation is decided after passing a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT). Supplementary motor area, which can be non-invasively assessed by measuring amplitude of pre-inspiratory potentials (PIPs), is recruited in case of increased respiratory effort. Weaning failure may be accompanied by increased respiratory effort. We hypothesized that early increased PIP amplitude from the start of SBT may be associated with SBT failure.Patients and methodsWe performed a single-center physiological prospective study in mechanically ventilated patients meeting weaning criteria. PIP amplitude was measured using electroencephalography under mechanical ventilation and then during the first 15-min of the SBT performed using a T-piece. We compared respiratory-related cortical activation between patients who passed a 1-h SBT and those who failed.ResultsAmong the 62 patients included, 17 (27%) failed the SBT. Whereas patients who failed the SBT had a lower median amplitude of PIPs under mechanical ventilation than those who passed the SBT (0.6 µV [IQR, 0–1.3] vs. 1.9 [1.2–2.9], p < 0.0001), they had a higher amplitude of PIPs at the beginning of the SBT (2.1 µV [IQR, 1.7–2.9] vs. 1.2 [0.5–2.0], p < 0.005). PIP amplitude between mechanical ventilation and SBT significantly increased in case of SBT failure whereas it decreased in case of SBT success (+ 1.7 µV [1.2–2.1] vs. -0.5 [-1.8–0.1], p < 0.0001). An increase of PIP above 0.43 µV was associated with SBT failure with sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 87%.ConclusionThe demonstration of an association between increased respiratory-related cortical activation and failure of the weaning trial is a physiological marker of interest for better phenotyping of patients ready for weaning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05706-y.

Background

The decision to extubate is challenging in ICUs. Delaying extubation exposes patients to undue prolongation of mechanical ventilation and the ensuing complications [1] whereas extubation failure requiring reintubation is associated with increased risk of mortality [2–5]. In mechanically ventilated patients, a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) is recommended before extubation in order to mimic physiological condition after extubation [1]. Judging SBT outcome may be difficult, as demonstrated by discrepancies in clinicians’ assessments [6, 7] and high incidences of SBT failure, which occurs in 18 to 30% of cases [2, 8, 9]. Since many factors can be responsible for the failure of an attempt to wean a patient from mechanical ventilation, predicting the success or failure of a SBT remains challenging even for an experienced physician. Predictive indexes have been proposed to identify the earliest time that a patient can resume spontaneous breathing and to identify patients in whom SBT is likely to fail, so that cardiorespiratory distress can be avoided (10). Numerous indexes have been developed, such as the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI) [1, 10, 11] or airway occlusion pressure (P0.1) [12]

No study has actually assessed the role of cerebral respiratory drive in SBT outcome. Up until now, critical care physicians had access only to clinical bedside parameters (respiratory rate, minute ventilation, signs of respiratory distress) which are indirect markers, and not direct measures, of the central control of respiration. Monitoring respiratory-related cortical activation could become a tool for continuous assessment of respiratory discomfort in patients undergoing mechanical ventilation, aided by the automation of EEG signal analysis, which is currently under development [13].

The brainstem coordinates automatic rest ventilation [14]. However, in case of increased inspiratory load, the cerebral motor cortex engages [15–17], facilitating the response of phrenic motoneurons to central commands via a corticospinal pathway [18–21]. In this case, respiratory-related cortical activation can be assessed by measuring pre-inspiratory potentials (PIP) on electroencephalogram recordings before each inspiration [22–24]. We hypothesized that strong respiratory-cortical activation may be associated with markedly increased inspiratory efforts during SBT failure. The main objective of this study was to determine whether respiratory-related cortical activation assessed by measuring PIP amplitude during a SBT, could be differently activated according to the success or failure of the SBT.

Patients and methods

Design and setting

Between July 2017 and April 2021, we conducted, a prospective single-center physiological study in mechanically ventilated patients admitted to the ICU of the University Hospital of Poitiers (France). The study was approved by the independent ethics committee CPP Ouest V, France (number 2016-A01814-47) and was registered at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03372252).

Spontaneous breathing trials

Patients enrolled in the study were eligible for participation if they had been intubated for more than 24 h and were ready for a SBT according to the usual criteria (described in the supplementary data). Patients underwent a 1-h SBT using a T-piece placed at the end of the intubation tube with additional oxygen (1). SBT failure was defined in accordance with the usual criteria (see supplementary data for definition).

Respiratory-related cortical activation assessment

Respiratory-related cortical activation was assessed by measuring pre-inspiratory potential (PIP) amplitude, a low-amplitude deflection of the EEG signal above nadir in 2.5 s preceding inspiration, obtained by continuously recording the EEG. PIPs were manually measured by a neurologist blinded to the success or failure of the SBT.

PIP was built by averaging EEG tracks starting 2.5 s before and ending 1 s after the onset of inspiration (Fig. 1). Under mechanical ventilation, as well as during the SBT, the inspiratory onset was defined as the point of negative inflection of airway pressure (Fig. 1). Our methodology followed that of Nguyen 2018 [25]. For EEG channel we indicated, using Yes/No, if there was a negative slope between −2000 ms and 0 ms. PIP amplitude was determined on the average signal, using a previously published method (17, 23). PIP was the voltage difference (µV) between nadir and the point corresponding to the deflection of inspiratory depression on the averaged airway pressure signal indicated by a flag in the AcqKnowledge software. (Fig. 1). PIP amplitude was measured first under mechanical ventilation during the 15 min (PIP amplitude under mechanical ventilation) immediately preceding the SBT, and then, during the first 15 min of the SBT (PIP amplitude during the SBT). Ineffective efforts were manually excluded from signal analysis and PIP construction. When the respiratory rate exceeded 17/min, which is common in ICUs, to avoid any overlap, the overlapped respiratory cycles were not included in the averaging. Tracks exhibiting movement artifacts were visually discarded.

Change in PIP amplitude (ΔPIP) was calculated between recordings under mechanical ventilation and during SBT.

Statistical analysis

Given an expected SBT failure rate of around 20%, and according to our previous study [23] showing an inter-group variability of 3 µV (standard deviation) for PIP amplitude, we calculated that 63 patients should be included to show a difference of 3 µV in PIP amplitude between patients passing the SBT and patients failing the SBT, with α risk of 5% and power of 80%. To take into account around 10% of unusable recordings, we planned to include 70 patients in this first physiological study investigating variability in the intensity of the respiratory-related cortical activation in ICUs; i.e. 56 patients in the SBT success group and 14 patients in the SBT failure group.

Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range [IQR, 25th-75th percentiles]. Previously, the normality of the distribution of these PIP variables was checked graphically. The two groups (SBT success and SBT failure) were compared using a non-parametric Mann Whitney U test for quantitative variables and a chi2 test or a Fisher exact test in case of an expected value < 5 for qualitative variables. Intra-group comparisons were made using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The association between PIP amplitude variables and SBT success or failure was adjusted on variables significantly associated with SBT failure using multivariable logistic regression. A receiver operating curve (ROC) was constructed and a Youden index calculated to determine a threshold for PIP amplitude variables significantly associated with spontaneous breathing trial success or failure. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Among the 70 patients included, 8 were secondarily excluded (1 for withdrawal of consent, 1 who did not undergo SBT, and 6 due to electroencephalogram artifacts) (Fig. 2). Out of the 62 patients retained in the analysis, 17 failed the SBT (27%). The SBT was the initial trial for 30 patients (48%), the second one for 19 patients (31%), and the third one for 13 patients (21%). Amplitude of PIPs during the SBT

PIP was detected in all patients who failed the SBT and in 78% of patients (35 out of 45 patients) who passed the SBT (p = 0.049). Respiratory variables

Fifteen minutes after the start of the SBT, respiratory rate tended to be higher in patients who failed as compared with patients who passed, albeit not significant (29 [26–33] cycles/min vs. 24 [19–30] cycles/min; p = 0.05).

RSBI and P0.1, measured under mechanical ventilation, were not different between patients who passed and patients who failed the SBT (Table 1).

Discussion

In this physiological study, we observed that PIP amplitude measured during SBT was significantly higher in patients who failed the SBT than in those who passed the SBT. By contrast, PIP amplitude under mechanical ventilation was lower in patients who failed than in those who passed the SBT. Consequently, a positive change of PIP amplitude when switching from mechanical ventilation to SBT was significantly associated with SBT failure, and an increase of at least 0.43 µV in PIP amplitude was associated with SBT failure with sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 87%.

PIP amplitude

During spontaneous breathing, we found that PIP amplitudes were higher in patients who failed than in those who passed the SBT. To our knowledge, PIPs had never previously been measured during SBT performed before extubation in ICUs. Patients who fail weaning develop an increased respiratory load during weaning which increases respiratory effort during SBT and ventilatory demand [26–29]. PIP reflects the activity of the supplementary motor area, and PIP amplitude reliably measures inspiratory-related cortical activation (30, 31). PIP is activated by increased inspiratory load [31–33]. The cortical contribution to breathing also increases in case of threshold or resistive load in healthy subjects (17) or to cope with weakness of respiratory muscles [34]. It has been shown on non-respiratory skeletal muscles that premotor potential amplitude increases with amount of voluntary force [35, 36], reflecting the increase in cortical resources allocated to muscle strength [30, 37–39]. The increased PIP amplitude at the beginning of SBT in patients who subsequently failed probably reflects an increase in the contribution of their premotor cortex to breathing in order to adapt to the respiratory overload caused by the cessation of ventilatory support. The increased attention and concentration of patients on their breathing when the ventilator is stopped could also contribute to the increased amplitude of the PIPs [35]. The evolution of PIP amplitude over time until the end of the SBT would have been relevant to potentially detect central fatigue, as has been shown during an inspiratory endurance trial in sleep-deprived healthy subjects [23]. However, half of the patients in the failure group failed the SBT before 30 min, meaning that there would be an overlap in PIPs between the measurement taken at the start of the trial (over 15 min) and the measurement taken at the end of the trial (over 15 min).

On the contrary, we surprisingly observed greater PIPs under mechanical ventilation in patients who passed the SBT than in those who failed the SBT, suggesting increased inspiratory-related cortical activation [23]. PIPs have been associated with respiratory discomfort during mechanical ventilation [33, 40, 41] and increased attention [35]. We hypothesize that patients ready to be extubated may be constrained in their breathing pattern by the ventilator and be relieved by its removal. Although we did not assess patients' dyspnea, the link between PIP amplitude and discomfort during mechanical ventilation [33, 40, 41] and the bridge formed by the supplementary motor area between sensory and motor cortexes (42) place our results in continuity with studies of dyspnea in non-communicating ICU patients. There are studies using EEG (41), observation scales [43] or a combination of observation scale and clinical measurements (44) to estimate dyspnea in non-communicative ICU patients and to predict weaning outcome. EEG can detect a pattern of dyspnea in intubated ICU patients and could help with ventilator adjustment to relieve dyspnea [41]. Presence of PIPs under mechanical ventilation could reflect sensory cortical activation in patients ready to breathe spontaneously [42] and has shown its value in ventilation adjustment [33]. Assessment of PIPs and dyspnea could be combined to adjust the ventilator settings and to predict SBT outcome [43]. Our results on respiratory-related cortical activation suggest that patients who passed the SBT were uncomfortable under mechanical ventilation. This constraint by the ventilator may trigger sensory activation of the supplementary motor area, which may be mitigated by disconnecting the patient from the ventilator.

Under mechanical ventilation, patients who failed the SBT have lower PIP amplitudes. It could be because ventilation was necessary and the level of assistance was appropriate. However, while this could also have been due to residual action of sedatives, the duration of sedative withdrawal did not differ between the two groups. This would have impacted both groups and both experimental conditions in the same way. In addition, patients in the SBT failure group had low PIPs under mechanical ventilation, but PIPs increased when the ventilator was turned off, so we can assume that they were not low due to sedatives. Hyperinflation caused by over-assistance can also be discussed. In case of over-assistance, low P0.1, intrinsic PEEP and especially ineffective triggering are usually observed, possibly leading to low PIP. But our protocol did not include measurements that support the diagnosis of over-assistance.

We did not measure respiratory effort, which would have made it possible to investigate the effect of PIP changes on the respiratory pattern. P0.1 did not differ between the two groups under mechanical ventilation, whereas PIPs were different. However, PIPs represent part of the cortical inspiratory drive, whereas P0.1 reflects the global drive to breathe not only cortically, but also at the brainstem level, and involving reflex pathways. For the same P0.1, PIPs may or may not be present. The best example is exercise, which can cause significant respiratory effort without PIP expression [45]. Thus, we did not expect to find obvious correlations between the presence/amplitude of PIPs and respiratory effort. PIPs appear more relevant for the analysis of respiratory discomfort. Furthermore, in cases of severe respiratory muscle weakness, or dynamic hyperinflation, the pressure does not reflect the drive to breathe [46]. However, it would have been interesting to measure P0.1 and respiratory mechanics during a SBT on the ventilator with Pressure Support set at 0 and PEEP 0 cmH2O [47], so as to compare its evolution with that of PIPs.

PIP and SBT outcome

When switching from mechanical ventilation to SBT, the very different change in PIP amplitudes between patients who passed and those who failed SBT allowed us to build a ROC curve. All patients who failed the SBT had increased PIP amplitude of at least of 0.43 µV. However, specificity was 87%, which means that several patients who had a successful SBT were above or even on the threshold of 0.43 µV. This ΔPIP threshold needs to be assessed prospectively on a larger cohort.

It is important to continue the search for new physiological index that can help clinicians to detect patients able to breathe on their own. Our results encourage us to explore this respiratory-cortical related activation. Our work requires prospective exploration of the ΔPIP threshold proposed in this study.

SBT duration

The recommended duration of an SBT is 30 to 120 min [1, 48, 49]. SBT must, of course, be stopped immediately in the event of poor patient tolerance. Esteban et al. [48] found no difference between 30 and 120 min of SBT in the risk of reintubation in patients who had spent less than 21 days in intensive care. However, 47% of our patients who failed SBT lasted more than 30 min. Our team showed a correlation between PIP amplitude at the beginning of an inspiratory endurance trial and inspiratory endurance in a group of healthy subjects deprived of sleep for 24 h [23]. Central fatigue was progressively established after sleep deprivation, with a gradual decrease in PIP amplitude during the inspiratory endurance test until task failure appeared with a median of 30 min. These elements lead us to confirm that a duration of at least 30 min seems necessary to judge a patient's respiratory endurance, and that analysis of respiratory-related cortical activation can help assess it.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Patients were not consecutively recruited due to the limited availability of investigator/research materials followed by the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, this study presents only a fraction of the eligible population.

The sample size is too small to establish the predictive value of the variation in PIP amplitude on SBT outcomes or set a threshold. However, this initial physiological study on respiratory-related cortical activation has shown promising results that need to be confirmed in a larger cohort.

Although ΔPIP was strongly associated with SBT failure, ΔPIP could be measured only in 14 among the 17 patients who failed the SBT. Among these 14 patients, 4 failed before 15 min, which limits the predictive power of the method. However, in these 4 patients, ΔPIP calculated at the time of their failure was well above the threshold of 0.43 µV predictive of SBT failure (0.51 µV for P10, 8 µV for P19, 2.21 µV for P65, 1.86 µV for P70). In addition, the threshold of 0.43 µV was obtained a posteriori. Its validation requires testing it prospectively on a larger cohort of patients. Moreover, ours was an exploratory physiological study that was not designed to assess the ability of operative indices to predict weaning success or failure.

PIP methodology is complex and requires EEG recording and sophisticated EEG signal analysis, which was performed off-line in our study. In addition, the threshold of 0.43 µV above which ΔPIP indicates a risk of SBT failure is quite low. Obviously, an EEG signal of high quality is required for reliable use in clinical practice. Given the expertise required to process PIP data, the need for offline analysis, and the inability to obtain data in some patients, PIP amplitude measurement cannot be used routinely at the patient's bedside. Studies have highlighted respiratory-related cortical activity during different respiratory exercises by continuous assessment of brain connectivity in relationship with respiratory status [41, 50]. This approach could enable a more refined and automatable analysis of respiratory-related cortical activity.

Our RSBI results must be interpreted with caution due to the methodology used. In fact, the measurement was carried out under mechanical ventilation, in spontaneous ventilation mode, but with the pressure support and continuous positive airway pressure determined for each patient by the physician in charge. Although measurement has been considered in the literature under mechanical ventilationm [11, 51], it was not standardized between our patients, and ventilatory support may have biased respiratory rate as well as tidal volume passively delivered in considerable part by the ventilator. Furthermore, this measurement is gender-dependent [52]. This may explain the lack of difference in RSBI between our two groups.

Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were at risk of dynamic hyperinflation, which could alter the accuracy of the measurement of PIPs by altering the neural inspiration starting point. This limitation may be more pronounced in patients while they are mechanically ventilated than during SBT. Dynamic hyperinflation should be reduced during SBT in both groups, as well in those who succeeded or failed the trial. Only 9 patients among the 62 studied and only 2 patients in the failure group had COPD. While we did not measure PEEPi or ineffective triggering, the proportion of COPD patients was equivalent in the two groups and the potential measurement error was comparable. One way of further increasing the accuracy of the measurement would have been to use an invasive nasogastric probe with diaphragmatic EMG.

Conclusion

Analysis of respiratory-related cortical activation might represent a future line of research for the further development of indices to aid medical decision-making in weaning from mechanical ventilation.
