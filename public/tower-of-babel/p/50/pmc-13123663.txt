Enabling precision medicine and targeted therapeutics in low- and middle-income country
Authors: Nisreen Amayiri, Girish Chinnaswamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood brain tumors are the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in children worldwide. Delayed diagnosis and inadequate diagnostic and treatment infrastructure are significant barriers to an optimal outcome in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where 80% of children with cancer live. Pediatric neuro-oncology has undergone a transformation with recent developments in molecular diagnostics and the emergence of targeted therapies. However, the implementation of precision medicine in LMICs is challenging in view of high costs, lack of trained personnel, and restricted access to essential medications. Targeted therapies such as MEK, BRAF & NTRK inhibitors have demonstrated promising efficacy with improved toxicity profiles in pediatric brain tumors with actionable mutations. Despite their potential, availability in LMICs is largely restricted to compassionate use programs and select clinical trials. Encouraging results, albeit from few institutions in LMICs, have demonstrated that targeted therapies have a significant impact on quality of life (QOL), in addition to comparable efficacy. Sustainable integration of precision medicine in LMICs requires improved access to affordable molecular diagnostics, inclusion of more targeted drugs in the WHO Essential Medicines List, and participation in international clinical trials. Collaborative models—such as twinning programs, telemedicine, regional reference laboratories, and capacity-building initiatives—can support implementation. Global programs like the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer and the ATOM Coalition play a vital role in improving drug access and policy advocacy. Enabling precision medicine in LMICs calls for a coordinated multi-stakeholder approach, including integration into the national cancer care policy, and is critical to bridging the survival gap and ensuring equitable outcomes for children with brain tumors globally.

Cancer is becoming a leading cause of death worldwide. While the absolute numbers of global childhood cancer incident cases and deaths are relatively small, the global burden of childhood cancer as represented in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) is substantial, with childhood cancer being the sixth leading cause of the total cancer burden globally and the ninth leading cause of childhood disease burden globally.1 More than 80% of children affected by cancer live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). LMICs represent diverse groups of regions with a wide range of cancer presentations, resources, capabilities, and cancer treatment infrastructure. The defining features of cancer in many LMICs are the advanced stage at presentation and the higher fatality rate when comparing cancers at the same stage in high-income countries (HICs).

CNS tumors are the leading cause of cancer related morbidity and mortality in children.2 These tumors are complex and need comprehensive multidisciplinary teams to provide good quality of care. This encompasses access to radiological imaging (MRI scans), pathological diagnostics, good neurosurgical equipment and skills, and access to radiotherapy machines with trained personnel. In addition, expertise in handling of chemotherapeutic drugs and supportive care, rehabilitation services, and long-term survivorship care are essential components of comprehensive care. Two major advancements recently affected the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric CNS tumors: the introduction of the fifth edition of WHO classification of CNS tumors (WHO-CNS-5) and the broader use of targeted drugs.3

The WHO-CNS-5 classification emphasizes the importance of integrated diagnoses and layered pathological reports through introduction of new tumor types, some based on new diagnostic technologies such as DNA methylome profiling. While this presents a major advancement to better diagnose tumors, it needs skilled neuropathologists with access to the new diagnostic tools. The new stratification of tumor types allows us to understand better the tumor trajectory, with differential response to therapy and variable patients’ outcome. “Fine tuning” of therapy based on the tumor characteristics and tailored therapy according to the prognostic molecular markers is expected and is the basis of several pediatric neuro-oncology clinical trials spanning childhood embryonal brain tumors and gliomas (NCT01878617, NCT02066220, NCT04201457, NCT03919071) Finding a targetable tumor alteration with an efficient drug yet minimal side effects is the ultimate goal. Few studies showed that this may be possible for some pediatric brain tumors harboring specific alterations4 (NCT04201457).

The current era has seen unprecedented use of targeted therapies and drug approvals for use in pediatric brain tumors. While the first FDA approval was in 2010 for Everolimus (mTOR inhibitor) to treat children and adults with subependymal giant astrocytoma (SEGA) requiring a therapeutic intervention, and are not candidates for surgical resection, several approvals of targeted therapies were made in the last 8 years. In 2018, Larotrectinib was granted accelerated approval for adult and pediatric patients older than 12 years old with solid tumors that have NTRK gene fusion (patients with either metastatic, or progressive disease or who have no satisfactory alternative treatments). In 2019, Entrectinib was granted an accelerated approval for the same indication, and the approval was further expanded in 2023 to include children ≥ 1 month old, with the approval of a new oral pellet formulation. In addition, in 2022, FDA granted accelerated approval for the combination, Dabrafenib and Trametinib, to treat patients older than 6 years of age with unresectable or metastatic solid tumors harboring the BRAFv600E mutation. A phase 2 trial on pediatric LGGs with BRAFv600E mutation showed that combined Dabrafenib and Trametinib resulted in significantly more responses, longer progression-free survival, and a better safety profile than standard chemotherapy(vincristine and carboplatin) (4). This led, in 2023, to the FDA approval of combining Dabrafenib and Trametinib as first line therapy for patients older than 1 year of age with LGG and BRAFv600E mutation who require systemic therapy. A year later, FDA granted accelerated approval for Tovorafenib, a pan RAF inhibitor, for use in children with relapsed or refractory LGG with BRAF fusion/rearrangement, or BRAFv600E mutation.While IDH mutant diffuse gliomas are uncommon in children, the recent FDA approval of Vorasidenib for patients older than 12 years old with grade 2 astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1 or IDH2 mutation following surgery constitutes a new opportunity for a first line therapy (Source: https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs).

Pediatric Neuro-Oncology in LMICs

While LMICs demonstrate significant heterogeneity in health care resources and infrastructure, most of them share significant challenges in delivery of care for children with brain tumors. Lack of awareness about early signs and symptoms of brain tumors in children among the public and health care providers in LMICs results in delayed diagnosis and referral.5-7 This consequently results in advanced disease at presentation, which is inherently harder to treat, necessitating more intensive therapy, with higher probability of long-term disease and treatment related sequelae. Limited availability of MRI machines is another challenge, leading to inaccurate presurgical assessment and inappropriate tumor staging. Neurosurgeons are scarce in LMICs, most of them lack adequate pediatric training and are overburdened with non-cancer cases. Besides, they have insufficient intraoperative facilities like neuromonitoring, endoscopic facilities, or navigation use.8,9 In addition, LMICs have few skilled (neuro)pathologists and limited ability and infrastructure for molecular testing required for the WHO-CNS-5 classification. Management of pediatric CNS tumors require trained pediatric (neuro)oncologists, (pediatric) radiation oncologists, and sustained accessibility to radiotherapy and chemotherapeutic agents, which are many a time deficient in LMICs. Above all, working within the context of multidisciplinary team for providing comprehensive care is not a well-established concept in LMICs.10 As per the WHO technical report (https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/277190), LMICs have considerably lower availability of anticancer medicines, including targeted therapies, with high prices cited as main contributing factor or availability only with high out-of-pocket patient payments. Similar deficiencies in childhood anti-cancer drug availability in LMICS was also highlighted in a survey conducted by the international Society of Pediatric Oncology(SIOP).11 Drug development and accessibility have primarily been dependent on participation in clinical trials which are mostly conducted in HICs and consequently, the participation of racial and ethnic minority populations are under-represented, further reducing generalizability of the results on the broader cancer population.12 This further widens the survival gap encountered between high and LMICs by limiting access to the newer therapies.

Despite this, there have been some successful experiences of establishing pediatric neuro-oncology services in some institutions in LMICs. Some were initiated and supported through strong twinning collaborations with institutions in HIC, like the Jordanian13 and the Pakistani experience,14 others were established within specialized cancer centers like the Egyptian and the Tata Memorial Centre, Mumbai experience.15,16 Multiple initiatives of teleconferencing in neuro-oncology have contributed significantly to providing care to children with brain tumors in LMICs,17 one such example is the monthly national neuro-oncology virtual tumor board held in India (https://www.ncgindia.org/). Tele-pathology to help reach a diagnosis and tele-radiotherapy to advise on the quality of radiotherapy planning proved to be effective in building local capacities and improving patient care.18,19 To improve oncology training in treating children with CNS tumors, short, dedicated hybrid multidisciplinary courses were created by St Jude Children’s Research Hospital20 and a new formal virtual pediatric neuro-oncology fellowship program was established (https://www.stjude.org/education-training). The first group of five fellows from LMICs in this program have just graduated hoping they will lead the pediatric neuro-oncology team in their home institutions and consequently impact the region. Participation in clinical trials dedicated to children with CNS tumors is scarce in LMICs. The Children’s Cancer Hospital Egypt (57357) runs a clinical trial comparing hypofractionated radiotherapy to conventional radiotherapy in diffuse brainstem glioma in children.21 This was a great local initiative trying to find a solution to a local challenge (in this case, the limited availability and access to radiotherapy machines), and a clinical trial utilizing low dose craniospinal irradiation in WNT medulloblastoma started at Tata Memorial Centre, Mumbai, to limit the morbidities associated with more intensive therapy in the favorable medulloblastoma subgroup (NCT04474964). Additionally, global trials such as GLOBOTRK (NCT06528691) targeting NTRK-rearranged brain tumors with Entrectinib and the U-R-Immune trial investigating the role of adaptive immunotherapy in children with Replication-Repair Deficient (RRD) high-grade glioma are underway with participation from centers in LMICs (NCT06519682).

Targeted Therapy Use in LMICs

There are limited publications from LMICs on the outcome and survival of children with CNS tumors utilizing the standard treatment protocols of chemotherapy with or without radiotherapy. Even fewer papers were published on the use of targeted therapies in pediatric CNS tumors, particularly since access to these drugs is limited due to their high cost and restricted clinical trials’ participation. Only a few case reports were published, and therapy was provided through compassionate extended access programs. Most used drugs were Dabrafenib and/or Trametinib and to a lesser degree, Larotrectinib used to treat low- and high-grade gliomas, usually in a well-advanced center in a LMIC.22–24 The experience of using other targeted therapies such as other NTRK inhibitors (Entrectinib), IDH inhibitors (Ivosidenib, Vorisidenib), ALK inhibitors (Lorlatinib, Alectinib) is limited due to the rarity of pediatric brain tumors that express the target, combined with the non-availability of these drugs in most LMICs.

More extensive experience with MEK/BRAF inhibitors was reported from King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC) in Jordan25 and Tata Memorial Center in India26 with a larger number of patients. The Jordanian experience on the compassionate use of BRAF/MEK inhibitors (namely dabrafenib and trametinib) in treating twenty children with low- and high-grade gliomas was promising. Responses were achieved in all LGGs, and a sustained response with good quality of life was possible for patients with HGG. Families were very satisfied with using these targeted therapies and preferred them over the standard chemotherapies. Sustained access to these drugs after closure of the compassionate program for new patients was the major challenge in this experience. Similar experience was reported from Tata Memorial Centre in Mumbai, where pediatric LGG treated with MEK inhibitor (Trametinib) had comparable (to chemotherapy)responses in 24 children with better QOL in both child and family. However, toxicities were also seen in children with MEK/BRAF inhibitors and commonly included paronychia (15%-32%), acneiform rash (22%-40%), majority managed on an outpatient basis, rarely requiring hospitalization. In a quarter of the children treated, toxicity necessitated drug interruptions.25,26

Although the optimal duration of treatment with targeted therapies remains uncertain, they offer several advantages—such as ease of administration (most are taken orally), fewer blood tests, and reduced hospital visits. These features allow children to continue schooling and engage in normal childhood activities, with minimal disruption to family life. Such benefits are especially significant in LMICs, where support systems for intensive chemotherapy are often limited, frequently compelling children (and their families) to stay near treatment centers with significant disruption to the family. These advantages potentially decrease therapy abandonment in LMICs, where distance to treatment centers or access to care has been a common etiological factors.

In the experiences of the two centers referenced above, several difficulties were encountered in procuring the targeted agents. These included arranging financial support from multiple sources, applying for compassionate use programs, drug supply interruptions (especially during COVID pandemic), obtaining patient-specific regulatory approvals, and coordinating drug importation where necessary. This was a complex and time-consuming process that required substantial effort from the healthcare staff. Importantly, delays in accessing these therapies can result in disease progression, and this risk must be carefully considered when planning such interventions.

Potential Initiatives and Solutions to Offset the Disparity

Improving Access to Molecular Testing

A critical component of implementing precision medicine in pediatric brain tumor care is the availability of adequate molecular diagnostics’ access in LMICs. While the 2021 WHO-CNS-5 Classification recommends methylome profiling for a layered diagnosis of pediatric brain tumors, its widespread use remains impractical in most LMICs due to challenges in accessibility and affordability. Significant barriers include underdeveloped infrastructure, high costs, and a shortage of trained personnel, making even conventional molecular testing methods difficult to implement widely.

A tiered approach to precision medicine diagnostics offers a feasible alternative. Starting with immunohistochemistry (IHC), followed by more affordable conventional techniques such as PCR and FISH for detecting clinically actionable targets, represents a practical pathway forward.27, 28 The establishment of regional reference laboratories that serve surrounding treatment centers could help consolidate expertise and resources. Importantly, many of these conventional tests can be performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues rather than requiring fresh samples, making them more compatible with existing workflows.

Investing in regional laboratory infrastructure and capacity-building initiatives focused on conventional molecular methods, in tandem with robust (neuro)pathology training, presents an achievable strategy for LMICs to deliver precision oncology care in pediatric brain tumors. Collaborative models such as “twinning” between centers in high-income countries (HICs) and LMICs can further enhance diagnostic capacity through sample processing support, training, and knowledge exchange. Telepathology, regional referral laboratories following a hub-and-spoke model, and future incorporation of AI-based tools can strengthen diagnostic networks. Long-term sustainability can be supported by initiatives such as the National Cancer Grid of India’s pathology and molecular External Quality Assurance Schemes (EQAS), the WHO’s Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer (GICC), and St. Jude’s Virtual Neuro-oncology Training Program. Ultimately, it is essential to develop locally relevant diagnostic algorithms that integrate histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular diagnostics in a way that reflects the therapeutic options available in the region and their impact on clinical decision-making. Improving Access to Targeted Therapies in LMICs

The World Health Organization (WHO) publishes the Model List of Essential Medicines for Children (EMLc), which is updated every two years.31 The WHO EMLc serves as a vital global tool for improving child health by promoting access to essential medicines, encouraging their rational and appropriate use, reducing costs, and supporting the development of child-specific formulations. Recommendations are based on parameters such as disease burden, efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness. The list includes antineoplastic drugs and targeted therapies that are intended to be consistently available, of assured quality, and affordable for individuals and healthcare systems. While inclusion in the list does not guarantee access, it provides a critical mechanism for governments and policymakers to advocate for the availability of these essential medicines. Currently, Everolimus—an mTOR inhibitor—is the only targeted therapy listed on the WHO EMLc for pediatric brain tumors (WHO Essential Medicines List for children, 9th List 2023). The future inclusion of clinically beneficial targeted therapies, such as MEK and BRAFV600E inhibitors, which have already been approved for pediatric brain tumors in Europe and North America, could influence government policy and stimulate initiatives to improve access. Additionally, increased demand may drive down costs and encourage the development of generic alternatives.

Global initiatives and advocacy efforts have the potential to significantly improve access to anti-cancer drugs, including targeted therapies, in LMICs. Programs such as the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer (GICC), in collaboration with St. Jude Global, have launched an ambitious five-year initiative to improve access to essential childhood cancer medicines. The WHO GICC focuses on improving outcomes in six priority pediatric cancers, including low-grade gliomas, by the year 2030. Through this initiative, key advancements are anticipated in areas such as early diagnosis infrastructure, development of multidisciplinary care teams, and improved access to essential medications, including targeted therapies. Additionally, the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) has established the Access to Oncology Medicines (ATOM) Coalition—a bold global partnership aimed at enhancing the availability and affordability of cancer medicines and diagnostics in LMICs. As more evidence emerges on the impact of precision medicine in managing pediatric brain tumors, these platforms could potentially expand their focus to include access to such advanced therapies.

Increased Access to Clinical Trials in LMICs

Improved participation in international prospective clinical trials offers multiple benefits. It provides an opportunity to strengthen local infrastructure and research capacity, enhance diagnostic capabilities, introduce access to novel therapies, and better understand region-specific challenges. Increased involvement also contributes to the efficiency of these trials by accelerating patient recruitment and shortening the time required to reach meaningful conclusions. When cancer genomics are integrated into diagnosis or treatment, data from LMICs add significant value to global knowledge—especially since biobanks have historically underrepresented these populations. However, most clinical trials involving newer therapies are still conducted in high-income countries (HICs), largely due to logistical barriers and limited research infrastructure in LMICs, which traditionally has made trial implementation more difficult. In adult oncology, global clinical trials such as the DESTINY-Lung04 (NCT05048797) and MARIPOSA trials have included non-small cell lung cancer patients from several LMICs.32 Although recent global pediatric brain tumor trials such as GLOBOTRK (NCT06528691), LOGGIC/FIREFLY-2 (NCT05566795), U-R-Immune (NCT06519682) have included centers from LMICs, many such trials need to be encouraged.

Collaboration with the Pharmaceutical Industry and Other Initiatives

Collaborations between pharmaceutical industry with global childhood oncology cooperative or advocacy groups can significantly improve access to newer therapies that are otherwise unavailable in many settings. One such initiative is the St. Jude Global Alliance Blinatumomab Humanitarian Access Program (BHAP), which aims to implement this life-saving therapy in LMICs. Similarly, a global coordinated effort involving international cooperative groups, non-governmental organizations and patient and parent advocacy groups could leverage the WHO GICC mandate to improving outcomes in Pediatric low-grade gliomas by ensuring worldwide access to targeted therapies.

Additional strategies to enhance access include compulsory licensing of patented drugs, promoting the development of generic alternatives, advocating for differential pricing policies, and enabling bulk procurement. An example of this approach is the bulk purchase model adopted by the National Cancer Grid of India where drugs, including targeted therapies, were procured at discounts varying from 23% to 99% (average 82%) due to high-volume purchasing.33 This model can also be extended to the procurement of reagents and laboratory equipment.

In conclusion, implementing precision medicine for pediatric brain tumors in LMICs demands strategic investment in diagnostics, equitable drug access, and collaborative research. While recent advances in molecular classification and targeted therapies have revolutionized care, disparities persist due to infrastructural, financial, and policy limitations. Promising models such as regional collaborations, telemedicine, and compassionate access programs underscore the feasibility of progress. Strengthening local capacity, advocating for policy reform, and engaging global stakeholders are vital to bridge the survival gap. With sustained efforts, precision medicine can become an achievable standard of care, ensuring equitable outcomes for children with brain tumors worldwide.
