Mechanistic insights into the divergent tenderness evolution of Wenchang and Huangma chickens during coconut water stewing
Authors: Libao Liu, Yucan Wu, Dong Han, Chunhui Zhang, Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to unravel the mechanism behind the paradoxical rapid tenderization of Wenchang chicken (WC) during coconut water stewing by contrasting its behavior with Huangma chicken (YC). WC exhibited a faster heating rate, allowing it to surpass critical protein denaturation temperatures ahead of YC. This accelerated thermal kinetics triggered an earlier and more intense protein oxidation and denaturation. The key mechanistic finding was that the faster heating induced a predominant transverse contraction of myofibrils in WC, which forcefully expelled immobilized water, leading to a swift tenderness transition. This was confirmed by a rapid decrease in T21 relaxation time and distinct microstructural changes. WC reached its optimal tenderness at 15 min, whereas YC required 20 min. This work moves beyond conventional paradigms by identifying the direction of myofibrillar shrinkage as a critical factor in meat texture, providing a theoretical foundation for precision cooking based on intrinsic material properties.Keywords: Wenchang chicken, Thermal kinetics, Tenderness mechanism, Transverse contraction, Water dynamics

Introduction

Chicken stands as one of the most prevalent agricultural commodities worldwide, valued for its high-quality protein, favorable nutritional profile characterized by low fat content, and exceptional feed conversion efficiency (Ren, Xu, Lin, Luo, & Zhang, 2024). According to the World Food and Agricultur-Statistical Yearbook 2025 published by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), global meat production reached 370 million tonnes in 2023. Between 2000 and 2023, only three species accounted for nearly 90% of the total global output: chicken, pig, and cattle. In 2023, chicken meat represented 34.2% of worldwide production. It achieved the fastest growth in both absolute and relative terms since 2000, and was the highest-produced meat type in 2023. This widespread consumption has spurred the development of diverse specialty products, profoundly shaped by geographical origins, local environments, and culinary traditions (Govoni et al., 2021). Stewed chicken with coconut water is a popular chicken products from Hainan Island, China (Zhang et al., 2023). The authenticity and quality of this product are intrinsically linked to the use of Wenchang chicken (WC), a specific yellow-feathered breed native to Hainan Island. Wenchang chicken is raised in a free-range system within forested mountains, where they forage on natural foods like wild fruits, insects, and snails, supplemented with small amounts of rice, bran, and sweet potatoes. After six months, they undergo a distinctive fattening period of approximately 60 days in quiet, dimly lit housing with restricted movement, this constitutes a characteristic practice within the Wenchang chicken production system. In 2004, WC was recognized as a National Geographical Indication product of China (Zhang, Wu, Zhang, & Huang, 2025). The unique island environment and tropical climate are credited with endowing WC with a tenderness significantly superior to that of inland chicken breeds (Zhang et al., 2023). This superior tenderness is empirically demonstrated in local culinary practice, where WC stewed in coconut water requires only 10–15 min of cooking to achieve a tender and smooth texture, aligning well with modern consumer preferences for quick preparation. However, a significant scientific paradox exists. General research on yellow-feathered chickens suggests that prolonged cooking is necessary to achieve optimal tenderness. Relevant studies have shown that cooking for up to two hours enhances the tenderness of the breast meat (Qi et al., 2018). This presents a significant contradiction between empirical practice and existing scientific reports. Therefore, investigating the tenderness evolution of WC during coconut water stewing and elucidating its underlying tenderization mechanism is of considerable scientific and practical importance.

Tenderness is generally governed by complex interplay of protein denaturation, moisture loss, and structural changes in muscle fibers (Christensen et al., 2012; Roldán, Antequera, Martín, Mayoral, & Ruiz, 2013). Heat-induced denaturation of myofibrillar proteins modifies the meat's ultrastructure, leading to moisture loss and consequently affecting textural properties such as tenderness and elasticity. Research indicates that cooking time and heating rate are critical factors influencing chicken texture (Wang et al., 2023). Moderate cooking can relax muscle fibers and enhance tenderness. However, prolonged high-temperature heating may cause excessive protein aggregation and moisture loss, thereby resulting in tougher meat (Yao et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024). Notably, the heating rate during cooking represents a crucial yet often overlooked variable, as it exerts a significant influence on the aforementioned processes (Cheng & Sun, 2008). From a general perspective, a slower heating rate is typically associated with enhanced water retention capacity and stable tenderness of meat products (Dominguez-Hernandez, Salaseviciene, & Ertbjerg, 2018). However, under identical external cooking conditions, different chicken breeds may exhibit distinct intrinsic thermal response patterns and heating rate characteristics. This discrepancy can be attributed to the variations in structural properties among different breeds. Therefore, this study aims to bridge the gap between empirical practice and scientific understanding by systematically investigating the tenderness evolution of Wenchang chicken (WC) in comparison with a typical inland breed, Huangma chicken (YC), during coconut water stewing. The specific objectives are to: 1) determine the optimal cooking time for both WC and YC based on the dynamic evolution of tenderness; and 2) elucidate the multifactorial mechanisms, thermal kinetics, protein denaturation/oxidation, water mobility, and microstructural changes, that influence the divergent tenderness development in WC and YC, with a particular focus on establishing a causal chain from heating rate to final texture.

Materials and methods

Materials

This study was approved by the Animal Experimentation Welfare and Ethics Committee of Institute of Food Science and Technology, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No.: IFST2026001). All animal experimental procedures were strictly carried out in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition) of the National Research Council (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), complying with the internationally recognized ethical standards for the use of animals in scientific research.

Wenchang chickens (WCs) and Huangma chickens (YCs) were selected as the representative breeds. All the chickens used in this study were female and reared under standard commercial conditions for 90 days prior to slaughter. Slaughter was performed in accordance with the Operating Procedure for Slaughter of Livestock and Poultry-chicken in China (GB/T 19478–2018) at a professional abattoir, and all slaughtered chickens were exclusively used for scientific research purposes. The WCs were provided by Hainan (Tan Niu) Wenchang chicken Co. Ltd. The YCs were provided by Xinjiang Pagelang Food Co., Ltd. Pectoralis major muscles (breast fillets) were dissected, with excess connective tissue and fat meticulously removed. Following preparation, the fillets were vacuum-sealed and stored at–40 °C until analysis. For the stewing process, fresh coconut water was extracted from young coconuts (Wenchang, China) within 24 h of purchase. To ensure consistency, juice from multiple coconuts of the same supplier batch was pooled to create a homogeneous experimental batch.

Preparation of sample

For cooking, chicken fillets were first obtained from an identical anatomical site on the Pectoralis major muscle and then cut into uniform pieces (50.0 g each) and placed into high-temperature resistant stewing bags with a material structure of Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET)/Aluminum foil (AL)/Retort-grade Cast Polypropylene (RCPP). Subsequently, 50.0 g of mixed coconut water (coconut water: water = 1: 1.5, w/w) was added to the bag and sealed under a vacuum. The sealed samples were placed in a boiling pot containing water at 98–100 °C. Cooking was performed in a JOANLAB WB100–4 programmable water bath (800 W, 220 V), which was set to and maintained at 100 °C throughout the experiment to ensure consistent boiling conditions. They were then boiled for 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min, timed from when the water temperature returned to 98–100 °C. The WC samples were labeled as WC0 (unstewed), WC5, WC10, WC15, WC20, WC25, and WC30, while the YC samples were labeled as YC0 (unstewed), YC5, YC10, YC15, YC20, YC25, and YC30. Upon completion of each predetermined stewing time, the retort pouch was immediately removed from the boiling water and transferred to an ice-water bath to rapidly halt the thermal process. The pouch was gently agitated in the ice bath for 5 min to ensure rapid and uniform cooling of the contents. Following cooling, the samples were brought to equilibrium with the ambient temperature in order to proceed with further testing.

Time-temperature value curve

The temperature distribution of chicken breast and coconut water was monitoredaccording to the method of (Xu et al., 2024). A scanning thermometer equipped with thermocouples was used to record temperatures. Probes measuring 1.5 cm in length and 2 mm in diameter were inserted and fixed at the geometric center of the chicken breast and the midpoint of the coconut water to track temperature changes. The temperature recorder was interfaced with a computer and recorded data at 2-s intervals throughout the experiment.

Shear force and texture determination

To ensure comparability of the data, all samples used for texture analysis and shear force measurement were obtained from an identical anatomical location.

Shear force was measured according to the method of (Wang et al., 2023) using a texture analyzer equipped with a blade that cut the samples perpendicular to the muscle fiber orientation. The operating parameters were as follows: pre-test speed of 1 mm/s, test speed of 2 mm/s, post-test speed of 10 mm/s, trigger force of 5 g, and a penetration depth of 10 mm.

Chicken breast meat was cut into 2 cm × 2 cm × 1 cm pieces along the muscle fiber. The texture properties were measured using a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, FTC) equipped with a P36 probe, and the experiment was conducted under room temperature conditions. The instrument parameters were set as follows: pre-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, test speed of 1.0 mm/s, post-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, trigger force of 5 g, and compression deformation of 30%.

Cooking loss determination

The cooking loss was determined by first blotting the surface moisture of the chicken and recording its weight (W₁). After cooking, the chicken was blotted dry and subsequently reweighed (W2). The cooking loss percentage was determined according to the following formula:W1−W2W1×100%.

Moisture distribution

Spin-spin relaxation time T2

The spin-spin relaxation time (T2) was determined using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analyzer (Niumag PQ001, Niumag Electric Corporation, Shanghai, China) operating at a resonance frequency of 23 MHz and an environmental temperature of 32 °C. Chicken samples of identical mass were placed in the LF-NMR probe, and T2 was measured by applying the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence under the following parameters: 4 repeated scans, 4000 echoes, and an inter-pulse delay of 250 μs between the 90° and 180° pulses (Xu et al., 2024). The resulting exponential decay curves were processed via inverse Laplace transformation using the MultiExp Inv Analysis software (Niumag Electric Corporation, Shanghai, China).

Magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted to visualize hydrogen proton signals in the chicken sample. The analysis was performed using a low-field nuclear magnetic imager (Model NMI20-60H-1, Shanghai Niumag Corporation, China) equipped with a Multi-Spin Echo (MSE) imaging sequence. The sample was housed in a tube with a diameter of 25 mm. The instrument parameters were set as follows: resonance frequency, 23.292 MHz; operating temperature, 32 °C, repetition time (TR), 800 ms; and echo time (TE), 18.2 ms. A total of six images were acquired by scanning the sample through six successive layers. All images were analyzed for proton density distribution and subjected to 3D reconstruction using the manufacturer's image processing software (Niumag Electric Company, China). Finally, the two-dimensional images were processed using Python: they were first converted to grayscale, and then a three-dimensional pseudo-colour image was constructed based on the grayscale values, following the methodology described by (Xu et al., 2025).

Extraction and content determination of myofibrillar proteins

Myofibrillar proteins were extracted following the method of (Tan et al., 2024) with slight modifications. Briefly, a 10.0 g chicken sample was homogenized with 60 mL of a 50 mmol/L phosphate extraction buffer (containing 0.1 mol/L NaCl, 10 mmol/L Na3PO4, 2 mmol/L anhydrous MgCl2, and 1 mmol/L EGTA, pH 7.0), using a homogenizer at 4 °C. The homogenization procedure consisted of 20 s bursts at 12000 rpm with 30 s intervals for cooling, repeated three times. Following centrifugation of the homogenate (2000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C), the pellet was collected and subjected to a washing procedure. This involved resuspension in 60 mL of phosphate extraction buffer, re-homogenization under the same conditions (30 s bursts with 10 s intervals, three repetitions), and centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C; this cycle was repeated three times. The washed pellet was then dispersed in 60 mL of 0.1 mol/L NaCl (pH 6.2) by vortexing, followed by centrifugation at 2000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was processed into a myofibrillar protein solution by resuspension and homogenization in phosphate buffer (containing 7.741 mmol/L Na2HPO4, 12.259 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 0.6 mol/L NaCl) for 30 s at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using the biuret method (Zheng, Wu, He, Yang, & Yang, 2017), and the solution was stored at 4 °C for use within 72 h.

Determination of protein secondary structure

The secondary structure of protein solutions was analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy according to the method of (Tan et al., 2019) with minor modifications. Protein solutions were adjusted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL and measured at room temperature across the infrared spectral range of 500–4000 cm−1. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) was specifically analyzed using PeakFit 4.12 software. The relative content of secondary structure components was calculated based on peak area ratios.

Surface hydrophobicity determination

Surface hydrophobicity was determined using the ANS fluorescence method (Wang et al., 2023). The protein samples were serially diluted and then reacted with ANS reagent (8 mmol/L), followed by 10 min of incubation in the dark. Fluorescence measurements were performed under the following conditions: excitation wavelength of 374 nm, emission wavelength of 480 nm, and slit width of 5 nm. A phosphate buffer containing 0.6 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0) served as the blank control. Surface hydrophobicity was expressed as the slope of the fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration curve.

Total sulfhydryl contents

The sulfhydryl group content was determined using the 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) method. In brief, 0.1 g of sample was homogenized with 1 mL of extraction buffer and centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 10 min. The protein content in the supernatant was quantified by the biuret method (Zheng et al., 2017). After adding DTNB reagent, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 412 nm.

Histological analysis

Histological analysis was performed according to the Sirius Red staining method described by (Xu et al., 2024) with minor modifications. Chicken breast samples were cut into small cubes (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm) along the muscle fiber orientation. The tissue cubes were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen at −160 °C for 1 h, and 10-μm-thick cross-sections were prepared perpendicular to the muscle fiber direction using a cryostat. The sections were transferred to acetone for fixation (24 h) followed by rinsing with tap water. After fixation, samples were stained in sirius red solution for 1 h. Post-staining, sections were washed with tap water, differentiated in 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid for 30 s, and rinsed again with tap water for 15 min. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated through three 10 min changes of absolute ethanol. Final clearing was achieved in xylene for 10 min before mounting with neutral gum. Slides were examined under a light microscope, and images were captured at 15 × magnification.

Scanning electron microscopy

The microstructure was examined according to the SEM method described by (Palka & Daun, 1999). Samples were cut into rectangular blocks (2 mm × 2 mm × 5 mm) and fixed in 2.5% (w/v) glutaraldehyde solution for 24 h. After fixation, the samples were rinsed with 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.3) and maintained at room temperature for 2 h. Following a distilled water wash, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (two changes per concentration, 1 h each). The dehydrated samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and fractured, then dried using a supercritical CO₂ dryer. The fractured surfaces were mounted on SEM stubs with the observation side facing upward and sputter-coated with gold using an Eiko IB5 ion coater. Microstructural imaging was performed with a Quanta 200 FEG field-emission environmental scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Representative micrographs were captured at 500 × magnification.

Transmission electron microscope

Microstructural changes in chicken samples were observed by TEM following the method of (Gawat, Boland, Chen, Singh, & Kaur, 2024). Samples were sectioned into longitudinal strips (2 mm × 2 mm × 1 mm) along the muscle fiber orientation and immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative (pH 7.4) for 48 h at 4 °C. Post-fixation was performed in 1% osmium tetroxide solution, followed by dehydration through a graded acetone series. After pretreatment, 100-nm ultrathin sections were prepared and stained with lead citrate for 4 min. The stained sections were maintained at room temperature until TEM examination. Ultrastructural observations were conducted using a transmission electron microscope. Sarcomere lengths were measured from TEM images (8000 × magnification) using ImageJ software (v1.53a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each sample, three random images were analyzed, with sarcomere lengths measured across 10 muscle fibers per image.

Statistical analysis

Data significance analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were conducted with at least three replicates, and results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Graphical representations were generated using Origin 2022.The IBM SPSS Statistics 20 program was used to execute one-way analysis of variance and Duncan's multiple-range tests. P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Time-temperature profile

The time-temperature profile at the geometric center of the chicken breast was monitored during cooking, as shown in Fig. 1A. The geometric center temperature of the chicken was consistently slightly lower than that of the surrounding broth. This phenomenon is consistent with the findings reported by (Wang et al., 2023).The curve could be divided into three stages: a rapid increase phase (0–10 min), a slow increase phase (10–20 min), and a stable phase (20–30 min). After 5 min of cooking, the temperature of the chicken broth reached 78 °C, while the core temperatures of WC and YC rose to 70 °C and 63 °C, respectively. At the 10 min of cooking, the broth temperature reached 89 °C, whereas WC and YC reached 87 °C and 83 °C, respectively. Throughout the rapid heating phase, YC consistently exhibited a lower core temperature than WC under identical external heating conditions. This suggests intrinsic differences in thermal properties between the breeds, potentially related to variations in muscle composition, density, or geometry, which consequently led to different heating rates. By 15 min, the broth reached 96 °C, with WC and YC at 95 °C and 92 °C, respectively. After 20 min, temperatures for both chicken breeds and the broth stabilized. Critically, WC reached key protein denaturation temperatures faster than YC. For instance, WC surpassed 70 °C within 5 min, a temperature range where significant myofibrillar protein denaturation and aggregation commence (Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). This differential heating rate, a factor often overlooked in breed comparisons, is crucial as it dictates the kinetics of subsequent physicochemical changes. The faster heating rate of WC likely initiated protein denaturation and structural changes earlier, setting the stage for its divergent tenderness profile compared to the slower-heating YC.

Shear force and texture profile analysis

The changes in chicken tenderness during cooking were assessed by measuring shear force and texture characteristics (Table 1). Uncooked chicken exhibited relatively low shear force values. After cooking, the shear force values increased. The peak shear force for WC occurred at 10 min, after which it decreased, stabilizing between 15 and 25 min (P > 0.05) before increasing slightly at 30 min. Conversely, YC showed peak shear force values between 10 and 15 min, followed by a decrease. The decrease in chicken tenderness was attributed to the temperature increase from 20 °C to 87 °C in WC and to 83 °C in YC. This rise in temperature caused the denaturation of myofibrillar proteins, resulting in muscle fiber contraction and a reduced water-holding capacity (Wang et al., 2023). Notably, WC demonstrated a tendency for improved tenderness after 15 min of cooking, while YC exhibited a similar enhancement after 20 min. This phenomenon likely occurred because the tenderizing effect of collagen breakdown partially counteracted the hardening effect caused by the denaturation of myofibrillar proteins in the meat (Christensen, Purslow, & Larsen, 2000). Similarly, the texture results indicated that the hardness and chewiness of WC decreased after 15 min of cooking, while elasticity decreased after 20 min. In contrast, the hardness, elasticity, adhesiveness, and chewiness of YC decreased after 20 min of cooking, whereas cohesiveness exhibited no significant changes throughout the cooking process (P > 0.05). The results indicated that 10 min and 20 min of cooking were critical time points for tenderness development in WC and YC, respectively. Additionally, the shear force of WC was consistently lower than that of YC throughout the cooking process, contributing to the superior tenderness of WC.

Cooking loss analysis

Cooking loss, reflecting the water-holding capacity during cooking, directly affected meat tenderness (Lu, Wu, Bian, & Zhao, 2025). As shown in Fig. 1B, cooking loss initially increased for both breeds, peaking at 15 min for WC and 20 min for YC, followed by a decrease. This trend paralleled the shear force results. The initial increase is due to protein denaturation and shrinkage, reducing water-binding capacity (García-Segovia, Andrés-Bello, & Martínez-Monzó, 2007). In addition, water possesses a relatively high thermal conductivity (∼0.6 W/m·K). The observation that WC exhibits more rapid water loss could imply that its structural organization facilitates both the initial retention and subsequent release of water, which may contribute to more efficient early-stage heat conduction. During the later stages of cooking, both chicken varieties exhibited decreased cooking losses, likely due to the conversion of collagen into gelatin, which can absorb additional moisture and reduce water loss. Furthermore, The aggregation of denatured protein molecules among the fibers increased the tortuosity of water migration pathways, potentially contributing to the observed reduction of cooking loss (Xiaomin Wang et al., 2023). The differences in cooking loss patterns between WC and YC during the cooking process may account for the variations in their final tenderness characteristics.

Changes in water distribution and MRI

To further investigate the differences in water holding capacity (WHC), low-field nuclear magnetic resonance was employed to determine the water distribution. The T2 relaxation time distributions of the samples are presented in Fig. 2A and B. WC and YC during the cooking process. In general, muscle water can be categorized into three distinct forms, as characterized by transverse relaxation time (T2): (a) bound water (T2b, 0–10 ms), which is tightly associated with macromolecules; (b) immobilized water (T21, 10–100 ms), which is entrapped within the myofibrillar network; and (c) free water (T22, 100–1000 ms), residing in the extra-myofibrillar spaces (Wang et al., 2023). As cooking time increased, the proportions of bound water and immobilized water in WC and YC exhibited a gradual decline. Table 2 showed the specific distribution of relaxation times. The relaxation times for uncooked WC were 1.81 ms, 48.83 ms, and 430.92 ms, whereas those for uncooked YC were 1.82 ms, 45.35 ms, and 420.30 ms. These findings are consistent with the results reported by (Lu et al., 2025). (Yao et al., 2023) attributed the reduced water-holding capacity of fresh muscle to its higher T21, which signifies enhanced mobility of immobilized water. Therefore, the immobilized water in WC exhibited greater mobility than that in YC, resulting in increased moisture loss during cooking, which aligns with the results of cooking loss. With the extension of cooking time, the T21 and T22 values of both WC and YC exhibited a decreasing trend with extended cooking time, which aligns with the findings of (Wang et al., 2023). This correlation indicates that prolonged thermal processing shortens the relaxation times of the cooked meat, consequently reducing water mobility (Shaarani, Nott, & Hall, 2006). Meanwhile, a decrease in T22 has been attributed to the loss of water molecules from the system during processing (Ishiwatari, Fukuoka, & Sakai, 2013). However, WC showed a more rapid decrease in T21 during cooking, indicating greater loss of immobilized water. This accelerated decline might be attributed to the rapid temperature increase that WC experienced during the initial cooking stage. At higher heating rates, the contraction of the protein network was accompanied by a decreased distance between the thick and thin filaments (Shi et al., 2021). These results agreed with the sarcomere length measurements of WC (Table 3). Similarly, WC demonstrated a rapid decrease in T22 during the 15–25 min cooking period, indicating significant moisture loss occurred during this cooking phase.

Protein secondary structure analysis

The secondary structure of proteins has been shown to significantly affect protein cross-linking and protein-water interactions, thereby influencing the tenderness of meat (Han, Zhang, Zheng, Li, & Shao, 2019). Fig. 4A depicts the distribution of protein secondary structures, which includes α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil. The results indicated a decrease in α-helix and β-turn structures, along with an increase in β-sheet and random coil in cooked chicken compared to uncooked chicken. A similar phenomenon was observed by (Wang et al., 2023) during the thermal processing of meat products. With prolonged cooking time, the contents of α-helix and β-turn in WC gradually decreased, while the contents of β-sheet and random coil structures progressively increased. YC displayed similar trends in secondary structure changes during cooking, mirroring those observed in WC. This phenomenon can be attributed to the conformational shift of protein secondary structure from α-helix and β-turn to β-sheet and random coils. Such transformations facilitate protein denaturation and subsequent aggregation, which ultimately induces myofiber contraction and water loss during heating (Sun et al., 2022). This indicated that the degree of protein oxidation may be positively correlated with heating time, similar to the sulfhydryl content results (Fig. 4B).

Protein denaturation and oxidation characteristics during stewing. (A) Relative content of protein secondary structures (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil). (B) Changes in total sulfhydryl content. (C) Changes in surface hydrophobicity.

The contents of α-helix and β-turn in uncooked WC were lower than those in YC, indicating that the initial oxidation degree of oxidation in WC was higher. Throughout the cooking period of 5 to 15 min, the contents of α-helix and β-turn in WC consistently remained lower than those in YC, while the content of β-sheet was higher than that in YC. The increase in β-sheet indicated the formation of an ordered structure, which was related to the improvement of the initial tenderness of WC. However, water loss remained evident at this stage. This was primarily because the formation of β-sheets could not fully compensate for the water loss induced by muscle fiber shrinkage (Xu et al., 2024). Meanwhile, the results also indicate that during the early stages of cooking, the rate of protein denaturation in WC was faster. When the temperature exceeded 70 °C, the aggregation of actomyosin became the predominant event, a process that coincided with the structural transition to β-sheet and random coil configurations (Yao et al., 2023). It can be known from the temperature rise curve that the center temperature of WC has reached 70 °C after 5 min of cooking (Fig. 1A), while the center temperature of YC was still below 70 °C. This might be the reason for the rapid denaturation of the protein in WC. However, within 25 to 30 min of cooking, the α-helix content of WC was lower than that of YC, indicating that the degree of protein denaturation in YC was more pronounced during the later stages of cooking. Therefore, changes in the secondary structure of proteins influenced the evolution of tenderness in chicken during cooking. Furthermore, the alterations in sulfhydryl content indicated that WC exhibits a characteristic of rapid protein oxidation, which significantly affected tenderness throughout the cooking process.

Changes in protein sulfhydryl content

Protein oxidation was associated with reduced meat tenderness (Li, He, et al., 2024; Li, Wang, et al., 2024). During thermal processing, the sulfhydryl groups in proteins become susceptible to free radical attack, leading to their oxidation into disulfide bonds. Therefore, the concentration of sulfhydryl groups is often measured to assess the extent of oxidation and denaturation of proteins (Jiao et al., 2024). A significant (P < 0.05) reduction in sulfhydryl content was observed in both WC and YC during cooking (Fig. 4B), confirming a comparable trend to that documented by (Wang et al., 2023). This indicated that the degree of protein oxidation was positively associated with the intensity of heat treatment (Tan et al., 2024). During the initial 0–5 min of the cooking process, a significant decline in sulfhydryl group content was observed. This decline was attributed to the core temperatures of WC and YC exceeding 60 °C within this time frame. Thermal treatment at 60 °C and 70 °C accelerated the oxidation of sulfhydryl groups in the samples due to the elevated temperature conditions (Yao et al., 2023). During the steaming period of 5 to 15 min, the formation rate of carbonyl compounds slowed down, followed by a subsequent decline. The sulfhydryl content of WC was lower than that of YC during the 5 to 15 min cooking period; however, the difference became insignificant after 20 min of cooking. This indicated that the protein denaturation rate of WC was faster in the early stages of cooking, which was consistent with the secondary structure results. The mechanical properties of protein gels can be improved by appropriate oxidation. In contrast, excessive oxidation resulting from prolonged high-temperature heating tends to promote protein over-aggregation, which is associated with a tougher texture and consequently reduced tenderness (Mitra, Lametsch, Akcan, & Ruiz-Carrascal, 2018). In summary, the different degrees of protein oxidation in WC and YC led to distinct tenderness characteristics.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

Heat-induced protein unfolding or aggregation is a process that involves the exposure or burial of polar groups. As such, surface hydrophobicity, which depends on the distribution of these polar groups, is a common indicator for monitoring changes in the tertiary structure of proteins (Wang et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 4C, raw meat from both WC and YC exhibited low protein surface hydrophobicity. As the temperature increased, the surface hydrophobicity gradually rose, indicating that the degree of oxidative denaturation of the protein intensified. This process exposed the hydrophobic amino acids within the protein molecule and altered the protein's conformation (Traore et al., 2012), both breeds showed a rapid initial increase in surface hydrophobicity, indicating massive protein unfolding and exposure of hydrophobic groups upon heating. WC exhibited a more pronounced and earlier rise, reaching its peak at 15 min, coinciding precisely with its identified window of optimal tenderness. This confirms that the accelerated protein denaturation in WC, driven by its faster heating rate, is a key initiator of its early tenderization.

During the late stage of cooking (25–30 min), the surface hydrophobicity exhibited a decreasing trend, which may be attributed to the formation of soluble or insoluble protein aggregates. Elevated temperature is a key driver of conformational changes during protein aggregation. These changes cause the re-embedding of exposed hydrophobic residues into aggregates, thereby diminishing surface hydrophobicity (Yao, Huang, et al., 2024; Yao, Zhao, et al., 2024). Notably, WC's hydrophobicity began to decline earlier and more sharply after 15 min, suggesting a more rapid progression into the aggregation stage. This aggregation likely contributes to the stabilization of its tenderized structure.

Tissue structure analysis

Sirius red staining and scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis

The microstructure of muscle fibers determined water mobility and mechanical strength of meat (Yao, Huang, et al., 2024; Yao, Zhao, et al., 2024). Fig. 5 visually presented histological cross-sections of raw and cooked chicken during heating. After sirius red staining (Fig. 5A), light yellow myofibrils and red collagenous connective tissues were clearly observed, providing intuitive evidence for the evolution of tenderness during cooking (Xu et al., 2024). In uncooked chicken, adjacent muscle fibers were tightly packed with minimal gaps. During the cooking process, the rising temperatures caused differential contraction of the muscle fibers, leading to their separation (Jia et al., 2022). In WC, protein denaturation caused transverse shrinkage after 10 min of cooking, resulting in elongated cross-sections. By 20 min, large distinct gaps emerged, causing immobilized water from the myofibrillar network to be expelled into these spaces. This phenomenon increased the free water content and elevated cooking loss (Yao et al., 2023). YC exhibited slender gaps at 20 min and larger cavities at 25 min, indicating significant free water loss that affects tenderness at these respective time points.

Additionally, SEM (Fig. 5B) revealed the three-dimensional structure of WC and YC. In uncooked chicken, the muscle fibers were tightly packed, which is consistent with the results of sirius red staining. After 10 min of cooking, distinct fissures appeared between the muscle fibers in both chicken varieties, indicating significant contraction of the myofibers due to high-temperature heating. YC exhibited particularly pronounced fissures and gaps after 25 min of cooking. Generally, prolonged high-temperature cooking caused significant contraction and degradation of muscle fibers. After 30 min of cooking, granular deposits or solutes were observed distributed across the cross-sections of muscle fibers and in the inter-fiber spaces of both WC and YC, which were associated with myofiber degradation. Consequently, heat treatment induced varying degrees of muscle fiber contraction in WC and YC, significantly affecting water loss. These structural differences likely contributed to the distinct tenderness characteristics observed between the two chicken varieties.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis

The myofibrillar structure of chicken at various cooking times is illustrated in Fig. 6. The results demonstrated that thermal processing induces structural changes in chicken. Throughout the cooking process, both WC and YC displayed increased Z-line thickness and weakened junctions between Z-lines and I-bands at all observed time points-findings that are consistent with typical heat-induced changes in meat products (Gawat et al., 2024). Fig. 6 illustrated the microstructural changes in the myofibrils of WC cooked with coconut water. The uncooked chicken exhibited a compact structure, with tightly arranged I-bands and A-bands connected to Z-lines. After 5 min of cooking, the Z-lines began to thicken, and by 10 min, the sarcolemma started to detach from the myofibrils. Fig. 6 illustrated the microstructural changes in YC cooked with coconut water. While the uncooked structure resembled that of WC, it exhibited greater compactness. After 5 min of cooking, Z-line thickening was observed, followed by weakened Z-line/I-band junctions and significant sarcolemma detachment after 20 min. Chian et al. (2019) proposed that such structural disruption positively correlates with meat tenderization as it reduces myofibrillar tensile strength. Therefore, the key time points for tenderness development were identified at 10 min for WC and 20 min for YC when cooked with coconut water.

Changes in myofibrillar structure also include the temperature-dependent contraction extent of contraction in myofibrils and the length of sarcomeres, both of which are known to significantly influence the quality characteristics of meat (Ertbjerg & Puolanne, 2017). Table 3 presented the variations in sarcomere length and shrinkage rate of WC and YC during cooking with coconut water. The sarcomere longitudinal and transverse lengths of uncooked WC and YC were 1.58 μm and 0.83 μm, and 1.92 μm and 0.90 μm, respectively. It was found that the sarcomere length of WC was shorter than that of YC. After cooking, the sarcomere lengths of WC and YC shortened, and their contraction intensity increased, which was a recognized phenomenon in the thermal processing of meat products (Astruc, Gatellier, Labas, Lhoutellier, & Marinova, 2010). Meanwhile, WC exhibited less longitudinal sarcomere contraction than YC during cooking process, but greater transverse contraction. This phenomenon can be attributed to transverse contraction enlarging the space between muscle fibers and the endomysium, which expelled water from the T21 domain into the T22 domain, as proposed by (Song et al., 2021). Our findings align with this model, as evidenced by the SEM results (Fig. 5B) and the reduction in relaxation times (Table 2). Additionally, WC demonstrated significantly greater transverse sarcomere contraction than longitudinal contraction during the initial cooking stage (0–15 min). This indicated that transverse contraction predominantly influenced microstructural changes in WC at early cooking stages, consequently affecting water status and tenderness evolution. The results demonstrated that transverse contraction of muscle fiber bundles occurred between 40 and 60 °C, while longitudinal contraction likely resulted from sarcomere shortening at 60–80 °C (Song et al., 2021). This indicated that the degree of contraction depended on the time-temperature distribution within the muscle (Ertbjerg & Puolanne, 2017). The study also found that WC and YC exhibited different heating rates during the first 15 min of cooking, which likely contributed to their distinct myofibrillar contraction patterns. In summary, the rapid heating rate in WC during initial cooking stages led to pronounced transverse sarcomere contraction, consequently altering its water distribution characteristics.

Principal components analysis (PCA)

To investigate the relationship between shear force, protein oxidation, moisture distribution and myofibrillar structure, PCA was conducted to examine the correlations between cooking time and various parameters, including shear force, relaxation time, sulfhydryl content, secondary structure, surface hydrophobicity, sarcomere length, and shrinkage rate. As shown in Fig. 7, PC1 and PC2 accounted for 84.0% and 9.7% of the total variance, respectively, indicating that the model demonstrated strong robustness and predictability. The uncooked WC and YC samples were positioned on the right side of PC1, while the other samples were distributed on the left side. During cooking, WC samples clustered in the lower left quadrant, whereas YC samples were primarily located in the upper left quadrant. This distribution suggested that WC and YC exhibited distinct tenderness characteristics throughout the cooking process. The shear force was observed on the left side of PC1, alongside cooking loss, surface hydrophobicity, and shrinkage rate. The close proximity of these parameters indicates significant positive correlations among them. In contrast, shear force was positioned in the opposite direction from relaxation time and sarcomere length, demonstrating negative correlations. The PCA results demonstrated that changes in water status and myofibrillar contraction degree most effectively reflected the tenderness variations between WC and YC during the cooking process. Previous studies have shown that moisture loss, protein denaturation, and myofiber shrinkage collectively influence meat tenderness (Roldán et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2024). It is generally accepted that the thermal denaturation of muscle proteins during cooking results in transverse and longitudinal myofibrillar shrinkage. This process forces out the water confined within the myofibrils into the larger extracellular spaces. The water in these spaces exhibits high mobility, facilitating its eventual loss (Yao et al., 2023). The study found that while these characteristics were linked to meat hardness, WC consistently demonstrated superior tenderness compared to YC during cooking. This difference may be attributed to the inherently lower shear force and hardness of raw WC in comparison to YC, or to distinct properties of the myofibrillar proteins (Kong, Tang, Lin, & Rasco, 2008). The PCA results also showed that WC samples cooked for 10 min and 15 min were located in the same quadrant and in close proximity to the transverse shrinkage rate, indicating that the tenderness changes in WC during 10–15 min of cooking were related to myofibrillar transverse shrinkage. In conclusion, the results demonstrated that the key factors influencing the evolution of tenderness in WC and YC during cooking with coconut water were: 1) the rapid heating rate, which caused accelerated protein denaturation and subsequently affected myofibrillar shrinkage; and 2) the transverse shrinkage in WC, which created structural voids within the muscle tissue that enhanced water mobility.

Integrated discussion: a multi-level mechanistic model for the rapid tenderization of Wenchang Chicken

The empirical superiority of WC in quick-stewing presents a fascinating paradox against the conventional wisdom of meat science, which often associates prolonged heating with improved tenderness. Our results demonstrate that this paradox arises not from a single factor, but from a unique, breed-specific tenderization trajectory driven by a cascade of physicochemical events. Herein, we propose a multi-level mechanistic model (Fig. 8) that delineates how WC's intrinsic properties enable its rapid attainment of optimal tenderness, fundamentally distinguishing it from the common YC

Mechanism of divergent tenderization pathways in Wenchang chicken (WC) and Huangma chicken (YC) during stewing.

(1)The Initiator-Divergent thermal kinetics and early protein denaturation

The primary divergence point between WC and YC lies in their thermal kinetics. Under identical external heating, WC exhibited a consistently faster heating rate, reaching denaturation-critical temperatures several minutes ahead of YC (Fig. 1A). This accelerated energy input is not merely a temporal advantage but the trigger for an entirely different denaturation pathway. The higher thermal load per unit time in WC led to a more abrupt and intensive denaturation of myofibrillar proteins, as evidenced by the rapid decline in sulfhydryl content (Fig. 4B) and the swift transition in protein secondary structure (lower α-helix, higher β-sheet, Fig. 4A) during the first 10–15 min. This early and aggressive protein denaturation set the stage for a distinct microstructural failure mode, which is the core of our proposed mechanism.

(2)The core mechanism-Preferential transverse myofibril collapse

Conventional tenderization in many meats involves gradual longitudinal sarcomere shortening and connective tissue dissolution. Our data reveal that WC follows a predominant and rapid transverse contraction of the myofibrillar lattice. TEM and sarcomere length analysis (Fig. 6, Table 3) provide direct evidence. While YC underwent significant longitudinal shortening, WC displayed markedly greater transverse shrinkage. The initial, faster heating of WC causes simultaneous and violent denaturation of a large number of myofilaments, which generates substantial lateral compressive stresses that force the thick and thin filaments into a more densely packed arrangement, effectively squeezing the myofibril from the sides. This lateral squeezing action directly expelled immobilized water (the T21 population) from within the myofibrils (Table 2, Fig. 2). The structural integrity of the myofibrils was compromised from the inside-out through transverse collapse, rather than just through longitudinal shortening.

(3)The Outcome-Accelerated water mobilization and early tenderness onset

The rapid transverse collapse dictated the fate of water and, consequently, the tenderness profile. For WC, the mechanism was Expulsion-Led Tenderization. The violent expulsion of water led to a rapid increase in cooking loss, peaking at 15 min (Fig. 1B). The MRI results (Fig. 3), showing a drastic drop in proton density by 15 min, visually confirm this massive, early-stage water mobilization. While this implies higher total water loss, it critically results in a swift reduction of internal mechanical resistance. The meat structure transitions from a tough, hydrated gel to a more fragile, porous matrix at an accelerated pace, defining the short 10–15 min optimal window. For YC, the mechanism was gradual and longitudinal shrinkage-led tenderization. The slower heating allowed for a more conventional, gradual denaturation and longitudinal contraction. Water was lost more slowly and steadily, with cooking loss peaking later at 20 min. The tenderness improvement was delayed, relying more on the gradual weakening of the perimysial connective tissue and slower moisture redistribution.

Overall, the faster heating rate, likely due to WC's intrinsic muscle composition, initiates a cascade: accelerated protein denaturation generates dominant lateral forces within the myofibril, leading to preferential transverse contraction. This structural breakdown forcefully releases trapped water all at once, leading to a quick and early drop in shear force and creating a short period of ideal tenderness. This mechanism moves beyond the traditional focus on longitudinal shortening and connective tissue, providing a new dimension to understanding how intrinsic material properties of different breeds dictate their optimal thermal processing. To further validate this hypothesis and fully decipher the breed-specific tenderization pathways, future studies should directly quantify collagen properties, including content, cross-linking types, and thermal solubility in WC and YC. Such work constitutes a valuable and logical next step toward a comprehensive mechanistic understanding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that WC consistently maintained higher temperatures than YC prior to reaching the peak temperature. This accelerated thermal input triggers an early and intensive denaturation of myofibrillar proteins, culminating in a predominant transverse contraction of the myofibrillar lattice, a phenomenon quantitatively verified through TEM and sarcomere length analysis. This specific microstructural response, which distinguishes WC from conventional longitudinal shrinkage patterns observed in YC samples, directly facilitates the rapid expulsion of immobilized water, as conclusively evidenced by the sharp decline in T₂₁ relaxation time and pronounced cooking loss. Consequently, WC undergoes a swift structural transition from a hydrated gel to a fragile matrix, achieving optimal tenderness (characterized by a shear force range of 17.71–19.99 N) within a brief 10–15 min window. These results establish that the superior and rapid tenderization of WC is not merely an empirical observation but is driven by a well-defined mechanistic cascade, progressing from a differential heating rate and accelerated protein denaturation/oxidation to predominant transverse myofibrillar contraction, rapid water mobilization, and an early tenderness onset.

These findings provide a scientific basis for the intelligent design of cooking strategies tailored to the structural characteristics of specific poultry breeds.Beyond mechanistic insights, this study provides practical guidance for both industry and culinary practice. The accelerated denaturation in breeds like WC indicates that shorter, more intensive processes can optimize tenderness and yield, improving efficiency and product differentiation. For consumers and chefs, the distinct “tenderness time windows” offer a clear guideline, thus helping to avoid over- or undercooking and ensure an ideal texture.
