A Physician Made Biological Stent Graft for the Replacement of a Left Renal Vein Leiomyosarcoma
Authors: Francesca Maria Feroldi, Bruno Gargiulo, Magali Fau, Xavier Berard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPrimary venous leiomyosarcoma is an unusual and aggressive tumour; its presentation on the renal vein is rare. This case study presents a 50 year old man with severe obesity who was incidentally diagnosed with a left renal vein leiomyosarcoma during pre-operative evaluations for a cholecystectomy. Following a multidisciplinary discussion with oncology and vascular surgeons, the patient underwent complete resection of the left renal vein along with the mass.TechniqueThis article proposes an innovative surgical technique for the reconstruction of the left renal vein in case of a left renal vein leiomyosarcoma. Reconstruction was achieved using a physician made biological stent graft, employing a nitinol bare stent wrapped in a pericardial patch. The video provides a step by step explanation of the procedure. Isolation and the surgical technique for the reconstruction of the left renal vein following en bloc resection of the leiomyosarcoma are shown, followed by reconstruction of the vein using a nitinol stent covered with bovine pericardium. Several precautions were adopted to minimise the risk of intestinal fistula, including heterotopic graft re-implantation and omentoplasty.DiscussionComplete surgical excision remains the cornerstone of vein sarcoma treatment and offers the best chance for disease control. The literature has only reported en bloc resection of renal vein sarcomas with nephrectomy. This case report presents an innovative surgical technique for left renal vein replacement, showing a potential alternative in the management of this rare condition in order to preserve the vein.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcomas are a rare and heterogeneous group of malignant neoplasms, accounting for <1% of all tumours.1 This case study presents a 50 year old man, a non-smoker, with severe obesity (BMI 42), a haemangioma of the left lower limb, and gastroesophageal reflux disease. His medical history included multiple previous operations, such as hemithyroidectomy for papillary thyroid carcinoma and polypectomy. During pre-operative exams for cholecystectomy, imaging revealed a suspicious mass in the left renal vein (LRV), prompting a biopsy (Fig. 1, Video). Histological analysis from computed tomography (CT) guided needle biopsy confirmed a smooth muscle cell neoplasm with heterozygous loss of function mutations in RB1 and p53, raising strong suspicion of malignancy, consistent with leiomyosarcoma. At the time of admission, his only ongoing medical treatment was 40 mg pantoprazole twice daily. His case underwent multidisciplinary discussion with a vascular surgeon and a surgeon who specialised in abdominal sarcoma to define a plan of treatment. Given the size of the mass (3×3×2.5 cm) and its distance from the renal hilum, en bloc resection of the mass and the LRV, followed by vascular reconstruction, was proposed to the patient. As part of the surgical planning, nephrectomy and vena cava reconstruction had been discussed as bail out strategies to achieve a clear surgical margin. Reconstruction of the LRV was achieved with a physician made biological stent graft involving bare nitinol stent (S.M.A.R.T Flex Stent 12x60 mm Cordis, Zurich, Switzerland) wrapped in a bovine pericardial patch (XenoSure 10x6 cm LeMaitre, Burlington, USA) sutured with 5–0 polypropylene (Fig. 2B). The distal anastomosis was completed first, connecting the covered stent to the LRV close to the hilum. Partial cross clamping of the vena cava on the left side allowed end to side anastomosis between the stent and vena cava with a continuous 5–0 polypropylene suture (Fig. 2C). To avoid the risk of duodenal erosion and to reduce bowel compression on the graft, the reconstructed LRV was transposed caudally onto the IVC. The IVC anastomosis was performed laterally on the left side of the vena cava to avoid excessive stretching and kinking of the graft. At the end of the surgery, gentle mobilisation of a retroperitoneal fat pad was used to cover the stent graft to avoid bowel contact.

The procedure was completed successfully with no intra-operative complications and the patient was discharged on the fourth post-operative day with rivaroxaban. The results of the histological examination confirmed that the mass was a leiomyosarcoma originating in the wall of the renal vein; complete excision with negative margins (R0) was seen. At the three month follow up, the patient was in good general condition. The angio CT showed graft patency without complications at the anastomotic sites and no recurrence of tumour disease; renal function was preserved.

DISCUSSION

Leiomyosarcoma of the renal vein is an exceedingly rare malignancy, representing a unique subset of soft tissue sarcomas originating from the smooth muscle cells of the vascular wall. Leiomyosarcoma of the IVC accounts for <2% of all leiomyosarcomas, with renal vein involvement being particularly uncommon. These tumours are notable for aggressive local invasion and significant potential for haematogenous spread. Renal vein leiomyosarcoma typically presents insidiously, with non-specific symptoms such as flank pain, haematuria, or a palpable mass. These vague clinical signs often delay diagnosis until the disease is advanced or metastatic, further complicating treatment strategies.2 Complete surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment and offers the best chance for disease control.3

Typical management involves nephrectomy, which was initially proposed to the patient. However, considering the patient's young age and pre-operative imaging showing a well localised mass, the multidisciplinary team opted for an innovative kidney sparing approach, maintaining nephrectomy as a bailout strategy if no safe margin could be achieved intra-operatively. To preserve renal function, LRV reconstruction was mandatory due to ligation of large collateral renal veins impairing venous outflow.

According to the literature, commonly used grafts for venous reconstruction include autologous saphenous vein, typically reserved for limited reconstructions, such as those performed during pancreatic cancer surgery, and synthetic graft expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) or ring reinforced grafts, which are preferred for extensive repairs involving large calibre veins, such as inferior or superior vena cava.4 Cryopreserved allografts can be employed in these settings but they offer similar compressibility of autologous veins.

A recent Delphi consensus highlighted the ongoing debate over optimal IVC lesion management.5 Nonetheless, ePTFE grafts are the current standard for infrarenal IVC reconstruction, although alternative materials may be chosen based on clinical factors and surgical expertise.

When short bypasses are needed, such as in pancreatectomy, vein grafts are suitable due to a lower risk of collapse under intra-abdominal pressure.6,7 The use of a ringed ePTFE graft prevents collapse in low pressure repairs and accommodates curved routes with minimal kinking risk, although it poses a potential for bowel erosion. In this case, these considerations were addressed by choosing an innovative solution: bare nitinol stent covered with a tubulized bovine pericardial patch. Given its retroperitoneal location, the graft must withstand external compression from surrounding structures and the collapse induced by negative intra-abdominal pressure after closure. This environment demands a robust material to maintain patency under variable pressures. Furthermore, the patient's obesity, oncological status, and high thrombotic risk influenced the choice of a bare nitinol stent, valued for its radial strength and resistance to deformation, and led to the patient's discharge with initiation of anticoagulant therapy. To mitigate bowel erosion risk, a biological covering, specifically, a pericardial patch, was applied. Additionally, technical adjustments were made to reduce graft bowel contact. Rather than placing the bypass in its anatomical position, the graft was re-implanted more distally onto the IVC. A retroperitoneal fat pad flap was also interposed between the graft and the bowel to provide an additional protective barrier.

Although the follow up period of three months represents a relatively short timeframe, the results obtained thus far confirm graft patency and the absence of tumour recurrence. This case adds to the literature by presenting a new surgical option for managing renal vein leiomyosarcoma, using a physician made biological stent graft.
