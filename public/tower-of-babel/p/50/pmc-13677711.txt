Dual benefit of esketamine-based opioid-free anesthesia on postoperative pain and mental health outcomes in patients undergoing bariatric surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chunxia Huang, Zichun Guan, Sheng Wang, Wu Liu, Fuqiang Zu, Jinkai Sun, Feng Geng, Rui Li, Gordon Tin Chun Wong, Ye Zhang, Andreas Reif, Dongxin Wang, Benli Jia, Lin Lu, Qi Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with morbid obesity are at a high risk of comorbid depressive and anxiety symptoms. While bariatric surgery is an effective treatment, conventional opioid-based anesthesia carries significant perioperative risks. Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) presents a safer alternative. Esketamine, known for its dual analgesic and rapid antidepressant effects, is a promising component of OFA. However, clinical evidence supporting its simultaneous benefits for both physical and psychological recovery in bariatric patients remains limited. In this double-blind, randomized clinical trial, patients scheduled for laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either esketamine-based anaesthesia or opioid-based anaesthesia with remifentanil during surgery. The co-primary outcomes were postoperative pain intensity within the first 24 h and mental health status at three months after surgery. Secondary outcomes included pharmacokinetics of esketamine and adverse events. A total of 84 patients were randomized, with 42 allocated to each group. Seventy-four participants (88.1%) completed the 3-month follow-up. Patients in the esketamine group experienced significantly lower postoperative pain intensity (P < 0.01) and a reduced incidence of moderate-to-severe pain (P < 0.05) within 24 h after surgery. Mental health outcomes at 3 months were also significantly better in the esketamine group, and adverse events rate did not differ significantly. Pharmacokinetic analysis showed that plasma esketamine concentrations increased rapidly during infusion, with early formation of the active metabolite (S)-norketamine. Compared to opioid-based anaesthesia, esketamine-based opioid-free anaesthesia was associated with improved postoperative analgesia and better mental health outcomes in morbidly obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery, without increasing adverse events.Subject terms: Depression, Pharmacodynamics

Introduction

While opioids provide potent analgesia, they can produce severe respiratory depression via their action on μ-opioid receptors expressed in the spinal cord, brainstem, and subcortical and cortical regions [1–3]. Furthermore, the use of postoperative opioids constitutes an independent risk factor for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Via both mechanisms, opioid-based anesthesia (OBA) delays recovery, prolongs the hospital length of stay, and subsequently increases health care costs [4–6]. These considerations are particularly pertinent for patients with morbid obesity undergoing bariatric surgery, as they frequently also suffer from obstructive sleep apnea, which renders them more prone to respiratory depression, and as PONV may lead to complications at the surgical site. Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) with multimodal analgesia has been developed to minimize such adverse outcomes. However, some OFA protocols elicited other safety concerns such as severe bradycardia and hypotension without reduction in overall postoperative opioid consumption or other improved outcomes as compared to OBA [7–9].

Laparoscopic bariatric surgery (LBS) confers significant benefits on long-term weight loss and physical comorbidities for patients suffering from morbid obesity [10, 11], and forms part of a global strategy to tackle the worldwide prevalence of obesity [12]. To improve postoperative outcomes by reducing complications and shortening hospital stays for patients with severe obesity, OFA protocols were modified regarding the Enhanced Recovery After Bariatric Surgery (ERABS) guidelines [13]. Intravenous non-opioid analgesics and adjuvants are crucial in multimodal analgesia regime, reducing opioid consumption and enhancing postoperative care in adult surgical patients with obesity [14]. Another critical aspect of opioid-sparing strategies is the use of combination drug therapies offering superior efficacy and reduced side effects using dexmedetomidine, ketamine, magnesium sulfate, dexamethasone and lidocaine [15–17]. However, it is currently difficult to standardize or optimize the ERABS guidelines with the existing inconsistent data for postoperative pain and PONV from different OFA regimes.

Esketamine is a powerful analgesic intravenous anesthetic that has a rapid onset and elimination effect, does not inhibit spontaneous respiration, and has a slight cardiovascular stimulatory effect. Its use as an opioid sparing agent can thus effectively combat opioid-related complications, such as life-threatening respiratory suppression [18]. Intranasally applied esketamine has been approved for treating resistant depression (which is associated with obesity [19]) and for the treatment of psychiatric emergencies in the context of depression [20]. However, the evidence for its safety and effectiveness as part of OFA, especially in LBS, is lacking [21, 22]. More data is required to assess whether intraoperative avoidance of opioids would affect overall opioid consumption and reduce opioid related side effects, while not compromising patient subjective well-being and long-term outcomes. This study aims to evaluate these objectives in the context of an esketamine-based OFA-protocol for LBS.

Experimental procedures

Study design

This prospective, double-blind, randomized mono-centric clinical trial was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200064460). Patients were enrolled from 9 October 2022 to 24 February 2024. All participants provided written informed consent following Declaration of Helsinki rules. This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

We recruited morbidly obese patients who were scheduled to undergo laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG). Inclusion criteria were patients aged between 18–65 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of Ⅱ- Ⅲ, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 40 kg/m² or ≥35 kg/m² with at least one co-existing disease. Exclusion criteria included (1) allergic to local anesthesia drugs; (2) pre-existing chronic pain or opioid use; (3) abnormal coagulation function; (4) local infection at the block site; (5) severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible patients were randomized using the centralized 24-h computerized randomization system on REDCap™ Cloud to allocate patients to the esketamine group or opioid group in a 1:1 ratio. Random numbers and group assignments were delivered to an independent researcher via opaque envelopes, who prepared the study drugs but did not participate in any other aspects of the trial. All patients and outcomes assessors were blinded to the group assignments. The 3-month follow-up assessments were conducted via telephone by trained investigators who were blinded to group allocation. The primary analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle by preserving randomized group assignment.

Intervention and anesthesia management

All patients received a standardized anesthetic and peri-operative management protocol, including general anesthesia, ultrasound-guided bilateral transverse abdominis plane (TAP) block (30 ml of 0.33% ropivacaine with 1 µg/kg dexmedetomidine), and intravenous dexmedetomidine (0.5 µg/kg over 10 min) (Supplement 1). Patients were then randomized to receive either: (1) opioid group: remifentanil 2 µg/kg bolus at induction followed by 5–15 µg/kg/h infusion for maintenance [23]; or (2) esketamine group: esketamine 0.5 mg/kg/h for induction and 0.25 mg/kg/h for maintenance. In both groups, propofol was continuously infused to maintain an appropriate bispectral index. The LSG procedure was performed by the same senior bariatric surgical team, and all intravenous anesthetics were dosed based on ideal body weight.

Outcome measurements

Our efficacy outcome was the postoperative pain intensity at predefined time points (0, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after surgery) using the visual analog scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the most severe pain) [24]. Mean VAS scores refer to the average VAS values at each individual time point across participants within each group. Postoperative pain trajectories refer to temporal changes in VAS scores across these time points, and were analyzed using the aligned rank transform method with Tukey-adjusted post hoc comparisons to account for repeated measures. The co-primary outcomes included: (1) the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and rescue antiemetic within postoperative 24 h; (2) the mental health evaluated by Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) and Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) before LSG and in postoperative month (POM) 3.

Analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat approach: all randomized participants with available data were included in the corresponding analyses. For mental health outcomes, participants lost to follow-up were handled through sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation and worst-case scenario methods (Supplement 1).

The secondary and other outcomes were the plasma pharmacokinetic parameters of esketamine, assessed in the esketamine group; a battery of scales considering gastrointestinal function, recovery quality and quality of life before LSG and in POM 3 (Supplement 1). Adverse events, defined as any symptom, sign, illness, or experience that develops or worsens in severity during the course of this study were also documented.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated with PASS software, version 15.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) using the pain score at postoperative hour 0 as first primary outcome. Assuming a difference of 1 point on the pain score with a standard deviation of 1.2, a sample size of 40 participants per group was needed to achieve 90% power with a two-sided alpha of 0.05, accounting for a 20% anticipated dropout rate.

Data were presented as means with standard deviations, medians with interquartile ranges, or frequencies and proportions, as appropriate. Group differences for baseline and follow-up assessments were assessed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables, and χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. For repeated measures data including pain scores and Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) scores, the aligned rank transform method with Tukey-adjusted post hoc comparisons was applied. Missing data were restricted to the scales of mental health status and quality of life collected during the 3-month follow-up; participants with such missing scale data were retained in the analysis, with their available baseline and primary outcome data fully included in relevant statistical evaluations. We also conducted an exploratory analysis to compare perioperative vital signs and cardiovascular events between two groups. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

We further performed a sensitivity analysis for missing data using multiple imputation by chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model included age, sex, body mass index, resting and movement VAS score at 30 min after surgery, treatment group, and baseline and postoperative questionnaire scores. For each imputed dataset, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for each questionnaire outcome, with the corresponding baseline score included as a covariate. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 277 obese patients were assessed for eligibility, and 193 patients were excluded based on the inclusion criteria (n = 97), patient refusal (n = 90), withdrawn or no consent (n = 6). In total, 84 patients comprising of 39 males (46.4%) and 45 females (53.6%), were randomized into opioid and esketamine groups. There were 4 and 6 patients respectively lost to follow-up at postoperative month 3 (Fig. 1). The median (interquartile range [IQR]) age and BMI was 30.0 years (25.0, 34.0) and 40.6 kg/m2 (37.4, 44.4), respectively. Patients in the esketamine and opioid groups shared comparable demographic variables, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, hyperlipidemia) and other baseline characteristics (e.g., scores of HAMA, HAMD, Gerd, and SF-36). Preoperative liver function (GGT, ALT, AST), renal function (Cr, BUN, eGFR), lipid profile (TG, TC), and fasting blood glucose showed no significant differences between groups, which further supported adequate baseline comparability and appropriate randomization procedures (Table 1).

Efficacy outcomes

Mean VAS scores were no higher than 3 in any of the participants (Fig. 2). The postoperative pain trajectories differed between the two groups (Pinteraction at rest = 0.006, Pinteraction during movement <0.001). Tukey-adjusted companions showed that obese patients in the esketamine group (n = 42) exhibited significantly lower VAS scores at rest than those in the opioid group (n = 42) at postoperative 1 h (T3). Significantly fewer patients in the esketamine group experienced moderate to severe pain at rest (VAS ≥ 4). For pain associated with movement, VAS scores were significantly lower at T1 and T2 in the esketamine group as well (PTukey-adjusted < 0.05). The incidence of moderate to severe movement-related pain was also less in the esketamine group at the same time points (Table 2).

Other efficacy outcomes included mental health, and quality of life (HAMA, HAMD, and SF-36) collected at 3-month following LSG (Table 2). Patients in the esketamine group (n = 36) showed lower scores in the HAMA (P = 0.044) and HAMD (P = 0.007) scale than those in the opioid group (n = 38), indicating lower levels of anxiety and depression symptoms in the esketamine group. However, they showed no difference on the SF-36.

Sensitivity analysis for missing data was consistent with the original findings. After multiple imputation, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA showed that the esketamine group had lower postoperative HAMA, HAMD, and Gerd-Q scores than the opioid group, with adjusted mean differences of −2.89 (95% CI, −5.23 to −0.56; P = 0.016), −2.79 (95% CI, −5.34 to −0.23; P = 0.033), and −1.07 (95% CI, −1.90 to −0.23; P = 0.013), respectively. No significant between-group differences were observed for SF-36 or PSQI. (eTable 4).

Safety and recovery outcomes

Intraoperative safety outcomes are shown in eTable 1. Hemodynamic parameters including coefficient of variation for heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), as well as cardiovascular events, and showed no significant differences between the two groups (eFig. 1). The median time to extubation was significantly longer in the esketamine group compared with the opioid group (1.0 [0.3, 2.7] vs 0.2 [0.2, 0.4] h, P < 0.001), without prolonging the duration of anesthesia intensive care unit stay (P > 0.05). Sedation levels, as assessed by the RASS, showed no difference between groups across seven postoperative time points.

Postoperative recovery parameters and adverse events are also presented in eTable 1. The QoR-15 score was significantly higher in the esketamine group (P = 0.025), indicating a better quality of recovery. In contrast, there were no significant differences in ambulation during the first 24 h, time to first flatus or postoperative length of stay. The incidence of nausea, vomiting, and the use of antiemetic medications during the first postoperative 24 h were also comparable between groups. At POM3, patients in the esketamine group showed slightly lower gastroesophageal reflux disease questionnaire (GerdQ) scores (P = 0.024).

Overall, esketamine administration was associated with a longer extubation time but improved subjective recovery quality, with no evidence of increased adverse effects or delayed physiological recovery.

Pharmacokinetics of esketamine

The mean plasma concentration-time profiles of esketamine (S-KET) and its four major metabolites are presented in Fig. 3A. Plasma esketamine concentrations rose rapidly during infusion and peaked at 41.24 ± 29.13 ng/ml at the end of administration (Tmax = 0.00 ± 0.00 h), followed by a rapid decline. The primary metabolite (S)-norketamine ((S)-norKET), which is also pharmacologically active, appeared shortly after esketamine and reached a comparable Cmax of 30.78 ± 14.00 ng/mL at Tmax of 0.14 ± 0.27 h. In contrast, hydroxynorketamine (HNK), dehydronorketamine (DHNK), and hydroxyketamine (HK) displayed delayed peak time and slower elimination rates (eTable 3).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrated both short-term and long-term benefits of esketamine-based OFA for patients undergoing LBS. Compared with opioid anesthesia, intraoperative esketamine provided dual benefits by reducing postoperative pain intensity within postoperative 24 h and improving mental health at POM 3, without increasing the incidence of psychotomimetic adverse events during hospitalization. This study also compared intraoperative hemodynamic profiles between esketamine and remifentanil in LBS. In addition, we characterized the pharmacokinetic profiles of intraoperative esketamine and its major metabolites in obese patients.

A large-scale survey of pain conducted in China showed that almost half of patients suffered from moderate-to-severe postoperative pain on POD1, of which 68% received systemic opioid [25]. Intravenous non-opioid analgesics and adjuvants are crucial in multimodal anaesthesia, reducing opioid consumption and enhancing postoperative care in adult surgical patients with obesity [14]. Apart from TAP blocks [23], there are inconsistent results for other opioid-alternative protocols such as OFA or opioid-sparing anesthesia (OSA) in reducing postoperative pain intensity or opioid reduction, or improving postoperative outcomes for obese patients undergoing LBS [8, 9, 26]. Intravenous esketamine as an adjunct to general anesthesia has been shown to reduce pain intensity and morphine requirements at postoperative 4, 12 and 24, without increasing the psychotomimetic adverse event rate [27, 28]. Within the first 30 min after arrival to the PACU, the mean of maximum pain intensity can be greater than 6 after LBS in both OBA and OFA protocol consisting of esketamine and dexmedetomidine without regional nerve blocks [9]. Here, the application of TAP in our OBA effectively prevented severe postoperative pain. This is consistent with a previous study where the combination of TAP and perioperative dexmedetomidine administration further reduces pain intensity [23]. Our modified esketamine-based OFA protocol successfully achieved further reductions in pain intensity as well as the incidence of moderate-severe postoperative pain compared with OBA. The significant decrease of the requirement for postoperative rescue analgesia within 24 h further supported the efficacy of perioperative replacement of opioids with esketamine. Collectively, OFA with multiple analgesia consisting of esketamine, dexmedetomidine, and TAP block resulted in significantly lower pain scores than OBA in laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy [16].

Opioid-free anaesthesia was initially applied to prevent PONV, given the strong correlation between opioid analgesics and PONV [29], in particular for patients undergoing LBS who are at high risk [8, 30]. However, a critical limitation of OFA is pronounced hemodynamic instability, including bradycardia and severe hypotension. The sympathomimetic properties of esketamine make it an attractive agent for OFA, as it helps stabilize hemodynamics [31–34], while mitigating opioid-induced respiratory depression, pulmonary complications, impaired gastrointestinal motility, and delayed mobilization [14, 18, 35]. In our study, esketamine-based OFA (continuous infusion of esketamine during induction and maintenance) provided a comparable and stable intraoperative hemodynamic profile to that of OBA in patients undergoing LBS [16], potentially improving peripheral perfusion and blood pressure [36, 37]. Esketamine also improves the postoperative recovery quality on POD-1, although the extubation time was prolonged by more than 30 min [38, 39]. Regarding PONV, its multifactorial pathophysiology complicates risk prediction and intervention [40], particularly in morbidly obese patients receiving combined antiemetic prophylaxis and multimodal OFA protocols. Despite multimodal PONV prophylaxis, including continuous dexmedetomidine and esketamine infusion combined with TAP blocks, PONV remains a prevalent complication that is more amenable to prevention than treatment [23, 41]. Furthermore, esketamine was associated with favorable long-term effects on gastrointestinal function, particularly gastroesophageal reflux at POM 3, without adversely affecting health-related quality of life [35, 38].

In the present study, both groups demonstrated improvements in HAMA and HAMD scores at 3 months, likely reflecting the positive psychological effects of bariatric surgery itself, including weight loss, improved self-image, and lifestyle changes [39]. Notably, the esketamine group showed statistically significant lower scores compared to the opioid group, consistent with the known anti-depressant properties of esketamine observed in perioperative and bariatric surgery settings [42]. However, this finding contrasts with a study reporting no benefit of intraoperative esketamine over placebo in patients with comorbid mild-to-moderate depression [43], a discrepancy that may be attributable to differences in patient populations, esketamine dosing regimens, or comparator interventions.

Importantly, participants in the present study were not enrolled based on psychiatric diagnoses, and baseline HAMA and HAMD scores were generally below the commonly used clinical thresholds for anxiety and depressive disorders. Nevertheless, individuals with severe obesity often experience varying degrees of psychological distress and emotional burden, even in the absence of diagnosed psychiatric disorders [44]. Although the between-group difference in HAMA scores was modest in absolute terms, the greater improvement in HAMD scores in the esketamine group may indicate a potential psychological benefit associated with perioperative single-dose esketamine exposure in this population. From this perspective, the present findings indicate that perioperative esketamine administration could have potential relevance beyond traditional psychiatric populations and may warrant further investigation.

The dual benefits of esketamine-based OFA observed in this study may be attributed to the complex pharmacological properties of esketamine and its metabolites [45, 46]. As an NMDA receptor antagonist, esketamine inhibits central sensitization and modulates pain processing, thereby enhancing postoperative analgesia [46]. Additionally, its affinity for μ-opioid receptors may provide supplementary analgesic effects [45]. Beyond pain control, NMDA receptor antagonism triggers downstream signaling cascades involving AMPA receptor activation, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) release, and mTOR signaling, which are associated with enhanced synaptic plasticity and have been implicated in the antidepressant effects of ketamine and its enantiomers [47]. These receptor-mediated mechanisms may collectively contribute to the improved postoperative pain control and psychological benefits observed in the esketamine group.

We further characterized the pharmacokinetic profiles of esketamine and its four major metabolites, (S)-norKET, HNK, DHNK, and HK, in obese surgical patients. Plasma esketamine concentrations increased rapidly during infusion, while (S)-norketamine appeared subsequently and exhibited a relatively prolonged concentration profile compared to the parent compound, consistent with established metabolic pathways [48–51]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that (S)-norketamine produces antidepressant-like effects comparable to those of esketamine in rodent models, including reductions in depressive-like behaviors in learned helplessness and chronic stress paradigms [52]. Interestingly, these effects may occur through mechanisms partially distinct from those traditionally proposed for ketamine, including AMPA receptor activation-independent pathways involving BDNF–TrkB and mTOR signaling [52]. The perioperative concentration-time profiles of esketamine and (S)-norketamine were broadly consistent with the temporal pattern of early postoperative analgesia, supporting a possible contribution of (S)-norketamine to acute postoperative effects [53]. Moreover, HNK and DHNK have been implicated in the antidepressant effects of ketamine [50, 54], and HK may have its own pharmacodynamic activity. Our study of the full esketamine metabolites spectrum helps clarify the potential roles of these metabolites. Therefore, the perioperative effects observed in this study may reflect the combined pharmacological actions of esketamine and its active metabolites rather than those of esketamine alone.

Previous studies have suggested that metabolic factors may influence the antidepressant response to ketamine. Niciu et al. reported that a higher BMI was associated with a greater antidepressant response to ketamine in patients with treatment-resistant depression [55]. In addition, Machado-Vieira et al. found that lower baseline adiponectin levels, indicative of a more adverse metabolic and inflammatory state, predicted a greater antidepressant response to ketamine [56]. The same study also observed reductions in the pro-inflammatory adipokine resistin following ketamine administration, suggesting that anti-inflammatory mechanisms may contribute to ketamine’s therapeutic effects [56].

Severe obesity is commonly associated with adipokine dysregulation, chronic low-grade inflammation, and insulin resistance. Consequently, the metabolic characteristics of this population may influence their responsiveness to esketamine. Given these obesity-related metabolic features, severely obese patients may represent a clinically relevant population for investigating the perioperative psychological and analgesic effects of esketamine. In the present study, all participants underwent bariatric surgery, which itself can improve metabolic and inflammatory status in addition to promoting weight loss and lifestyle changes [57]. Therefore, the psychological benefits observed in the esketamine group may reflect a complex interaction among perioperative esketamine exposure, obesity-related metabolic factors, and postoperative metabolic recovery. However, because adipokine levels and BMI-related treatment interactions were not specifically analyzed in this study, these interpretations remain speculative and should be investigated in future mechanistic studies.

Several additional domains potentially relevant to perioperative esketamine administration warrant further investigation. Dissociative symptoms following esketamine administration have been reported in the treatment of depression [58, 59]. However, this study was conducted in a perioperative anesthesia setting, where intraoperative dosing was clinically titrated, and perioperative safety assessments primarily focused on clinically significant adverse events and recovery outcomes rather than detailed psychiatric symptom profiling. Previous studies have also suggested that esketamine may influence postoperative sleep quality [60]. However, postoperative sleep in patients with morbid obesity is often affected by multiple factors, including obstructive sleep apnea, perioperative hypoxemia, postoperative pain, sedative medications, and surgical stress responses [61, 62]. Given these potential confounding factors, dissociative symptoms and postoperative sleep quality were not considered a primary evaluable outcome in the current study design.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this trial was conducted in a single center, and our participants were limited to Chinese obese patients. Therefore, the findings may lack generalizability to wider global populations, and therefore multicenter studies involving different regions, countries, and races and ethnicities are required. Second, considering the regular follow-up after LBS [63] and the peak period for the progress of postoperative mental health, we set our follow-up end at postoperative 3 months. However, the long-term sustainability of any treatment response beyond this specified period remains uncertain. In addition, some potentially relevant perioperative domains, including dissociative symptoms and postoperative sleep-related outcomes, were not systematically assessed and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Conclusions

This randomized clinical trial showed that an optimized opioid-free anesthesia with intravenous infusion of esketamine during laparoscopic bariatric surgery was effective in improving postoperative analgesia without increasing psychotomimetic adverse events. Moreover, esketamine anesthesia to the patients demonstrated stable intraoperative hemodynamics and postoperative improvement of the mental health. Overall, our study demonstrated a potential beneficial effect of esketamine-based OFA on the perioperative management for bariatric surgery to optimize ERABS.
