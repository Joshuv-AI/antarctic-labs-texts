Beyond Passive Substituents: Tosyl-Directed Self-Templation Enables Selective Pillar[4 + 1]arene Formation and Topology Switching
Authors: Araya Ruengsuk, Korawit Khamphaijun, Pipatpong Laoviwat, Nuntaporn Kamonsutthipaijit, Jonggol Tuntirungrotechai, Yasuteru Shigeta, Kowit Hengphasatporn, David J. Harding, Thanthapatra Bunchuay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Substituents in supramolecular chemistry are usually treated as passive handles that tune solubility or reactivity rather than as active determinants of assembly pathways. Here we show that tosyl groups promote directional noncovalent recognition and thereby control both cocyclization selectivity and postassembly topology in pillararenes. Under otherwise identical conditions, brominated analogues give statistical mixtures in which the pillar­[4 + 1]­arene product appears at only 1–19% distribution, whereas tosyl-substituted monomers undergo pseudorotaxane-like preassembly that enables highly selective self-templated pillar­[4 + 1]­arene formation without added external templates. A crystallographic survey of 12 single-crystal structures (N = 12), together with VT NMR, SAXS, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and fragment molecular orbital (FMO) analysis, establishes a valency–topology relationship in which increasing tosyl valency drives a progression from discrete monomers to interpenetrated dimers and higher-order aggregates. This substituent-dependent behavior extends across a broader alkoxy series, indicating that the effect is not limited to a single monomer pair. Upon benzoquinone oxidation, the tosyl-containing copillar­[4 + 1]­arene undergoes temperature-dependent switching between interpenetrated and self-included states, accompanied by changes in aggregation, a charge-transfer spectral shift, and reversible thermochromism quantifiable by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy and smartphone colorimetry. These findings establish substituent identity as an active design parameter for constructing reconfigurable and functionally responsive macrocyclic systems.

Introduction

Archetypal supramolecular systems are typically constructed from carefully chosen molecular building blocks that assemble into complex architectures via covalent preorganization, templating strategies, or statistical self-assembly. Although substitution patterns are routinely adjusted to tune solubility, reactivity, or binding strength, − substituents themselves are rarely treated as active supramolecular determinants that encode assembly pathways or topological outcomes. This exposes a conceptual gap: can a substituent be elevated from a passive synthetic handle to an active supramolecular element that directs organization and topology?

Pillararenes represent a rapidly expanding class of shape-persistent macrocycles that have become key platforms in modern supramolecular chemistry. − Their rigid π-symmetric cavities, accessible synthesis, and modular peripheral functionalization have enabled advances in molecular recognition, host–guest chemistry, and responsive materials. − Yet, a central challenge remains, how to exert reliable control over cocyclization selectivity and the supramolecular topology of pillararene derivatives. Typical cocyclization reactions frequently yield statistical mixtures or require external templates and large excesses of one monomer, limiting predictability and scalability. − These limitations, combined with the modularity of the pillararene scaffold, make pillararenes an ideal platform for probing whether substituents can direct supramolecular behavior rather than merely modulate reactivity.

Halogenated monomers are commonly used as precursors for cocyclization but generally lack directional noncovalent interaction motifs that could assist monomer preorganization or impose supramolecular hierarchy. Consequently, they often provide low selectivity and poorly controlled aggregation under comparable conditions. In contrast, we show that tosyl-bearing monomers (M-OTs) exhibit unexpected and highly structured supramolecular behavior prior to cyclization. Their tosyl motifs introduce preorganized CH···O and CH···π interaction sites that promote pseudorotaxane-like preassembly, enabling self-templated, highly selective cocyclization. Notably, under otherwise identical conditions, tosyl substitution affords pillar­[4 + 1]­arene formation with high constitutional selectivity, whereas the corresponding brominated analogues show low selectivity.

Beyond enabling selective cyclization, tosyl substitution also controls supramolecular topology. By integrating crystallography (N = 12), VT-NMR, SAXS, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and fragment molecular orbital (FMO) analysis, we identify a valency–topology relationship in which the number of tosyl substituents governs whether the resulting architectures exist as monomers, dimers, or higher-order aggregates. Crucially, this behavior is not confined to a single alkoxy substituent: the same supramolecular signatures and selectivity trends are retained across a broader series (M-OMe, M-OEt, M-OAk and M-OBr), indicating that the tosyl-directed effect is transferable across related monomer motifs.

We further demonstrate that this substituent-dependent principle extends to adaptive function. Oxidation of a tosyl-containing copillar­[4 + 1]­arene yields a macrocycle that undergoes temperature-dependent switching between interpenetrated and self-included states, coupled to a charge-transfer response that manifests as reversible thermochromism quantifiable by smartphone imaging. Thus, tosyl substitution is transformed from a conventional protecting or leaving group into an active supramolecular directing element, providing a general strategy for designing function-encoding and adaptively reconfigurable macrocyclic systems (Figure ).

Tosyl-Induced Pseudorotaxane-like Preorganization

Tosyl groups are widely employed as leaving groups in synthetic organic chemistry, yet their potential as supramolecular synthons have remained largely unexplored. During this study, we observed a striking contrast in solubility when comparing the ditosylated monomer (M-OTs) with its dibrominated analogue (M-Br). While M-Br dissolved readily in organic solvents, M-OTs exhibited markedly reduced solubility, suggesting that unfavorable dispersion alone could not account for its behavior. Instead, this contrast indicated a strong propensity for supramolecular self-association in M-OTs, hinting that the tosyl pendants may actively promote intermolecular recognition even prior to cyclization.

To investigate this phenomenon, M-OTs and M-Br (for comparison) were crystallized and subjected to single-crystal X-ray diffraction. While M-OTs crystallizes in the monoclinic C2/c space group and M-Br in P21/c, both contain half of the molecule in the asymmetric unit (Figure a for M-OTs and Figure S40 for M-Br). Although the molecular structure of M-OTs appears unremarkable, its packing reveals highly ordered and unexpected supramolecular organization. The monomers assemble into discrete supramolecular boxes stabilized by multiple CH···O interactions between the sulfonate oxygens and neighboring alkyl C–H bonds (Figure b). Most notably, the tosyl pendants of adjacent monomers insert into the cavities of these supramolecular boxes, generating an interdigitated, pseudorotaxane-like architecture (Figure c). This arrangement closely resembles early stage macrocyclic assembly and constitutes a rare example of precyclization pseudorotaxane geometry. In contrast, the dibrominated analogue exhibits no directional packing in the solid state nor any detectable aggregation in solution, confirming that the supramolecular behavior is unique to tosyl substitution (Figure S40).

To test whether the solid-state preorganization of M-OTs persists in solution, we performed concentration-dependent 1H NMR experiments in CDCl3. M-OTs showed pronounced upfield shifts with increasing concentration, whereas M-OMe displayed only minor changes, indicating much stronger self-association of the tosylated monomer (Figure S43). DOSY NMR further supported the presence of associated dimeric M-OTs species (Figures S45,S46), and titration of M-OMe into concentrated M-OTs (200 mM) caused systematic perturbation of the M-OMe signals (Figure S47), consistent with interaction with the preassociated M-OTs ensemble. These results indicate that the pseudorotaxane-like crystal packing of M-OTs reflects a genuine solution-phase association tendency relevant to the cocyclization pathway, rather than a purely solid-state packing effect, although the exact solution-phase geometry may differ from that in the crystal.

Together, the solubility contrast, crystallographic packing, and solution-phase association studies indicate that tosyl substitution induces monomer preorganization through directional noncovalent interactions. Rather than acting as a passive synthetic handle, the tosyl group serves as a supramolecular directing element that prearranges the monomers into macrocycle-compatible geometries. These findings provide a plausible mechanistic basis for employing M-OTs in selective cocyclization.

Self-Templated and Selective Cocyclization of Pillar[4 + 1]arene

The supramolecular preorganization observed in M-OTs suggested that tosyl groups may encode recognition motifs relevant to covalent assembly. To test whether this prearrangement influences cyclization outcomes, we conducted a cocyclization reaction between 1,4-dimethoxybenzene (M-OMe) and M-OTs under standard conditions using paraformaldehyde (PFA) and BF3·Et2O in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) (Figure d,e). The resulting macrocycles were purified by silica-gel chromatography and fully characterized by NMR, ESI-MS, and TLC analyses. Theoretically, this system permits eight possible pillar[5]­arene products: two homopillar[5]­arenes (P­[5]­OMe (3a) and P­[5]­OTs (3f)) and six copillar[5]­arene derivatives (3b, 3c1, 3c2, 3d1, 3d2, and 3e) (Figure d,e).

Molar feed ratio emerged as a decisive variable. When the ratio of M-OMe:M-OTs was set to x:5-x, broad product distributions were obtained for x = 2–4 (Table S2, Entries 3–6). As x increased, the yield of 3a increased, while 3f decreased. However, at a ratio of 1:4, only a single P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e) product was formed exclusively, with an isolated yield of 33%. Decreasing the PFA from 15 to 10 equiv further enhanced the yield of 3e to 47% (Table S2, Entry 7). Neither prolonged reaction time nor additional PFA improved product selectivity (Table S2, entries 7–9), indicating that the selectivity arises from an intrinsically favored self-preorganized cyclization pathway under these conditions.

Control experiments using M-Br under identical conditions yielded the full statistical distribution of possible products as well as detectable polymeric byproducts, with no evidence of intrinsic selectivity or templation. In contrast to M-OTs, the M-Br system showed no preference for specific copillar[5]­arene compositions and failed to generate any isolable pillar­[4 + 1]­arene analogue, underscoring the critical role of tosyl substitution in directing cyclization outcome.

DCE is well-known to favor formation of the pillar[5]­arene framework over other pillar­[n]­arene homologues and should therefore be regarded as a ring-size-directing solvent in the present system. Our results do not suggest that tosyl substitution overrides this established solvent effect. Rather, they indicate that within the DCE-biased pillar[5]­arene-forming manifold, the tosyl-bearing monomer exerts an additional influence on constitutional selectivity, favoring formation of the pillar­[4 + 1]­arene as the only isolable discrete macrocyclic product under the optimized conditions. Thus, the role of M-OTs is best understood as self-preorganizing or self-templating within a solvent-defined ring-size regime, rather than as a solvent-independent template.

To investigate the origin of this selectivity, we examined the reaction rates of the corresponding homocyclization reactions. As reported previously, M-OMe afforded 3a within 15 min with 71% yield, while M-OTs required about 4 h to form 3f in a comparable 70% yield (Figure d). , These two monomers exhibited significantly different cyclization rates therefore P­[5]­OMe was expected to form in all reaction conditions (Figure e). In fact, P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e) was exclusively observed. Despite its slower reaction kinetics, M-OTs promoted highly selective cocyclization when present in excess, revealing that reaction selectivity is not driven by reactivity but by supramolecular preorganization induced by the tosyl substituent. This constitutes a rare example of substituent-driven cyclization controlled by supramolecular preorganization of the tosyl groups. To examine the selective incorporation behavior of M-OTs, the synthesis of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­X was applied to a series of alkoxy-substituted monomers (M-OEt, M-OAk and M-OBr) affording the target pillar­[4 + 1]­arenes as the exclusive characterized macrocyclic products, although the isolated yields remained moderate (28%, 20%, and 19%), likely because part of the consumed material formed unidentified oligomeric byproducts.

Tosyl-Number–Dependent Supramolecular Hierarchy

The self-templated cyclization of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e) based macrocycles suggested that tosyl groups do more than enhance reactivity; they also appear to define supramolecular valency, governing the assembly hierarchy of the resulting structures. To investigate this, we examined the solid-state structures of three representative macrocycles with different tosyl numbers: 3b (one tosyl panel), 3e (four tosyl panels), and 3f (five tosyl panels). Their single-crystal X-ray structures revealed a clear correlation between tosyl number and supramolecular architecture, in stark contrast to crystal structures of nontosylated pillar[5]­arene derivatives (N = 12, Table S3).

P­[1]­OTs­[4]­OMe (3b): Monomeric Assembly (Low Valency)

Macrocycle 3b, bearing a single tosyl panel, forms discrete monomeric units in the solid state with minimal supramolecular interactions between neighboring molecules, similar to behavior observed in most P[5]­OR (Table S3). The lone tosyl substituent engages only in weak CH···O interactions, preventing hierarchical aggregation (Figures a and S41).

Substituent-guided supramolecular hierarchy. (a–c) Views of the asymmetric unit of P­[1]­OTs­[4]­OMe (3b), P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e) and P­[5]­OTs (3f), forming monomeric, dimeric and polymeric assembly, respectively.

P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e): Dimeric Assembly (Medium Valency)

Increasing tosyl content to four panels in 3e generates dimeric supramolecular architectures, stabilized via directional CH···O and CH···π interactions between the tosyl pendants and adjacent aromatic rings. This dimeric packing mode aligns with the pseudorotaxane-like preorganization observed in monomeric M–OTs, suggesting that the supramolecular tendency established at the monomer stage is retained after macrocyclization (Figures b and S42).

P­[5]­OTs (3f): Polymeric Assembly (High Valency)

At high tosyl density, 3f forms a remarkable infinite interpenetrated polymeric network, propagating along a single crystallographic axis through recurrent insertion of tosyl pendants into adjacent cavities (Figure c). No analogous organization is observed in the brominated analogue, highlighting that tosyl groups uniquely promote directional supramolecular propagation.

These observations converge into a design principle of supramolecular valency that increasing tosyl number increases interaction valency, which dictates the supramolecular hierarchy, from monomer to dimer to polymer. This substituent-dependent assembly logic represents a previously unrecognized mechanism in macrocyclic design. It further suggests that interaction valency may be predictively tuned through substituent identity, enabling controlled access to discrete architectures, mesoscale aggregates, or extended supramolecular frameworks. Thus, supramolecular hierarchy emerges not from macrocycle symmetry but from substituent-guided recognition, introducing the concept of “supramolecular valency” as a design parameter.

Synthesis and Structural Characterization of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq

The supramolecular hierarchy encoded by tosyl substituents suggested potential tunability beyond static structures. To probe whether supramolecular logic can extend toward stimuli-responsive behavior, we focused on the medium-valency macrocycle 3e, which contains four tosyl groups and one methoxy unit. Oxidation using (NH4)2[Ce­(NO3)6] (CAN) yielded P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq in 33% yield, preserving the supramolecular architecture while introducing optical properties and a redox-active bridge capable of electronic modulation (Figures a and S48–S53).

(a) Synthesis of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, (b) the asymmetric unit in P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq with a toluene molecule, and (c) the supramolecular dimers “handshake” motif that forms in P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq. The toluene is represented by the green spacefill molecule.

Direct structural evidence for supramolecular prearrangement was provided by single-crystal X-ray analysis of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq. Crystals obtained via vapor diffusion of MeOH into a toluene solution at room temperature exhibited the triclinic P1̅ space group, with one macrocycle in the asymmetric unit (Figure b). One toluene molecule resides inside the cavity, while another is disordered between adjacent macrocycles. Remarkably, a tosyl arm from one macrocycle penetrates the cavity of a neighboring molecule, generating a dimeric “handshake” motif analogous to that previously observed in 3e. This dimeric interaction is stabilized through clear C–H···O hydrogen bonding between the tosylate and the benzoquinone core {C37–H37B···O2 = 2.315(4) Å} (Figure c). The presence of two distinct toluene molecules suggests cooperative solvent assistance: one acts as a cavity guest, while the other stabilizes the external supramolecular interface, preventing polymeric propagation. Indeed, such suppression of polymerization is consistent with the behavior observed in solution, and strongly contrasts with 3f, which forms an infinite supramolecular network when no such steric modulation is present. Thus, supramolecular dimerization in P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq is both solvent-regulated and substituent-directed, implying an inherent tendency toward dynamic topology rather than irreversible supramolecular entrapment. This solid-state geometry provides a direct structural foundation for the temperature-dependent behavior observed spectroscopically.

Temperature-Induced Topology Switching

Variable-temperature (VT) 1H NMR spectra of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq confirmed this dynamic nature. At low temperature, the VT 1H NMR spectra exhibit pronounced broadening, indicating slow exchange within a strongly associated supramolecular state. Although VT NMR alone does not uniquely define the aggregation topology, the combined crystallographic, SAXS, and MD data support assignment of this state as an interpenetrated dimeric assembly.

Above 323 K, the NMR signals became noticeably sharper, revealing distinct and nonsymmetrical peaks, particularly for the tosyl protons (Ha, Hb, and Hc, see a full spectrum in Figure S54), the aromatic panels (Haromatic), and the benzoquinone segment (HBq) (Figure a). With increasing temperature, Ha–Hc exhibited systematic upfield shifts, while HBq shifted downfield. The combined analysis of chemical shift variations and peak-area integrals subtly indicates a temperature-induced topology transition to a self-included monomeric conformation, wherein the tosyl group is positioned within the pillararene cavity. The steric hindrance of the tosylate groups inhibited the fully oxygen-through-the-annulus rotation that is observed in the case of pillararenes substituted with small substituents. ,

(a) Temperature-dependent NMR spectra of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, (b) Comparison of the radius of gyration (R g) for P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq (orange) and P­[4]­OMe­[1]­Bq (red) reveals differences in their temperature-dependent structural behavior, (c) UV–vis spectra of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq (2.0 mM) in CHCl3, and (d) Linear correlation between absorbance and temperature (K) of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq in CHCl3.

The topology transition was further supported by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS). A progressive increase in the Guinier slope (q < 0.05 Å–1) was observed with increasing temperature, followed by a plateau above 323 K. Guinier analysis revealed that the radius of gyration (R g) increased from 9 to 17 Å between 278 and 323 K, followed by stabilization at 16–17 Å, consistent with a reorganization from interpenetrated dimer to self-included monomer. In contrast, the tosyl-free analogue P­[4]­OMe­[1]­Bq showed no significant change in R g across the same temperature range, indicating absence of supramolecular rearrangement and highlighting the essential role of the tosyl substituents (Figures b and S55).

The UV–vis spectra display a charge-transfer (CT) absorption band originating from the benzoquinone (BQ) motif. − In contrast, P­[5]­OTs, which lacks a BQ unit, shows no comparable absorption in the visible region, confirming that the CT band is directly associated with the presence of BQ. Notably, P­[4]­OTs­[1]­BQ exhibits a well-defined CT band at 426 nm in CHCl3, which is blue-shifted relative to that of P­[4]­OMe­[1]­BQ at 443 nm, indicating an increase in the CT transition energy (Figure c). Moreover, the molar extinction coefficient (ε) of the CT band for P­[4]­OTs­[1]­BQ (ε = 601 M–1 cm–1) is slightly lower than that of P­[4]­OMe­[1]­BQ (ε = 611 M–1 cm–1), reflecting a modest decrease in the CT transition probably associated with differences in supramolecular organization.

The temperature-induced topology switches also produced a distinct charge-transfer shift, visually detectable as thermochromism. The solution of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq in CHCl3 transitions from red to orange to yellow at 233, 298, and 333 K, respectively. Using a standard smartphone camera, the RGB output could be tracked and correlated linearly with temperature, demonstrating a structure-dependent optical reporting mechanism controlled by substituent-dependent supramolecular organization (Figure d, Tables S5,S6).

These findings demonstrate that supramolecular topology, and not covalent structure, is the determining factor in this optoelectronic behavior. Tosyl-substituted macrocycles therefore function as adaptive molecular systems, in which substituent-guided supramolecular interactions couple structure and function.

Temperature-Dependent MD Simulations: Mechanistic Origin of Topology Switching

Temperature-dependent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were undertaken using the X-ray crystal structure of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq in a handshake-like dimer (Figure c) as the initial geometry to investigate the molecular origin of the thermally induced topology switching. MD simulations were conducted in explicit toluene at five temperature conditions (278, 293, 313, 333, and 353 K) over 300 ns, with three independent trajectories each. The supramolecular organization was monitored through measurement of the distances between each tosyl group in monomer (i) and the aromatic core of monomer (j) (d i tosylate j –core) and vice versa, forming a 2D time-resolved map of intermonomer proximity (Figure a).

(a) Two-dimensional (2D) plot showing the stability of the P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq dimer over time at various temperature conditions (278, 293, 313, 333, and 353 K) based on MD simulations. The X-axis represents the distance between the center of mass of the tosylate group in monomer (i) and the core ring structure in monomer (j) (d i tosylate j –core), and the Y-axis represents the distance between the center of mass of the tosylate group in monomer (j) and the core ring structure in monomer (i) (d j tosylate i –core), with color indicating time from 0 to 300 ns. and (b) Visualization of the self-inclusion of tosylate groups (orange spheres) into the core ring of monomer (j) (orange CPK structure) at 353 K, shown at 6.3, 15.9, 164.0, and 300.0 ns. The movie of this self-inclusion is available as Supporting Information II.

At 278 and 293 K, P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq persisted as a stable dimer, consistent with the low-temperature VT-NMR data. In each case, the intermonomer distances remained within <7.0 Å, indicating a persistent network of CH···O and CH···π interactions that stabilizes the interpenetrated topology. At 313 K, one tosyl group began to dissociate from its neighboring monomer, generating a transient semidissociated state. This transition matches the onset of spectral sharpening observed in the NMR experiments.

At 333 and 353 K, the system transitioned to a fully dissociated state in all trajectories. Remarkably, following dissociation, one tosyl pendant consistently inserted into the interior cavity of the same monomer, stabilized by π–π interactions with the quinone unit. In particular at 353 K, this self-included conformation remained stable throughout the remainder of the simulation, supporting the experimental observation that the self-included monomer is the thermodynamically preferred topology at elevated temperature (Figure b). Perhaps, the self-inclusion also interrupts both the intra- and intermolecular CT transition of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, making the solution turn yellow similar to the unique color of an individual benzoquinone (see ESI, Section , for computational details).

These results closely mirror the behavior observed by VT-NMR and SAXS, and together support a consistent mechanism in which the tosyl substituent directs the intermolecular contacts that underlie temperature-dependent topology switching. This reinforces that supramolecular dynamics, rather than covalent structure alone, govern the observed conformational transition.

Thus, the topology switch can be viewed as a temperature-gated supramolecular event in which substituent identity influences both association behavior and functional output. These findings provide a useful framework for designing responsive macrocycles through substituent-dependent supramolecular organization.

Fragment Molecular Orbital (FMO) Analysis: Mechanistic Origin of Substituent-Directed Supramolecular Organization

To evaluate the underlying energetic origins of supramolecular hierarchy and topology switching, fragment molecular orbital (FMO) analysis on the dimeric assemblies of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, 3e, and 3f obtained from the crystal structure were performed (computational details are provided in the ESI, Section ).

Each macrocycle was divided into fragments, with the core aromatic framework designated as F0, while individual tosyl substituents were treated as discrete fragments . Pair interaction energy (PIE) and its decomposition (PIEDA) were evaluated using RI-MP2 in implicit solvent both in toluene and chloroform at DFT B3LYP/6–31G* level of theory, revealing the energetic fingerprint of tosyl-driven supramolecular recognition (Figure ).

3D structure of P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, P­[4]­OTs­[1]­OMe (3e), and P­[5]­OTs (3f) packed in a dimeric form comprised of monomer (i) and (j). The pair interaction energy (PIE) and its decomposition-free analysis (PIEDA) for each dimeric unit calculated using the fragment molecular orbital (FMO) method are illustrated as a stacked bar graph with values in the plot. The first and second bars in the double-stacked bar graph represent calculations in toluene and chloroform, respectively. Each portion in the stacked bar graph refers to the electrostatic interaction (ΔE ij ES), the charge transfer with higher-order mixed terms energies (ΔE ij CT + mix), dispersion (ΔE ij DI ), exchange-repulsion (ΔE ij EX), and the solvation (ΔG Sol) colored red, yellow, purple, green, and blue, respectively.

Across all systems, the total interaction energy strongly correlated with tosyl number, following the experimental hierarchy: 3f > P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq > 3e, which matches their observed tendencies to form polymeric and dimeric assemblies, respectively. Notably, 3f exhibited exceptionally strong stabilization (−208.0 kcal/mol in toluene and – 205.8 kcal/mol in chloroform), consistent with its ability to form an infinite interpenetrated supramolecular polymer in both solid-state and solution environments.

In P­[4]­OTs­[1]­Bq, the benzoquinone fragment (F0) showed enhanced charge-transfer (ΔE ij CT + mix) interactions and electrostatic (ΔE ij ES ) complementarity relative to 3e, providing additional stabilization of the supramolecular dimer while simultaneously promoting topology switching at elevated temperatures. This agrees with the MD simulations and supports the view that the quinone bridge functions as a supramolecular hinge, enabling reversible geometry reorganization under thermal activation.

Particularly striking was the interaction between F1 (inserted tosyl group) and F0 (core), which showed an interaction energy comparable to that of the central aromatic fragments, signifying that the tosyl group behaves as an active supramolecular module rather than a passive substituent. The strong dispersion (ΔE ij DI ), together with electrostatic and charge-transfer contributions, indicates π–π stacking and CH···π interactions between the tosyl ring and the quinone core (F0). These computational results support the view that supramolecular organization in this system is governed by substituent-dependent noncovalent interactions.

Collectively, the FMO analysis reveals that supramolecular behavior does not arise from the macrocycle itself, but is written into the substituent architecture, suggesting that supramolecular logic and function may be computationally predicted before synthesis. This highlights a promising strategy for constructing adaptive molecular systems and topology-dependent supramolecular functions through the deliberate placement of directing substituents.

Conclusion

Tosyl groups, commonly treated as disposable leaving groups, are shown here to operate as active supramolecular directing elements that program macrocycle formation, assembly hierarchy, and dynamic function. Their directional CH···O and CH···π interactions induce pseudorotaxane-like preorganization of monomers and enable highly selective pillar­[4 + 1]­arene formation through self-templation, a behavior not observed in brominated analogues.

Crystallographic analysis of macrocycles with different tosyl numbers reveals a supramolecular valency relationship, in which increasing tosyl content drives a progression from discrete monomers to dimers and finally to infinite polymers in solid-state and solution. Oxidation of a medium-valency macrocycle generates a benzoquinone-bridged system that undergoes temperature-induced switching among a highly aggregated state, interpenetrated dimer, and self-included monomer, with topology directly controlling a CT-based thermochromic response that is visually observable and quantitatively tracked by UV–visible spectroscopy or a smartphone. MD simulations and FMO calculations corroborate these observations and show that the energetic origin of this behavior resides in substituent-dependent noncovalent interactions.

In summary, this work establishes substituent identity as an active design element in supramolecular chemistry. By embedding recognition patterns, supramolecular valency, and dynamic function within substituent arrays, this strategy should be transferable to other macrocyclic and supramolecular platforms, providing a general route to selective macrocyclization and controllable topology switching in adaptive materials and responsive supramolecular systems.
