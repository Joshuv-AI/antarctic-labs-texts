Evaluation of the role of salivary survivin and caspase-3 in apoptosis regulation in oral submucous fibrosis and oral squamous cell carcinoma among gutka chewers
Authors: Arushi Chaure, Jaya Joshi, Sakshi Arora, Neha Choudhary, Supreet Kaur Gurdatta, Astha Pusame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study evaluates salivary Survivin and Caspase-3 as key regulators of apoptosis across a disease spectrum—from gutka chewers without lesions to oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). Survivin, an anti-apoptotic protein, and caspase-3, the principal executioner caspase, represent crucial biomarkers of malignant transformation, especially in populations with high smokeless tobacco exposure.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional analytical study included 92 participants divided into: controls (n = 17), gutka chewers without lesions (n = 25), OSMF (n = 25), and OSCC (n = 25). Unstimulated saliva samples were collected and analysed for Survivin and Caspase-3 using ELISA. Statistical analysis included Pearson correlation and Kruskal–Wallis.Results:Mean survivin levels demonstrated a progressive increase from healthy subjects (1.00 ± 0.73 pg/ml) to gutka chewers without lesions (2.00 ± 1.36 pg/ml), OSMF (3.50 ± 2.82 pg/ml), and OSCC (5.00 ± 3.04 pg/ml), indicating a trend towards increased anti-apoptotic activity with disease progression. In contrast, Caspase-3 levels showed a biphasic pattern, measuring 4.90 ± 4.43 ng/ml in healthy subjects, 4.50 ± 2.71 ng/ml in gutka chewers without lesions, decreasing to 3.50 ± 2.82 ng/ml in OSMF, and subsequently increasing to 5.50 ± 3.62 ng/ml in OSCC.Conclusion:Survivin demonstrated potential as a biomarker of malignant transformation in gutka-associated oral potentially malignant disorders, while the non-linear pattern of Caspase-3 highlights the complex regulatory interactions involving apoptosis suppression, hypoxic adaptation, and possible apoptosis-induced proliferation. However, both biomarkers may serve as potential indicators of disease progression, pending validation through larger multi-centre studies with longitudinal follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is a major global health burden with over 660,000 new cases and 325,000 deaths every year, and its incidence is projected to increase by nearly 30% by 2030.[1] Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for more than 90% of oral malignancies[2] and ranks as the eleventh most common cancer worldwide.[3] India contributes almost one-third of global OSCC cases.[4] Rising incidence trends underscore the need for early detection strategies and biomarker-based screening.[1]

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) are conditions associated with increased risk of transformation into OSCC.[5] Among these, oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF/OSF) is one of the most prevalent and high-risk lesions. The exact aetiology of OSF is not well understood. Various factors are being studied, such as genetic, auto-immune, nutritional, and environmental agents. Among the environmental causes, various chewing habits are observed to be associated with OSF.[6] It is a chronic, progressive disorder characterised by mucosal rigidity, burning sensation, and progressive limitation of mouth opening.[7] Gutka containing areca nut and tobacco is the major aetiological factor implicated in both OSMF development and malignant progression.[8]

Gutka contains toxic carcinogens including arecoline and tobacco-specific nitrosamines, which produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and DNA damage, contributing directly to dysregulation of apoptosis.[7] Disturbances in apoptotic pathways are central to carcinogenesis, consistent with the hallmarks of cancer described by Hanahan and Weinberg, particularly the ability to resist cell death and enable replicative immortality.[9]

Apoptosis is a highly regulated programmed cell death mechanism essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis. It involves characteristic nuclear and cytoplasmic changes, including chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, and membrane blebbing, which lead to apoptotic body formation.[10] Two principal pathways initiate apoptosis: the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway and the extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) pathway.[11]

The intrinsic pathway is activated by internal cellular stresses such as DNA damage and oxidative stress. These stimuli shift the balance between pro-apoptotic proteins (Bax, Bak) and anti-apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2 family), leading to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation and release of Cytochrome c.[12] Cytochrome c binds Apaf-1 to form the apoptosome, which activates Caspase-9 and subsequently effector caspases like Caspase-3.[13]

The extrinsic pathway is triggered when death ligands such as FasL or TRAIL bind to their receptors, forming the death-inducing signalling complex (DISC) and activating Caspase-8.[14] Both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge to activate executioner caspases, particularly Caspase-3, which carry out the final dismantling of cellular structures.[15]

Caspase-3 is the key executioner caspase responsible for DNA fragmentation and degradation of nuclear and cytoskeletal proteins.[16] It is synthesised as Procaspase-3 and becomes active upon cleavage into its functional subunits. Morphological changes such as cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, and apoptotic body formation are defining hallmarks of apoptosis and indicate Caspase-3 activity.[17]

Apoptosis is regulated by pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, including Survivin, a member of the Inhibitor of Apoptosis (IAP) family.[18] Survivin plays dual roles in suppressing apoptosis and regulating mitosis. Its function is modulated by post-translational modifications including phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination, which influence its stability and localisation.[19]

Survivin’s anti-apoptotic function is mediated largely through its interactions with XIAP, which enhance suppression of caspase activity. It also binds Smac/DIABLO, preventing caspase activation and promoting cell survival.[20] Signalling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, Ras, MAPK, and STAT enhance Survivin expression and promote cell survival in response to inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress.[21] Gutka-induced ROS activates NF-κB and PI3K/Akt pathways, significantly contributing to apoptotic resistance in exposed tissues.[22]

The tumour suppressor protein p53 plays a crucial role in DNA damage response by inducing cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis when genomic damage is irreparable.[23]

Cancer progression is driven by the selection of apoptosis-resistant clones. Mechanisms include TP53 mutations, downregulation of Caspase-3 and Bax, upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 and Survivin, and persistent activation of survival pathways such as NF-κB and PI3K/Akt.[2425]

Apoptosis-resistant cell populations expand due to phenomena such as apoptosis-induced proliferation (AiP) and anastasis. AiP occurs when Caspase-3 activates iPLA2, releasing arachidonic acid, which is converted into prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), stimulating proliferation of neighbouring cells via the “Phoenix Rising” mechanism.[26] Anastasis refers to the reversal of apoptosis when death stimuli are removed, allowing damaged but surviving cells to proliferate and contribute to tumour heterogeneity.[27]

In the progression of OSMF, the steady accumulation of fibrous tissue leads to microvascular occlusion and chronic tissue hypoxia. This environment stabilises the transcription factor HIF-1, which promotes the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Survivin (BIRC5) and suppresses the activity of Caspase-3. Additionally, HIF-1 triggers angiogenic mediators like VEGF, which helps cells that have become resistant to programmed death survive under extreme metabolic stress. Chronic hypoxia also disrupts normal apoptotic signalling and mitochondrial stability, further facilitating the malignant transformation of the atrophic epithelium. While various investigations have utilised tissue or serum, there is a notable gap in literature regarding the simultaneous evaluation of Caspase-3 and Survivin using saliva a safe and non-invasive diagnostic medium, specifically in the context of gutka consumption.[28293031]

Thus, this study was designed to evaluate the effect of Gutka on apoptotic regulation by analysing Caspase-3 and Survivin levels in OSMF and OSCC. The aim of the current study is to evaluate the role of salivary Survivin and Caspase-3 in apoptosis regulation across gutka chewers without lesions, OSMF, and OSCC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Oral Pathology, Government College of Dentistry, Indore, following ethical approval (289/IEC/SS/2024).

Study groups

The sample size was determined based on an anticipated medium-to-large effect size (f = 0.40), an alpha error of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80% for comparison among four study groups. The calculated minimum sample size was approximately 84 participants. To enhance statistical reliability and compensate for potential sample loss, a total of 92 participants were included in the study. The final distribution comprised healthy controls (n = 17), gutka chewers without lesions (n = 25), OSMF patients (n = 25), and OSCC patients (n = 25). The unequal distribution in the control group was primarily due to logistical constraints associated with the ELISA assay. Specifically, the 96-well ELISA plate required allocation of wells for one blank and three standards (performed in replicates), thereby reducing the number of wells available for study samples. To maximise efficient utilisation of the assay plate while maintaining equal representation of disease groups, the control group was limited to 17 samples. Despite this unequal allocation, the control sample size remained adequate for statistical comparison and did not compromise the validity of the analyses performed.

The inclusion criteria comprised regular Gutka chewers with a habit duration of ≥2 years, individuals with clinically and/or histopathologically confirmed OSMF or OSCC, body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m², and participants without systemic conditions known to affect salivary biomarker levels. A BMI cut-off of <25 was selected to minimise the confounding effects of overweight and obesity, which are known to influence inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and apoptosis-related biomarkers. OSMF cases were graded according to the Khanna and Andrade classification, while OSCC cases were histopathologically confirmed and were included in a same group irrespective of the histopathological grade. The exclusion criteria included the presence of any systemic disease, a history of treatment for malignancy, ongoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and the use of medications known to influence salivary or serum protein levels.

Unstimulated whole saliva (3–5 mL) was collected between 9 and 11 AM. Samples were centrifuged and stored at −80°C [Figure 1]. After saliva collection, samples were immediately centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C to remove cellular debris and impurities. The clear supernatant was carefully transferred into sterile Eppendorf tubes under aseptic conditions. To maintain sample integrity, all processed saliva samples were stored at -80°C until further biochemical analysis. The expression levels of Survivin and Caspase-3 were quantified using commercially available sandwich Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, standards and appropriately diluted saliva samples were added to microplate wells pre-coated with specific capture antibodies and incubated to allow antigen binding. After incubation, the wells were washed thoroughly to remove unbound substances.

Collection, centrifugation, and storage of salivary samples

Subsequently, a biotin-labelled detection antibody specific to the target analyte was added and incubated, forming an antibody–antigen–antibody sandwich complex. Following a washing step, streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate was added and incubated. Streptavidin binds strongly to biotin, thereby linking the enzyme horseradish peroxidase to the immune complex.

After further washing to eliminate excess conjugate, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added. Horseradish peroxidase catalysed the conversion of TMB into a blue-coloured product. The enzymatic reaction was stopped using the supplied stop solution, resulting in a yellow-coloured end product. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations of Survivin and Caspase-3 were calculated from the standard calibration curve generated using known concentrations of the respective standards.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained were entered into MS excel sheets and analysed in IBM SPSS ver 24 for windows (IBM corp). Descriptive statistics mean, standard deviation, median, and standard error were calculated. Kruskal–Wallis test was performed for intergroup comparisons. Pearson correlation and P value were assessed relationships among biomarkers, disease severity. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

The study population included participants across a broad age spectrum, ranging from 15 to 85 years. Males constituted the majority of the study population (57.61%), while females accounted for 42.39% [Table 1]. The most prevalent age group was 41–50 years, representing approximately 31.0% of participants, followed by the 21–30 years and 31–40 years age groups. Healthy individuals were free from oral lesions and deleterious habits, whereas participants in the other study groups reported varying durations and frequencies of Gutka consumption. Among patients with OSMF, disease severity ranged from Grade I to Grade IVA, reflecting different stages of progression. In the OSCC group, lesions were observed at multiple oral sites, including the buccal mucosa, tongue, palate, gingiva, alveolar mucosa, and retromolar region. A general trend of longer duration and higher frequency of Gutka consumption was observed among participants with OSMF and OSCC, suggesting a cumulative effect of chronic Gutka exposure on disease progression [Table 1].

The findings examine the relationship between group progression from healthy individuals with no habit to gutka chewers with no lesions to those diagnosed with OSMF and further to OSCC and salivary levels of the apoptosis-related biomarkers Caspase-3 and Survivin.

Caspase-3 levels exhibited a non-linear pattern across the study groups, with mean values of 4.90 ± 4.43 ng/ml in healthy controls, 4.50 ± 2.71 ng/ml in Gutka chewers without lesions, 3.50 ± 2.82 ng/ml in OSMF patients, and 5.50 ± 3.62 ng/ml in OSCC patients [Figure 2]. Correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive association between disease progression and Caspase-3 levels (r = 0.500), which was not statistically significant (P = 0.491). The observed trend suggests an initial decline in Caspase-3 expression from healthy controls to OSMF, potentially reflecting suppression of apoptotic activity during the early stages of fibrotic transformation. Subsequently, Caspase-3 levels increased in OSCC, which may indicate reactivation of apoptotic pathways in response to malignant progression, increased cellular turnover, or apoptosis-associated proliferative mechanisms associated with advanced disease stage.

Caspase levels across groups

In contrast, Survivin levels demonstrated a strong and statistically significant positive correlation with disease progression, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.981 and a P value of 0.002 [Table 2]. Mean Survivin levels increased progressively across the study groups, measuring 1.00 ± 0.73 pg/ml in healthy controls, 2.00 ± 1.36 pg/ml in Gutka chewers without lesions, 3.50 ± 2.82 pg/ml in OSMF patients, and 5.00 ± 3.04 pg/ml in OSCC patients [Figure 3]. This consistent upward trend indicates a gradual increase in Survivin expression with advancing disease severity, reaching its highest levels in OSCC. The findings support the hypothesis that Survivin, a key anti-apoptotic protein, may play an important role in the pathogenesis and malignant transformation of Gutka-associated oral lesions by promoting cell survival and facilitating evasion of apoptosis during oral carcinogenesis.

DISCUSSION

The present study examined salivary Caspase-3 and Survivin expression in healthy individuals, Gutka chewers without lesions, individuals with OSMF, and patients with OSCC. The goal was to understand how Gutka-related carcinogens affect apoptosis as the disease progresses. The findings showed a non-linear pattern of Caspase-3 and a steady increase in Survivin expression. This indicates a stage-dependent disruption of apoptosis from regular exposure to premalignancy and malignancy. These results match studies exploring other salivary biomarkers in OSMF and OSCC.[3233] Increased p53 levels have been found in OSMF and OSCC, showing rising genomic instability and DNA damage during cancer development.[34] Likewise, Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a pleiotropic inflammatory cytokine that plays a critical role in the progression, treatment resistance, and clinical outcome of OSCC.[35] Research on Cyfra 21-1, a fragment of cytokeratin-19, has shown much higher levels in OSCC patients compared to healthy controls and OSMF subjects.[36] This suggests increased epithelial turnover and tumor burden. Similarly, raised salivary LDH activity has consistently been reported in OSMF and OSCC, indicating more cellular damage, changed metabolic activity, and tissue breakdown.[37] These findings support the idea that salivary biomarkers can be effective, non-invasive tools for detecting molecular changes related to oral potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer. Caspase-3 and Survivin offer additional insights into how apoptosis works in these conditions.

Salivary Caspase-3 vs study groups

The non-linear shift in Caspase-3 observed across study groups reflects the complex interplay between inflammatory injury, fibrotic remodelling, hypoxia, and oncogenic transformation. Among gutka chewers without lesions, exposure to tobacco-specific nitrosamines and areca-nut alkaloids generates oxidative stress and DNA damage, which may activate intrinsic apoptotic mechanisms.[38] Nevertheless, chronic irritation and mild inflammation may temper apoptosis, resulting in variable Caspase-3 responses.

In OSMF, Caspase-3 showed clear suppression. Early OSMF is dominated by chronic inflammation, where persistent cytokine activity alters the stromal–epithelial interface and reduces apoptotic sensitivity.[39] As fibrosis advances, collagen deposition restricts vascularity, leading to sustained hypoxia. Hypoxia stabilises HIF-1α, which is known to downregulate multiple pro-apoptotic proteins including Caspase-3.[40] This shift represents an adaptive survival response permitting cell persistence under ischemic stress.

Furthermore, OSMF is strongly associated with overexpression of TGF-β, a major fibrogenic cytokine that inhibits caspase activation while promoting anti-apoptotic signalling.[41] Oxidative stress–generated ROS from tobacco and areca nut exposure further impair mitochondrial Cytochrome-c release, an essential step in caspase activation.[42] These mechanisms collectively explain the reduced salivary Caspase-3 expression seen in OSMF.

A paradoxical rise in Caspase-3 was observed in OSCC subjects. Similar trends have been documented, suggesting malignant cells may show enhanced caspase expression due to intense proliferative stress and genomic instability.[43] However, elevated Caspase-3 in OSCC does not imply successful apoptosis. Cancer cells often repurpose caspase signalling to support tumour progression.

One such process is apoptosis-induced proliferation (AiP), where Caspase-3 activates iPLA2, resulting in arachidonic acid release and subsequent PGE2 synthesis, which stimulates proliferation of adjacent cells.[44] Here, Caspase-3 paradoxically promotes growth rather than cell death.

Another contributing factor is anastasis, the ability of cells to recover even after caspase activation. Evidence suggests that apoptosis-initiating cells may reverse the death process and survive, accumulating mutations that enhance carcinogenic potential.[45] Thus, increased Caspase-3 expression may reflect transient apoptotic attempts combined with escape mechanisms.

Overall, the observed pattern suppressed Caspase-3 in OSMF and elevated Caspase-3 in OSCC-aligns with established models describing fibrosis-associated apoptosis resistance followed by malignant reactivation of dysfunctional apoptotic pathways.[46] This highlights Caspase-3 as a dynamic biomarker capable of shifting from suppression in premalignancy to dysregulation in malignancy.

Salivary Survivin across study groups

In contrast to Caspase-3, Survivin showed a consistent rise from gutka chewers without lesions to OSMF and further into OSCC. Survivin, a crucial member of the IAP family, regulates caspase inhibition and mitotic control.[47] Its stepwise increase suggests a progressive reinforcement of anti-apoptotic pathways with disease advancement.

In OSMF, chronic inflammation driven by IL-6 and TNF-α activates signalling pathways such as STAT3 and NF-κB, both of which strongly induce Survivin transcription.[48] This establishes a positive survival feedback loop, enabling epithelial cells to persist despite ongoing genotoxic stress.[49] This persistence promotes accumulation of molecular errors.

Hypoxia arising from dense fibrosis in OSMF further induces Survivin. HIF-1α directly upregulates Survivin gene expression under low-oxygen states.[50] Hypoxia-induced acidosis additionally stabilises Survivin post-translationally by inhibiting its degradation.[51] Combined, these mechanisms sustain a robust anti-apoptotic microenvironment conducive to malignant transformation.

The further rise in Survivin from OSMF to OSCC aligns with its recognised role in OSCC, where it is linked with aggressiveness, enhanced survival, and reduced treatment sensitivity.[5253] In cancer cells, Survivin inhibits caspase-dependent apoptosis, stabilises mitotic spindles, and maintains chromosomal fidelity, enabling uncontrolled proliferation.

Survivin also contributes to anastasis. Cells that initiate apoptosis may recover if Survivin suppresses downstream caspase cascades and stabilises microtubular structures.[54] This allows survival of genetically unstable cells that may evolve into malignant clones.

Thus, the progressive increase in Survivin expression observed in this study appears to represent a transition from adaptive cellular survival mechanisms during early Gutkha exposure to enhanced resistance to apoptosis in premalignant lesions, culminating in a pronounced malignant survival phenotype in OSCC. These findings highlight the potential value of Survivin as a biomarker for assessing disease severity and monitoring disease progression from OPMDs to OSCC. Nevertheless, further large-scale, multi-centre, longitudinal studies incorporating tissue correlation are needed to confirm its clinical significance and establish its utility as a reliable diagnostic and prognostic marker.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that saliva is a valuable non-invasive tool for detecting gutka-related apoptotic changes. Survivin proved to be a strong marker of disease progression, with markedly higher levels in OSMF and OSCC. Caspase-3 showed a dual pattern—reduced in OSMF and elevated in OSCC indicating early apoptosis suppression followed by potential apoptosis-induced proliferation in advanced cancer. Overall, the findings clarify how gutka promotes apoptotic imbalance, contributing to the development and progression of oral cancer.
