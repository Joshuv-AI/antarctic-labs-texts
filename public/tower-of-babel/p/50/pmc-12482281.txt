Circulating-free DNA and myocardial infarction: From diagnostic promise to clinical translation
Authors: Jeff Clyde G Corpuz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis correspondence responds to the review by Rafiei et al. on circulating-free DNA (cfDNA) as a biomarker for early detection of myocardial infarction (MI). CfDNA shows potential for rapid diagnosis and real-time monitoring, but major barriers remain. Lack of standardized testing methods, uncertain utility in emergency care, and high costs risk delaying adoption and widening health inequities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Key priorities include harmonizing laboratory protocols, developing point-of-care platforms, and integrating cfDNA into diagnostic algorithms with cardiac troponins and electrocardiograms (ECG). Research should focus on large, long-term cohorts that include diverse populations, and test multi-marker strategies combining cfDNA with microRNAs and DNA methylation profiles. Ensuring affordability and accessibility will be critical to translate cfDNA from experimental promise to routine cardiovascular practice.Keywords: Circulating-free DNA, Myocardial infarction, Biomarker standardization, Point-of-care diagnostics, Equity in cardiovascular care

Dear editor,

I commend the timely review by Rafiei et al. on circulating-free DNA (cfDNA) as a novel biomarker for early detection of myocardial infarction [1]. Their article highlights cfDNA's ability to identify myocardial injury within 0–2 hours, well before conventional troponins, and provides a comprehensive discussion of its mechanistic basis, diagnostic performance, and potential integration into multi-marker strategies.

Key challenges remain. First, the absence of a unified clinical standard for cfDNA quantification limits translation. Protocols for sample handling, assay calibration, and reporting must be harmonized to reduce variability and enable regulatory approval [2]. Building collaborative networks, akin to those that advanced troponin standardization, would accelerate this process.

Second, real-world applicability in emergency care is unclear. Emergency departments need rapid, affordable, and simple assays. Development of point-of-care cfDNA platforms, ideally integrated with rapid troponin testing, should be prioritized. Hybrid diagnostic algorithms that use cfDNA for early screening, followed by confirmatory troponin and ECG, merit pilot evaluation [3].

Third, issues of equity demand attention. The greatest burden of myocardial infarction lies in low- and middle-income countries, yet access to cfDNA testing may be constrained by cost and infrastructure. Research on low-cost, simplified assays suitable for resource-limited settings is urgently needed [4]. Finally, prognostic value requires clarification. Integrating cfDNA with other molecular markers into composite risk scores may enhance predictive accuracy [5].

Overall, Rafiei et al. provide a strong foundation. The field now requires rigorous standardization, translation to point-of-care use, equitable access, and prognostic validation to achieve clinical impact.
