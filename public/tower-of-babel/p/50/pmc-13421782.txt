Prophage induction stimulates ribosomal RNA operon recombination and facilitates genome mobility
Authors: Janine Z Bowring, Freja C Mikkelsen, Hanne Ingmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial genomes contain multiple ribosomal RNA (rRNA) operons that by homologous recombination facilitate genome rearrangements. Here, we show that a Staphylococcus aureus temperate bacteriophage stimulates homologous recombination between the rRNA operons flanking the prophage and promotes a novel route of horizontal gene transfer we term rrn-linked lateral transduction. By polymerase chain reaction, sequencing of phage-packaged bacterial DNA, and phage transduction assays, we show that upon induction of the prophage, large circles of DNA formed by rRNA operon recombination are packaged and transduced by the phage via the mechanism of lateral transduction. This phenomenon is likely to be widely occurring, with prophage-linked rRNA operon recombination also shown here in Salmonella. Our results challenge the concepts of mobile and core genomes and establish rrn-linked lateral transduction as a new form of gene transfer involving rRNA operons.

Introduction

Bacterial viruses (bacteriophages or phages) are key in transferring genetic material between bacteria, including the human opportunistic pathogen Staphylococcus aureus. In this organism phage-mediated transduction is the main route of horizontal gene transfer promoting dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes [1]. Transduction is mostly observed for temperate phages, which either replicate lytically to form new phage particles, or integrate into bacterial genomes at specific ‘attB’ sites to form bacterial lysogens that occasionally are induced [2]. Known transduction mechanisms are generalized, specialized, and lateral transduction [3]. Lateral transduction occurs when an induced prophage, prior to excision from the genome, initiates in situ bidirectional replication and unidirectional packaging of bacterial sequences downstream of the phage [4, 5]. Specialized transduction has long been established as the incorrect excision of a prophage, leading to small flanking fragments of bacterial DNA being packaged together with the incomplete phages [6], while generalized transduction involves incorporation of random DNA fragments based on pseudo-pac sequences found in bacterial genomes [7]. In previous work, we observed transduction-mediated transfer of bacterial DNA that was not explained by any of these established mechanisms [8]. Presently, we have explored the mechanism behind this novel type of transfer.

Most bacteria harbour more than one ribosomal RNA (rrn) operon [9, 10], and many species harbour multiple homologous rrn operons distributed throughout the genome [11, 12]. This genetic redundancy has been attributed variously to benefitting fitness and adaptation [13, 14] and as facilitating differential protein production levels [15, 16, 17]. Here, we have investigated how a DNA region not known to be transduced by lateral or specialized transduction can display elevated transfer between S. aureus cells when compared to the rest of the staphylococcal genome. We link the phenomenon to the rrn operons flanking the prophage and show that prophage induction stimulates recombination at these highly homologous regions leading to extrachromosomal DNA circles, which subsequently through lateral transduction are replicated by the induced prophage and transduced at elevated frequencies. Interestingly, we find similar genomic locations of prophages and rrn operons in Salmonella, suggesting that rrn operon redundancy generally contributes to horizontal gene transfer via prophage replication and bacterial recombination.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Staphylococcus aureus strains were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) and on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates (Oxoid), with or without antibiotics as appropriate. Salmonella enterica strains were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and agar plates (Oxoid).

DNA methods

Primers used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table S2 and plasmids in Supplementary Table S3. Ppolymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed using Platinum Taq high fidelity polymerase for cloning and DreamTaq Green master mix for circularization checks (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmids were constructed using the seamless ligation cloning extract method [18]. Sanger sequencing checks of cloned mutations were sequenced at Eurofins Genomics.

Bacteriophage infection and induction

Bacteriophage infection and induction was performed as previously described [8]. Unless otherwise stated in the specific methodology or legend, infection volumes were 10 ml starting and 5 ml post-pellet, and induction volumes were 5 ml. Briefly, for phage infection bacteria were grown to 0.15 OD600, pelleted and resuspending in a half volume of 1:1 TSB and phage buffer (MgSO4 1 mM, CaCl2 4 mM, Tris–HCl, pH 8, 50 mM, NaCl2 0.1 M). The culture was infected with phage at MOI 1 and incubated at 30°C, 80 rpm until full lysis. Lysates were filtered at 0.22 μM and stored at 4°C. For inductions, lysogens were grown to 0.1–0.2 OD600 and induced with 2 μg ml−1 mitomycin C (Sigma, from Streptomyces caespitosus). The culture was then incubated at 30°C, 80 rpm until lysis and filtered using 0.22 μM filters.

Bacteriophage titer

Phage titers were performed as previously described [8]. Briefly, recipient strains were grown to 0.35 OD600 and 100 μl of recipient were mixed with different phage lysate dilutions in phage buffer (100–108). Mixtures were incubated for 10 min at room temperature before 3 ml molten PTA (phage top agar; Oxoid nutrient broth no. 2, agar 3.5% wt/vol) was added and plated on PB plates (phage base; nutrient broth no. 2, agar 7% wt/vol). Plates were incubated at 37°C overnight and plaques were enumerated as plaque forming units per ml (PFU ml−1).

Bacteriophage transduction

Transductions were performed essentially as previously described [4, 8], using lysates that had been filtered using 0.22 μM filters and treated with DNase/RNAse as described in the ‘DNA sequencing’ section. Briefly, recipient strains were grown until OD600 1.4 and 4.4 mM CaCl2 was added. One millilitres aliquots of recipients were mixed with 100 μl of phage lysate dilutions (100–104). Mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 20 min before adding 3 ml liquid TTA (transduction top agar; TSA, 50% agar) and plating on selective TSA plates. Plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C and colonies counted as colony forming units per ml (CFU ml−1). Transduction units (TrU) were calculated by normalizing the CFU ml−1 to the relevant lysate PFU ml−1 and adjusting to a standard PFU (1 × 109 PFU) [4]. Full statistical comparisons of TrU values can be found in Supplementary Table S4.

qPCR test of eDNA removal from lysates

Phage lysates were obtained and treated with RNase and DNase according to the DNA sequencing preparation described in the ‘DNA sequencing’ section. qPCR reactions were made in triplicates for each condition using FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche) and run on LightCycler® 480 (Roche) with primers targeting pyk and 16S. Three biological replicates of the lysates were tested. Data were processed using raw Cq values and an adjusted 2^(−ΔΔCt) method using the untreated samples as the control group. Statistical analyses (Welch’s t-test) was made on Cq values.

DNA sequencing

Preparation of phage lysates for DNA sequencing of transductomics was done as previously described [8]. Initially, 100 ml induced lysates were filtered at 0.22 μM (Millipore® Steritop®, vacuum bottle top filtre) treated with DNase (2.5 U/ml, Thermo) and RNase (1 μg/ml, Sigma) at 37C for 1.5 h. Capsids were then precipitated in the following manner: NaCl2 (1 M) was added and mixed on the ice at 4°C for 1 h. The lysate was centrifuged at 11 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected, mixed with PEG 8000 (10% wt/vol), before incubation on ice overnight at 4°C. The lysate was centrifuged at 11 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and supernatant discarded, with falcon tubes dried. The pellet appeared as two vertical white lines and was resuspended in 8 ml phage buffer per 500 ml lysate (1.6 ml), with resuspension for at least 1 h at 4°C.

DNA extraction for sequencing was performed using the Sigma Bacterial GenElute kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions for Gram-positive bacteria, with an adjustment for phage lysates of skipping the lysostaphin incubation step and extending the proteinase K incubation to 1 h. DNA concentration and absorbance peaks were checked using a Thermo NanoDrop 2000. Samples were sent to Eurofins Genomics for Illumina NGS sequencing. ≥600 ng of DNA was sent to Eurofins for each sample with a minimum volume of 40 μl. DNA libraries were QC checked on entry for purity (OD260/280: 1.8–2.0), prepared, and sequenced by Eurofins Genomics using the following pipeline: library preparation, DNA fragmentation, adapter ligation, size selection, amplification, Illumina-compatible PCR-based library preparation using unique dual indexing. Sequencing specifications technology: Illumina NovaSeq X + system, run type: paired end, read length: 2 × 150 bp.

Total numbers of paired reads obtained were: 8325–4 wild type (wt) φ11 rep 1, 21 189 160, rep 2, 218 343 562, rep 3, 20 257 124; 8325–4 ∆rrn φ11 rep 1, 11 415 026, rep 2, 54 491 260, rep 3, 23136734. The resulting reads were analysed and visualized in Geneious Prime. The BBDUK plug in was used for quality control and trimming with the following settings:

Trimmed output was aligned to the reference bacterial genome (NCTC 8325 and 8325–4) using the Geneious assembler on medium-low sensitivity/fast, with standard settings with additional detection of short insertions and large deletions up to 3000 bp, to look for phage packaged bacterial DNA.

To compare transductomics read coverage across the genome between the bacterial wt and ∆rrn propagated lysates, the genomes were split into 270 bins of 10 Kbp using mosdepth with -n –by 10 000 settings [19], with bin size selected to limit sequencing noise while capturing overall phage packaging trends, as in [8] but with increased resolution. Coverage in regions predicted to be transferred by lateral (bins 108–188), specialized (bins 189–193), and homology transduction (bins 194–199) were compared between the 8325–4 φ11 and ∆rrn φ11 derived lysates by independent t-tests.

Oxford Nanopore sequencing was performed by Eurofins Genomics using their standard pipeline. Briefly, 20 μl of 30 ng/μl DNA was sent per sample, with amplification-free, including minimal fragmentation library preparation and sequencing using v14 library prep chemistrywith an Oxford Nanopore GridION (flow cell R10.4.1). Reads were aligned to references using the minimap2 plug in in Geneious Prime (minimap2_macos -x map-ont --secondary = yes -N 5 -p 0.8 -a refSeq.fasta input.fastq -o output.sam). Extraction of reads based on length (>30 kb) was performed using Geneious Prime’s interface. Graph visualization is shown as coverage per base.

Large circle DNA extraction

For extraction of the large plasmid-like structures, samples were grown for 1 h, before induction with MitC as described. Samples were initially grown in 50 ml volumes to 0.1–0.2 as for inductions, before splitting into two (induced with MitC, noninduced). Samples were then harvested at 1 h after induction, with noninduced samples taken simultaneously. Twenty-five milliliters of the culture was pelleted per condition and prepared using the previously described method for large circular DNA extraction [8, 20], adding 5 μl lysostaphin with incubation at 37°C for 1 h to the resuspension step and resuspending the extracted DNA in an initial 50 μl water. Fifty nanograms of template was used for subsequent PCRs using a DreamsTaq polymerase kit.

COG classification analyses

Using the Cluster of Orthologous Genes (COG) database [21], genomes were divided into two regions: the region bracketed by the rrn1 and rrn2 rrn operons versus the rest of the genome. For each genome, these two sequences were extracted and the COGclassifier program [22] was used to classify the genes in these sequences by assigned function. The proportion of each COG classification was calculated as a percentage using the total number of genes found in each sequence.

Analysis for S. aureus carriage of Sa5 prophages and the location relative to rrn operons

The conserved regions of the different integrase types of temperate S. aureus phages were established in [23]. The conserved DNA sequence in the Sa5 type integrase was used to search the NCBI database for S. aureus genomes encoding Sa5 integrase prophages (Supplementary Table S5). These were filtered for genomes carrying rrn operon annotations, using Geneious Prime annotation selection tools, and the location of these operons relative to the Sa5 type integrase was assessed manually using Geneious Prime visualization of the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene annotations relative to the Sa5 conserved region.

Recombination analyses

A customized nucleotide database was created made up of complete annotated genome sequences of S. aureus strains, where half were representative of the species diversity [4] and half encoded Sa5 prophages flanked by rrn operons identified in Supplementary Table S6. The Sa5 prophage encoding strains were determined as such as described in the ‘Analysis for S. aureus carriage of Sa5 prophages and the location relative to rrn operons’ section. The FASTA genomes were annotated using Prokka [24] and the core genome alignment for the complete database was obtained using Roary with default parameters [25] and a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed with FastTree [26] using a general time reversible model of nucleotide substitution. BLASTn was used to identify the core gene most proximal to the Sa5 attB site in the genome of S. aureus strain NCTC8325. The 300 kb up- and downstream of this gene was extracted for all strains in the database using Bedtools [27]. The extracted regions were annotated using Prokka [24] and clusters of orthologous genes were identified with the OrthoMCL algorithm [28] from the open-source get_homologues software [29] with parameters -n 8 -M -C 50 -S 30 -t 161 (80%) -e -F 1. To analyse the potential impact of recombination on the region flanking the Sa5 attB sites, the conserved clusters (defined as present in over 80% of the isolates) were aligned by MUSCLE [30] and concatenated into a super alignment with the perl script catfasta2phyml.pl (Nylander 2010; https://github.com/nylander/catfasta2phyml). Potential recombinant regions were identified with ClonalFrameML [31] using default parameters and visualized in R [32].

Analysis for Salmonella carriage of P2-like prophages and location relative to rrn operons

Conserved genes (>85% carriage) for the 32 P2-like phages identified as positioned between rrn operons were identified using OrthoMCL as described above. A conserved DNA sequence in the replication endonuclease of the P2-like phages was identified using MUSCLE alignments of the conserved genes and used to search the NCBI database for Salmonella genomes encoding probable P2-like prophages (Supplementary Table S5). These were filtered for genomes carrying rrn operon annotations and the location of these operons relative to the P2-like endonuclease was assessed manually using Geneious Prime visualization of the rRNA gene annotations relative to the Sa5 conserved region.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 10, and Geneious Prime 2024. Specific analyses and statistical tests are indicated and described in the ‘Materials and methods’ section and figure legends, where appropriate.

Results

Prophage induction stimulates rrn operon recombination and phage packaging

In a previous study, we screened for regions of the S. aureus NCTC 8325-4 genome that in comparison to the rest of the bacterial genome showed elevated transduction frequencies with the transducing temperate phage φ11 [8]. One such region was located upstream of the φ11 integration site (attB) and was not explained by commonly known transduction types (Fig. 1A) [8]. Here, we re-analysed the bacterial DNA sequences packaged in the φ11 phage and observed that, in addition to the elevated reads of DNA downstream of the φ11 integration site known to be transferred by lateral transduction [4], the region of unexplained elevated reads was flanked by two rrn operons (Fig. 1A). These two rrn operons have 99.78% identity (5.24 kb, 96% coverage) and here we have named them rrn1 and rrn2 (Fig. 1A).

Given the occurrence of in situ replication of the φ11 prophage reported during lateral transduction in S. aureus [4], we hypothesized that the region including the prophage could circularize at the rrn operons via homologous recombination and subsequently be replicated and packaged by the phage. Therefore, we designed primers that flanked the rrn operons and examined circularization by PCR in strains with or without the φ11 prophage, and with or without treatment with mitomycin, a DNA damaging agent known to induce φ11 [33] (Fig. 1B–E). Circularization was detected by traditional PCR using primers located outside of the rrn operons as the homologous and repetitive nature of the rrn operons prevented design of adequate primers for quantitative PCR (Fig. 1E).

Importantly, circularization between rrn operons was only detected in the presence of the φ11 prophage. In cells harbouring φ11, circularization was at the limit of detection in the absence of mitomycin but was clearly observed in cells treated with mitomycin that induces the prophage (Fig. 1B). Circularization still occurred in deletion mutants of the phage-encoded integrase and the ith phage gene previously annotated as an excisionase (Fig. 1B). The latter finding supports recent observations that this ‘excisionase’ is in fact a phage defense protein [34]. Conversely circularization was at the limit of detection in cells carrying a defective prophage that is uninducible due to a mutation in the phage cI repressor, giving a faint band only in the presence of mitomycin (φ11 cIG131E; Fig. 1C) [35]. Also, circularization was not detected in mutant cells lacking the homologous recombinase, RecA, irrespective of the presence or induction of the prophage (Fig. 1B). To distinguish between the role of RecA in homologous recombination [36, 37] and in proteolytic cleavage of the phage repressor during prophage induction [38], we assessed circularization in a ∆recA,recAN303D mutant strain carrying φ11, where the SOS response and LexA cleavage functions are intact so prophages are inducible but recombinase activity and homologous recombination are abolished [4, 39]. This mutation is located in the C-terminal domain, which has been implicated in recombinase function and acts as a secondary dsDNA binding pocket [39]. No circularization was detected in this mutant, confirming that RecA-mediated recombination is essential to the circularization of the region (Fig. 1D). Lastly, when the rrn1 operon was deleted, no circularization occurred confirming the necessity of both homologous rrn regions for recombination to take place (Fig. 1C).

Elevated transduction of the rrn-flanked region eliminated by deletion of rrn1 operon

Based on the finding that induction of the φ11 prophage stimulates circularization mediated by the flanking rrn operons, we hypothesized that this DNA circle is replicated and packaged by the prophage, directed by the pac site located within the prophage. To test this, we performed DNA sequencing of phage lysates derived from induction of the wt φ11 lysogen, as well as the lysogen with the rrn1 operon deleted in which circularization was prevented (Fig. 1C). Phage lysate treatment with DNase and Rnase successfully eliminated eDNA from outside the phage capsids (Supplementary Table S6). Figure 2 shows the reads for these phage preparations mapping to the 8325–4 S. aureus genome, while mapping to 8325 can be seen in Supplementary Fig. S1. Importantly, in DNA of φ11 induced from the rrn1 deletion mutant we observed significantly fewer reads mapping to the region between the two rrn operons, compared to φ11 induced from the wt strain (Fig. 2A–C). In contrast, there were no differences between the two phages preparations in the number of bacterial reads aligning to regions known to be transduced by traditional lateral or specialized transduction (Fig. 2B and C).

Long read Oxford Nanopore sequencing of lysates has been used to analyse the S. aureus mobilome interaction with phages [40]. Supplementary Fig. S2 shows transductomics using Oxford Nanopore sequencing from wt φ11 induced lysate DNA mapping to 8325–4 and 8325. Trends are comparable to the illumina data (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2A–C). Interestingly, there are spikes in reads mapping at all rrn operons (Supplementary Fig. S2C) and, when reads over 30 kb were mapped to the 8325–4 genome, individual reads could be mapped to lateral, specialized and rrn flanked regions (Supplementary Fig. S2D), in likely incidences of packaged transducing headful’s of DNA.

Following this, we measured transduction of erythromycin resistance markers by induction of φ11 from the wt and rrn1 deletion mutant strains. The resistance markers were part of the Nebraska transposon mutant library [41, 42] and were chosen in locations to be transduced by lateral (Tn_1799, Tn_1852), specialized (Tn_1865), and generalized transduction (Tn_RotB, Tn_2026), as well as in the region linked to the rrn operons (Tn_1916; Fig. 2C). Remarkably, the Tn_1916 transposon in the rrn operon linked region showed a significant reduction in TrUs in the rrn1 deletion background compared to the wt (Fig. 2D, P = .0011**). Transduction was also abolished when using a packaging deficient ϕ11 for transfer (ϕ11 ΔterS, Supplementary Table S7). Based on our data, we propose to term this novel horizontal gene transfer mechanism, rrn-linked lateral transduction.

rrn operon circularization correlates with bacterial genome recombination

In S. aureus strains, the rrn operons are generally located in the same region of the genome [43]. Likewise, S. aureus prophages integrate into specific attB sites depending on the phage-encoded integrases, which form the basis for dividing the prophages into subfamilies (Sa1–Sa9) [23]. To address whether the potential for prophage mediated rrn operon circularization is widespread for S. aureus strains, we searched the S. aureus genomes of NCBI for the DNA sequence defining the Sa5-type prophage subfamily (Supplementary Table S5) to which φ11 belongs using BLASTn [23]. We identified 398 bacterial genomes encoding Sa5-type integrases and all had ≥97.8% identity (with 100% coverage) to the φ11 prophage integrase. From these, 374 contained rrn annotation data (Supplementary Table S8) and 328 (88%) harboured the same structure as S. aureus strain 8325–4 φ11, with a Sa5 integrase-type prophage being flanked by two rrn operons distanced by ~180–230 kb (Fig. 3A, selection; full list Supplementary Table S8).

To assess the potential impact of rrn-linked lateral transduction on horizontal gene transfer in S. aureus, we constructed a custom database containing genomes with rrn operons flanking Sa5 integrase genes (Fig. 3A) and S. aureus genomes reported to be representative for S. aureus genetic diversity [4] (Supplementary Table S9). Using this database, we examined the incidence of recombination within the rrn1 and rrn2 flanked regions, using the pipeline previously described for assessing recombination linked to lateral transduction [4, 31]. Multiple regions of predicted recombination were detected throughout the core genome flanking the φ11 integration site (a total of 599 predicted recombinant sites). As shown previously [4], the region downstream of the φ11 prophage integration site transferred by lateral transduction (‘Lateral’ in Fig. 2B) showed a significantly higher number of predicted recombinant fragments compared to the generalized transduced region (‘Upstream’, P = <.0001****, Friedman test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test comparing three DNA regions). Importantly, significantly higher numbers of recombinant fragments were predicted for the region between rrn1 and rrn2 (‘rrn operon’) compared to the upstream region (‘Upstream’; P = <.0001****). The traditionally lateral transduced region and the rrn operon linked region showed comparable numbers of predicted recombinant fragments (‘Lateral’ versus ‘rrn operon’, P = >.9999ns). The clustering of the recombination events and high levels of homoplasy implicates a degree of clade-specificity, likely due to variation in the MGE presence/absence and activity together with R-M barrier variation [44]. Additionally, COG analysis of the original S. aureus strain 8325 (the parental strain of the prophage free strain 8325–4) comparing the region flanked by the rrn operons (between rrn1 and rrn2) with the rest of the genome indicated that this region contains a higher percentage of mobilome genes (13.0%–0.9%, Fig. 3C). For both 8325 and 8325–4 there were also slightly higher percentages of defense and recombination classified genes in region flanked by rrn1 and rrn2 and less of other classifiers compared to the rest of the genome (Supplementary Table S10).

rrn operon flanking prophages also occurs in Salmonella

To examine whether transduction mediated by rrn operon recombination is likely to occur in other species than S. aureus, we examined Salmonella enterica where transduction is widely reported [6, 45–47] and where there are multiple reports of rrn operon mediated genomic rearrangements and duplications [48–51]. From a small database of Salmonella complete genomes representing 123 serovars of high quality [52], we analysed for the presence of rrn operons distanced up to 200 kb apart and flanking predicted prophages (Supplementary Table S11). Here, we identified 32 Salmonella genomes in which predicted prophages were flanked by rrn operons (Fig. 4A). In these 32 cases, the prophages identified belonged to the P2 family of highly transducing phages. To search for such prophages in larger databases, we identified a representative gene that was conserved within the P2-like phages (replication endonuclease, present in >87%) and searched the Salmonella genomes of NCBI for a conserved DNA sequence from this P2-like prophage subfamily using BLASTn (Supplementary Table S5). We identified 1254 Salmonella genomes containing the sequence and selected the top 500 hits to analyse further. From these, 453 contained rrn annotation data (Supplementary Table S12) and 430 (94.9%) harboured the ‘prophage flanked by rrn operons’ structure distanced by ~180–230 kb. Indeed, one of the NCBI hits was an assigned plasmid of 194 kb containing the P2-like prophage and two rrn operons (Fig. 4B). This ‘plasmid’ gave no plasmid hits in PlasmidFinder [53], BLASTn results indicated Salmonella chromosomal hits, including in P2-like prophage/rrn operon flanked regions from other strains.

To determine whether circularization occurs, we obtained 2 different Salmonella serotypes (Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serotype Braenderup NCTC5750 and Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serotype give NCTC5778) containing predicted P2-like prophages flanked by rrn operons. When examined by PCR, circularization between the rrn operons was seen for both strains (Fig. 4C and D). In contrast to S. aureus, treatment with mitomycin was not required for circularization to occur (Fig. 4C and D), which correlates with the fact that P2-like phages are not inducible by mitomycin and excise spontaneously [54]. As with S. aureus, the COG classification of the genome between the rrn operons indicated elevated percentages of genes classified as mobilome compared to the rest of the genome (9.6%–1.8% for serotype Brænderup and 11.0%–0.8% for serotype give, Fig. 4E, see Supplementary Table S13 for full COG comparisons). These data suggest that prophage-stimulated rrn-linked recombination is a conserved mechanism across bacterial genera. Further, the identification of a 194 kb circular element annotated as a plasmid in NCBI, yet structurally consistent with an rrn-recombined DNA circle containing a prophage, highlights the potential for phage-mediated mobilization of such elements.

Discussion

The dogma for the genetic redundancy of rrn operons has been that they convey fitness benefits and can assist with varying levels of protein production in the cell [13–17]. Here, we show that, in S. aureus and Salmonella, multiple rrn operons of high sequence identity enables bacterial genome plasticity, by facilitating both inter- and intra-genomic homologous recombination. Furthermore, this recombination facilitates a new form of bacteriophage mediated transduction in S. aureus, which we have named rrn-linked lateral transduction. In rrn-linked lateral transduction, induction of a prophage stimulates RecA-mediated homologous recombination at the flanking rrn operons producing large extrachromosomal circles of DNA. Subsequently and reflecting traditional lateral transduction [4], bidirectional replication initiated from the prophage in situ leads to replication of the entire extrachromosomal circle of DNA. Packaging into phage capsids produced by the ongoing parallel phage lytic cycle then begins from the pac site present in the prophages residing in the extrachromosomal circles and continues in a non-sequence specific manner, as previously shown for lateral transduction [4], thus packaging the bacterial DNA of the rest of the circle.

Recombination between rrn operons has been described before. In Salmonella [51] DNA circles formed by rrn recombination have been isolated and visualized [55] and in E. coli recombination between rrn operons removed an antibiotic resistance cassette inserted into the 16S rRNA gene [56]. In Streptomyces the rrn operons act as borders for chromosomal compartments [57], while in Bacillus rrn operons have been linked to domain boundaries within which horizontal gene transfer is more common [58]. Spatially, the rrn operons appear to co-locate in E. coli likely increasing the potential for recombination [59]. Furthermore, recombination may be stimulated by rrn transcription that blocks DNA replication resulting in DNA breakage [14]. Thus, our finding that prophage induction stimulates recombination of flanking rrn operons may be explained by collision between replication forks initiated at the induced prophage and transcription of the ribosomal genes leading to DNA breakage and stimulation of recombination.

Transduction mediated by rrn operons requires the RecA recombinase and homologous rrn operons that, within around 200 kb, flank a transducing prophage, which packages its DNA via a headful packaging mechanism. We predict that many bacteria fulfil these criteria. Indeed, in silico we identified the required genomic organization in numerous Salmonella genomes and in vitro we observed circularization of Salmonella rrn operons flanking P2-like prophages. Thus, we suspect that rrn transduction is a common phenomenon. In addition, we found significantly higher numbers of predicted recombinant sites in rrn transduced regions, compared to regions transduced by generalized transduction, as well as greater proportions of mobilome associated genes in rrn transduced regions for both S. aureus and Salmonella compared to elsewhere. This indicates that rrn operon recombination and subsequent rrn transduction has increased the mobility and horizontal transfer of the regions where homologous rrn operons and prophages co-locate. Adding to this perspective is the fact that a rrn operon copy will be located on the DNA circle formed by rrn operon recombination and packaged into transducing particles. Given the conservation of rrn operons between strains and species, such transducing particles are likely capable of recombination beyond closely related strains which adds to the potential of rrn mediated transduction to disseminate bacterial DNA.

Here, we link the co-occurrence of rrn operons and prophages in bacterial genomes to extensive recombination and mobility, indicating their co-localization can facilitate bacterial inter- and intra-genomic exchange. Further, we show that rrn operon recombination could be a common phenomenon in bacteria encoding multiple homologous rrn regions and that this can facilitate plasticity and mobility of these regions by phage transduction. This rrn-linked lateral transduction expands the proportion of the S. aureus genome that can be considered mobilizable, encompassing a region not covered by previously recognized transduction mechanisms and it adds yet another perspective to the role of multiple highly homologous rrn operons within bacterial cells.
