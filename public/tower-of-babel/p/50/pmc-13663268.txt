Densitometric gains do not uniformly translate into clinical and psychosocial recovery: a 12-month multidimensional analysis of romosozumab therapy in postmenopausal osteoporosis
Authors: Ömer Faruk Bucak, Cigdem Cinar, Eser Kalaoglu, Umit Yalcin, İbrahim Halil Erdem, Evrim Coskun, Yusuf Celik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough romosozumab produces substantial increases in bone mineral density (BMD), these densitometric gains may not necessarily translate into parallel clinical and psychosocial recovery. This study aimed to determine whether improvements in BMD reflect meaningful changes in pain, quality of life, kinesiophobia, and psychological status during romosozumab therapy, and to define the multidimensional structure of treatment response. Densitometric, clinical, and psychological responses were hypothesized to follow partially independent and region-specific trajectories.MethodsThis single-center cohort study included 46 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis treated with romosozumab and followed for 12 months. BMD was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total femur at baseline, 6, and 12 months. Pain (VAS), kinesiophobia (TAMPA), anxiety and depression (HADS), and quality of life (QUALEFFO-41) were evaluated at the same time points. Longitudinal changes were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA, correlation analyses, and exploratory hierarchical cluster analysis.ResultsBMD increased significantly at all skeletal sites over 12 months (all p < 0.001), with the greatest gains in the lumbar spine (~ 19%), followed by the femoral neck (~ 9%) and total femur (~ 6%). All clinical and psychosocial outcomes improved significantly (all p < 0.001; partial η² = 0.625–0.934). Changes in lumbar spine BMD showed statistically significant but modest associations with quality of life and depressive symptoms (r = − 0.304 to − 0.422, p < 0.05–0.01). Similarly, changes in femoral BMD parameters were modestly associated with pain (r = − 0.341 to − 0.481, p < 0.05–0.01) and kinesiophobia (r = − 0.308 to − 0.342, p < 0.05). Exploratory hierarchical cluster analysis identified distinct clusters separating patient-reported outcomes from densitometric parameters, with prior osteoporosis treatment history emerging as a bridging variable between these domains.ConclusionDensitometric gains during romosozumab therapy do not uniformly translate into patient-perceived recovery. Treatment response is multidimensional and region-specific, indicating that BMD alone is insufficient to capture therapeutic effectiveness. The region-specific pattern of these modest associations, femoral BMD changes with pain and kinesiophobia, and lumbar BMD changes with quality of life and depressive symptoms, supports the need for anatomically informed, patient-centered evaluation frameworks in osteoporosis management.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Romosozumab, Bone mineral density, Kinesiophobia, Pain, Anxiety, Depression, Quality of life

Introduction

Modern osteoporosis management has largely evolved within a densitometric paradigm in which bone mineral density (BMD) serves as the central determinant for diagnosis, therapeutic decision-making, and evaluation of treatment response [1–3]. Among available pharmacological agents, romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin, occupies a unique position by simultaneously stimulating bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption, thereby inducing rapid spine-dominant BMD gains and substantial reductions in fracture risk [4–6].

However, an increasingly recognized discrepancy may be observed in clinical practice: despite substantial densitometric gains, some patients may not perceive themselves as clinically improved [4, 7, 8]. Pain complaints may persist, daily activities may remain restricted, and confidence in movement may not be fully restored [7–9]. This observation suggests that defining treatment success solely on densitometric grounds may be insufficient, and that a clinically meaningful dissociation may exist between objective skeletal improvement and patients’ perceived well-being.

One plausible explanation for this discordance is that pain and functional limitation in osteoporosis cannot be attributed solely to structural damage or fracture healing [10]. Psychological and behavioral factors, particularly fear of movement (kinesiophobia), play a central role in shaping pain perception, sustaining activity avoidance, and contributing to functional decline [11, 12]. Kinesiophobia is increasingly recognized as a key mediator linking physical pathology to disability across chronic musculoskeletal conditions [12, 13], yet its relevance within osteoporosis treatment response models remains insufficiently characterized. Furthermore, fracture history, fear of falling, and concerns about re-injury may independently limit physical activity and contribute to long-term deterioration in health-related quality of life [10, 14, 15].

Collectively, these observations suggest that treatment response in osteoporosis operates across at least two partially dissociated dimensions: a biological layer reflecting objective densitometric change, and a clinical-psychosocial layer encompassing pain perception, fear of movement, emotional burden, and patient-reported functional status. When assessment is restricted to densitometric endpoints, treatment success may be systematically overestimated [16]. Despite the widespread use of anabolic therapies, data systematically evaluating the alignment between BMD gains and patient-centered outcomes remain scarce, particularly for romosozumab in real-world clinical settings [4–6].

In this context, the present study aimed to determine whether densitometric gains achieved with romosozumab reflect concurrent clinical and psychosocial recovery, and to define the multidimensional structure of treatment response in postmenopausal osteoporosis. To this end, validated patient-reported outcome measures, including pain intensity, kinesiophobia, psychological status, and disease-specific quality of life, were integrated alongside densitometric assessments within a single analytical framework incorporating exploratory hierarchical cluster analysis. To our knowledge, these domains have not been systematically evaluated together in patients undergoing anabolic osteoporosis therapy, and the present study represents the first attempt to characterize the region-specific and multidimensional nature of this response.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center retrospective observational cohort study based on collected routine clinical follow-up data. The study was conducted at the osteoporosis outpatient clinic of the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at a tertiary university hospital. Postmenopausal women diagnosed with osteoporosis who initiated romosozumab therapy between January 2023 and July 2025 were screened for eligibility. A total of 46 patients who completed a full 12-month follow-up and had available densitometric and multidimensional clinical outcome data were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). All patients were routinely monitored according to a standardized clinical follow-up protocol, including predefined assessments at baseline and scheduled follow-up visits at 6 and 12 months. Outcome measures were collected prospectively as part of routine clinical care, allowing for the evaluation of within-subject changes over time. Patients with incomplete follow-up or missing key outcome data were excluded to ensure data integrity and consistency of longitudinal comparisons. Romosozumab was selected as the sole study agent to ensure pharmacological homogeneity and to provide a standardized anabolic framework for evaluating multidimensional treatment response [4, 5]. Following ethics committee approval (Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital, Approval No: 2025 − 272), these longitudinally collected data were retrospectively retrieved and analyzed. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, as defined by World Health Organization criteria [2], who were evaluated using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and had a T-score ≤ − 2.5 in at least one of the following regions: lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, or total hip. Inclusion criteria required completion of at least 12 months of continuous romosozumab therapy and availability of complete densitometric and clinical outcome data at all predefined time points (baseline, 6, and 12 months). Exclusion criteria included secondary osteoporosis (metabolic bone disease, malignancy-related bone loss, or chronic systemic glucocorticoid use ≥ 5 mg/day for ≥ 3 months), inflammatory rheumatic diseases, severe degenerative joint changes, structural deformities, neurological or orthopedic conditions substantially limiting ambulatory capacity, and missing outcome data at any time point.

Clinical outcome measures

Pain intensity was evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0–10 cm), where higher scores indicate greater perceived pain [17]. The fear of movement was assessed using the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK; range, 17–68), a validated instrument widely used to quantify movement-related fear in musculoskeletal populations [18]. Psychological status was measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), consisting of anxiety and depression subscales (0–21 points each), with higher scores reflecting greater psychological distress [19]. Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Quality of Life Questionnaire of the European Foundation for Osteoporosis (QUALEFFO-41), which captures physical function, pain, and psychosocial dimensions specific to osteoporosis [20]. All measures were administered at baseline, 6, and 12 months by the same assessor under standardized clinical conditions. Validated Turkish versions of TSK, HADS, and QUALEFFO-41 were used to ensure cultural and linguistic reliability [21–23].

Densitometric assessment

Areal bone mineral density (aBMD; g/cm²) measurements were performed using DXA at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months follow-up. All scans were acquired using the same densitometry system and were analyzed according to the recommendations of the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) [24]. Measurement sites included the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, and total hip regions. Daily phantom calibration was performed in accordance with manufacturer guidelines, and all scans were conducted by the same certified technician to minimize inter-operator variability. T-scores were derived using manufacturer-provided reference databases and were used for diagnostic classification, whereas absolute BMD values were used for longitudinal evaluation of treatment response. Changes in BMD were calculated as both absolute differences and percentage change from baseline to capture clinically meaningful skeletal improvement.

Laboratory assessment

Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and calcium levels were measured at baseline, and 12 months as part of routine clinical monitoring and were included in the hierarchical cluster analysis to characterize the broader metabolic context of treatment response.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Continuous variables were expressed as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, while non-parametric variables (HADS and VAS scores) were presented as Median and Interquartile Range (IQR). To evaluate the changes in clinical, and densitometric parameters over time (Baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), a one-way repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted. The assumption of sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test. In cases where the sphericity assumption was violated (p < 0.05), degrees of freedom were corrected using the Greenhouse–Geisser estimate. For variables where the ANOVA indicated a significant main effect of time, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to identify specific differences between time points. The magnitude of the changes (effect size) was reported using partial eta-squared (partial η2), categorized as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between changes in densitometric and clinical–psychosocial variables across the 0–6 and 0–12 month intervals. Scatter plots with fitted linear trend lines were generated for the main statistically significant correlations to visually assess the distribution of individual data points, the direction of associations, and the potential influence of outliers. HCA was conducted to explore multidimensional treatment response patterns. Variables representing densitometric, clinical, and psychosocial changes were included in the clustering model. The agglomerative clustering method with Ward’s linkage was applied, and cluster structure was evaluated using dendrogram inspection [25]; findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

The study cohort had a mean age of 74.4 ± 6.5 years and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 25.0 ± 2.3 kg/m². Regarding prior osteoporosis treatment history, 25 patients (54.3%) had received no previous pharmacological treatment, while 16 patients (34.8%) had been treated with bisphosphonates, 3 patients (6.5%) with denosumab, and 2 patients (4.3%) with sequential bisphosphonate and denosumab therapy. The median number of previous fractures was 2 (range: 2–4), and 58.7% had a positive family history of osteoporotic fractures. On-treatment fractures were observed in 2 patients (4.3%) (Table 1).

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant temporal changes across all clinical, psychosocial, and densitometric parameters over the 12-month follow-up period (Table 2; Fig. 2). HADS-A and HADS-D scores decreased significantly across all time points (p < 0.001), with large effect sizes (partial η² = 0.642 and 0.625, respectively). QUALEFFO-41 scores declined markedly from 69.2 ± 8.3 at baseline to 53.8 ± 7.9 at 12 months (F = 313.87, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.934). Pain intensity (VAS; F = 181.41, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.801) and kinesiophobia (TAMPA; F = 221.10, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.831) also demonstrated significant and progressive reductions throughout the study period, with all pairwise comparisons reaching statistical significance (p < 0.001). Lumbar spine BMD increased significantly from 0.58 ± 0.07 g/cm² at baseline to 0.69 ± 0.07 g/cm² at 12 months (F = 531.75, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.922), representing an approximately 19% gain. L1–4 T-scores improved correspondingly from − 4.18 ± 0.65 to − 3.25 ± 0.54 (F = 508.78, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.919).

In the femoral region, femoral neck BMD (F = 36.38, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.447) and T-scores (F = 35.33, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.440) increased significantly across all time intervals, representing approximately 9% BMD gain. Total femur T-scores demonstrated a significant time effect (F = 146.51, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.765), and total femur BMD also increased significantly over the study period (F = 118.67, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.725), with all pairwise comparisons reaching significance, including the 6–12 month interval (p = 0.003), reflecting a progressive but decelerating femoral response pattern.

Correlation analyses identified statistically significant, region-specific, but generally modest associations between changes in BMD parameters and selected clinical–psychosocial outcomes (Table 3). In the lumbar region, changes in L1–4 BMD were modestly associated with changes in QUALEFFO-41 and HADS-D scores across both the 0–6 and 0–12 month intervals (r = − 0.401 to − 0.422, p < 0.01), while L1–4 T-score changes showed modest associations with QUALEFFO-41 across multiple intervals (r = − 0.318 to − 0.371, p < 0.05). In the femoral region, total femur BMD changes were modestly associated with VAS scores across the 0–6 and 0–12 month intervals (r = − 0.341 to − 0.481, p < 0.05–0.01). Femoral neck T-score changes showed modest associations with TSK scores at the 6–12 and 0–12 month intervals (r = − 0.308 to − 0.342, p < 0.05). Notably, no significant associations were identified between any BMD parameter and HADS-A scores, suggesting that anxiety improvement may follow a trajectory relatively independent of skeletal response.

Exploratory hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) identified three main clusters (Fig. 4). Main Cluster I (MC-I) comprised two subclusters: SC-I included all patient-reported outcome changes (ΔTAMPA, ΔHADS-A, ΔHADS-D, ΔVAS, and ΔQUALEFFO-41), while SC-II comprised demographic variables (age and BMI). Main Cluster II (MC-II) consisted solely of prior osteoporosis treatment history, occupying an intermediate position between the patient-reported and densitometric domains. Main Cluster III (MC-III) comprised two subclusters: SC-III included femoral densitometric changes (ΔFemoral Neck BMD, ΔFemoral Neck T-score, ΔTotal Femur BMD, ΔTotal Femur T-score) together with Δ25-hydroxyvitamin D, while SC-IV comprised lumbar densitometric changes (ΔL1–4 BMD, ΔL1–4 T-score) and ΔSerum calcium. The consistent separation between the patient-reported outcome cluster (SC-I) and the densitometric clusters (SC-III and SC-IV) supports the multidimensional and partially dissociated nature of treatment response, while the intermediate positioning of prior treatment history suggests its potential role as a modulating variable bridging biological and psychosocial recovery domains.

Discussion

Romosozumab therapy over 12 months produced significant gains in BMD at all skeletal sites alongside substantial improvements in pain, kinesiophobia, depression, anxiety, and osteoporosis-specific quality of life. These findings are broadly consistent with existing evidence on the biological efficacy of romosozumab [4–6]. What the present study adds is not confirmation of these gains but an examination of their internal structure: to what extent do densitometric and clinical–psychosocial improvements travel together, and where do they diverge? The present findings suggest that densitometric and clinical–psychosocial improvements may follow partially independent and anatomically differentiated trajectories, which may have implications for how therapeutic success is defined and monitored in routine osteoporosis care.

The approximately 19% lumbar BMD gain observed in the present cohort reflects the upper range of published real-world benchmarks and is consistent with the relatively high proportion of treatment-naive patients (54.3%) in our sample. Treatment-naive patients consistently achieve more pronounced densitometric gains than those with prior antiresorptive exposure, and longer duration of prior antiresorptive use is inversely correlated with lumbar spine BMD response to romosozumab [26, 27]. When romosozumab is used as initial therapy, BMD gains at both hip and spine are substantially larger compared with the reverse sequence, with women pretreated with alendronate achieving approximately 50% to 75% of the BMD increments observed in treatment-naive women [28].

The mechanistic basis involves suppression of the bone remodeling space during prolonged antiresorptive therapy, reducing available remodeling sites for Wnt/β-catenin activation [28, 29]. Additionally, rising serum Dickkopf-1 levels, a compensatory Wnt pathway inhibitör, are temporally associated with declining bone formation markers despite continued romosozumab treatment [30], which may partly explain the decelerating total femur BMD response observed in the 6–12 month interval of the present study (p = 0.003). These observations reinforce the importance of early anabolic sequencing before prolonged antiresorptive exposure in patients at high fracture risk [11, 26–28].

The region-specific correlation patterns identified here are, to our knowledge, previously unreported. Lumbar BMD changes showed statistically significant but modest associations with quality of life (QUALEFFO-41: r = − 0.371 to − 0.408, p < 0.01) and depressive symptoms (HADS-D: r = − 0.304 to − 0.422, p < 0.05–0.01). In exploratory adjusted multivariable analyses including age, BMI, prior osteoporosis treatment, and baseline depressive symptoms, lumbar spine BMD showed a non-significant trend toward an association with osteoporosis-specific quality of life. Although exploratory, this finding suggests that the observed relationship may not be fully explained by basic demographic and psychosocial factors. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the analysis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The QUALEFFO-41 captures physical function, pain, and psychosocial dimensions simultaneously [20, 31], and its parallel improvement with lumbar structural gains suggests that spinal recovery extends beyond local biomechanics to influence patients’ broader functional confidence and psychological well-being. Vertebral fracture sequelae, height loss, progressive kyphosis, and chronic axial pain, are among the strongest determinants of disability and depressive burden in postmenopausal women [10, 14, 32], and structural restoration at the lumbar spine may reshape self-perception and mood through pathways not reducible to pain relief alone.

The femoral region showed a distinct pattern. Femoral BMD changes were modestly associated with pain intensity (VAS: r = − 0.341 to − 0.481, p < 0.05–0.01) and kinesiophobia (femoral neck T-score: TSK r = − 0.308 to − 0.342, p < 0.05), whereas no comparable associations were observed with anxiety, depression, or quality-of-life parameters. In postmenopausal osteoporosis, kinesiophobia may arise partly from perceived skeletal fragility and fracture vulnerability, potentially contributing to reduced physical activity, further functional decline, and increased fracture risk [33, 34]. The hip and proximal femur are the biomechanical foundation of ambulation and load transfer, and perceived vulnerability at weight-bearing sites perpetuates movement-related fear through fear-avoidance mechanisms [11, 12]. The association between femoral BMD gains and reduced kinesiophobia in our cohort suggests that structural changes at load-bearing skeletal sites may be related to improvements in movement confidence; however, this potential pathway remains speculative and cannot be interpreted causally.

The relationship between densitometric response and patient-reported recovery has been investigated with other osteoporosis therapies, although the available evidence remains heterogeneous and rarely site-specific. In the HORIZON Pivotal Fracture Trial, once-yearly zoledronic acid reduced patient-reported days with back pain, activity limitation, and bed rest, indicating that bisphosphonate therapy may influence clinically relevant patient-centered outcomes in addition to skeletal and fracture-related endpoints [35]. More direct evidence is available for denosumab, for which prospective data showed concurrent BMD increases and improvements in health-related quality of life, with BMD gains correlating with quality-of-life changes [36]. By contrast, in an active-controlled trial comparing teriparatide with risedronate, greater BMD gains and fewer vertebral fractures with teriparatide were not accompanied by superior improvements in back pain, disability, or quality of life [37]. Taken together, these findings suggest that BMD gain, fracture reduction, pain, function, and quality-of-life outcomes are related but not interchangeable dimensions of treatment response. Direct evidence linking regional BMD changes to domain-specific patient-reported recovery remains limited. In this context, the present study provides exploratory evidence that the relationship between densitometric improvement and patient-perceived recovery may vary across clinical domains and skeletal sites. This distinction may be clinically relevant because pain, movement-related fear, mood, and quality of life are influenced not only by skeletal strength but also by vertebral fracture burden, degenerative musculoskeletal disease, physical function, fear-avoidance behavior, and psychological adaptation. Accordingly, BMD gain should be interpreted as an important but incomplete marker of treatment response, and multidimensional patient-reported outcome assessment, including osteoporosis-specific quality of life, may provide complementary information during osteoporosis follow-up.

Despite showing the largest effect size among psychosocial parameters (partial η² = 0.642 vs. 0.625 for HADS-D), HADS-A showed no significant correlation with any BMD parameter across all time intervals. The modestly greater anxiety improvement relative to depression may reflect the particular salience of anticipatory fracture fear in this population, which responds readily to the reassurance of active therapeutic engagement and structured monitoring [38]. However, its decoupling from densitometric change indicates that anxiety in osteoporosis is driven by factors operating beyond skeletal status, anticipatory fracture fear, altered body image, and persistent preoccupation with fragility, that are relatively resistant to structural intervention alone [33, 38]. These findings suggest that anxiety management in this population may require psychosocial strategies in parallel with pharmacological bone-directed therapy [14].

The exploratory hierarchical cluster analysis was broadly consistent with these observations. Patient-reported outcome changes clustered as a coherent psychosocial domain clearly separated from the densitometric clusters. Within the densitometric domain, femoral changes and Δ25-hydroxyvitamin D formed one subcluster, while lumbar changes and Δserum calcium formed another. Although broadly consistent with the correlation findings, these clustering patterns should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The clustering of Δ25-hydroxyvitamin D within the femoral densitometric subcluster may reflect its closer relationship with skeletal mineralization and calcium homeostasis than with direct psychosocial recovery [39].

Prior osteoporosis treatment history occupied an intermediate position between the psychosocial and densitometric clusters, bridging rather than aligning with either domain. This pattern is consistent with its dual modulatory role: from a biological standpoint, prior antiresorptive exposure modulates Wnt signaling responsiveness and the available remodeling space [6, 29]; from a psychosocial standpoint, the biomechanical stabilization achieved during prior therapy may have partially pre-conditioned patients’ movement confidence and pain appraisal, reducing fear-avoidance adaptations and facilitating greater psychosocial recovery during the subsequent anabolic phase [11, 12]. Whether this bridging pattern reflects a genuine biological–psychosocial interaction or shared covariance driven by disease chronicity warrants dedicated prospective investigation.

These findings support a more multidimensional approach to monitoring therapeutic response in osteoporosis [16]. A patient may achieve substantial BMD gains while continuing to experience pain restriction or kinesiophobia, indicating that densitometric response alone may not fully capture patient-perceived recovery. The region-specific pattern observed in this study may help explain why densitometric and patient-reported recovery do not always proceed in parallel and supports the integration of validated patient-reported outcome measures alongside DXA in clinical follow-up [40]. This approach may help identify patients with persistent pain, movement-related fear, or psychosocial burden despite adequate BMD response, who may benefit from targeted rehabilitative or psychological interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the single-center design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results to broader osteoporosis populations. Second, the absence of a control group prevents causal attribution of clinical and psychosocial improvements to romosozumab therapy; observed gains may partly reflect regression to the mean, therapeutic attention effects, or the natural trajectory of structured clinical monitoring. Third, although exploratory adjusted analyses were performed where feasible, residual confounding from musculoskeletal comorbidities, baseline clinical status, and other patient-related factors cannot be excluded; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than causal. Finally, the hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted as an exploratory, data-driven approach in a limited sample; the identified response patterns should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and replication in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts is needed.

Conclusion

Romosozumab therapy over 12 months was associated with significant increases in BMD at all skeletal sites, together with improvements in pain, kinesiophobia, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and osteoporosis-specific quality of life. However, the associations between densitometric gains and clinical–psychosocial improvements were generally modest and region-specific, suggesting that BMD response alone may not fully capture patient-perceived recovery. In this cohort, femoral BMD changes were modestly associated with pain and kinesiophobia, whereas lumbar BMD changes were modestly associated with quality of life and depressive symptoms. These exploratory findings support the concept that treatment response in osteoporosis is multidimensional and may require assessment beyond DXA-based skeletal outcomes alone. Integrating validated patient-reported outcome measures alongside densitometric assessment may help identify patients with persistent pain, movement-related fear, or psychosocial burden despite adequate biological response, and may guide targeted rehabilitative or psychosocial interventions. Given the single-center observational design and limited sample size, these findings should be confirmed in larger prospective studies.
