Co-Designing a Consult Patient Decision Aid for Continuation Versus Deprescribing Cholinesterase Inhibitors in People Living with Dementia
Authors: Nagham J Ailabouni, Wade Thompson, Sarah N Hilmer, Lyntara Quirke, Janet McNeece, Alice Bourke, Chloe Furst, Emily Reeve
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAs dementia progresses, people living with dementia may take high-risk, unnecessary, or ineffective medicines. Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) may have benefit in some people with dementia; however, up to one third are continued when no longer necessary or safe. Our aim was to co-design a consult patient decision aid (CPtDA) to support shared decision making between healthcare professionals and consumers about continuing or deprescribing ChEIs.MethodsA systematic process was employed to design and test the CPtDA prototype. First, a steering group composed of healthcare professionals and a consumer representative was assembled. Guided by the International Patient Decision Aids Standards, the steering group defined the CPtDA’s purpose, scope, and target audience and drafted the prototype for further testing. Interviews with consumers and healthcare professionals were conducted to gain feedback on the content, format, structure, comprehensibility and usability of the CPtDA prototype.ResultsAfter the steering group developed the CPtDA prototype, interviews were conducted with 11 consumers and six healthcare professionals. The content and format of the decision aid were improved iteratively over three rounds after consolidating the feedback at each round. The main changes included rewording the purpose of the decision aid and simplifying its layout and format. Participants reported that the decision aid is comprehensible and may be useful in practice.ConclusionsLimited available resources guide shared decision making about deprescribing. This study resulted in a co-designed and alpha-tested CPtDA for people living with dementia and carers to help them review the ongoing need for their ChEIs. Further research is needed to explore using the CPtDA in practice to support people living with dementia and their carers engage in the shared decision-making process about continuing or deprescribing their ChEIs. Our co-designed CPtDA could help people living with dementia and their carers review their goals of care alongside their healthcare professional. This may prompt conversations about appropriately using ChEIs and increase the uptake of deprescribing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-024-01146-5.

Key Points

We co-designed a consult patient decision aid to empower people living with dementia and their carers to engage in a shared decision-making process with their clinician about continuing or deprescribing their acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.The consult patient decision aid was tested and found to be comprehensible and useful for practice.The consult patient decision aid can assist a clinician to plan and implement deprescribing using the deprescribing plan template which includes tailored follow-up and monitoring instructions for the consumer.

Introduction

Worldwide, approximately 50 million people have dementia and more and more people are living with dementia each year [1]. In Australia, it has been estimated that in 2024 more than 421,000 Australians live with dementia and 1.6 million people care for them, signifying the burden dementia is associated with [2]. Dementia describes a syndrome that is characterised by a progressive loss in cognition, and change in function and behaviour [3]. People living with dementia often take many medications to treat multiple chronic conditions (e.g. cardiovascular disease). All medications have potential benefits and risks. Balancing these risks and harms regularly is particularly important for a progressive disease such as dementia because the person’s goals of care can change over time [4]. Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) such as donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine are used to treat the symptoms of dementia [5]. These medications can have benefits to some people living with dementia, predominantly through the improvement of cognitive function [6]. However, the use of ChEIs is associated with potential harms through their mechanism of action and increased cholinergic stimulation centrally and physically (e.g. gastrointestinal adverse effects, headache and dizziness, tremor).

The appropriate use of ChEIs involves both prescribing these medications to individuals who are likely to benefit, and deprescribing (reducing or stopping) them for individuals where the risks outweigh the benefits [6]. Up to one third of ChEI use is potentially inappropriate with the likely harms of using ChEIs outweighing the likely benefits, or their use not aligning with the individual’s current goals of care [7–10]. This implies that deprescribing these medicines is not occurring as often as it could.

An evidence-based deprescribing guideline for ChEIs was developed, which contains recommendations, mainly for healthcare professionals and clinicians, regarding when trialling the deprescribing of these medicines may be suitable [6]. The guidelines’ main recommendations include considering deprescribing in people who may be taking ChEIs for an indication that is inappropriate or unclear, and how to taper the medicine whilst monitoring the person during the deprescribing process. Specifically, a trial discontinuation is recommended in people taking a ChEI for an indication other than Alzheimer’s disease, dementia of Parkinson’s disease, Lewy body dementia or vascular dementia. Additionally, a trial discontinuation could be suitable during a person’s later stages of dementia as ChEIs have shown limited benefit for these people, as well as for people with adverse drug effects or non-adherence that cannot be resolved.

The deprescribing guideline notes that the decision to deprescribe these medications should be made during a shared decision-making process involving the healthcare professional, the person living with dementia and their carer, where appropriate. However, limited resources and tools have been co-designed to empower people living with dementia and their carers to genuinely engage in this shared decision-making process.

Given the importance and complexity of decision making around deprescribing ChEIs, a specific resource to facilitate such shared decisions may be useful. Patient decision aids are tools designed to inform people about the potential benefits and harms of different decision options whilst enabling them to clarify their values and goals of care to help inform their decision. Patient decision aids are useful for preference-sensitive decisions [11]. Consult patient decision aids (CPtDAs) are a type of decision aid meant to be used jointly by people with their healthcare professional (e.g. doctor, pharmacist) during a clinical interaction.

Given the current lack of resources to support people living with dementia and their carers deciding about the continued need of their ChEIs, we aimed to develop a CPtDA for this purpose. We also aimed to determine its usability and comprehensibility with people living with dementia, their carers and healthcare professionals who care for them. This CPtDA, complementary to the evidence-based deprescribing guideline, can be used by healthcare professionals and consumers during a clinical interaction to review the continued need for ChEIs to jointly decide whether to continue or deprescribe them.

Methods

Our methods were informed by Coulter et al.’s systematic process for the development of patient decision aids [12]. The process of development and alpha testing the comprehensibility and usability of the CPtDA is outlined in Fig. 1.

Defining the Purpose, Scope and Target Audience

When developing the evidence-based guideline for deprescribing ChEIs and memantine [6] in 2019, it was recognised by the guideline development team that there is an unmet need for a complementary decision aid. The purpose, scope and target audience of the CPtDA was informed by the scope of the guideline. However, for clarity, the research steering group (SG) decided that the CPtDA should focus solely on ChEIs, with the potential to develop a separate CPtDA for memantine in the future.

Purpose: to enable shared decision-making and support people living with dementia and/or their carers to make informed decisions based on their values and preferences.

Assembling the Research SG

To develop the CPtDA, we assembled a research SG consisting of three pharmacists, a carer of a person living with dementia, a trainee geriatrician and two geriatricians, one of whom is also a clinical pharmacologist. The role of the SG was to overlook and review the feedback and input gained from participants to ensure that all views and voices were considered.

Reviewing and Synthesising the Evidence and Determining the Format

To develop the evidence-based clinical practice guideline for deprescribing ChEIs and memantine, a systematic review was undertaken to determine the potential harms and benefits of deprescribing ChEIs. GRADE methods were then followed to convert evidence to recommendations, which consider various factors including consumer values and preferences. Additionally, the components of the CPtDA were informed by the International Patient Decision Aids Standards [11] and Item 11 of the Standards for UNiversal reporting of patient Decision Aid Evaluations (SUNDAE) guidelines [13].

In addition to the CPtDA, we also created a 2-page deprescribing plan template with sections on tapering and monitoring that could be used if a decision was made to trial discontinuation. Knowledge of ‘how’ to deprescribe is highly valued by consumers and planning for tapering and monitoring is an essential part of the deprescribing process [14, 15]. In this article, we include the deprescribing plan template when referring to the CPtDA.

Throughout the drafting process, the readability of the content was checked according to the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level Score and the Gunning Fog Index, aiming for a grade level of 8 so that it is readable for the general public [16]. We used these tools to ensure our phrasing and content were more readable. The first draft of the CPtDA (pre-alpha tesing) is available as Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

Conducting Interviews to Alpha Test the CPtDA

We included those who are aged 18 years and over who have a lived experience with dementia. This included people who are living with dementia of any type, memory problems or mild cognitive impairment. We also included carers of people living with dementia and healthcare professionals who have experience caring for people living with dementia including general practitioners, geriatricians, medical trainees, neurologists, clinical pharmacologists, pharmacists, registered nurses or nurse practitioners. We excluded individuals who were unable to communicate in or read English.

We obtained written informed consent from people living with dementia and their carers. We obtained implied consent from healthcare professionals (i.e. their consent was determined via them agreeing to participate in the interview). We identified and recruited potential participants via personal contacts, networks and advertising through StepUp for Dementia, a registry of consumers who are interested in participating in dementia-related research. We also identified participants via professional organisations, via geriatricians at the Royal Adelaide Hospital’s geriatric outpatient department, personal contacts and snowballing, where participants are asked to suggest other possible participants. We applied a purposive sampling approach to include the perspectives of a diverse group of people who may be the potential end users of our CPtDA in clinical practice. We aimed to recruit people from various disciplinary healthcare backgrounds and consumers living in different regions (e.g. states) and with varying experience with these medications.

The target sample size was five to ten participants per group to gain diverse perspectives of end users, guided by sample sizes of similar previous studies [17, 18]. Recruitment continued until the SG felt that no further changes to the CPtDA were required. We were also guided by the sample size of previously conducted similar research and aimed to gain diverse perspectives from end users [17, 18].

We invited participants to an interview that took approximately 30–60 minutes. Participants were only interviewed once. A copy of the CPtDA was provided to participants for them to read 1 week prior to the interview. The CPtDA was sent electronically or via post depending on the preference of the participant. Healthcare professional participants had the option of providing feedback via e-mail (rather than interview); however, no participants took this option.

Interviews were conducted online with videoconferencing software (Zoom®), or via the telephone and were conducted one-on-one with the participant or with person-carer dyads. We developed the interview guide from previous studies related to user testing of decision aids (Table 1) [19, 20]. This guide consisted of questions grouped in three main sections:

CPtDA consultant patient decision aid

A researcher, who is a pharmacist with experience working with older people and people living with dementia (NJA), conducted the interviews using the interview guide. During the interview, the researcher directly annotated the CPtDA to note the participants’ suggestions to improve the decision aid’s wording, content and formatting. Issues highlighted by participants could be clarified and suggestions of wording from participants obtained. For example, if there was a sentence that was unclear, the researcher explained the intended message of the sentence and asked the participants how it could be phrased better. The interviews lasted for 15–30 minutes and were not audio-recorded. Participants were provided with a $30 AUD gift card as an honorarium. Basic participant characteristic data were collected to describe the population (Table 2).

Information gained during the interviews was used to make changes to the CPtDA iteratively. After every five to six interviews with roughly the same number of consumers and healthcare professionals, two members of the research SG (ER and NA) reviewed the comments and made changes to the CPtDA. In totality, three rounds of interviews were conducted with revision of the CPtDA draft occurring after the 5th, 11th and 17th (final) interview. All changes were shared with the whole SG for comment. After completion of all interviews, the research SG reviewed and revised the decision aid again, considering all the feedback and approved the final version (ESM).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Eleven consumers (ten carers and one person living with dementia) and six healthcare professionals participated in 17 interviews (Table 2) to inform the development of the final version of the CPtDA (ESM). The mean age of consumer and healthcare professional participants was 63.5 years and 42.6 years, respectively. All consumer participants (or their care recipient) had experience taking a medicine for dementia and all healthcare professionals had previous experience deprescribing a ChEI through a shared decision-making process.

Changes Made to the CPtDA

Overall, participant feedback resulted in simplification of the CPtDA. A summary of the feedback and changes made are outlined in Table 3. Most participants agreed with the content and format of the CPtDA; therefore, no major changes were made to content or overall format. However, in general, they communicated they would like more white space, simpler language throughout and relevant examples to help them understand the purpose of how they could apply the information in real-life practice.

CPtDA consultant patient decision aid

We revised the entirety of the CPtDA to improve its flow including the clarity of the wording. Most participants agreed that the CPtDA demonstrated a balance between the option of continuing the medicine and the option of trialling discontinuation. However, some participants commented on certain sections being posed in such a way to encourage the decision of ‘deprescribing’ rather than ‘continuing’ the ChEI. These sections were revised to improve the balance. Regarding overall presentation, there were mixed preferences for the use of the icons, with some participants strongly liking and others disliking them. Most of the consumers we interviewed liked the icons and so some were kept while others were removed to reduce the overall ‘clutter’ and create more white space. Additionally, the outcomes were reordered to better reflect person-important outcomes being presented first (Sect. 4; ESM).

Few changes were made to the deprescribing plan template, except, similar to the CPtDA, to reduce clutter and provide more space to take notes.

Summary of Participants’ Views on the CPtDA’s Usability and Implementation

Participants conveyed that the CPtDA is valuable and addresses a current unmet need to help them and/or people they care for to make such a decision. Participants reported that simplifying the CPtDA in terms of content, layout and language improves its usability in practice. Participants viewed the CPtDA to be a useful tool to guide the conversation between them and the clinician about continuing or deprescribing their ChEI. Carers specifically reported that they could use the CPtDA to review periodically the continued need of these medicines for the person they care for in the context of their goals of care. Healthcare professional participants reported that they could use the CPtDA as a way to better understand the person’s goals of care in the context of their dementia progressing.

Discussion

We co-designed a CPtDA with people living with dementia, carers and healthcare professionals who care for them. Consumer participants conveyed that this tool would be useful to guide conversations with healthcare professionals to jointly decide whether to continue the ChEI or trial a discontinuation. Importantly, this tool was appreciated as participants reported that there are limited resources available to assist them reviewing the continued need of their medicines alongside their healthcare professional.

The time of diagnosing dementia and beyond is often a challenging time for people living with dementia and their carers as they navigate making various complex, interrelated healthcare and medicine-related decisions [4]. Evidence suggests that limited resources to review medicines and make medicine-related decisions have been co-designed alongside consumers and tailored for their needs [21]. By co-designing our CPtDA with people living with dementia, carers and healthcare professionals who care for them, we aimed to improve the quality of the shared decision-making process about deprescribing ChEIs to address this pertinent gap.

Decision aids are more than handouts [22, 23]. Whilst printed material in medical centres provides some information, consumers often find these to be inadequate by being either too simple or too complex [21, 24, 25]. Additionally, such information may exclude the discussion of treatment choices they are most interested in. Decision aids, such as our co-designed CPtDA, are thought to facilitate better treatment decisions for patients by creating an opportunity for consumers to ask questions and have conversations about the appropriate use of medicines. A review of over 100 randomised controlled trials has found that using patient decision aids led to increased patient participation in decision making without increasing anxiety, improved the overall decision quality and lowered decisional conflict [26]. A patient decision on deprescribing proton pump inhibitors has also been shown to increase decisional confidence and knowledge. Evidence also shows that patient decision aids can assist in realigning the knowledge of patterns of use and patient values to change either overuse or underuse of certain healthcare treatments or procedures. Additionally, there may be some clinical benefits such as lowering anxiety and a reduction in weight loss in people living with dementia; however, evidence on this is limited.

In particular, decision aids used by people living with dementia and their carers have demonstrated an improvement in a variety of outcomes, including a reduction in decisional conflict, improved communication and an increased concordance rate of preferences of care [22]. In a systematic review of decision aids developed for people living with dementia and their carers, an emphasis on an interactive component of the decision aid was deemed important [22]. Hence, throughout our decision aid, we incorporated several opportunities for the person living with dementia and their carer to explore and evaluate the goals of care that are most important to them, take notes, and complete a checklist of the key items related to the conversation they had with their healthcare professional about continuing or deprescribing their ChEI.

Thus far, the use of patient information in deprescribing interventional studies has proven to be a successful deprescribing implementation strategy. For example, in the D-PRESCRIBE trial, pharmacists used the EMPOWER brochure, which contained information about whether a medicine may be inappropriate, potential alternative treatments and a visual tapering protocol, to discuss deprescribing recommendations with patients and prescribers. This led to discontinuation rates as high as 43% for some high-risk medicines such as sedative-hypnotic medicines [27]. Educational resources may also be successful in reducing inappropriate medicine use in people living with dementia. In the OPTIMIZE trial [28], an educational brochure and questionnaire on attitudes towards deprescribing was sent to people living with dementia prior to an upcoming medical visit. This coupled with provider education led to a reduction in inappropriate medicine use, but only in those taking seven or more regular medicines. However, the widespread implementation of person-centred deprescribing resources remains limited. This is because of various identified barriers such as the limited ability to easily integrate such resources within existing workflows and software [29], time limitations and inertia in medical culture.

Limitations and Future Directions

Interviews were conducted via Zoom® and not face-to-face because of the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. This may have limited our ability to comprehensively explore the implementation and usability of the CPtDA in practice owing to the limited exploration of body language. It also limited the participation of people with reduced function (e.g. difficulty using technology). Additionally, we had a limited number of participants, meaning that our findings may not be representative. We did not collect any data on the type of stage of dementia of the consumers or from their carers. Consumer participants were predominantly carers. It was challenging to recruit and gain consent from people living with dementia, especially as the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic meant we were unable to recruit in person from health clinics. Lack of inclusion of people living with dementia in research is a chronic problem, and more research and support are needed to improve this. Further research is needed to explore the real-world implementation and impact of the CPtDA, in a setting where the recruitment of people living with dementia can be supported, such as memory clinics.

Conclusions

Use of potentially inappropriate medicines is more prevalent in people living with dementia in comparison to people who do not have a dementia diagnosis [30, 31]. Consumers and clinicians have highlighted their need for additional support and guidance in navigating deprescribing and shared decision-making about medicines in people living with dementia [33]. To the best of our knowledge, we have co-designed and alpha tested the first decision aid (CPtDA) for people living with dementia and carers and healthcare professionals to help them review the ongoing need for their ChEIs. Future implementation of our CPtDA into clinical practice has the potential to prompt and increase conversations about deprescribing ChEIs to reduce the inappropriate use of these medicines. This may improve the alignment of medicines taken with people’s current goals of care. This is important as many people living with dementia report they are taking too many medicines and are willing to have their medicines deprescribed [32]. Future research efforts should focus on developing and sustainably implementing multi-modal tools that support person-centred deprescribing in people living with dementia to reduce their risk of experiencing medicine-related harm. Co-designing such tools, resources and interventions with people living with dementia, their carers and healthcare professionals will ensure that research can be effectively translated into practice.
