Acute myocardial infarction as presenting symptom of thymoma with compression on a coronary stent
Authors: Beatrice Leonardi, Giovanni Natale, Paolo Laperuta, Roberto Scaramuzzi, Gianfranco Apostolico, Francesco Leone, Carlo Bergaminelli, Alfonso Fiorelli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the clinical case of a patient with acute myocardial infarction due to coronary stent compression as first manifestation of a large thymoma. The patient underwent a coronarography and thrombus aspiration + plain old balloon angioplasty restoring the stent patency. The mass resection was performed through left robotic‐assisted thoracic surgery (RATS), resulting in a type A thymoma pT1a, IIb Masaoka‐Koga. An uncommon presentation led to early diagnosis and treatment of a thymoma with both oncological and functional significance.

INTRODUCTION

Mediastinal masses are frequently asymptomatic at diagnosis. Paraneoplastic syndromes and local invasion/compression of surrounding structures are the most common presenting symptoms. 1 Compression of the great vessels is not uncommon, particularly innominate vein and superior vena cava, and can cause characteristic clinical manifestation like superior vena cava syndrome. Acute coronary syndrome is rarely seen as first manifestation of a thymoma, being often related to intrapericardial or intracardiac tumor growth. 2 We report the clinical case of a patient that manifested an acute myocardial infarction due to coronary stent compression as first manifestation of a large thymoma.

CASE REPORT

A 62‐year‐old man came to our attention for a recently diagnosed mediastinal mass. The patient had a history of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, cholelithiasis, and acute myocardial infarction (STEMI) treated with percutaneous coronary intervention + drug‐eluting stent placement (2022) in the right coronary artery (CDx). In December 2023, the patient was admitted to the emergency department with a diagnosis of STEMI, and he underwent a coronarography that showed thrombotic occlusion of right artery coronary artery along the proximal tract of the previously implanted stent. Thrombus aspiration and plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA) restored the stent patency. During the hospital stay in the cardiac intensive care unit, the patient's X‐ray showed a left enlargement of the mediastinal silhouette. At discharge, dual antiplatelet therapy was started (ticagrelor + cardioaspirin), and a CT scan of the thorax was recommended. The patient did not have any symptoms compatible with myasthenia. Anamnestic data and laboratory data excluded a coagulopathy.

The chest CT scan showed a 7.1 × 6.5 cm mass in the superior mediastinum with inhomogeneous contrast enhancement and colliquative necrosis areas (Figure 1), showing signs of compression on the coronary stent in its proximal tract.

(a,b) Chest CT showing the anterior mediastinal mass in different sections. (c) The arrow shows the coronary stent compression by the mediastinal mass. (d) 3D reconstruction of the mediastinal mass and relations with mediastinal structures on a sagittal plane.

The patient was referred to the thoracic surgery department of Hospital “Vincenzo Monaldi” (Naples) and scheduled for surgical mediastinal mass resection in February 2024. Anesthesiologic and cardiologic evaluation recommended suspension of ticagrelor and bridging with tirofiban, according to hospital protocols. The patient underwent mediastinal mass resection through left tri‐portal RATS. The mediastinal mass, measuring 12 × 8 × 5 cm, was resected en bloc with mediastinal and pericardial fat and sent for pathological examination (Figure 2, Video 1). At the end of the procedure, as single 33 Fr chest drainage was placed.

(a) Intraoperative image showing dissection of the mediastinal mass from the pericardial fat. (b) Mediastinal mass resected.

The postoperative stay was uneventful. A control coronary computed tomography angiography was performed on postoperative day 5, showing patency of the coronary arteries and of the stent on CDx. The chest drainage was removed on postoperative day 6, and the patient was discharged the same day. The histopathological analysis diagnosed type A thymoma (pT1a, IIb Masaoka‐Koga). The patient has been directed to the oncologist for follow‐up and recovered well after surgery, being stable at the 3‐month follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

Among thymic epithelial tumors, thymoma is the most common, accounting for 15%–21% of mediastinal tumors and 47% of anterior mediastinal tumors. Thymoma has a slow growth pattern, and it is characterized by a low rate of distant spread. Particularly, type A and AB thymomas are considered indolent tumors, while type B1, B2, and B3 thymomas have a higher propensity for intrathoracic spread. 3

Locally advanced thymoma can cause compression or invasion of surrounding mediastinal structures, manifesting with symptoms like persistent cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, dyspnea, voice hoarseness, and superior vena cava syndrome. Direct cardiac invasion of thymoma is uncommon, yet possible: intravascular and intracardiac growth are described in the literature, constituting a surgical challenge in terms of resectability.

Only few cases report acute coronary syndrome as presenting symptom of a thymoma. Piqueras‐Flores et al. 4 reported a case of STEMI as first manifestation of a type A thymoma that was compressing the right ventricle and pulmonary artery and associated with pericardial invasion. Surgery was ruled out due to pericardial invasion, and radiation and chemotherapy were started.

Tsai et al. 5 reported a case of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) as first presentation of type A thymoma. The patient was treated with radiotherapy instead of surgery, due to her old age. The mechanism for myocardial infarction was recognized in a possible coronary thromboembolic event due to the patient's prothrombotic state or coronary vasospasms. Acute coronary syndrome has been also reported as a symptom of thymoma progression, with evidence of tumoral invasion of the coronary arteries. 6

In our case, extrinsic compression on the coronary artery along with tumor‐promoted hypercoagulability were the most logical explanations for the displacement and thrombosis of the stent. Restoring patency of the stent and treatment with dual antiplatelet therapy allowed the patient to undergo the surgical treatment, under strict cardiological monitoring in the pre‐ and postoperative period. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of first manifestation of a thymoma with coronary stent compression. Surgical resection of the mediastinal mass had the double purpose of oncological treatment and of removing the mass compression on the coronary artery stent, thus maintaining the stent restored patency obtained with emergent coronarography.

For masses >10 cm in diameter, the indication for minimally invasive surgery is still debated, 7 but recent evidence shows that RATS allowed the resection of mediastinal masses >10 cm, with some benefits in comparison with VATS. RATS magnified 3D vision and high maneuverability of the instruments facilitates a precise and radical resection of the tumor. 8 , 9

The peculiar condition of acute coronary syndrome due to coronary stent compression as presenting symptom allowed discovery and treatment of a thymoma at early stage. The prompt mass resection, after the acute cardiac condition treatment, reduced the risk of acute coronary syndrome recurrence caused by the mediastinal mass compression on the stent.
