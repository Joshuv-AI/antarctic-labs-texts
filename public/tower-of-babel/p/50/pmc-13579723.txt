Engineered iMSCs delivering wild-type IL-2 achieve dose-sparing tumor control via CD25-dependent CD8+ T-cell activation
Authors: Shuqing Tang, Mengting Han, Peiyun Wang, Fan Lu, Ying Zhang, Zhixing Li, Shenglan Zhou, Yusang Zhang, Zujia Wang, Junya Zhao, Yuquan Luo, Zhixin Tian, Mai Feng, Guanshan Zhu, Lingqian Wu, Desheng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterleukin-2 (IL-2), the first FDA-approved cytokine-based immunotherapy, can induce durable antitumor responses but its broader use is curtailed by life-threatening, dose-dependent toxicities. This narrow therapeutic window has limited clinical translation for decades. Genetically engineered mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have emerged as attractive IL-2 delivery vehicles due to their low immunogenicity and intrinsic tumor-homing capacity.MethodsHuman induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were edited by CRISPR/Cas9 to knock in wild-type IL-2 (wtIL-2) or a receptor-biased variant (IL-2v) into the B2M or B3 safe-harbor loci, generating cytokine-secreting iPSC-derived MSCs (iMSCs). Antitumor activity was evaluated in co-cultures of iMSCs, tumor cells, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by flow cytometry. In vivo, systemically administered iMSCs were tracked by live imaging in mice bearing subcutaneous tumors to assess biodistribution and tumor targeting. Tumor control was monitored by longitudinal tumor growth and end-point volume; toxicity was evaluated by lung wet weight and blood biochemistry. Immune cell states within the tumor microenvironment were characterized by flow cytometry and RNA sequencing. Data (mean ± s.e.m.) were analyzed in GraphPad Prism using t-tests, ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate (n = biological replicates; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).ResultsSurprisingly, we found that under low-dose conditions, only iMSCs with IL-2 overexpression—but not unmodified iMSCs or free IL-2 alone—induced robust upregulation of CD25 on CD8+ T cells. This effect likely increases T-cell sensitivity to wtIL-2 and may contribute to the improved tumor control achieved at reduced IL-2 doses. Systemic administration of wtIL-2-iMSCs preferentially activated intratumoral T cells and achieved tumor growth inhibition comparable to high-dose rhIL-2 in our model, while attenuating systemic toxicity. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that wtIL-2-iMSCs enhanced effector programs in tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells more effectively than rhIL-2 under the tested conditions. Notably, targeted integration of wtIL-2 at a novel locus within the B2M region (B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs) led to a higher transgene expression and further augmented antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo with no clear increase of systemic toxicity. Moreover, B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs exhibited synergistic antitumor effects when combined with anti-PD-1 blockade, thereby strengthening overall tumor control.ConclusionsThese findings point to a mechanism by which iMSC-delivered IL-2 reshapes the tumor microenvironment through CD25-dependent CD8⁺ T-cell activation. Furthermore, the results support the concept that this platform enables a more dose-efficient cytokine delivery strategy with an improved safety profile, thereby facilitating the clinical translation of off-the-shelf allogeneic iMSC-based gene therapies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05035-z.Keywords: Engineered iMSCs, Wild-type IL-2, CD25 signaling, Tumor immunotherapy, Targeted delivery

Background

The advent of cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology, with cytokine-based approaches representing a powerful method of modulating immune responses [1]. Among cytokines, interleukin-2 (IL-2), the first cytokine to be cloned in 1983 [2], has exhibited an exceptional ability to activate and expand CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes [3], leading to its approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of metastatic melanoma and renal cell carcinoma in the 1990s [4]. However, the therapeutic utility of IL-2 is constrained by its complex receptor biology. The IL-2 receptor (IL-2R) system comprises three subunits (α, β, and γ), which assemble into receptors of distinct affinities [5, 6]: the IL-2Rβγ heterodimer has intermediate affinity (Kd ≈ 1 nM), whereas the inclusion of the IL-2Rα (CD25) chain yields a high-affinity trimeric complex (Kd ≈ 10 pM) [7, 8]. Importantly, the expression of these subunits differs among T-cell subsets: effector T cells initially express only the βγ complex but upregulate CD25 expression following activation, while regulatory T cells (Tregs) constitutively express high-affinity trimers [9, 10]. This receptor distribution presents a major therapeutic challenge. High-dose IL-2 can stimulate potent antitumor responses but is associated with severe toxicity, including hepatic and renal dysfunction, capillary leak syndrome, and cardiovascular complications [11, 12]. In contrast, low-dose administration preferentially expands immunosuppressive Tregs, undermining therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, the extremely short plasma half-life of IL-2 (on the order of minutes) requires frequent high-dose administration, thereby exacerbating toxicity [13, 14]. Collectively, these limitations severely restrict the broader clinical application of IL-2 despite its demonstrated antitumor activity. Therefore, the development of therapeutic strategies that potentiate CD8+ T-cell activation and antitumor efficacy at reduced IL-2 doses holds substantial clinical significance.

The clinical limitations of IL-2 have driven the development of engineered biotherapeutics designed to enhance selectivity for effector immune cells, reduce systemic toxicity, and extend pharmacokinetic exposure. Major strategies include PEGylation for half-life extension [15], βγ-biased variants intended to minimize Treg activation [16], and antibody‒cytokine conjugates for tumor-directed delivery [17]. Despite encouraging preclinical results, no engineered IL-2 derivative has thus far received regulatory approval. Notably, the phase III failure of bempegaldesleukin (NKTR-214), the most advanced βγ-biased IL-2 variant developed to date, has challenged the prevailing paradigm of receptor-biased cytokine engineering [18, 19]. This outcome underscores the persistent gaps in the mechanistic understanding of IL-2 receptor signaling, particularly regarding the precise balance required between effector cell activation and therapeutic efficacy. Together, these findings highlight the urgent need for alternative strategies that not only improve the pharmacological and immunological properties of IL-2, but also facilitate a dose-sparing mode of cytokine delivery to better balance efficacy and safety.

Cell-based delivery systems, particularly mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), have recently emerged as favorable vectors for IL-2 administration. Compared with synthetic carriers, MSCs provide extended circulation, reduced immunogenicity, and a favorable safety profile [20, 21]. They can be readily isolated from diverse tissues, including bone marrow and adipose tissue [22–24], and are characterized by immunomodulatory functions as well as intrinsic tumor-homing capacity, rendering them well suited for targeted cancer therapy [25, 26]. Genetic modification expands their versatility, enabling MSCs to secrete a broad spectrum of therapeutic agents, such as cytokines, prodrug-converting enzymes, suicide genes, and oncolytic viruses [27–30].

However, the clinical translation of primary MSCs has been impeded by their inherent heterogeneity and limited scalability. Variability in tissue source, donor characteristics, and manufacturing processes introduces significant inconsistency [31–33], while a restricted proliferative capacity and inefficient genetic modification complicate their standardization for therapeutic use [34]. Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (iMSCs) represent a promising alternative. Derived from clonal iPSC lines with unlimited expansion capacity and high genetic tractability, iMSCs overcome these limitations [35–37]. iMSCs retain core MSC properties, including a fibroblast-like morphology, a characteristic surface marker profile (CD73+/CD90+/CD105+), and multilineage differentiation, while exhibiting notably greater proliferative capacity compared with that of tissue-derived MSCs. This combination of scalability and consistency positions iMSCs as attractive platforms for engineered cytokine delivery in immunotherapy.

The feasibility of MSC-mediated IL-2 delivery has been demonstrated in pioneering studies, such as the use of adenovirus-transduced adipose-derived MSCs that exhibited antitumor activity [38]. More recent work has used lentiviral-engineered murine bone marrow MSCs secreting an optimized IL-2 variant (SIL-2) with attenuated CD25 binding and enhanced βγ-chain affinity, achieving tumor-localized delivery with improved safety and efficacy in preclinical models [39]. Nevertheless, the reliance on viral vectors and intratumoral administration limits clinical applicability. Compounding these challenges, the clinical discontinuation of NKTR-214 [19], a leading βγ-biased IL-2 candidate, has intensified the debate regarding optimal receptor-targeting strategies. Emerging evidence suggests that CD25 engagement may in fact be essential for effective T-cell activation [40, 41], raising critical questions about the balance between wild-type IL-2 and engineered variants. Notably, no direct comparisons of these cytokine formats have been conducted in MSC delivery systems, where carrier biology may profoundly influence therapeutic outcomes.

To address these limitations, we used a CRISPR-Cas9-based genome-editing platform to stably integrate either wild-type IL-2 (wtIL-2) or a βγ-biased IL-2 variant (IL-2v) into iPSCs [42], which were subsequently differentiated into homogeneous, expandable iMSCs. These engineered iMSCs secreted IL-2 in a stable and sustained manner, enabling tumor-targeted cytokine delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity. Furthermore, comparative analyses revealed that the combination of iMSCs with IL-2 overexpression—but not either component alone—markedly upregulated CD25 on CD8+ T cells. This effect sensitized the T cells to wtIL-2, achieving potent tumor suppression at substantially reduced doses while minimizing systemic toxicity through targeted delivery. This integrated strategy combines the advantages of tumor localization, dose-sparing efficacy, and reduced toxicity with hallmark features of off-the-shelf cell therapy, including standardized quality, unlimited expandability, and suitability for allogeneic application. Overall, these properties provide compelling preclinical support for the clinical translation of IL-2-iMSC-based immunotherapy.

Methods

Animals

Six-week-old female BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China) were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 8 × 10⁴ CT26 (BALB/c-derived) or MC38 (C57BL/6-derived) tumor cells suspended in 50 µL DPBS. Once tumors reached 100 ± 10 mm³ (calculated as 0.5 × length × width²), mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 8–10 per group) using stratified block randomization based on tumor volume. Mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections every other day for a total of five doses of one of the following: DPBS (200 µL), rhIL-2 (50,000 IU), wtIL-2-iMSCs, or IL-2v-iMSCs (2 × 10⁶ cells resuspended in 200 µL DPBS). In experiments combining wtIL-2-iMSCs with anti-mouse PD-1 (Selleck, Houston, TX, USA, #A2122, clone: RMP1-14), mice were treated i.p. every other day for three doses with anti-mouse PD-1 (5 mg/kg), wtIL-2-iMSCs (2 × 10⁶ cells), or the combination of both. Tumor volumes were measured every 48 h using digital calipers and calculated as (width² × length)/2. Body weights were recorded concurrently.

In the context of the present study, the term dose-sparing is used operationally to indicate that comparable antitumor activity was achieved with iMSC-mediated IL-2 delivery despite a substantially lower estimated IL-2 output than that achieved by administration of the recombinant protein, rather than on the basis of a formal in vivo dose-titration or systemic exposure analysis. Based on our in vitro characterization, 2 × 10⁶ low-expressing wtIL-2-iMSCs were estimated to produce approximately 100 IU IL-2 over 24 h, assuming full survival of all injected cells during that interval, whereas the comparator group received 50,000 IU rhIL-2 per injection. The observation that these regimens produced broadly comparable antitumor effects provides the basis for describing the iMSC-based strategy as dose-sparing in the context of the current dataset.

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the School of Life Sciences, Central South University (ethics number 2021-2-54). A maximal tumor burden of 2,000 mm³ was permitted Humane endpoints, including tumor volume exceeding this threshold, ulceration affecting > 15% of the tumor surface area, or > 20% body weight loss, were strictly applied. We confirm that this maximum tumor size/burden was not exceeded for any animal in this study. Animals were monitored regularly and humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation at the predefined endpoint or upon showing signs of distress, prior to reaching the size limit. All mice were group-housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in individually ventilated cages (20–24 °C, 40–60% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with access to irradiated chow and autoclaved water. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and established animal welfare guidelines.

Human PBMCs

In accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, peripheral blood from healthy adult donors was obtained under protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Life Sciences, Central South University (Changsha, China) (ethics number: 2021-1-39). All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, assured of confidentiality, and provided written consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Cell lines, iPSCs, and routine culture

A human iPSC cell line, which was reprogrammed from exfoliated renal tubular epithelial cells (urine cells) obtained from a healthy male donor by our laboratory [42] was used in this study. HEK 293T (RRID: CVCL_0063), CTLL-2 (RRID: CVCL_0227), human tumor cell lines (786-O, RRID: CVCL_1051; A375, RRID: CVCL_0132; HCC827, RRID: CVCL_2063), and murine tumor cell lines (CT26, RRID: CVCL_7256; MC38, RRID: CVCL_B288) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) between July 2022 and December 2023. The acquisition dates were as follows: HEK293T and CT26 in July 2022; A375 on August 20, 2022; CTLL-2 on March 20, 2023; 786-O and HCC827 in November 2023; and MC38 on December 9, 2023. All cell lines were authenticated and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. HEK293T, A375, and MC38 cells were maintained in DMEM/high glucose (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA, #C11995500) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, #F9423). CTLL-2, 786-O, HCC827, and CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, #11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ and passaged at subconfluence using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, #25200056).

Plasmid construction and screening in HEK293T Cells

A nonviral human B2M locus-targeting vector (E-1002) was derived from prior work. B3 locus homology arms (PacI/NheI) were synthesized (GenScript, Nanjing, China) to generate the B3-targeting vector. Human IL-2 (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_000586.4) and IL-2v (F42A, L80F, R81D, L85V, I86V, I92F) cDNAs were synthesized by Sangon (Shanghai, China) and PCR amplified to append the indicated signal peptides and promoters (EF1α/CAG/CMV). Fragments were ligated into B2M and B3 vectors using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, #C112), generating the following constructs: pB2M-wtIL2, pB2M-IL2v, pB3-wtIL2, and pB3-IL2v. All constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing (HZYKANG Biotech, Hangzhou, China). HEK 293T cells were transfected using jetPRIME (Polyplus, Strasbourg, France, #101000027). At 48 h, supernatants and lysates were assayed by Western blot and ELISA to identify high-expressing constructs (see Method Details for parameters).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeted integration in human iPSCs

CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were assembled by incubating 25 pmol Cas9 protein (GenScript, #Z03702) with 50 pmol B2M-targeting sgRNA (5′-GGCCGAGATGTCTCGCTCCGTGG-3′; GenScript) in 20 µL Buffer R (Invitrogen, #MPK10025B) at 37 °C for 15 min. Simultaneously, human iPSCs (passage 15–20) were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE™ Express (Gibco, #12604021) for 5 min at 37 °C. A total of 1.5 × 10⁶ cells were resuspended in 100 µL Buffer R containing 4 µg targeting vector plasmid and the preassembled RNP complexes. Nucleofection was performed using the Neon™ Transfection System (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, #MP927733) under the following parameters: 1,200 V, 20 ms and 2 pulses. Immediately after nucleofection, the cells were plated on Matrigel®-coated dishes (Corning, #354277) in mTeSR™ Plus medium (STEMCELL, Vancouver, BC, Canada, #100–0276) supplemented with 10 µM Y-27,632 (STEMCELL, #72304). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ with daily medium changes. After 2–3 passages (7–10 days) to eliminate episomal DNA, edited iPSCs were dissociated to single cells and seeded at clonal density (500 cells per 6-cm Matrigel-coated dish) in mTeSR™ Plus supplemented with 10% CloneR™ (STEMCELL, #05888). (Matrigel, BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA, Cat#354277). Monoclonal colonies reaching 1.5–2 mm diameter were mechanically isolated under 5× magnification and transferred to Matrigel-coated 48-well plates for expansion.

Cell culture

Human iPSCs were maintained in mTeSR™ Plus medium on Matrigel-coated culture plates, with daily medium replacement. When cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were passaged following incubation with 0.05 mM EDTA solution (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA, #15575020) for 4 min at room temperature, and seeded onto freshly coated Matrigel plates. iMSCs were cultured in α-MEM basic medium (Gibco, #12571063) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, #35050061), and 10 ng/mL recombinant human basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA, #3718-FB) on culture dishes (Corning, NY, USA) pre-coated with 0.1% gelatin (STEMCELL, #07903) for at least 30 min at room temperature. Cells were passaged using 0.05% TrypLE™ Express upon reaching 80–90% confluency.

Isolation and culture of human PBMCs

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from heparinized blood by density gradient centrifugation using Histopaque®-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, #10771) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, blood diluted with DPBS was carefully layered over Histopaque®-1077 and centrifuged at 300 × g for 30 min at room temperature. The PBMC layer at the interface was collected, washed twice with PBS, treated with Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #C3702) at room temperature for 10 min, and finally resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Freshly isolated PBMCs were stimulated with Dynabeads™ Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (ThermoFisher, #11131D) at a cell-to-bead ratio of 10:1 and cultured in the presence of 100 IU/mL recombinant human interleukin-2 (rhIL-2; SL PHARM, Beijing, China, #S19991007) to promote T cell proliferation. Activated PBMCs were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. After 72 h of activation, PBMCs were harvested and used for subsequent co-culture experiments with iMSCs.

Validation of targeted integration in iPSC clones

Site-specific integration at the B2M locus was verified through PCR screening and Sanger sequencing. iPSC clones were lysed, and PCR amplification was performed using 2 × Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, #P222). Dual primer sets were used to span the integration junctions: 5’ junction: TYB-GSH-2-UP-F / TYB-CAG-R; 3’ junction: TYB-GSH-2-DOWN-IL-2-F / TYB-GSH-2-DOWN-R (All primer sequences provided in Additional file 1: Table S1). PCR products were purified (FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini-Kit, Vazyme, #DC301) and validated by bidirectional Sanger sequencing (HZYKANG Biotech). Intracellular IL-2 expression was quantified by flow cytometry: dissociated iPSCs were fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences, #554714) to enable intracellular staining. Following permeabilization, cells were incubated with PE-conjugated anti-human IL-2 (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA, #500306) for 30 min, and analyzed on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo v10.9.

Pluripotency characterization and karyotypic analysis of iPSCs

Surface and intracellular pluripotency markers were analyzed by flow cytometry using the Human Pluripotent Stem Cell Transcription Factor Analysis Kit (BD Biosciences, #560589). Briefly, iPSCs were dissociated with TrypLE™ Express, and 1 × 10⁶ cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with conjugated antibodies against OCT4 (APC, clone 40/Oct-3), SOX2 (PE, clone 245610), NANOG (PerCP-Cy5.5, clone 259D/C7), and SSEA-4 (FITC, clone MC-813-70). For karyotyping, cells were treated with colchicine (0.08 µg/mL, 2.5 h), swollen in 0.075 M KCl (37 °C, 30 min), fixed in Carnoy’s reagent (methanol: acetic acid 3:1), and G-banded after trypsin digestion (0.025%, 45 s) and Giemsa staining (7%, 8 min). At least 20 metaphase spreads per clone were analyzed according to ISCN 2020 guidelines.

Off-target detection

Potential off-target sites were predicted using CAS-OFFinder (hg38, ≤ 4 mismatches) (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-offinder/) and ranked by predicted cleavage efficiency. Genomic DNA extracted from iPSCs (Vazyme, #DC102-01) served as the template for amplifying the top three loci (Primer pairs off-target-1-F/R, off-target-2-F/R, and off-target-3-F/R are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1) with Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme, #P525-AA; 35 cycles, 60 °C annealing). Products underwent sanger sequencing (≥ 500 bp coverage). Unedited iPSCs served as negative controls.

Differentiation and validation of iPSC-derived MSCs

iPSCs were differentiated into iMSCs using the STEMdiff Mesenchymal Progenitor Kit (STEMCELL, #05240), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, iPSCs were cultured in mTeSR™ Plus medium on Matrigel-coated plates until approximately 30% confluency. The medium was then replaced with STEMdiff -ACF Mesenchymal Induction Medium, which was changed daily for 4 days, followed by culture in MesenCult-ACF medium for 2 days. On day 7, cells were detached using TrypLE™ Express and seeded onto MesenCult™-ACF Attachment Substrate. Upon reaching 80–90% confluency, cells were passaged into 6-cm dishes pre-coated with MesenCult-ACF Attachment Substrate and cultured continuously up to passage 5. Finally, cells were seeded into 10-cm dishes pre-coated with gelatin and cultured in MSC medium once the cell morphology resembled that of MSCs. To identify the surface antigens of iMSCs, 1 × 10⁶ cells were collected, washed with cold DPBS, and resuspended in 100 µL DPBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the following antibodies at room temperature for 30 min: BV421 anti-human CD34/CD45/HLA-DR, BB515 anti-human CD44, PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-human CD73, APC anti-human CD105, and PE-Cy7 anti-human CD90 (BD Biosciences). Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Tri-lineage differentiation assays

The multipotency of iMSCs was validated using STEMdiff™ kits for Osteogenesis (#05465; 21 days), Adipogenesis (#05412; 14-day cycles), and Chondrogenesis (#05455; pellet culture). After 2–3 weeks of differentiation culture, the osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation capabilities of iMSCs were evaluated using Alizarin Red staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, #G1038), Oil Red O staining (Cyagen, Santa Clara, CA, USA, #S0132), and Alcian Blue staining (Cyagen), respectively. Stained cells were analyzed using a light microscope.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR) Analysis

RNA was extracted with TRIzol™ (Invitrogen, #15596018), DNase-treated, and reverse-transcribed (Vazyme, #R223-01) using random primers/Oligo (dT)23 VN primer Mix. RNA purity and integrity were verified by A260/A280 ratio > 1.8 and agarose gel electrophoresis. Reverse transcription was performed at 42 °C for 2 min, 50 °C for 15 min, and 85 °C for 5 s. qPCR was performed in triplicate using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q711-02) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch™ system with validated primers (Additional file 1: Table S1). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with normalization to the geometric mean of GAPDH, ACTB, and RPLP0 as internal reference genes.

Quantification of IL-2 secretion by ELISA

Conditioned media from iPSCs (5 × 10⁵ cells/12-well plate) and iMSCs (1 × 10⁶ cells/6-cm dish) were collected after 24 h of incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min), and stored at -80 °C. Cell counts were determined post-harvest using TrypLE™ Express. IL-2 was quantified using an ELISA kit (Sino Biological, Beijing, China, #KIT11848) with TMB development. Samples were measured at 450 nm (Elx800, BioTek) against recombinant standards (0-1000 pg/mL, R² > 0.99). All samples were run in triplicate with inter-assay CV < 10%.

Western blot analysis of IL-2 expression

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, #P10013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor (Selleck, #B14001). Secreted proteins from iMSCs were methanol-chloroform precipitated. Samples (10 µg) were separated on 15% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk in 0.1% Tris-buffered saline with Tween and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-human IL-2 antibody (1:2000, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA, #CL488-60306) and anti-α-tubulin (1:5000, Antgene, Shanghai, China, #ANT326). The following day, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody. (1:10,000, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA, #115-035-003) at room temperature for 1 h. Blots were developed with an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (ZENBIO, Durham, NC, USA, #17046) and imaged on a Bio-Rad imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab.

Functional validation of iMSC-secreted IL-2 via CTLL-2 bioassay

The biological activity of IL-2 secreted by iMSCs was evaluated using a CTLL-2 cell proliferation assay. iMSCs (6 × 10⁶ cells) were seeded in 0.1% gelatin-coated 10-cm dishes. After 12 h of attachment, the medium was replaced with serum-free α-MEM. Conditioned media (CM) collected after 24 h were concentrated using an Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filter (Millipore, UFC901096; 10 kDa MWCO, 4,000 × g, 30 min, 4°C) to 200 µL. The CM samples were normalized by supplementing with 20 µL FBS to match the control medium composition and diluted with RPMI-1640 for 2 × and 4 × working concentrations. For the proliferation assay, CTLL-2 cells (6 × 104) were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 200 IU/mL rhIL-2 as positive control. CTLL-2 cells were stimulated with CM for 24 h, followed by incubation with CCK-8 (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, #RM02823) reagent (37 °C, 3 h). Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA, #Elx800). Bioactivity was calculated against an rhIL-2 standard curve (0–200 IU/mL). For the CTLL-2 bioassay, the IL-2 standard curve centered around 50 IU/mL, and conditioned-medium dilutions were optimized in preliminary experiments so that samples fell within the linear detection range, allowing reliable quantification of bioactive IL-2.

Longitudinal proliferation analysis of iMSCs

iMSCs at passage 12 were enzymatically dissociated using 50% TrypLE™ Express, followed by neutralization with α-MEM containing 10% FBS. Precisely 1 × 10⁶ viable cells (as determined using AOPI; Countstar, Shanghai, China, #RE010212) were seeded in a 0.1% gelatin-coated 10-cm culture dish in complete growth medium. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ with medium renewal every 48 h, until reaching 90% confluency, at which point cells were harvested via the same dissociation protocol and quantified using a fluorescence cell analyzer (Countstar Rigel S2). This cycle of standardized passaging—comprising seeding at 1 × 10⁶ cells/dish, expansion to 90% confluency, and precise cell counting—was repeated for five consecutive passages to generate a population doubling curve.

Tumor cell-PBMC-iMSC co-culture assay

Tumor cells were stained in the dark with 5 µM CellTrace™ Violet (Invitrogen, #C34557A; 20 min, RT). iMSCs and CellTrace™ Violet-labeled tumor cells were co-seeded at a 5:1 ratio in 6-well plates (3 × 105 iMSCs and 6 × 104 tumor cells per well) for 12 h before adding 6 × 105 bead-depleted PBMCs in the absence of exogenous IL-2. This tumor cell-to-PBMC ratio was selected based on commonly used effector-to-target ratios in in vitro immune cell–tumor co-culture systems and was further supported by preliminary optimization experiments to ensure robust immune–tumor cell interactions. The initial iMSC seeding density (3 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate) was determined in pilot experiments to provide sufficient accumulation of iMSC-secreted IL-2 while avoiding excessive cell crowding during the 48 h co-culture period. After the potent activity of wtIL-2-iMSCs had been established, the iMSC input was further titrated, and 8.5 × 104 cells per well was identified as an appropriate lower density that maintained normal cell growth in the same culture format. Based on the amount of IL-2 secreted under this condition, 2.5 IU/mL rhIL-2 was selected as the low-dose comparator. For experiments using exogenous cytokine, 50 IU/mL rhIL-2 was used based on prior literature [43] and preliminary optimization, as this concentration is within a functionally relevant range for PBMC activation in vitro. After 48 h, all cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (tumor cells apoptosis: Annexin V/7-AAD; PBMCs proliferation, activation, and functional characteristics). Supernatants were subjected to IL-2 ELISA.

Quantification of tumor cell apoptosis by flow cytometry

After 48 h of co-culture, cells were harvested using TrypLE™ Express. Apoptosis was quantified by PE-Annexin V/7-AAD dual staining (BD, #559763). Specifically, cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1 × Binding Buffer, add 5 µL of PE-Annexin V and 5 µL of 7-AAD staining solution, and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora™ with gating on CellTrace Violet (CTV)-positive singlets. Early (Annexin V+/7–AAD–) and late apoptotic (Annexin V+/7–AAD+) populations were analyzed.

Multidimensional profiling of T-cell functionality in co-cultured PBMCs

Viable PBMCs harvested from co-culture systems were stained using a three-step protocol. First, cell viability was assessed with Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend, #423105; 1:1000 dilution, 30 min, RT, dark). Cells were then surface-stained with a pre-titrated antibody cocktail containing lineage markers (CD45-PE/Cy5 [BioLegend, #304010], CD3-BV605 [#344836]), subset identifiers (CD4-AF700 [#344622], CD8-BV570 [#301038]), functional detectors (CD25-PE [#356104], CD69-PE/Cy7 [#310912], CD107a-APC/Cy7 [#328630]), differentiation markers (CD56-BV510 [#318340], CD45RA-SparkBlue550 [#304185]), and exhaustion indicators (CCR7-PE/Fire810 [#353269], CD95-APC/Fire810 [#305664], PD-1-PE/Fire700 [#621622], TIM-3-BV785 [#345032]) in FACS buffer (DPBS + 1% FBS) for 30 min at RT protected from light. Following surface staining, cells were fixed/permeabilized using the eBioscience FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Invitrogen, 00-5523-00), then incubated intracellularly with BV421-FoxP3 [#320124], BV750-IFN-γ [#502550], BV650-Ki67 [#151215], and FITC-Granzyme B [#372206] for 45 min at 4 °C. After the final wash, at least 20,000 CD45+ events were acquired on a Cytek Aurora™.

Lentiviral-mediated EGFP reporter engineering in iMSCs

Passage-10 iMSCs were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in 0.1% gelatin-coated 6-well plates (Corning, #3516), with triplicate wells allocated for transduction, cell counting, and puromycin control groups. At 30–40% confluency (typically 24 h post-seeding), cells in designated wells were enzymatically dissociated and quantified via Countstar Rigel S2 to calculate the lentiviral volume using the formula: Virus (µL) = [30 × cell count × 10³] / titer (TU/mL). The pASLenti-pA-LuC2-CMV-EF1-EGFP-P2A -PurO-WPRE lentivector (Obio, #GL161; titer = 8.30E + 08 TU/mL) was thawed on ice and combined with Polybrene-Plus transduction enhancer (Obio, Shanghai, China, #PTSJ-GR001; 1 mg/mL final concentration) in serum-free α-MEM. Cells were incubated with 2 mL viral mixture for 16 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, followed by complete medium replacement. At 72 h post-transduction, EGFP expression was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Leica K5 BZ03; 488 nm excitation). Puromycin selection (Beyotime, #ST551) commenced at 1 µg/mL in complete medium, and was progressively escalated to 5 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL over 7 days as cell viability stabilized. Transduced cells were expanded and enriched as described above, and cells with > 95% EGFP positivity, as confirmed by flow cytometry on a Cytek Aurora™, were used for downstream experiments. In the present study, EGFP was used as the primary reporter for histological identification of iMSCs in tumor sections, whereas luciferase, although included in the original vector construct, was not used for biodistribution analysis because bioluminescence imaging requires strict timing control and is less suitable for ex vivo organ imaging.

In vivo tracking and tumor tropism assessment of engineered iMSCs

For in vivo tracking, iMSCs were labeled with the near-infrared lipophilic dye XenoLight DiR (Revvity, MA, USA, Cat#125964) prior to administration. Tumor-bearing mice (subcutaneous MC38 model, tumor volume, 500 ± 50 mm³) received intraperitoneal administration of 2 × 10⁶ DiR-EGFP-iMSCs. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed 24 h post-injection using the PerkinElmer In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, MA, USA) under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. DiR fluorescence was detected using the appropriate near-infrared filter settings (excitation/emission: 748/780 nm), and signal intensity was quantified using Living Image software. For ex vivo biodistribution analysis, major organs, including tumor, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested and imaged immediately after sacrifice using the same IVIS settings. To validate intratumoral homing, mice injected with DiR-EGFP-iMSCs were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tumors were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Servicebio, #G1101; 24 h at 4 °C). Tissues underwent paraffin embedding, sectioning at 5 μm thickness, and antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0) prior to GFP immunofluorescence staining using a rabbit anti-GFP primary antibody (AiFang, Hunan, China, #AF10778; 1:1000) and counterstaining with DAPI (AiFang, #AFIHC044). Slides were imaged using KFSlideOS-assisted scanning and the density of GFP+ cell infiltration was quantified in three representative tumor sections for each tumor tissue using ImageJ software.

Toxicity profiling in murine models

At the experimental endpoint, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane, and peripheral blood was collected via the infraorbital venous plexus, followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation. Blood samples were allowed to clot for 2 h at 25 °C and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min (4 °C). Serum aliquots were stored at − 80 °C prior to biochemical analysis using chemistry analyzer (Chemray 240,420,800) with comprehensive panels. Hepatic function was assessed by alanine aminotransferase (ALT; reference range: 10.06–96.47 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; reference range: 36.31–235.48 U/L), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP; reference range: 22.52–474.35 U/L). Renal function was assessed by blood urea nitrogen (BUN; reference range: 10.81–34.74 mg/dL) and creatinine (reference range: 10.91–85.09 µmol/L) [44, 45]. For pulmonary edema assessment, mice were euthanized 24 h after the final treatment, and the thoracic cavity was opened immediately. Lungs were immediately weighed to obtain the wet weight (WW) on an analytical balance (± 0.1 mg), photographed for morphological documentation, then desiccated at 37 °C for 24 h in a forced-air oven to constant weight to obtain the dry weight (DW). Edema index was calculated as WW – DW.

Multiparametric immunophenotyping of the tumor microenvironment and peripheral compartments

Subcutaneous tumor-bearing C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice (MC38 or CT26 models, respectively) were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane at one-week post-treatment endpoint. Peripheral blood collected via the infraorbital venous plexus into tubes containing sodium citrate anticoagulant (Phygene, Fujian, China, #PH1904) was processed immediately. Aliquots of 100 µL were stained with a surface antibody cocktail (Additional file 1: Table S2). Splenocytes were isolated by mechanical dissociation through 40-µm cell strainers (Falcon, # 352340) followed by RBC lysis (BioLegend #420301; 10 min RT, dark) and then washed in FACS buffer (DPBS supplemented with 1% FBS). Tumor tissues underwent enzymatic digestion in RPMI-1640 containing 1 mg/mL collagenase I, II, IV (Gibco, # 17018029, 17101015, 17104019) with orbital shaking at 37 °C for 45 min, followed by sequential filtration sequentially through 40-µm cell strainers. All single-cell suspensions were adjusted to 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL in FACS buffer for surface staining with optimized antibody panels (Additional file 1: Table S2). For intracellular targets (FOXP3, Ki67, and Granzyme B), cells were fixed and permeabilized using the eBioscience FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set before antibody incubation. Data acquisition employed a Cytek Aurora™. More than 50,000 live-cell events, defined by Zombie NIR™ exclusion, were collected per sample and analyzed using FlowJo v10.9, with Boolean gating strategies validated by fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls.

Transcriptomic profiling of engineered iMSCs and tumor-infiltrating T cells

For bulk RNA-seq, total RNA was extracted from three biological replicates each of Blank-iMSCs, wtIL-2-iMSCs, and IL-2v-iMSCs (passages 11–14) using TRIzol™ reagent. RNA integrity was verified (RQN ≥ 4.5, Agilent 5300) before library construction using the TruSeq™ RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina). Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed using the HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm (Shanghai, China), yielding ≥ 40 million reads per sample. Raw data were processed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform, including adapter trimming, alignment to GRCh38, gene quantification (RSEM), and differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (thresholds: FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1). Clusters of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized via pheatmap (v1.0.12), and functional enrichment (GO/KEGG) were performed using clusterProfiler (v4.4.4). For RNA sequencing of tumor-infiltrating T cells, CD8⁺ T cells were isolated from CT26 tumors (BALB/c mice, n = 3/group) using FACS. Tumor tissues were dissociated with collagenase I/II/IV, stained with Zombie Red™ (BioLegend, #77475), anti-CD45-BV785, anti-CD3-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD8a-APC, and sorted using a BD FACSAria III. A total of 20,000 cells per sample was submitted to Majorbio for library construction. Sequencing data were analyzed using Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.2.3. For comparisons between two independent groups that meet parametric assumptions, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for normally distributed data or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction for nonparametric datasets. The sample size (n) represents the number of biological replicates in each experimental group. Statistical significance thresholds were defined as P < 0.05, with significance levels denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Targeted integration of IL-2 at the B2M locus in iPSCs enables efficient IL-2 production

To enhance IL-2 secretion, we compared the native IL-2 signal peptide with SP1 [46], a signal peptide previously demonstrated by our group to mediate efficient protein secretion in MSCs. SP1 consistently mediated the highest amount of IL-2 secretion, regardless of whether the CMV or CAG promoter was used (Fig. 1A). On this basis, SP1 was selected to drive IL-2 expression and secretion. We next constructed vectors encoding either wtIL-2 or IL-2v which harbors mutations at established sites known to reduce CD25 binding while enhancing the affinity for IL-2Rβ [47, 48] (Fig. 1B). The B2M locus was selected as the genomic target site given its status as a well-characterized safe harbor. Additionally, our preliminary data indicated that the B2M locus can robustly support high-level transgene expression. In vitro genome-editing experiments showed that the selected sgRNA targeting the B2M locus mediated B2M knockout in iPSCs, with an efficiency of more than 60% in the absence of drug selection (Fig. 1C). Using a targeting vector containing B2M homology arms, we inserted wtIL-2 or IL-2v expression cassettes into the B2M locus via homologous recombination, as illustrated in Fig. 1D. Clonal screening identified five correctly edited lines including three wtIL-2 lines and two IL-2v lines (Fig. 1E-G). Expression analyses confirmed IL-2 transcription and protein expression in all five clones (Fig. 1H-I). Since housekeeping-gene expression may vary under certain culture or differentiation conditions [49, 50], we first examined the raw Cq values of three commonly used reference genes across the different iPSC clones. Although GAPDH, ACTB, and RPLP0 were expressed within a relatively narrow range, modest inter-clone variation was observed (Additional file 2: Fig. S1A-C). Therefore, IL-2 mRNA levels were normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH, ACTB, and RPLP0 (Fig. 1H). In the wtIL-2 group, clone 95 showed the highest mRNA expression and protein secretion, secreting 450 pg of IL-2 per 1 × 106 cells over 24 h. In the IL-2v group, although clone 114 showed lower mRNA expression than clone 2, it exhibited the higher protein secretion, reaching 550 pg under the same conditions (Fig. 1I). Based on these results, clone 95 and clone 114 were selected for subsequent experiments. The edited iPSCs retained pluripotency, normal morphology, and a diploid karyotype (46, XY) (Fig. 1J, K; Additional file 2: Fig. S1D). Off-target analysis detected no unintended alterations at other genomic loci (Additional file 2: Fig. S1E). Because B2M encodes a component of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), its knockout may affect immunogenicity. Therefore, we generated iPSCs with B2M knockout alone as a negative control and used the same parental cell line for all experimental groups to ensure genetic consistency. Sequencing and flow cytometry confirmed complete disruption of the B2M locus and the absence of B2M expression in all the cell lines (Fig. 1L).

Engineered iMSCs with robust proliferation and stable IL-2 secretion exhibit potential as an off-the-shelf allogeneic therapy

Edited iPSCs were differentiated into iMSCs using a standard commercial protocol (Fig. 2A). Control iMSCs derived from iPSCs in which only the B2M locus was knocked out were designated as Blank-iMSCs. The resulting iMSCs displayed a typical spindle-shaped morphology (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A), expressed CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 (> 95%), and lacked CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (< 5%) (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B), consistent with the International Society for Cell and Gene Therapy (ISCT) criteria [51, 52]. They also retained trilineage differentiation potential (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C). We next assessed IL-2 transcript levels in differentiated iMSCs by RT-qPCR using the geometric mean of GAPDH, ACTB, and RPLP0 for normalization. IL-2 transcripts were detected in both wtIL-2-iMSCs and IL-2v-iMSCs, with significantly higher expression in wtIL-2-iMSCs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B; Additional file 2: Fig. S2D). Notably, compared with the iPSCs stage, IL-2 transcript levels appeared higher at the iMSCs stage, although the pattern did not appear to increase linearly across differentiation stages and may partly reflect clone-dependent variation (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the current targeted integration site may exhibit relatively higher transcriptional activity in iMSCs. ELISA confirmed robust secretion within 24 h, wtIL-2-iMSCs and IL-2v-iMSCs secreted approximately 7 ng and 2 ng of IL-2 per 106 cells, respectively (n = 3, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2C). This represented a substantial increase relative to the iPSCs stage, suggesting that at this specific integration site, iMSCs exhibit superior transgene expression and secretion capacity compared with iPSCs. Importantly, IL-2 expression and secretion remained stable over serial passages (Fig. 2D). Functional assays using CTLL-2 cells showed that secreted IL-2 was biologically active, corresponding to approximately 50 IU per 106 cells within 24 h (Fig. 2E, F). Both IL-2-engineered lines exhibited strong proliferative capacity, expanding more than 500-fold within 14 days (Fig. 2G), and maintained robust growth until passage 20 (P20), underscoring their suitability for large-scale production.

RNA sequencing identified 237 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in IL-2-expressing iMSCs, including 145 genes that were consistently upregulated (cluster 1) (Fig. 2H), indicating a coordinated transcriptional upregulation pattern following IL-2 overexpression. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis highlighted biological processes related to anatomical structure development, multicellular organismal processes, and developmental processes (Fig. 2I). Further analysis revealed that IL-2 overexpression was associated with increased expression of genes involved in MSC adhesion, including laminin subunit alpha 3 (LAMA3), as well as proinflammatory factors such as interleukin-33 (IL-33) and tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 15 (TNFSF15). In addition, increased expression of genes linked to cell proliferation and self-renewal, notably homeobox B4 (HOXB4), was observed (Fig. 2J) [53].

These transcriptomic changes suggest that IL-2 expression may modulate MSC properties, including adhesion, inflammatory responses, and proliferative capacity, although further studies are required to confirm the biological relevance of these associations.

wtIL-2-iMSCs enhance the antitumor cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells through CD25-dependent activation

We next evaluated whether engineered iMSCs could potentiate immune cell-mediated tumor clearance (Fig. 3A). Coculture of IL-2-engineered iMSCs (IL-2-iMSCs) with PBMCs and A375 melanoma cells resulted in markedly enhanced cytotoxicity compared with blank controls. wtIL-2-iMSCs increased PBMC-mediated tumor cell death by nearly fourfold, whereas IL-2v-iMSCs improved cytotoxicity by only approximately 50% (Fig. 3B). This superior effect of wtIL-2-iMSCs was reproducible across multiple donors and extended to additional tumor cell lines, including HCC827 and 786-O (Fig. 3C; Additional file 2: Fig. S3A).

Mechanistically, wtIL-2-iMSCs significantly expanded the CD8+ T-cell population, whereas IL-2v-iMSCs had a weaker effect (Fig. 3D; Additional file 2: Fig. S3B and C). Both wtIL-2-iMSCs and IL-2v-iMSCs promoted CD8+ T-cell activation, as evidenced by increased CD25 and CD69 expression (Fig. 3E, F), with consistent results across PBMCs from multiple donors (Additional file 2: Fig. S3D and E). CD25 is a component of the high-affinity trimeric IL-2 receptor, and its upregulation enhances T-cell sensitivity to wtIL-2, thereby amplifying downstream signaling. In this context, wtIL-2-iMSCs exhibited greater efficacy than IL-2v-iMSCs. CD69, an early T-cell activation marker [54], was significantly upregulated by blank-iMSCs, highlighting the intrinsic immunostimulatory capacity of MSCs. Notably, IL-2-iMSCs further increased CD69 expression (Fig. 3F), suggesting a synergistic interaction between the iMSCs platform and IL-2-mediated immune modulation. Functional assays confirmed that wtIL-2-iMSCs enhanced granzyme B (GZMB) release from CD8+ T cells and increased the proportion of CD8+PD1+CD25+ effector cells, whereas IL-2v-iMSCs were less effective. Although wtIL-2-iMSCs also activated Tregs (Fig. 3G), CD8+ T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity was not abrogated.

To directly assess cytokine potency independent of dosage, we titrated wtIL-2-iMSCs to match IL-2v secretion levels of IL-2v-iMSCs. Even when the IL-2 output was equivalent, wtIL-2-iMSCs induced approximately twice as much tumor cell death as IL-2v-iMSCs (Fig. 3H). Despite increasing the Treg proportion, wtIL-2-iMSCs significantly increased CD25 expression on CD8+ T cells, expanded the CD8+ T-cell population, and reduced T-cell exhaustion (Fig. 3I and Additional file 2: Fig. S3F). Notably, at the same dosage, IL-2v-iMSCs showed a greater ability than wtIL-2-iMSCs to induce CD69 expression on T cells (Fig. 3I), suggesting that IL-2v modification provides specific activation benefits. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that although both engineered iMSC types exhibited unique activation profiles, wtIL-2-iMSCs conferred superior antitumor efficacy relative to IL-2v-iMSCs, primarily through strong CD25-dependent activation of CD8+ T cells.

wtIL-2-iMSCs achieve dose-sparing antitumor effects via enhanced CD25+CD8+ T-cell activation, outperforming recombinant IL-2 at equivalent concentrations

We next compared the bioactivity of wtIL-2-iMSCs with that of recombinant human IL-2 (rhIL-2) under standardized conditions. Normalization experiments showed that supernatants from 3 × 105 wtIL-2-iMSCs contained approximately 3 ng of IL-2 after 48 h, whereas 50 IU/mL rhIL-2 (a standard concentration) corresponded to approximately 26 ng (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). On the basis of these values, we established dose-matched groups for direct comparison. In A375 cells, wtIL-2-iMSCs at only 0.35 ng/mL significantly increased tumor cell death (from 5.4% to 17.6%), whereas, rhIL-2 was ineffective at the same concentration. At higher doses, wtIL-2-iMSCs (1 ng/mL) and rhIL-2 (8.75 ng/mL) each induced more than a twofold increase in cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A). Notably, wtIL-2-iMSCs achieved comparable efficacy at only approximately 11% of the rhIL-2 dose. Similar results were observed in HCC827 cells, whereas in 786-O cells, wtIL-2-iMSCs at 1 ng/mL produced effects that exceeded those induced by high-dose rhIL-2 (8.75 ng/mL) (Fig. 4B, C).

To further investigate the immunological basis of the dose-sparing antitumor effect, we next performed mechanistic analyses in the A375-PBMC coculture system (Fig. 4D-H). wtIL-2-iMSCs robustly upregulated CD25 expression on CD8+ T cells at both low and high doses. Notably, this effect remained stronger than that of rhIL-2 even when rhIL-2 was administered at an eightfold higher concentration. CD69, another activation indicator, showed a similar trend: compared with wtIL-2-iMSCs, rhIL-2 induced a significantly smaller increase in CD69 expression. These findings suggest that wtIL-2-iMSCs are more effective than rhIL-2 at promoting early T-cell activation. Although wtIL-2-iMSCs induced slightly higher Treg frequencies than rhIL-2 at equivalent doses (Fig. 4D), cytotoxic T-cell activity remained dominant, supporting a favorable therapeutic balance. However, compared with high-concentration rhIL-2, wtIL-2-iMSCs showed a more limited ability to increase Ki67 expression, highlighting functional differences between iMSC-secreted IL-2 and rhIL-2 (Fig. 4E).

Interestingly, wtIL-2-iMSCs also promoted expansion of a CD4brightCD8dim T-cell subset (Fig. 4F). More than 90% of these cells expressed high levels of CD25 (Fig. 4G), and flow cytometry analysis suggested gradual acquisition of CD8 expression within CD4+CD25+ population (Fig. 4G). These observations support a potential association between the CD4brightCD8dim subset and high CD25 expression. Previous studies have linked this subset to PMA-induced T-cell activation, although its precise function remains unclear [55, 56]. In our study, CD4brightCD8dim T cells contained a higher proportion of effector-like cells than either CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells, as indicated by an increased frequency of effector T cells and effector memory T cells (Fig. 4H). Together, these findings underscore the importance of CD25 in T-cell activation and effector differentiation and further support the ability of IL-2-iMSCs to induce CD25 expression at low doses.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that wtIL-2-iMSCs achieve dose-sparing antitumor effects relative to those of rhIL-2, driven by potent CD25-dependent CD8+ T-cell activation and the emergence of CD25high effector subsets.

Tumor-targeted wtIL-2 delivery via engineered iMSCs shows antitumor potential without overt systemic toxicity

We established a CT26 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model to evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy and safety of engineered iMSCs (Fig. 5A-J). The treatment and sample collection schedule is outlined in Fig. 5A. Bioluminescence imaging revealed preferential accumulation of wtIL-2-iMSCs at tumor sites within 24 h, whereas non-tumor-bearing controls showed diffuse systemic distribution (Fig. 5B). Ex vivo organ analysis confirmed marked enrichment of iMSCs in tumors relative to the heart, spleen, lung, and kidney (Fig. 5C), and these findings were further corroborated by histological analysis (Fig. 5D-E). Indeed, both blank-iMSCs and wtIL-2-iMSCs showed clear intratumoral GFP signals (Fig. 5E). Functionally, wtIL-2-iMSCs showed a trend toward reduced tumor burden relative to blank-iMSCs, with a lower mean endpoint tumor weight, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.08; Fig. 5F-H). The mean tumor burden in the wtIL-2-iMSCs group was also slightly lower than that in the high-dose rhIL-2 group. Comparison with the DPBS group did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.5), likely because of the high variability observed within the DPBS cohort, despite an apparent reduction in absolute tumor burden. Notably, this degree of tumor control was achieved despite an estimated 24-h IL-2 output of only approximately 100 IU from wtIL-2-iMSCs. In contrast, IL-2v-iMSCs did not appreciably inhibit tumor growth and showed a tumor burden similar to that of blank-iMSCs.

Toxicity assessment showed that 2 of 3 rhIL-2-treated mice developed severe pulmonary edema, whereas no overt edema was observed in the wtIL-2-iMSCs, IL-2v-iMSCs, Blank-iMSCs or DPBS groups. Lung wet weight tended to be higher in the rhIL-2 group than in the wtIL-2-iMSCs group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.16; Fig. 5I). Neither wtIL-2-iMSCs nor IL-2v-iMSCs induced significant hepatic or renal abnormalities (Fig. 5J; Additional file 2: Fig. S5). In contrast, previous studies have reported liver and kidney toxicity in mice receiving high doses of rhIL-2 (> 200,000 IU). The absence of such effects in our model likely reflects both the lower IL-2 dose and the reduced cross-reactivity of human IL-2 with murine T cells [57, 58].

Tumor-restricted CD25 amplification by engineered iMSCs enhances CD8+ T-cell effector function while limiting exhaustion within the TME

We next quantified changes in T-cell subsets in the tumors and peripheral tissues of CT26 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6A-F). In the spleen, neither wtIL‑2‑iMSCs nor rhIL‑2 induced statistically significant changes in Ki67, CD25, Treg frequency, or CD69 relative to controls (Additional file 2: Fig. S6A-D). In peripheral blood, rhIL‑2 produced the highest mean proportion of CD25⁺ T cells, consistent with its stronger systemic activity, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.13; Fig. 6A). In the tumor microenvironment (TME), however, a distinct profile emerged. wtIL-2-iMSCs significantly increased CD25 expression on tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6B) and enhanced their cytotoxic function, as indicated by elevated surface expression of CD107a, a marker of cytotoxic T-cell degranulation [59] (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, wtIL-2-iMSCs reduced the frequency of exhausted CD8⁺PD-1⁺TIM-3⁺ T cells than rhIL-2 [60] (Fig. 6D), while expanding the PD-1+CD25+ subset, which has been associated with tumor-specific cytotoxicity and regression (Fig. 6E) [40, 41]. Although a modest increase in number of intratumoral Tregs was observed (Fig. 6F), overall response was characterized by enhanced cytotoxic activity and a trend toward better tumor control, indicating that wtIL-2-mediated immune stimulation outweighed potential Treg induction. Collectively, these findings suggest a functional distinction between rhIL‑2 and wtIL‑2‑iMSCs: rhIL‑2 preferentially promotes peripheral T‑cell activation, whereas wtIL‑2‑iMSCs more effectively enhance CD25 expression on tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells within the TME, potentially sustaining local IL‑2 responsiveness.

To further investigate the immunological effects of wtIL‑2‑iMSCs treatment, we performed transcriptomic profiling of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells isolated from CT26 tumors. Differential expression analysis revealed distinct gene expression patterns in the wtIL‑2-iMSCs group relative to the DPBS-treated control group. GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted cytokine activity, cytokine-mediated signaling, immune system processes, and responses to stimuli (Fig. 6G). Heatmap analysis further showed that wtIL‑2-iMSCs induced a more pronounced transcriptional shift in genes related to T-cell receptor signaling, interleukins, chemokines, colony-stimulating factors, and adhesion molecules, whereas rhIL‑2 and blank-iMSCs induced only modest changes (Fig. 6H; Additional file 2: Fig. S6E). Collectively, these data indicate that wtIL‑2-iMSCs reshape the transcriptional program of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells in a manner consistent with enhanced local immune activation [61].

Engineered iMSCs with IL-2 integrated at the B3 locus exhibit stable and enhanced IL-2 secretion

Given that wtIL-2-iMSCs enhanced intratumoral T-cell activation but only partially suppressed tumor growth, we sought to further increase IL-2 output without increasing the cell dose, which could raise safety and manufacturing concerns. To this end, we leveraged the B3 locus, a noncoding region downstream of B2M that we previously identified as a superior site for targeted gene integration. Using CRISPR-Cas9, we precisely inserted the wtIL-2 cassette into the B3 locus of iPSCs, and achieved a knock-in efficiency of 60% without selection (Fig. 7A-C). Comparative analyses showed that the B3 locus consistently supported higher integration efficiencies for diverse transgene cassettes (~ 2 kb), with an average of ~ 50% and values reaching up to 80%, representing approximately a fourfold increase over the B2M locus (< 10%) (Fig. 7D). Importantly, B3-targeted iPSCs retained normal morphology, pluripotency, and a stable karyotype (Fig. 7E-G), supporting genomic stability. Integration of wtIL-2 or IL-2v driven by the EF1α promoter at the B3 locus (Fig. 7B-C; Additional file 2: Fig. S7A-D) markedly increased cytokine secretion. Peak IL-2 levels were more than 1,000-fold higher than those observed with B2M-targeted integration, increasing from approximately 700 pg to approximately 800 ng (Fig. 7H). Clones with moderate expression were selected for differentiation into iMSCs (Fig. 7I), which met standard MSC identity criteria, including expression of CD44, CD73, CD105, and CD90 (> 95%) and minimal expression of CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (< 5%) (Fig. 7J). Although IL-2 secretion decreased after differentiation relative to the iPSCs stage, it remained substantially higher than in B2M-targeted controls, with increases of up to 70-fold, and was stably maintained over serial passages (Fig. 7K-L). Together, these findings establish the B3 locus as an efficient and apparently well-tolerated genomic site for targeted integration, enabling stable and markedly elevated IL-2 secretion in iMSCs without compromising cellular identity or differentiation potential.

B3 locus-engineered wtIL-2-iMSCs enhance CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy

In the A375-PBMC coculture system, we assessed whether B3-driven enhancement of IL-2 expression could further improve tumor cell killing and alter immune phenotypes in cocultured PBMCs (Fig. 8A-D). Compared with IL-2v-iMSCs, B3-IL-2v-iMSCs increased A375 cell apoptosis. However, B3-driven enhancement of wtIL-2 expression did not further increase apoptosis relative to wtIL-2-iMSCs in this donor-derived assay (Fig. 8A), suggesting that the effect of lower-level wtIL-2 secretion may already have approached a near-saturating range under these conditions. A similar trend was observed in HCC827 cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S8A). Meanwhile, 786-O cells remained responsive to further increases in wtIL-2 output, showing additional enhancement of tumor cell death with B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs. Notably, wtIL-2-expressing iMSCs mediated greater cytotoxic effects than IL-2v-expressing clones despite lower overall secretion levels (Additional file 2: Fig. S8B).

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that high-expression wtIL-2-iMSCs did not further expand Tregs, whereas high-expression IL-2v clones induced robust Treg proliferation (Fig. 8B), indicating that the Treg-sparing effect of IL-2v may diminish at higher expression levels. Further analysis of CD25 expression patterns helped clarify the differences between wtIL-2 and IL-2v. In control PBMC cultures, CD8⁺ T cells showed a diffuse CD25 staining profile, with a modest fraction of CD25⁻ cells (~ 26%) that did not form a discrete negative population, and very few CD25bright cells (Fig. 8C). B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs induced a clear and compact CD25⁻ population (increasing to approximately 43%), likely reflecting epitope masking by bound wtIL-2, and also substantially expanded CD25bright CD8⁺ T cells (to approximately 21%). In contrast, IL-2v-iMSCs expanded CD25bright cells to an even greater extent (approximately 34%) but did not generate a compact CD25⁻ population, consistent with the reduced CD25-binding capacity of IL-2v (Fig. 8C). Together, these findings indicate that although both wtIL-2 and IL-2v can promote CD25 upregulation on CD8⁺ T cells in the presence of iMSCs, only wtIL-2, with preserved CD25 affinity, effectively translates to enhanced cytotoxic function. We also observed an increase in CD4brightCD8dim double-positive populations that paralleled CD25 upregulation, suggesting that strong IL-2/CD25 signaling may promote CD8 acquisition by activated CD4+ T cells (Fig. 8D). This population was detected at relatively lower frequencies in the HCC827 and 786-O coculture systems (Additional file 2: Fig. S8C and D), whereas it was most pronounced in cocultures with A375 melanoma cells, suggesting potential tumor-type specificity (Additional file 2: Fig. S8E).

In vivo, B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs with elevated IL-2 secretion significantly inhibited tumor growth, as evidenced by delayed tumor progression, decreased excised tumor weight (P < 0.05), while endpoint tumor volume showed a trend toward reduction (P = 0.0588) (Fig. 8E, F). Importantly, this enhanced antitumor efficacy was not associated with increased lung wet weight, suggesting no obvious increase in pulmonary edema (Additional file 2: Fig. S8F). However, IL-2v failed to control tumor growth, possibly indicating that the level or mode of immune activation induced by IL-2v was insufficient for tumor control in this model. We next investigated whether B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs could potentiate the response to anti-PD-1 therapy. In the MC38 tumor model, mice received anti-PD-1 monotherapy or anti-PD-1 combined with B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs according to the schedule shown in Fig. 8G. Compared with anti-PD-1 monotherapy, the combination treatment significantly reduced tumor volume at the final observation time point (Fig. 8H) and decreased excised tumor weight at the endpoint (Fig. 8I). Representative gross images of excised tumors were consistent with these quantitative findings, showing an overall reduction in tumor burden in the combination group relative to anti-PD-1 monotherapy (Fig. 8J).

Collectively, our results demonstrate that B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs exert potent antitumor activity and markedly improve the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 blockade, highlighting their promise as a cell-based immunotherapy platform for cancer treatment.

Discussion

In this study, we established engineered iMSCs with stable expression of wtIL-2 and IL-2v using a nonviral CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene-targeting approach. By integrating IL-2 expression cassettes into the B2M locus of iPSCs and subsequently differentiating them into iMSCs, we created a platform for safe and stable cytokine delivery. Furthermore, compared with B2M, relocating the integration site to the newly identified B3 locus resulted in substantially enhanced transgene expression, achieving up to 70-fold greater IL-2 secretion. This technical innovation effectively circumvents the potential risks associated with traditional viral vectors [62], providing a safer and more precise alternative for the potential clinical application of engineered iMSCs. In addition, compared with tissue-derived MSCs, iPSC-derived MSCs offer greater homogeneity, genetic stability, and proliferative potential, making them a more promising candidate for off-the-shelf allogeneic therapies. Functionally, this platform enabled sustained IL-2 delivery, revealed a clear therapeutic advantage of wtIL-2 over IL-2v in the iMSC context, and supported the use of engineered iMSCs to improve the response to immune checkpoint blockade.

Compared with IL-2v-iMSCs, wtIL-2-iMSCs showed stronger antitumor activity in most comparisons. In the in vivo wtIL-2-iMSCs monotherapy group, however, the endpoint tumor weight was not significantly different from DPBS, although a trend toward tumor inhibition was observed and the difference versus Blank-iMSCs approached significance (P = 0.08). When the IL-2 cassette was integrated into the B3 locus, B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs achieved a significantly stronger tumor-suppressive effect than Blank-iMSCs, indicating that, within a certain range, higher IL-2 expression can enhance therapeutic benefit. Compared with rhIL-2 at the tested dose and schedule, wtIL-2-iMSCs achieved a similar tumor growth inhibition while delivering a much lower cumulative IL-2 dose. Notably, wtIL-2-iMSCs alone did not induce complete tumor regression but instead partially slowed tumor growth and improved the local immune contexture. When combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, B3-wtIL-2-iMSCs further enhanced tumor control and reduced interindividual variability compared with anti-PD-1 monotherapy, supporting their value as a means to stabilize and potentiate checkpoint blockade rather than as a stand-alone curative treatment. Our findings further suggest that preserved CD25 binding capacity is critical for maximizing the therapeutic benefit of IL-2 delivery in this setting. Compared with rhIL-2, IL-2-iMSCs, including both wtIL-2 and IL-2v, significantly increased CD25 expression on CD8+ T cells in vitro. As an essential component of the high-affinity IL-2 receptor, CD25 markedly enhances T-cell sensitivity to IL-2 and may establish a positive feedback loop that promotes the proliferation, activation, and cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells [63]. In line with this interpretation, wtIL-2-iMSCs outperformed IL-2v-iMSCs in both in vitro and in vivo settings at both high and low doses. Notably, although IL-2v was originally designed to minimize Treg activation, we observed that IL-2v-iMSCs paradoxically induced significant Treg expansion at high concentrations. Together with recent evidence that reducing CD25 engagement may be insufficient to enhance efficacy [64, 65], our data highlight important limitations of current IL-2v designs. More broadly, by systematically comparing the effects of wtIL-2-iMSCs, IL-2v-iMSCs, and rhIL-2 across multiple settings, our study suggests that, in the context of iMSC-mediated delivery, retaining CD25 binding may be more advantageous than minimizing it.

The clinical application of IL-2 has long been limited by dose-limiting toxicity, which has hindered its widespread use in cancer therapy. Despite recent efforts by researchers to develop better IL-2 therapeutic strategies, no safe or efficacious drug has been approved for marketing thus far. In this study, combining wtIL-2 with iMSCs allowed us to leverage both the immune-regulatory characteristics and the inherent tumor-homing properties of iMSCs. In vivo imaging showed that intraperitoneally administered iMSCs preferentially accumulated at the tumor site, with signal intensity at tumors markedly exceeding that in several non-target organs. These findings support the idea that iMSCs can partially overcome the limitations of conventional systemic cytokine administration through targeted enrichment at tumors. Consistent with this concept, analysis of the TME revealed that wtIL-2-iMSCs preferentially activated intratumoral T cells and showed a trend toward promoting tumor cell killing, achieving tumor suppression numerically comparable to that of rhIL-2 at a substantially lower cumulative dose. In contrast, rhIL-2 predominantly stimulated immune cells in the peripheral tissues. Thus, iMSC-based delivery may not simply substitute for recombinant IL-2 administration, but may instead reshape its biodistribution and spatial immunologic activity in a therapeutically favorable manner. Under the conditions tested, wtIL-2-iMSCs also showed a potentially improved safety profile relative to high-dose rhIL-2. Administration of 50,000 IU rhIL-2 induced overt pulmonary edema in 2 out of 3 mice, whereas no such events were observed in the wtIL-2-iMSC group. Although quantitative lung wet-weight measurements did not reveal a statistically significant difference between the two groups, this qualitative difference suggests that localizing IL-2 activity through iMSCs may reduce pulmonary toxicity. Clinically, high-dose rhIL-2 is also associated with severe hepatic and renal toxicity; however, we did not observe obvious liver or kidney abnormalities in either treatment group. This discrepancy may partly reflect the limited cross-reactivity between human IL-2 and murine IL-2 receptors, which can lead to an underestimation of systemic toxicity in xenogeneic mouse models. Therefore, the safety profile of wtIL-2-iMSCs should be reassessed in models more relevant to humans before clinical translation.

To date, relatively few studies have explored IL-2 delivery via MSCs. Early work demonstrated the feasibility of this concept by using adenoviral transfection to enable adipose-derived MSCs to express IL-2 [38]. More recently, lentiviral gene modification was used to engineer mouse bone marrow-derived MSCs to express high levels of an IL-2 variant with reduced CD25 binding activity [39]. These studies showed that MSC-based delivery can increase intratumoral IL-2 exposure while reducing systemic toxicity. However, important limitations remain, including the potential safety risks of random viral integration, the restricted applicability of local administration to deep-seated or metastatic lesions, and the lack of direct comparisons between different IL-2 formats in the MSC context. Our study extends this field by combining CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeted integration with systemic iMSC administration and by directly comparing wtIL-2 and IL-2v in the same platform. Together, these advances provide a preclinical foundation for developing safer and more broadly applicable MSC-based IL-2 therapies.

Beyond efficacy, our findings also highlight the complexity of MSC immunomodulation. MSCs have been reported to suppress T-cell responses through factors such as TNF-β, IDO-1, and IL-10 [66, 67], and in our study, iMSCs likewise showed context-dependent effects that varied across donor PBMCs and tumor cell types. In some settings, iMSCs reduced PBMC-mediated tumor cytotoxicity. whereas in others they enhanced it. Despite this heterogeneity, one consistent feature across all the conditions was the upregulation of CD69 expression on CD8+ T cells, supporting an intrinsic priming effect that is consistent with previous reports [68–70]. Importantly, compared with blank-iMSCs, wtIL-2-iMSCs consistently promoted T-cell activation and enhanced tumor cytotoxicity across PBMCs from multiple donors and in cocultures with multiple tumor cell lines. These findings suggest that although the immunomodulatory effects of unmodified iMSCs are complex and context dependent, appropriate cytokine engineering can shift their net function toward a more robust antitumor phenotype. Nevertheless, this conclusion is currently limited to relatively short-term observations and additional tumor models will be needed to fully assess the long-term safety and stability of the engineered iMSCs.

An additional notable finding of this study is the identification of the B3 locus as a highly efficient integration site for iPSC engineering. Without changing nucleofection conditions or applying drug selection, targeted integration at B3 reached efficiencies of up to 80%, and similarly high efficiencies were observed across the integration of multiple exogenous cassettes larger than 1.5 kb. To our knowledge, few reported loci have achieved comparably high targeting efficiency under selection-free conditions. This exceptional targeting efficiency substantially facilitates the isolation of optimally engineered clones and may streamline the preclinical development of iMSC-based therapeutics. At the same time, although B3 locus targeting resulted in markedly elevated transgene expression in iPSCs without detectable adverse effects on the growth or major biological properties, this advantage was partially attenuated after MSC differentiation, indicating that locus activity may be cell type dependent [71, 72]. Moreover, the increase in IL-2 secretion after differentiation was not uniform across clones, suggesting that the final functional output in iMSCs cannot be fully predicted from expression levels in iPSCs alone. Thus, although the integration site clearly contributes to transgene performance, differentiation-associated regulatory changes and clonal heterogeneity are also likely to be important. In addition, because B2M expression itself has been reported to change during stem cell and MSC differentiation [73, 74], such variation may also influence regulation of the B2M-IL-2 cassette after differentiation and potentially after in vivo administration. Together, these observations suggest that the stability and activity of integration loci may vary across differentiation stages, and that alternative loci with more consistent performance warrant further investigation. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying the high integration efficiency and strong transgene expression observed at the B3 locus, as well as its long-term safety, will improve our understanding of locus-dependent transgene regulation and may facilitate the identification of more suitable integration sites for therapeutic engineering.

Several limitations should also be considered when interpreting this study. First, human iMSCs were administered to immunocompetent mice, where xenogeneic immune rejection would be expected [75]. This may affect immune cell composition within the tumor microenvironment and complicate the interpretation of the transcriptomic analyses. To reduce this confounding effect, we included Blank‑iMSCs as a control group, allowing us to subtract part of background influence attributable to the cells themselves. Nevertheless, the immune behavior of human iMSCs in an allogeneic clinical setting is likely to differ substantially from that observed in a xenogeneic mouse model [76, 77] and additional models will therefore be needed. Second, although we observed tumor-targeted accumulation of IL-2-iMSCs after systemic administration, this property may not be specific to IL-2-engineered cells, because iMSCs intrinsically possess tumor-homing capacity. While transcriptomic analysis suggested that IL-2 engineering may alter genes related to migration, homing, and immunomodulation, we did not directly compare the in vivo biodistribution of Blank-iMSCs and IL-2-iMSCs. The extent to which IL-2 modification itself affects iMSC trafficking thus remains unclear. Third, although our title emphasizes a “dose-sparing” strategy, we did not perform formal in vivo multi-dose pharmacokinetic analyses or directly measure IL-2 concentrations in tumor tissue and peripheral blood under different dosing regimens. Therefore, the dose-sparing potential of wtIL-2-iMSCs relative to rhIL-2 is currently inferred from expression and antitumor efficacy data rather than directly supported by in vivo PK evidence. Future studies should include systematic PK/PD analyses in larger animal cohorts to define how intratumoral IL-2 exposure at different cell doses and expression levels relates to efficacy and toxicity. These data will be important for rational dose selection in future clinical translation. Fourth, it should be noted that tumor volume may exhibit non‑linear or non‑monotonic changes during treatment, as early inflammation or iMSC infiltration can cause transient enlargement before cytotoxic effects reduce tumor burden. Our endpoint measurements do not capture this dynamic. Future studies using time‑resolved quantitative histology or molecular analysis of tumor composition are warranted to better resolve these kinetics.

For clinical translation, due to species compatibility, allogeneic or autologous iMSCs with lower immunogenicity may persist longer in the host [78], potentially increasing the risk of stromal remodeling, immunosuppressive effects, transition into cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-like cells, or other tumor-protective functions. To further improve safety, inducible suicide switches such as herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase could be incorporated into future engineered iMSC products [79]. Notably, we have identified several safe-harbor loci, including B2M and B3, that can accommodate multiple large exogenous gene cassettes without impairing normal cell function, making the introduction of safety switches and reporter genes is technically feasible. In addition, we have established preliminary GMP-compliant manufacturing processes and cell‑factory systems, laying the groundwork for clinical‑grade iMSC production. These efforts collectively move engineered iMSC-based immunotherapies closer to practical application. In this study, off-target integration was assessed by PCR using primers specific for predicted off-target loci. Although this approach allowed us to exclude unintended integration at predefined sites, it could not fully rule out rare events at unknown genomic locations. More comprehensive approaches, such as genome‑wide integration‑site mapping or whole‑genome sequencing anchored at vector–genome junctions, should therefore be applied in future studies to provide a more sensitive and global assessment of genomic safety [80]. Collectively, these findings establish a precise and scalable iMSC engineering strategy for localized IL-2 delivery and indicate that wtIL-2, rather than IL-2v, is the more effective cytokine format in this context. By combining targeted genome engineering, tumor-directed cell delivery, and checkpoint blockade sensitization, this work provides a strong preclinical rationale for the further development of wtIL-2-iMSC–based cancer immunotherapy.

Conclusions

We established a safe, efficient, and versatile platform for engineering iMSCs with stable IL-2 expression. In our preclinical models, wtIL-2-iMSCs mediated antitumor effects at reduced doses without overt systemic toxicity and further synergized with PD-1 blockade to enhance therapeutic efficacy. By combining precise genome editing, tumor-targeted delivery, and the immunostimulatory properties of wtIL-2, this strategy offers a potential approach to improving the therapeutic index of IL-2. Collectively, these findings provide a basis for the further translational development of IL-2-based therapies and support the use of engineered iMSCs as a platform for tumor immunotherapy.
