ABSTRACT
The human genome reference established a shared coordinate system for genome function, but it is incomplete and not fully representative of human diversity. Here, we benchmark how genome representation and corresponding analytical frameworks for each representation shape functional genomics using chromatin accessibility sequencing (ATAC-seq), RNA sequencing, whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, and chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data from lymphoblastoid cell lines derived from five individuals with fully phased genome assemblies. We compare results across hg38, CHM13, the draft human pangenome, and each individual’s maternal and paternal assemblies. Because current pipelines and quality control conventions are tuned to hg38, several of these comparisons reflect genome representation in the context of available methods, rather than sequence alone. Individual identity accounts for 57.52-78.47% of total variance in functional estimates, whereas genome choice contributes 0.002-7.85% and sample-by-genome interactions contribute 0.63-5.43%. About 2% of biological signals are detectable only with personal assemblies. Although these effects are modest overall, some biologically important features remain inaccessible to linear references. Consistent with this, graph-based DNA methylation analysis in the human pangenome reveals a non-reference AluY5a insertion within a putative TNKS enhancer at chromosome 8p23.1 that becomes visible and hypermethylated only in the pangenome.

Introduction

Genomes contain the complete set of instructions that define individuality, comprised of genes, regulatory regions, and other functional elements that collectively govern biological processes1–4. Understanding the sequence, structure, and function of genomes is crucial for uncovering the machinery of life and its breakdown in disease, traditionally relying on the human genome reference5.

This reference has provided a framework for identifying, locating, and coordinating genes and genomic features, forming the foundation for modern human disease research5–7. However, three key limitations of this single genome reference obscure important aspects of genome biology.

First, the human genome reference, built from a small number of individuals, does not capture the genetic diversity across global populations8–12. Second, it is missing regions of the genome10,13–15. Finally, no single cell is fully represented by this reference. Here, representativeness refers to how closely a genome reflects the true sequence and organization of the individual being studied. This has created an urgent need for an updated and comprehensive genomic resource16.

The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) addresses these limitations17 by creating a draft pangenome graph that integrates fully phased sequences from 47 individuals, including the complete CHM13 assembly18 and the hg38 reference. All but the hg38 assembly have a corresponding and commercially available cell line19–21.

Functional genomics, which studies the functions and interactions of genes and regulatory features, employs sequencing technologies such as ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, Hi-C, and WGBS to probe genomic function. In these assays, DNA and RNA are fragmented, chemically sequenced, mapped back to a genome, and used to infer functional outputs1,22–25 such as gene expression25, chromatin accessibility26–28, chromatin interactions24,29, and DNA methylation23,30. Mapping rates and read sequence content in libraries are also used to infer how well the assay was performed and by extension how much confidence one can place in resulting functional estimates31. However, such quality control (QC) metrics can rely on annotation sets and on assumptions about the structure and content of the assembly to which reads are aligned. Consequently, the interpretation of QC metrics is not independent of the chosen reference assembly, as different references may alter the apparent meaning of these metrics.

While the human genome reference has played a central role in these analyses, the extent to which genome representation and the analytical frameworks coupled to it impact functional genomics analysis remains unclear. Previous efforts have explored aspects of this question. Groza et al.32 examined the effects of using personalized linear and personalized graph references on short-read ChIP-seq assays, showing that individualized assemblies can improve signal interpretation for histone modification data. The draft Human Pangenome publication33 also assessed the impact of graph-based representations relative to hg38 on simulated read mapping and gene expression quantification across a subset of samples.

However, prior analyses have generally focused on individual assays or specific methodological questions and have not systematically benchmarked the relative magnitude or structure of effects arising from genome representation together with the analytical frameworks used to interrogate it across multiple functional genomics data types using collections of de novo fully phased assemblies34–37.

Notably, increasing representativeness may not be unilaterally beneficial if analytical methods are not adjusted to accommodate underlying differences. Representativeness necessarily entails deviation from the assumptions embedded in current analytical frameworks, including pipelines, annotations, and quality control interpretations, that were developed and tuned for hg38. As analyses transition from hg38 to complete assemblies, personalized genomes, and pangenomes, it becomes increasingly important to evaluate both the effect of genome representation itself and the effect of the analytical frameworks currently required for each representation, even though these contributions cannot be cleanly disentangled in current practice.

In this study, we leverage fully phased genomes from five individuals to systematically quantify how genome choice (hg38, CHM13, and the draft human pangenome) versus personal assemblies (maternal and paternal haplotypes) affects functional genomics results across ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, WGBS, and Hi-C assays (Fig. 1). These individuals were included in the draft pangenome33. Specifically, we evaluate how genome choice—in the context of the analytical frameworks coupled to each representation—influences QC metrics, read alignment, and functional outputs. Lastly, we examine how the resulting genome choice effects are distributed across regions of elevated genetic variation, where reference assumptions are most likely to break down.Fig. 1Overview of experimental design.In this study we utilize five samples from the 1000 genomes project for which we have fully phased and assembled genomes. Overview of experimental design.

In this study we utilize five samples from the 1000 genomes project for which we have fully phased and assembled genomes. We generate functional genomics data using RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, WGBS, and Hi-C assays. For each sample we analyze it using one of five genomes: hg38, CHM13, its maternal assembly, its paternal assembly, and the draft human pangenome. For each sample (in duplicates) of each assay type, we process and analyze how alignment, QC metrics, and functional outputs are affected by genome reference. We evaluate these effects both genome-wide and at local levels. We then characterize the locations of affected features and proximal genes. Lastly, we test this set of proximal genes for enrichment in biological processes.

In summary, our study characterizes the magnitude, variability, and context of genome choice effects, highlighting both the methodological considerations that arise when moving beyond hg38 and the biological insights that become accessible through more representative assemblies. Among these, transposable elements provide a particularly clear example: non-reference insertions cannot be effectively characterized using short-read data mapped to hg38, yet they contribute meaningfully to individual genomic and epigenomic landscapes when examined within personalized assemblies. Through this lens, we demonstrate both the need to reflect on methodological assumptions when adopting representative genomes and the opportunities for biological discovery that such assemblies make possible.

Results

Effects of genome choice on quality control and alignment rate metrics

QC metrics in functional genomics are often calculated from read alignments over specific genomic features used to evaluate quality control (QC features). These include, for example, promoter and transcription start site annotations used to assess ATAC-seq enrichment, ribosomal DNA annotations used to evaluate RNA-seq contamination, and the mitochondrial genome, which functions as a major QC feature because reads mapping to it do not represent accessible chromatin. Together, these QC features and the structural composition of the reference genome determine the observed QC and alignment metrics (Fig. 2A). ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, WGBS, and Hi-C data were processed using established pipelines (ENCODE for RNA-seq and Hi-C38, AIAP for ATAC-seq31, and Lee et al. for WGBS39), along with pangenomic methods where feasible40,41. These analyses produced a comprehensive set of QC and alignment metrics (Supplementary Data 1). To evaluate the relative influence of sample identity and genome reference on these metrics, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) using all quantitative QC and alignment metrics as input variables (Fig. 2B–E). This analysis summarizes how variance in quality control and alignment metrics across samples and references is structured, revealing whether between-sample differences in experimental quality or genome choice account for most of the observed variation. To quantify these effects, we used eta squared (ηp²) to partition variance among Sample, Genome, and Sample:Genome interaction terms (Supplementary Data 2), providing a measure of how much variation each factor contributes to the observed metric differences42.

Across assays, Sample identity accounted for most of the variance in QC and alignment metrics (67.46%, 35.86%, 55.20%, and 95.79% for ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, WGBS, and Hi-C, respectively)(Supplementary Fig. 1), while Genome choice explained a smaller but measurable fraction (11.60%, 27.62%, 13.01%, and 2.47%). The Sample:Genome interaction contributed comparatively little (3.2%, 3.08%, 6.16%, and 1.74%), consistent with the PCA showing that differences between individuals drive most of the variation.

We next tested whether the RNA-seq annotation set influenced alignment and QC metrics by analyzing data with GENCODE annotations43,44, lifted annotations45, or no annotations. Annotation choice significantly (ANOVA, FDR < 0.05) affected several STAR-reported metrics, including unmapped reads due to too many mismatches and splice-junction counts (AT-AC, GC-AG, GT-AG, annotated, non-canonical, and total), and several Picard metrics, including percent of bases overlapping coding exons, percent of bases overlapping untranslated regions, median coefficient of variation of coverage across transcripts, median 5’ coverage bias, median 3’ coverage bias, and median 5’-to-3’ coverage ratio (Supplementary Data 1). Genome by annotation interaction effects were not detected for STAR metrics and were limited to median 3’ coverage bias and median 5’-to-3’ coverage ratio for Picard metrics (Supplementary Data 1).

Two representative cases illustrate how structural differences among genome assemblies can strongly affect calculated QC and alignment metrics, even when the experimental data are identical.

In ATAC-seq, the mitochondrial contamination rate (%ChrM), a QC feature used to estimate the fraction of fragments derived from mitochondrial DNA, dropped from 18.5 to 5.5% for sample HG00621 when using the maternal assembly (Fig. 2F). For RNA-seq, the average total alignment rate decreased from 92.1 ± 1.6% with hg38 to 77.5 ± 3.5% with CHM13 (mean ± sd), accompanied by a corresponding increase in reads classified as unmapped due to alignment being too short in CHM13 relative to hg38 (Fig. 2G).

Effect of NuMT variation on ATAC-seq %ChrM contamination

The ATAC-seq mitochondrial contamination rate (%ChrM) estimates the fraction of sequenced fragments derived from mitochondrial DNA, which does not provide information about chromatin accessibility since mitochondrial DNA is not packaged into chromatin27,31. High %ChrM values therefore indicate a reduction in usable reads and can reflect lower library complexity or sample degradation31. Given the large decrease in %ChrM observed for HG00621 when aligned to the maternal assembly, we next examined what aspects of that assembly’s content or structure might explain this difference. In this pipeline, the %ChrM rate is calculated as the fraction of reads that map uniquely to the mitochondrial genome. To investigate the observed decrease in this metric, we identified reads that aligned uniquely to ChrM in other assemblies (hg38, CHM13, and the paternal assembly) but failed to do so in the maternal assembly. These reads instead multi-mapped between ChrM and Chr20 in the maternal assembly (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C), resulting in the apparent reduction in %ChrM for this sample. Visualization using the WashU Epigenome Browser46–48 confirmed that these multi-mapped reads correspond to a large insertion on Chr20 in the maternal assembly (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Comparison of the inserted sequence to the NCBI nucleotide database using49,50 showed 99.99% identity to the mitochondrial genome, confirming that it represents a large nuclear mitochondrial transfer (NuMT)51,52 specific to the maternal assembly of HG00621. We validated this NuMT using: (1) Bionano optical maps53,54, (2) confirmation of insertion break coverage in short-read whole genome sequencing data from the mother (HG00620), and (3) the absence of coverage over the insertion break point in the father’s sequencing data (HG00619) (Supplementary Fig. 2D–F).

Effect of genome choice on specific read alignment

We next examined how genome choice affects the alignment of individual reads. Reads were aligned to four assemblies (hg38, CHM13, maternal, and paternal) to assess how read placement changes across references. Hi-C data were excluded from this analysis because each properly generated read pair represents multiple ligated genomic loci rather than a single continuous fragment. This chimeric structure increases the number of possible mapping outcomes for each read, since either or both mates can align to different chromosomes or positions across assemblies. As a result, defining a single read fate or comparing read placement between genome choices becomes complex and impractical. To quantify the effect of genome choice on read alignment, we identified reads whose mapping fate (unmapped, uniquely mapped, or multi-mapped) differed between assemblies (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We next examined whether reads that were uniquely mapped in both the query assemblies (CHM13, maternal, paternal, and pangenome) and hg38 (reference) were placed consistently in the same positions. To compare read positions across assemblies, we used liftover to translate coordinates from each query assembly to hg3855. Between 96.5 and 99.05% of reads that were uniquely mapped in both assemblies could be successfully lifted over (Fig. 3B). Among all reads, 98.5–99.99% were placed on the same chromosome before liftover, and 98.6–99.5% remained on the same chromosome after liftover (Fig. 3C). Of those that mapped to the same chromosome after liftover, 98.75–99.35% were placed at the identical genomic position in both assemblies (Fig. 3D).

Accurate representation of repetitive regions breaks alignment

Across all samples, RNA-seq data aligned to CHM13 showed consistently lower total alignment rates (Supplementary Data 1) and a higher fraction of reads that changed fate relative to hg38 (Fig. 3A). Despite these differences in read fate, positional concordance remained high among reads that mapped uniquely in both assemblies (Fig. 3D). Although CHM13 is the most complete human assembly to date, it consistently showed lower RNA-seq alignment rates across samples (Fig. 2A). To investigate this pattern, we examined how individual reads changed fate between hg38 and CHM13. Most reads that changed fate were multi-mapped in hg38 but unmapped in CHM13 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). STAR alignment statistics showed that unmapped reads increased from 7.86 ± 1.62% in hg38 to 22.45 ± 3.52% in CHM13. Reads classified as too-short increased from 7.41 ± 1.59% in hg38 to 17.92 ± 3.06% in CHM13, and reads marked as other increased from 0.04 ± 0.01% in hg38 to 3.69 ± 0.44% in CHM13 (Supplementary Fig. 4). To identify the source of these unmapped reads, we traced the positions of reads that were unmapped in CHM13 but multi-mapped in hg38, which localized to a region on Chromosome 21 corresponding to an rDNA array. Ribosomal RNA contamination rates were estimated using bbDUK56 to determine whether differences in rRNA content could account for the reduced alignment rates (Supplementary Data 1). These estimates showed that the lower alignment rates in CHM13 were largely explained by ribosomal contamination (R² = 0.85 for too-short and R² = 0.64 for other). A comparison of the rDNA region on Chr20 between CHM13 and hg38 revealed that CHM13 contains 52 rDNA copies, compared to only three in hg38 (Supplementary Fig. 4H–K). Among the individual assemblies, rDNA copy number ranged from 0 to 8. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that STAR’s seed-and-extend alignment strategy57 struggles with large copy number variation, such as the rDNA repeats in CHM13. To test this hypothesis, we reprocessed the data using Subjunc and BBMap, which use a seed-and-vote rather than a seed-and-extend strategy58,59. These aligners yielded RNA-seq alignment rates for CHM13 comparable to hg38 (Supplementary Fig. 4A), indicating that STAR’s extension step is disrupted by the high rDNA copy number in CHM13. To confirm that copy number drives this effect, we generated a modified hg38 with its rDNA array expanded to 52 copies, matching CHM13. Alignment to the expanded hg38 produced rates and unmapped-read patterns comparable to CHM13 (Supplementary Fig. 4B–G). This confirms that the high-resolution rDNA array in CHM13 challenges the seed-and-extend alignment process. These results highlight the need to revisit the tuning of alignment algorithms while maintaining awareness of how genome structure and content variation interact with both the assay being performed and the biological question of interest.

Effect of genome choice on linear genome functional outputs

Because functional genomic signals are quantified from read alignments, mapping to more complete or individualized assemblies may directly influence downstream estimates of genomic function.

To quantify these effects, we compared assay-specific functional signals, including chromatin accessibility for ATAC-seq, gene expression for RNA-seq, cytosine methylation for WGBS, and chromatin interactions for Hi-C, across genome references using the same set of reads. Functional outputs were classified as thresholded annotations (e.g., ATAC-seq peaks, Hi-C loop calls) or continuous values (e.g., gene expression, DNA methylation).

To quantify the effects of genome choice on continuous-value outputs, we performed PCAs for each assay (ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and WGBS) to summarize overall variance structure (Supplementary Fig. 5). We then used η² to partition this variance among Sample, Genome, and Sample:Genome terms (Supplementary Data 2), where η² represents the amount of variance attributable to a term reflects thus the overall effect that term has on the data. Across assays, Sample identity accounted for 68.28%, 57.52%, and 78.47%, Genome choice contributed 1.26%, 7.85%, and 0.002%, and Sample:Genome interaction explained 5.43%, 0.63%, and 1.55% for ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and WGBS, respectively. These values show that genome choice effects are modest but measurable, varying across assays in proportion to their overall variance structure.

Variation between individuals was the largest source of difference across assays, consistent with biological rather than reference effects. Replicate consistency remained high for all data types. ATAC-seq peak reproducibility ranged from 77.98 to 84.24% using a SignalValue threshold of ≥8. RNA-seq replicates were highly correlated across genome choices (R² = 0.849–0.893), and unscaled gene expression showed strong genome-to-genome correlation (R² = 0.996–0.999) (Supplementary Data 4).

For RNA-seq, 33.89% of transcripts with transcripts per million greater than 1 had more than 20% of expression variance explained by Sample, 0.32% by Genome, and 1.29% by Sample:Genome interaction (Supplementary Fig.s 6A-D). Transcripts with larger genome-choice effects tended to have lower abundance, whereas sample effects were not related to abundance (Supplementary Fig. 6E–H).

For WGBS, we identified 2289 ± 140 differentially methylated regions (DMRs)60 when comparing methylation profiles generated from the same data using hg38 versus the corresponding individual genome (methylation difference of ≥0.25, Supplementary Data 5). These DMRs represent reference-dependent differences between syntenic regions rather than biological variation.

At a high level, functional landscapes constructed using different genome choices vary visibly, reflecting both structural and sequence-level differences. Using the comparative genome visualization function of the WashU Epigenome Browser61, we compared the combined chromatin accessibility, RNA-seq coverage, and CpG methylation landscapes of HG00621 across the short arm of chromosome 8 (Fig. 4A). Within this region, we examined the structural and functional context of chromosome 8p23.1, where large-scale variation is evident among assemblies. Comparison of the paternal, maternal, and CHM13 assemblies to hg38 reveals two highly similar β-defensin arrays that flank a large intervening segment. In CHM13, breakpoints occur within each array, and the intervening segment is inverted relative to hg38 (Fig. 4B–D). These relationships are summarized schematically in Fig. 4E. Zooming into one of these arrays at the DEFB4B neighborhood (Fig. 4F), we observed that genome choice alters multiple modalities of the reconstructed functional landscape. Expression of the DEFB4B gene, local ATAC-seq accessibility, and CpG methylation patterns all differ depending on the reference used. CpG sites present in the individual assemblies are absent from the linear references, producing individual reference-specific unmethylated sites. Alignability within the DEFB4B gene body and at the nearby accessibility feature showing a genome choice effect is lowest in hg38, intermediate in the paternal assembly, and highest in the maternal assembly. These alignability differences coincide with loci that correspond to many variants in the HPRC pangenome graph, indicating that the same regions are polymorphic across individuals. Because interpretation of chromatin architecture and expression depends on the accuracy of the underlying structure, an incorrect structural model can distort the apparent relationships among features, as seen in CHM13 where the inversion of the intervening segment shifts the placement of Hi-C loop calls. While regions such as the β-defensin locus show clear local differences between genome choices, genome-wide we observed overall consistency in the number of functionally annotated features. ATAC-seq peak calls and topologically associated domain boundaries were highly concordant across genome choices (Supplementary Data 3 and 6). At a SignalValue threshold of ≥8, which represents a signal-to-noise enrichment score for ATAC-seq peaks, 90.1 ± 1.39% of peaks were shared across assemblies, with only 1.3 ± 0.11% specific to an individual assembly. Increasing the threshold to ≥16 raised the proportion of individual-specific peaks slightly to 1.6 ± 0.27%. The impact of hg38 blacklisted regions, defined by the ENCODE project as genomic regions prone to artifactually high signal or unreliable mapping, was minimal, with 0.13% of peaks unique before SignalValue filtering and 0.01% unique after applying the threshold. The resulting shift in the total number of annotated regulatory or structural features is therefore modest, but, as the β-defensin example suggests, the effects of genome choice are not random and appear concentrated in specific, highly variable regions rather than distributed uniformly across the genome.

Differences in representation of genome structure have a notably larger impact on Hi-C loop calling. Of loop anchors called using parental assemblies, 14.6–20.3% were not detected at the same site (after liftover) using reference assemblies (Supplementary Fig. 7). This discrepancy reflects the greater fragility of Hi-C loop detection compared to other genomic features. Unlike ATAC-seq peaks, which are single-locus signals tolerant of small coordinate differences, Hi-C loops depend on enrichment between two genomic loci, amplifying the impact of assembly mismatches. In addition, Hi-C reads are often shorter and chimeric due to ligation junctions and restriction fragment constraints, making them more sensitive to sequence divergence and reference-specific mappability. As a result, loop anchors are more susceptible to alignment perturbations or structural discrepancies between genome representations, leading to reduced concordance despite high liftover success.

Genome choice effects are enriched in regions of elevated variation

The enrichment of immune, cell surface, and neuronal processes among affected genes show that genome choice effects are not random but instead occur preferentially in biological contexts already known to vary across individuals. We next asked whether this pattern reflects the underlying rates of natural genetic variation across the genome as one should expect. Specifically, we tested whether genes directly affected by genome choice, or those near affected features, are preferentially located in regions of elevated genetic variation, reasoning that structural and sequence divergence from the reference should drive localized differences in functional estimates.

To test this, we divided the genome into non-overlapping 24 kb bins (the median gene length) and used variant calls from the individuals included in the draft human pangenome to identify regions enriched for variation relative to hg3833. We quantified the number of unique SNPs, small indels (≤50 bp), and structural variants (SVs) per bin and compared these counts to null distributions generated by permutation within each chromosome. Regions with FDR ≤ 0.005 were considered enriched for variation (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Data 8). At this threshold, 10.1% of bins were enriched for SVs, 6.9% for SNPs, and 10.1% for small indels. The overlap among variant classes was non-random, with regions enriched for one variant type more likely to be enriched for other variant types (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 9).

We next asked whether genes directly affected by genome choice, or those near features showing genome choice effects, were preferentially located in regions enriched for natural variation. Genes in the affected set were significantly more likely to fall within regions enriched for SNPs, indels, or structural variants than expected by chance (p < 0.001), regardless of assay type or variant class (Fig. 7A–E, Supplementary Data 7). In contrast, genes not in the affected set did not show enrichment in regions of elevated variation. Comparing linear methods to an early pangenomic paradigm

The regions identified as enriched for genetic variation highlight the types of sequence diversity that the pangenome was designed to capture. By incorporating the variation present across multiple individuals into a single graph-based reference, the pangenome provides a more representative model of human genomic diversity than any linear assembly. To address the challenges that natural variation introduces for functional genomic analyses, we next compared graph-based methods implemented using the draft human pangenome to traditional linear approaches. This comparison allows us to assess where pangenomic analyses improve representation and functional estimation, where current limitations remain, and what unique value these approaches offer relative to linear reference frameworks.

We compared functional genomics outputs derived from individual genome assemblies and two linear references (hg38 and CHM13) to those generated using the draft human pangenome. Because the pangenome is graph-based, it requires specialized tools distinct from those used for linear references. ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data were analyzed using graph-aware methods. Although a graph-based workflow for WGBS exists and is implemented later in this study, that approach was previously benchmarked for this question in its original publication, and WGBS was therefore not re-evaluated here. Hi-C data were analyzed only under linear frameworks, as graph-compatible tools for this assay are not yet available.

For ATAC-seq, the Target-2 AIAP pipeline31 was adapted for pangenomic analysis by replacing the BWA-MEM aligner with vg Giraffe. Alignments generated in graph space were then surjected to hg38 coordinates. Surjection converts graph-based alignments into linear coordinates by anchoring each read to graph segments shared with the target linear reference and locally realigning the portions that fall outside those shared regions to their corresponding syntenic positions. This approach allows reads aligned within the graph to be represented in the coordinate system of a linear reference while preserving the positional relationships defined by the graph alignment.

Quality control metrics illustrate how read classifications shift when moving from a single linear reference to the graph (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Data 1). Among reads that mapped uniquely in hg38, 6.32 ± 0.60% became unmapped in the pangenome and 3.40 ± 0.58% became multi-mapped. The overall proportion of uniquely mapped reads was slightly lower in the pangenome (84.5 ± 1.21%) than in individual assemblies (86.5 ± 6.14%), with a modest decrease in multi-mapped reads as well (6.82 ± 1.12% vs. 8.01 ± 5.09%). The overall rate of read fate changes was larger for pangenome-hg38 comparisons than for parental genome-hg38 comparisons (Fig. 3A). Together, these results indicate that incorporating alternate haplotypes and structural diversity in the graph redistributes reads among mapping categories, resolving local mapping ambiguity at the cost of a modest decrease in overall mapping rate. Consistent with this interpretation, alignment quality improved under the pangenomic framework, with lower mismatch rates (0.34 ± 0.05% vs. 0.39 ± 0.05% in hg38) and higher ungapped-alignment rates (97.24 ± 0.30% vs. 93.94 ± 1.75% in hg38).

When comparing downstream functional outputs, the pangenome produced a slightly lower number of chromatin accessibility peaks (103,943 ± 9215) than hg38 (108,944 ± 9797) (Supplementary Data 3). Supplementary Fig. 11 shows that peaks identified as specific to either reference generally reflect modest changes in local signal rather than unique events. In both directions, peaks retained substantial coverage, with the pangenome either boosting support just above the calling threshold or lowering it slightly below. These threshold-crossing effects indicate that pangenome-associated differences in peak calls arise from small redistributions of coverage at similar loci, not from the appearance or disappearance of distinct accessibility sites. To characterize where such changes occur, we classified peaks into four categories based on how their signal changed when using the pangenome (Supplementary Data 10). Peaks missing across all individuals (n = 743) represent sites where accessibility consistently dropped below the detection threshold, whereas those missing in only a subset of individuals (n = 18,584) indicate variable reductions in coverage. Peaks appearing in at least one individual after pangenome alignment (n = 11,808) correspond to loci where signal was modestly boosted above the threshold, while a very small number (n = 20) were uniquely detected with the pangenome. Dimensionality reduction of genome-wide accessibility patterns showed consistent shifts between samples analyzed with the pangenome and linear references (Fig. 4A).

We used HOMER to quantify the enrichment or depletion of each peak set across genomic annotations. All major groups were concentrated in gene-proximal sequence and depleted from repetitive regions, indicating that the overall genomic context of accessible sites is preserved. Promoters remained strongly enriched, with observed/expected (obs/exp) ratios of 4.0x, 4.2x, and 3.7x across the fully missing, partially missing, and pangenome-boosted sets, respectively. CpG islands showed progressively stronger enrichment with increasing missingness (pangenome-boosted ~6x; partially missing ~8x; fully missing ~11x). Exons, transcription termination sites (TTS), and introns were modestly enriched in pangenome-boosted and partially missing peaks (exons = 1.6x and 1.4x; TTS = 1.3x and 1.3x; introns = 1.2x and 1.2x) but neutral or depleted among fully missing peaks (exons = 0.3x). Intergenic regions were consistently depleted (0.7x in pangenome-boosted and partially missing sets) and near neutral in the fully missing group (0.96x). Repetitive elements were uniformly depleted, with satellites showing the strongest depletion (0.04x, 0.02x, and 0.17x across the three sets) and LINEs and SINEs remaining below background (0.4–0.7x).

Together, these results show that pangenome alignment most often alters peak detection by slightly reweighting signal within existing regulatory neighborhoods. Most peak differences reflect small coverage shifts around shared loci rather than true creation or loss of distinct accessibility sites.

For RNA-seq, we used a graph-based pipeline optimized for pangenomic analysis. Because no Picard-like tool yet exists for graph-space quality control, direct comparison of some QC metrics was not possible. However, alignment-level statistics are directly comparable across assemblies and were therefore used to assess performance (Supplementary Data 1). To compare read fates between references, alignments generated in graph coordinates were surjected to hg38. Surjection converts graph-based alignments into linear coordinates but is not perfectly reversible, as some reads aligned entirely within graph-specific regions cannot be projected back onto hg38. Consequently, part of the observed shift in read fates reflects technical loss during surjection rather than biological differences in mapping. Alignment to the pangenome reduced the number of unmapped reads relative to all other assemblies, with 4.01 ± 0.98% and 3.61 ± 0.77% of reads shifting to uniquely or multi-mapped categories, respectively, when compared to hg38 (Supplementary Fig. A-B). This resulted in an overall 14.8 ± 3.08% change in read fate (Fig. 3A). Among reads that were uniquely mapped in both references, positional concordance was high: 95.23% were placed at the identical position in both, and 99.14% mapped within 16 bp of their hg38 coordinate (Fig. 3D), indicating strong spatial agreement despite the added haplotypic and structural complexity represented in the graph. Pangenome-based alignments showed higher concordance at the chromosome level but lower exact positional concordance after liftover to hg38 (Fig. 3). This reflects technical properties of graph alignment and surjection rather than conflicting mapping behavior. Reads aligned in graph coordinates are projected onto linear coordinates during surjection, which can introduce small positional shifts when alignments traverse alternative haplotypic paths or graph-specific sequence. Consequently, reads may liftover to the same chromosome while differing at the exact genomic coordinate. In addition, pangenome alignment reduces the inclusion of low-quality or ambiguously mapped reads in structurally complex regions, further affecting positional concordance after liftover.

Despite the shift in read fates observed with the pangenome, the resulting genome-wide coverage profile remained highly concordant with hg38 (R2 = 0.96), indicating that overall read distribution across transcripts and genomic regions is preserved. Per-transcript abundance estimates were reproducible across technical replicates in both frameworks but showed slightly lower between-replicate concordance in the pangenomic runs (R2 = 0.849) compared to linear assemblies (R2 = 0.892). Comparison between RSEM (linear) and RPVG (pangenomic) quantification yielded R2 values of 0.758–0.763. The reduced correlation between methods, despite highly concordant coverage profiles, reflects differences in how abundance is modeled. RPVG estimates transcript abundance in graph space, where multiple haplotype-specific isoforms must be considered simultaneously, whereas RSEM quantifies expression relative to a single linear transcriptome. As a result, the two approaches differ systematically in how they allocate reads across overlapping or structurally variable isoforms, producing lower cross-method concordance even when read placement itself is nearly identical (Supplementary Fig. 6). Principal component analysis of expression profiles further confirmed that while the pangenomic approach introduces a systematic shift relative to linear methods, the relative relationships among samples are preserved genome-wide, indicating that the overall biological structure of the data is maintained (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Characterizing methylation of non-reference transposable elements

WGBS relies on short-read alignments to a reference genome, rendering it effectively blind to the methylation state of sequence absent from that reference. Reads originating within non-reference transposable elements cannot be placed correctly because the insertion sequence is missing, preventing methylation profiling of these loci. Long-read sequencing has been shown to recover such information by spanning non-reference insertions and directly capturing methylation within them63. The pangenome resolves this limitation for short-read data by embedding non-reference insertions, including transposable elements, within its graph structure, enabling both alignment and methylation quantification for sequence that linear references lack.

Using Methylgrapher, we aligned WGBS reads to the HPRC pangenome, which directly calls CpG methylation on the graph data structure64. To relate these calls to individual genomes, we then performed haplotype-specific surjection of methylation annotations, linearizing the graph-based methylation data along each individual’s two haplotype paths through the graph rather than to hg38 coordinates. This approach preserves haplotype-specific sequence context and allows direct quantification of methylation for both reference and non-reference transposable elements (Fig. 8A). Discussion

We set out to quantify how genome representativeness shapes functional genomics, both in what becomes measurable and in how current analytical methods behave when the sequence content and structure of the genome become more representative. We observe the impact of genome choice on read handling in a given region depends on the interaction of two factors: (1) the sequence dissimilarity between the individual’s genome and the reference, and (2) the variation in how that region relates to the rest of the genome (e.g., copy number variation, NuMTs). Both variation within a feature and variation in its genomic context can lead to altered read handling, which in turn translates to differences in functional estimates. The amount of variation attributable to these phenomena is a function of the feature’s relationship to genetic variation, its size, and the overall number of such features in the genome.

Greater completeness and population diversity make it possible to recover sequence and signal that are missing in hg38, yet pipelines and QC metrics tuned to hg38’s content and structure can shift when those assumptions no longer hold. The intent here is not to present representativeness as a universal and unqualified improvement, but to treat genome representation as an experimental variable whose effects are expressed through both the reference itself and the analytical frameworks currently available for it. In that sense, several of the benchmarks in this study are best interpreted as comparisons of genome representation in the context of the methods used to analyze each representation, rather than of sequence alone in complete isolation.

The practical consequences are clearest in the specific cases we analyzed. NuMT content altered the ATAC-seq mitochondrial contamination metric by changing which reads counted as uniquely mitochondrial and fully resolved rDNA arrays in CHM13 reduced STAR’s RNA-seq alignment rate by stressing its seed-and-extend strategy. These are not intractable problems. They are predictable outcomes once genome content diverges from the expectations built into a method, and they are readily addressed by parameter tuning or aligner choice. The broader lesson is that as references become more representative and individualized, pipelines must be examined for hidden dependencies on the structure and copy number landscape of the genome under analysis.

On the other hand around 2% of accessible chromatin regions are only detectable when using an individual’s personal genome. While generally the genome-wide magnitude of genome-choice effects on functional estimates is modest (1–6% of sample variation), their distribution is not uniform. They occur preferentially in regions already known to harbor elevated genetic variation and structural complexity, including loci enriched for immune, cell-surface signaling, and neuronal functions. It is well established that both mutational processes and the biological processes most affected by them are non-uniformly distributed across the genome. Our results connect these established observations to the behavior of functional genomics pipelines, showing that genome choice effects concentrate where variation and biological sensitivity already coincide. In such regions, increased representational completeness can improve not only the recovery of reads but also the effective resolution of repetitive sequence. By introducing population variation at precisely the loci where hg38-based analyses lose signal, the pangenome increases local variant density and with it, short-read mappability. In Fig. 4, the variant-density histogram and short-read mappability track illustrate this coupling between diversity and resolution at the beta-defensin (DEFB4B) locus. Beta-defensin clusters harbor substantial genetic variation65. This improvement is an emergent property of representational completeness: accumulated variants act as landmarks that break repeat driven mapping ambiguity and sharpen quantitative estimates in regions where linear references struggle.

At the same time, the inclusion of additional closely related sequences means that using a pangenome can produce subtle shifts in read placement between similar sequences, as we observe when analyzing ATAC-seq data with the pangenome.

Despite these advantages, there are important limitations to current pangenomic methods. The analytical framework is still incomplete, and in several cases exact comparisons are not yet possible. For most applications, analyses performed on the pangenome must ultimately be surjected to linear coordinates in order to interface with existing tools, and surjection is an imperfect and non-lossless process unless the path of a given individual through the graph is known in advance, as it is for the transposable element analysis presented here. This leads to a small decrease in usable reads. Furthermore, surjection can introduce a small shift in alignment position. In addition, key methodological components remain to be developed, including fully on-graph approaches for ATAC-seq and Hi-C analyses.

Comparisons involving RNA-seq are particularly challenging because multiple factors vary simultaneously. To align reads to the pangenome, one must also change the aligner, introducing algorithmic differences that are independent of genome sequence. On top of this, annotations must be projected through graph space onto non-reference paths, adding another small but unavoidable source of difference. Finally, transcript abundance must be estimated using a model that operates on graph-based alignments and behaves distinctly from those used by linear methods. As a result, it is not possible to isolate the effect of genome sequence representation entirely. Instead, we focus on whether relative relationships among samples within a given analytical framework are preserved, because with the current tooling an exact one-to-one comparison across representations cannot yet be achieved.

Even within these constraints, the pangenome enables access to functional information that a reference simply cannot capture. Using a graph-based approach, we recovered DNA methylation patterns within non-reference transposable elements, revealing molecular features that are invisible in hg38. Across individuals, non-reference Alu elements show higher body methylation than reference-mapped Alus, whereas L1 elements show no consistent difference. For both families, methylation in flanking sequence is largely unchanged, indicating that the observed hypermethylation is intrinsic to the inserted sequence rather than a local perturbation of surrounding chromatin in most cases.

A representative case occurs at 8p23.1, where a non-reference AluY5a lies within an intronic enhancer of TNKS. Mapping to hg38 yields sparse CpGs and limited methylation signal because the inserted sequence is missing from the reference, while mapping to the individual assembly and to the pangenome with haplotype-specific surjection reveals dense CpG coverage and strong hypermethylation across the element. The element itself is heavily methylated, suggesting that its insertion may disrupt a previously active regulatory element. However, public data from GTEx and ENCODE indicate that this enhancer is primarily active in brain and neuron-related tissues, with little to no activity in lymphoblastoid cells. This distinction means that the enhancer’s regulatory role occurs in a tissue context different from that of our functional data, and in lymphoblastoid cells the enhancer and its embedded Alu insertion are hypermethylated, consistent with transcriptional silencing in this environment.

These results highlight how representational completeness directly alters the scope of possible biological inference. By including non-reference TEs, the pangenome framework captures a class of repetitive variation that contributes substantially to individual epigenomic diversity. At the same time, they demonstrate that functional estimates in such regions are sensitive to how sequence identity and genomic context are represented. The capacity to quantify methylation in inserted sequence therefore exemplifies both the promise and the methodological challenge of adopting individualized or population references: what was previously unmeasurable becomes visible.

Representational gains enable direct measurement in previously inaccessible sequence and can improve repeat resolution where it matters most, while the same changes expose the assumptions and limits of methods built around hg38. Realizing the benefits of individualized and pangenomic references therefore requires representation-aware practice: parameters tuned to genome content, algorithmic choices cognizant of copy number and sequence diversity, and QC frameworks calibrated to assemblies that differ from hg38 in both structure and completeness.

The selection criteria for these cell lines were based on their diverse or admixed genetic ancestries to encompass a spectrum of human genetic variation. Genome assembly and scaffolding

The genome assemblies used in this study were produced for the draft human pangenome, combining PacBio HiFi long-read and Illumina short-read sequencing for de novo assembly with parental short-read data used to for phasing of these assemblies. Phased contig assembly was performed using hifiasm66. Post-assembly, the RagTag67 toolset was employed for reference-guided scaffolding, organizing contigs into structured, phased chromosomal assemblies (Supplementary Data 9). Validation was performed using BioNano optical mapping53,54, to detect and amend potential assembly inaccuracies. Alignment to the hg38 reference genome was conducted with minimap255, facilitating the liftover of annotations and enabling comparative analysis to refine the assemblies. This methodology, derived from the efforts to construct a draft human pangenome, emphasizes the generation of accurate and comprehensive genomic assemblies to support our study’s goals.

Alignment of assemblies to hg38

Post-assembly, each individual genome was aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38). This critical step employed minimap255, chosen for its efficiency in handling long-read alignments. The alignment process was initiated with the command:

minimap2 -x asm5 -a -L --cs=long -t 16./ref/hg38.fa $f -o ${f%_ragtag.fasta}.hg38.sam;paftools.js sam2paf -p -L ${f%_ragtag.fasta}.hg38.sam > ${f%_ragtag.fasta}.hg38.paf

This generated PAF files which are used to liftover genomic bins and annotations, essential for comparisons across assemblies. Furthermore, they enable visualization and comparison of data regardless of assembly using the WashU Epigenome Browser48. PAF files are available[https://wangftp.wustl.edu/~xzhuo/pangenome_alignments/].

Gene annotations preparation

Gene Transfer Format (GTF) files for this study were generated using Liftoff version 1.6.3.645. Liftoff, designed for annotating novel genome assemblies, aligns known annotations from a reference genome (hg38) to a target genome. In our case, target genome assemblies included both maternal and paternal diploid genomes, provided in FASTA format. The process began with preparing input files: FASTA files of the target genome assemblies and reference annotations (GRCh38 FASTA and Gencode v29 GTF)43. Liftoff then mapped these reference annotations to the target genome assemblies. This method allows for the precise transfer of gene structures, such as exons, introns, and other genomic elements, from the reference to the target genomes, accommodating sequence variances. The execution of Liftoff produced individual GTF files for each genome assembly, which were then employed in subsequent analyses.

ATAC-seq library preparation and sequencing

For ATAC-seq library preparation, cells from each LCL were harvested upon reaching confluence, with cell numbers ranging from approximately 100,000 to 200,000. The cells were centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully aspirated, and the cell pellets were resuspended and lysed in 100 µl of ATAC-seq RSB (10 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2) containing 0.1% NP-40, 0.1% Tween-20, and 0.01% Digitonin. This mixture was pipetted up and down before incubation on ice for 3 min, facilitating cell membrane disruption while preserving nuclear integrity.

Subsequently, 1 mL of ATAC-seq RSB with 0.1% Tween-20 was added to the lysate, mixed gently, and nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and nuclear pellets were carefully resuspended in 25 µl of 2x TD buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 10 mM MgCl2, 20% Dimethyl Formamide). The quality and quantity of isolated nuclei were assessed using trypan blue exclusion under a microscope.

For the transposition reaction, 25 µl of transposition mix (2.5 µl of Tn5 Transposase, 16.5 µl of PBS, 0.5 µl of 1% digitonin, 0.5 µl of 10% Tween-20, and 5 µl of nuclease-free water) was added to the nuclei. This mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, with gentle tapping every 10 min to ensure mixing. The reaction was purified using Zymo DNA Clean and Concentrator-5 columns according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Library amplification was performed by adding Nextera primers to the transposed DNA and cycling the reaction for 9 cycles (R1 TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG, R2 GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG). Post-amplification, libraries were cleaned using Sera-Mag Select beads with a double size selection protocol to ensure the removal of unwanted fragments. Final ATAC-seq libraries were quantified using Qubit fluorometric quantitation. Sequencing of the paired-end libraries was conducted on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform.

ATAC-seq pipeline

In this study, we adapted the ATAC-seq Integrated Analysis Pipeline (AIAP), developed by the TaRGET 2 consortium, for ATAC-seq data analysis31,68. AIAP integrates quality control (QC) and analytical tools tailored for ATAC-seq, focusing on several QC metrics: (1) Reads Under Peak Ratio (RUPr): Measures the proportion of reads that fall within peak regions, indicative of the enrichment for transcriptionally active regions. (2) Background Noise: Assesses the level of nonspecific signal, highlighting the specificity and cleanliness of the data. (3) Promoter Enrichment: Evaluates read concentration in promoter regions, reflecting chromatin accessibility in key areas for gene expression regulation. (4) Subsampling Enrichment (SubEn): Determines signal enrichment across subsampled datasets to evaluate the consistency and reliability of peak detection. (5) Library Complexity: Examines the diversity of sequence reads, assessing the depth and breadth of the genomic coverage, which can influence the detection sensitivity for genomic features.

Further details of our analytical process using AIAP, including the rationale behind modifications and their impact on our study, will be provided in subsequent sections.

ATAC-seq AIAP genome and pangenome preparation

For the ATAC-seq Integrated Analysis Pipeline (AIAP), genome indexes, genome sizes, and annotation files are required. BWA (Burrows-Wheeler Aligner)69 indexes were generated for all linear reference and individual genomes, including the mitochondrial genome in both maternal and paternal assemblies to assess mitochondrial contamination.

The pangenome reference minigraph-cactus70 HPRC pangenome graph version 1.0 was utilized in a modified version of the AIAP, accessible at github.com/human-pangenomics/hpp_pangenome_resources#minigraphcactus.

For both the linear and pangenome-based approaches within AIAP, auxiliary files are crucial for quality evaluation. These include chromosome length files, which outline the lengths of chromosome, and BED files for promoters and coding promoters. These files are needed for assessing ATAC-seq data quality. The BED files, specific to each individual genome, were generated through a liftover process using rustybam [https://github.com/mrvollger/rustybam], adapting hg38 annotations to the genomic coordinates of each individual assembly.

ATAC-seq read trimming, adapter removal, and QC

In the AIAP, initial steps focus on read trimming, adapter removal, and quality control (QC). Cutadapt71 processes raw sequencing reads to trim adapters and remove low-quality bases, preparing the data for analysis. The tool trimmed adapters with sequence CTGTCTCTTATACACATCT from both ends of the reads. Parameters include a quality threshold of 15 for the leading end and 10 for the trailing end, with a minimum length of 36 bases set for read retention. These parameters aim to balance the removal of unwanted sequences against preserving valuable sequence data. After trimming, FastQC72 evaluates the reads’ quality to ensure they meet the necessary standards for further analysis within AIAP.

ATAC-seq read alignment and surjection

ATAC-seq reads underwent alignment to linear references (hg38, CHM13, and parental genomes) using BWA-MEM and to the minigraph-cactus HPRC pangenome graph version 1.0, using vg giraffe73. Alignments to the pangenome produced.GAM files, representing graph-space coordinates. These were subsequently converted to.BAM files via vg surject74, mapping the graph-based alignments to the linear hg38 reference system. This surjection was crucial for integrating the diverse genetic information from the pangenome into the linear analysis workflow.

ATAC-seq de-duplication

In AIAP, read de-duplication proceeds through three steps. Initially, FastQC evaluates sequencing data quality, highlighting potential duplication to gauge the library’s complexity. The script then estimates duplication extent by assessing library complexity, ensuring the dataset reflects unique genomic events. Finally, methylQA75 (methylqa.sourceforge.net) adjusts read counts for duplication. This normalization, critical for interpreting chromatin accessibility accurately across samples, supports analyses based on unique sequencing events.

Estimating ATAC-seq mitochondrial contamination rate

Within AIAP, the mitochondrial contamination rate, calculated by dividing the number of reads uniquely mapped to chrM by the total uniquely mapped reads, serves as an indicator of assay cleanliness for ATAC-seq experiments. While not invalidating assay results, a higher rate suggests a lower fraction of usable reads, as chrM mappings are uninformative for assessing nuclear chromatin accessibility. This metric guides optimization efforts in sample preparation or sequencing to enhance the informative read yield, ensuring data quality and the efficiency of chromatin accessibility studies (Supplementary Data 1).

Normalizing ATAC-seq signal and filtering by ENCODE blacklist

Before normalization, the AIAP script removes regions with known artifacts identified by the ENCODE blacklist for hg38 runs, offering a parallel dataset without such intersection for separate analyses. This selective removal is specific to hg38, with other genomic runs proceeding without blacklist adjustments.

AIAP’s normalization process, detailed in the s3.2_nomral_bg function, aims to make data across samples comparable by adjusting for sequencing depth and library size, crucial for a true representation of chromatin accessibility. The normalization calculates a scaling factor from the total count of non-redundant reads, targeting a reference point of 10 million reads. This factor adjusts the signal intensity in bedGraph files, which are then converted to bigWig format for analysis and visualization, enabling comparisons of chromatin accessibility across conditions.

Calculating ATAC-seq QC and alignment metrics

AIAP assesses sequencing and alignment quality through a set of quality control and alignment metrics. These include sequencing depth, alignment efficiency, chromosome distribution, chrM contamination rate, library insert fragment size distribution, library complexity, and the total number of useful single ends adjusted for Tn5 insertion. These metrics are crucial for determining the library’s suitability for chromatin accessibility studies. QC metrics are compared against the TaRGET2 AIAP standards to ensure library quality aligns with established benchmarks. This comparison facilitates the early detection and correction of issues, aiding in the generation of robust and dependable ATAC-seq data (Supplementary Data 1).

Calling accessibility peaks

In AIAP, the identification of accessibility peaks is carried out by the s3.3_peakcall function using MACS276, with inputs prepared via the s3.1_methylQA function. This function processes the ATAC-seq BAM files, adjusting for fragment sizes and distributions pertinent to ATAC-seq analysis. For hg38 runs, they are run both with and without an additional preprocessing step that excludes regions flagged by the ENCODE blacklist. Peak calling by MACS2 is performed under a stringent q-value cutoff of 0.01, with the command incorporating parameters --keep-dup 1000 --nomodel --shift 0 --extsize 150.

Clustering accessibility peaks across assemblies

All peak calls from non-hg38 assemblies were lifted-over to hg38 coordinates using PAFtools from the minimap2 package55. Clustering peaks first aggregates all peaks into a single file, removing those that could not be lifted over to hg38 coordinates. Peaks are then sorted by chromosomal location. Using a distance threshold of 200 base pairs, the sorted peaks were merged to identify clusters using bedtools merge77. Finally, each peak is mapped its respective cluster. Allowing for peaks to be compared across assemblies. The fraction of peak clusters specific to one or both individual assemblies was calculated.

Filtering accessibility peaks

After processing with the AIAP, an additional filtering step is applied to further refine the set of accessibility peaks based on signal strength. This post-processing step retains peaks that with a signal value greater or equal to 8.

RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Library preparation was performed by the Genome Technology Access Center (GTAC) at the McDonnell Genome Institute. Total RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using 500 ng to 1 µg of total RNA. Ribosomal RNA removal was conducted via an RNase-H-based method, utilizing RiboErase (Kapa Biosystems). Following ribosomal RNA depletion, mRNA was fragmented by incubation in reverse transcriptase buffer and heating at 94 °C for eight minutes. This fragmented mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase enzyme (Life Technologies), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, and employing random hexamers. A second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed, converting the single-stranded cDNA into double-stranded cDNA (ds-cDNA). The ds-cDNA was then subjected to end repair processes to create blunt ends, followed by the addition of an adenine base to the 3’ ends. Illumina sequencing adapters, which contained unique dual index tags, were ligated to these prepared ends. The adapter-ligated cDNA fragments underwent PCR amplification for 12 to 15 cycles using primers that included unique dual index tags. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system, producing paired-end reads that extended 150 bases in length. Resulting sequencing data is un-stranded.

RNA-seq analysis pipeline

The RNA-seq pipeline used here, derived from the ENCODE DCC RNA-seq pipeline (github.com/ENCODE-DCC/rna-seq-pipeline)38, has been modified to support analysis across various genome assemblies beyond GRCh38. It performs alignment using STAR57 and quantifies genes and isoforms with RSEM78, catering to comprehensive transcriptomic analysis. Conversion of alignment BAM to bigWig format is essential for generating tracks of all and unique alignments, facilitating detailed genomic transcription activity visualization. Quality control metrics assess sequencing data integrity by quantifying reads across gene categories, ensuring reliable sequencing and library preparation. Integration of Picard79 CollectRnaSeqMetrics provides an in-depth analysis of read distribution across genomic features. Lastly the pipeline is run with and without annotations, without these files, outputs are limited to STAR alignments, CollectRnaSeqMetrics results, and bigWig tracks.

RNA-seq Index building

Indices for alignment and quantification, utilizing STAR and RSEM, were constructed following ENCODE’s pipeline modifications for custom genome assemblies. Prebuilt indices for GRCh38 were sourced from the ENCODE repository (github.com/ENCODE-DCC/rna-seq-pipeline/blob/dev/docs/reference.md#genome-reference-files), using Gencode annotation v29. For non-GRCh38 assemblies, indices were created by using annotation GTF files, excluding tRNA annotations.

For STAR, the genomeGenerate mode was employed, inputting merged GTF, the target genome FASTA, and spike-ins, with the sjdbOverhang parameter set to read length minus one. This process resulted in a directory of reference index files necessary for STAR alignment. The preparation of RSEM indices involved aligning the target genome and merged annotation GTF, generating the requisite index files for RSEM analysis. This step was facilitated by the rsem-prepare-reference command, incorporating the target genome FASTA and merged annotation GTF.

The indexing phase initiates the bulk RNA-seq pipeline, establishing the foundation for subsequent alignment and quantification steps. It involves creating a JSON file (build-indxs.inputs.json) detailing paths to the target genome FASTA, the corresponding GTF annotation, and specifying the assembly’s name and annotation version. This JSON serves as input for the build-idxs.wdl pipeline, which accesses a Docker repository containing all necessary software for the pipeline’s execution.

RNA-seq pantranscriptome

The publicly available pantranscriptome reported with the draft human pangenome was used as the representative of pangenomic methods. It was generated as described as follows. It was generated by augmenting the allele-filtered graph with graph edges corresponding to splice junctions to create a spliced pangenome graph40. This was done using the vg toolkit (v1.38.0) RNA command with a max node length of 32. Transcript annotations used in this process were generated using the comparative annotation toolkit (CAT) starting with GENCODE (v38) annotations. The spliced HPRC pangenome graph was pruned using a stricter set of parameters (vg prune -r -k 64 -M 64) than the general pangenome.

RNA-seq read alignment

RNA-seq data was processed using both linear and pangenomic methods. For linear analysis, the data sets were aligned against their corresponding parental assemblies (Maternal, Paternal), the CHM13 assembly, and the hg38 assembly. This was performed using no-annotations, gencode v29 annotations, gencode-derived lifted over annotations, or the original chm13 annotations. Alignment was performed using the STAR aligner, adhering to the parameters outlined in the ENCODE protocol, adjusted for specific read lengths, and insert sizes. The alignments were processed against the custom-built STAR index’s, included options for quantMode to facilitate downstream RSEM analysis. Outputs included sorted BAM files and associated metrics, transcriptome-aligned BAM for RSEM input, and unmapped reads in FASTQ format.

For pangenomic analysis, the data sets were aligned against the HPRC pantranscriptome using the multi-path graph alignment tool, vg mpmap, (vg v1.43.0-67-gc3bdf2771). Each alignment job was run using 16 threads and performed by inputting the paths to the spliced pangenome graph in XG format, the corresponding GCSA index file, the snarl distance index, and the read 1 and read 2 fastq files. Three separate vg mpmap jobs were run for each replicate dataset in order to output the alignment in GAMP, GAM, and BAM formats. This was done to ensure compatibility with downstream analysis applications.

Calculating RNA-seq QC and alignment metrics

For linear analysis, post-alignment QC was executed using Picard CollectRnaSeqMetrics, analyzing distribution of reads across genomic regions to assess library quality. Including number and percentage of ribosomal, intergenic, UTR, intronic, mRNA, and useable bases along with median cv coverage, median 5 prime bias, median 3 prime bias, and median 3 prime 5 prime bias. Additional QC metrics were compiled, including read counts by gene type categories and comprehensive alignment statistics provided by SAMtools flagstat80. The pipeline also integrated a BAM to signals step, converting BAM files to bigWig format for visual inspection of alignment distribution.

For pangenomic analysis, the application vg stats was used to generate a set of alignment metrics from the GAM files. Samtools stats was used to generate a set of alignment metrics from the surjected BAM files (Supplementary Data 1).

Estimating expression abundances

RSEM is a tool for estimating gene and isoform expression levels. RSEM was used to process annotation guided alignments into per transcript abundance estimates. For graph-based analysis RPVG was used to estimate expression abundances40. RPVG is designed for the purpose of inferring expression of haplotype-specific transcript paths in a pantranscriptome. RPVG takes in a GAMP alignment file, the spliced pantranscriptome graph in XG format, and a GBWT index file, which represents the spliced pantranscriptome paths compressed and indexed using the graph extension (gPBWT) of the positional Burrows-Wheeler transform (PBWT). RPVG has three inference models: haplotypes, transcripts, and haplotype-transcripts. Briefly, haplotype mode infers joint posterior probabilities of haplotype combinations. It uses a branch and bound algorithm to infer the most probable combination of haplotypes. Transcripts mode infers abundances using an expectation maximization algorithm and requires the input of a file containing the transcript origin of each path in the spliced pantranscriptome graph. The haplotype-transcripts mode infers abundances using a combination of haplotype sampling and Expectation Maximization. The most likely haplotype combinations are inferred using the same algorithm utilized in the haplotypes inference model. This involves inferring equivalent haplotypes for all clustered transcripts. This mode also requires input of a file containing the transcript origin of each path in the spliced pantranscriptome graph.

Whole genome Bisulfite library preparation and sequencing

The process of library preparation and sequencing was conducted at the McDonnell Genome Institute’s Genome Technology Access Center (GTAC). Initially, genomic DNA (gDNA) from LCLs was quantified utilizing the Qubit 1x HS dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen, Cat# Q33231). The library preparation used 200 ng of gDNA, to which 0.2% unmethylated Lambda DNA was added as a control to assess the efficiency of bisulfite conversion. This mixture was then fragmented into approximately 350 bp segments using the Covaris LE220, followed by a 1.5x AMPure purification step on the EpMotion 5075 (Eppendorf), yielding a final volume of 20 µL. The gDNA was then subjected to bisulfite conversion employing the EZ-96 DNA Methylation-Gold Mag Prep Kit (Zymo Research, Cat# D5043) in accordance with the protocols recommended by the manufacturer, executed on the EpMotion 5075. The converted DNA (bsDNA) was quantified employing the Qubit ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# Q10212). The construction of automated WGBS libraries utilized roughly 100 ng of this quantified bsDNA, leveraging the Accel-NGS Methyl-Seq DNA Library Kit (Swift BioSciences, Cat# 30096) combined with unique dual indexes (UDI) (Swift, X9096) on the EpMotion 5075. The indexing PCR step comprised eight cycles, succeeded by a 0.85x AMPure cleanup to finalize the library. Assessment of the final library metrics, including average size and concentration, was conducted using a GX instrument. Precise quantification before sequencing was achieved using qPCR with the KAPA Library Quantification Kits (Roche) to ensure appropriate cluster densities. Sequencing was then performed to yield 2 × 150 paired-end reads using the S4 300 Cycle kit with the XP workflow on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 systems.

WGBS analysis pipeline

The WGBS pipeline employed in this study is designed to comprehensively analyze DNA methylation patterns across the genome. This pipeline integrates steps for quality control, alignment, and methylation calling. Initial QC checks ensure data integrity, utilizing FastQC for preliminary assessment and MultiQC81 for aggregated reporting. Sequencing reads are then aligned to the reference genome using Bismark82, a tool specifically developed for bisulfite-treated sequences, ensuring accurate mapping while accounting for the C-to-T transitions induced by bisulfite conversion. Duplication rates are assessed to identify and remove potential PCR duplicates. Library complexity is evaluated to confirm the sequencing depth and diversity necessary for comprehensive coverage of the methylome. Conversion metrics, assessing the efficiency of the bisulfite treatment, are closely monitored to ensure the reliability of methylation status calls. CpG coverage and bias analyses are conducted to methylation calling is unbiased across CpG-rich and CpG-poor regions. Following alignment, the pipeline quantifies methylation levels at CpG sites genome-wide, utilizing Bismark Methylation Extractor. Further details will be provided in subsequent sections.

WGBS genome index preparation

The alignment of WGBS reads requires bisulfite-specific indexes. Indexes are prepared by converting cytosines to thymines (and guanines to adenines for the reverse strand) in the reference, simulating the bisulfite treatment effect. This index generation is critical for aligning bisulfite-converted reads against the reference. Indexes were constructed using Bismark, a tool developed for bisulfite sequencing data analysis. Indexes were prepared for hg38 and each of the individual genome assemblies.

WGBS read trimming and adapter removal

Raw sequencing reads from WGBS were first subjected to adapter sequence removal and quality trimming using the cutadapt tool. This step is crucial to eliminate sequencing adapters and low-quality bases from the reads, which could otherwise interfere with the alignment accuracy and methylation calling.

WGBS read QC

Following trimming, the FastQC tool was employed to assess the quality of the reads. FastQC provided visual and quantitative reports indicating the quality of the sequencing output, allowing for the identification of potential issues that might affect the analysis, such as low-quality ends, over-represented adapters, or contamination. This step ensured that only high-quality reads were forwarded to the alignment stage.

WGBS read alignment

The reads were aligned using Bismark (hg38, CHM13, Maternal, Paternal). This tool performs a two-step process where bisulfite-treated reads are first converted in silico, and then aligned to the bisulfite-converted reference genome indexes. Bismark utilizes Bowtie 2 as the underlying alignment engines83. The alignment process is optimized to discriminate between methylated and unmethylated cytosines effectively, resulting in the generation of BAM files that detail the alignment positions and methylation states of reads across the genome.

Estimating bisulfite conversion

Accurately measuring DNA methylation requires near-complete bisulfite conversion of unmethylated cytosines. An incomplete reaction produces false-positive methylation signals. Bisulfite conversion rate was measured by spiking in lambda phage DNA (2% of total DNA) to DNA samples used to generate WGBS libraries. Reads are aligned against the lambda phage genome using the Bismark aligner. Methylation on the lambda phage genome is then estimated by Bismark. The bisulfite conversion rate is taken as one minus the lambda phage methylation rate.

WGSB read de-duplication

In WGBS, read de-duplication proceeds through three steps. Initially, FastQC assesses sequencing data quality, highlighting potential duplication to gauge the library’s complexity. The script then estimates duplication extent by assessing library complexity, ensuring the dataset reflects unique genomic events. Finally, the Bismark deduplicate_bismark function is used to remove duplicates.

Calculating WGBS QC and alignment metrics

In WGBS analysis, data quality is scrutinized through a series of QC and alignment metrics. These metrics include sequencing depth, alignment efficiency, bisulfite conversion efficiency, CpG coverage, duplicate read rate, library complexity, and CpG bias. Sequencing depth and alignment efficiency are measured to evaluate the coverage across the genome and the proportion of reads mapped to the reference genome, respectively. CpG coverage is analyzed to ensure that CpG sites, the primary focus in methylation studies, are adequately captured. The duplicate read rate quantifies the extent of PCR amplification artifacts, while library complexity examines the diversity of genomic fragments represented in the sequencing library. Lastly, CpG bias measures whether there is biased coverage as a function of CpG density. This is meant to investigate potential sequencing or alignment biases toward CpG-rich or CpG-poor areas. These metrics are essential for confirming the quality of WGBS libraries (Supplementary Data 1).

Estimating DNA methylation

Methylated CpGs are not converted by bisulfite treatment. Identifying cytosines which fail to convert allows us to infer DNA methylation. Methylation analysis within this study was conducted using bismark_methylation_extractor. This component of the Bismark suite processes bisulfite-converted read alignments to differentiate between methylated and unmethylated cytosines (Supplementary Data 1).

Hi-C library preparation and sequencing

These Hi-C libraries were generated by the HPRC33. They were generated by employing the Dovetail Omni-C kit (Dovetail Genomics). Specific modifications were incorporated as follows. Initially, 1 million cells were aliquoted from each line, subjecting them to fixation with formaldehyde and DSG. Next, the chromatin was digested using DNAse I to yield DNA fragments of the targeted length. Per the protocol, chromatin end repair was conducted, then attachment of a biotinylated bridge oligonucleotide and then the ligation of the free chromatin ends. Crosslinking was reversed, and the proximity-ligated DNA was purified. This DNA was converted into a sequencing library compatible with Illumina platforms using the NEB Ultra II library preparation kit (NEB), employing a Y-adaptor. To enrich for ligation products, streptavidin bead capture was applied to the final library. To preserve library complexity, each capture reaction was divided into two aliquots prior to the PCR amplification stage. All libraries (2X150bp) were uniquely dual indexed and were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq Platform.

Hi-C analysis pipeline

The Hi-C analysis pipeline, adapted from the ENCODE DCC Hi-C pipeline [https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/hic-pipeline]29,38, is designed to elucidate the three-dimensional chromatin organization within the nucleus. This workflow integrates critical steps for data quality control, alignment, and the elucidation of chromatin interactions. This pipeline was used to process data with two replicates per sample, each comprising four paired-end sets.

First, read quality is checked with FastQC. Then the pipeline aligns reads to the reference genome using BWA-MEM69. Subsequent to alignment, Juicer tools are applied to process the BAM files29, removing chimeric reads and adjusting insert sizes, followed by merging and deduplicating steps to refine the dataset. Library statistics are calculated to gauge the quality of the Hi-C library, focusing on metrics like duplication rates and library complexity, for interpreting the data’s depth and coverage.

For each sample, replicates are merged to create a comprehensive, sorted, and deduplicated BAM file. This merged file undergoes further processing, where mapping quality filters are applied before converting to the pre format, a prerequisite for generating Hi-C files. These files are then subjected to normalization and analyzed to identify loops and TADs using Juicer’s hiccups and arrowhead commands, respectively. Loop predictions were also performed with DELTA84. Further details will be provided in subsequent sections.

Hi-C read alignment

The alignment was performed using the BWA-MEM algorithm, part of the BWA software suite. BWA-MEM is specifically designed to efficiently align sequence reads of varying length, making it particularly suited for Hi-C data, which can encompass a wide range of fragment sizes due to the nature of chromatin interactions being captured. The command used for this step was: ‘bwa mem -SP5M-K 320000000 <reference_index_path > <read_1.fastq > <read_2.fastq> | samtools view -hbS - > aligned.bam‘

Hi-C alignment processing, merging, and deduplication

Following alignment, each resulting BAM file was processed with Juicer tools to identify and manage chimeric reads—a common occurrence in Hi-C data due to the ligation of distant DNA fragments. The scripts adjust_insert_size.awk and chimeric_sam.awk from Juicer were utilized for this purpose, allowing for the accurate representation of ligation events within the BAM files. The BAM files resulting from the chimeric read processing were merged and sorted using Samtools ‘merge’. Subsequently, duplicated reads were identified and removed using Juicer’s dups_sam.awk script, ensuring the uniqueness of each DNA-DNA interaction captured. Quality control statistics were generated for each library using Juicer’s stats_sub.awk script and juicer statistics subcommand. Following individual quality assessments, replicates for each sample were merged with Samtools ‘merge’, resulting in a sorted, deduplicated BAM file for further analysis.

Generating and normalizing Hi-C maps

To prepare for Hi-C analysis, the merged BAM files were converted to Juicer’s pre format, using the sam_to_pre.awk script. Hi-C maps were generated from the pre files using Juicer’s pre command, with subsequent normalization performed to correct for biases in the data, allowing for accurate comparisons of chromatin interaction frequencies across the genome.

Calculating Hi-C QC and alignment metrics

Quality control and alignment metrics are calculated to ensure the accuracy and reliability of inferred chromatin interactions. These metrics are alignment rates, percentages of unique and chimeric reads, duplication rates, proportions of unmapped reads, and contributions of Hi-C contacts to inter- and intra-chromosomal interactions. Chimeric reads, indicative of the ligation of distal DNA fragments and a hallmark of Hi-C data, are quantified to understand ligation efficiency and potential artifact presence. Lastly, we consider the difference between intra- and inter-chromosomal contact rates. (Supplementary Data 1). ENCODE thresholds for QC values were considered, but are not entirely applicable due to the difference in library generation.

Evaluating genome choice effects on QC and alignment metrics

In order to quantify the genome-wide effect of genome choice on QC and alignment metrics representative metrics were selected for each assay. The selected metrics are denoted in Supplementary Data 1. For each of the four assays we performed principle components analysis on the selected QC and alignment metrics using the R statistical package factoextra85. Then MANOVA was performed to estimate the effects of Sample, Genome, Sample:Genome, and Replicate on variation in principle components space (Supplementary Data 2). Further for each PC we perform partition the variances into the same terms (Supplementary Fig. 1). This is done by dividing the sum of squares for a given term by the total sum of squares across the model.

Evaluating genome choice read alignment

In order to quantify the genome-wide effects of genome choice on the process of read alignment, we first traced the fate of reads {Uniquely mapped, multi-mapped, unmapped}. For every read we attempted to align, we determined if it was uniquely mapped, multi-mapped, or unmapped. For ATAC-seq, fate was determined using the MapQ score. A value > 10 was considered uniquely mapped and a value of exactly 0 was called multi-mapped. For RNA-seq, the number of alignments with a given read mate’s name was used to determine the fate. 0 times for unmapped, 1 time for uniquely mapped, >1 for multi-mapped. For WGBS, Bismark returns uniquely mapped reads and a list of reads that were multi-mapped. The fate of each read is compared between query genomes {CHM13, Maternal, Paternal} and the reference genome {hg38}. The 9 possible combinations are combined into a contingency table. We then calculate the fraction of reads which do not share the same fate between compared assemblies. To then quantify the effect of genome choice on read position, first the chromosomal placement for each read between assemblies was compared. The reads placed on the same chromosome in both assemblies were lifted over with PAF tools to hg38 coordinates. Of those which could be lifted over, the chromosomal placement was compared after liftover. Of those on the same chromosome, the exact read position placement was compared and used to calculate the percent of reads with altered position.

Characterizing HG00621 maternal NuMT affecting ATAC-seq QC

In order to determine what genome sequence difference was driving the change in estimated mitochondrial contamination rate; calculated as the fraction of reads uniquely mapped to chrM over the total reads uniquely mapped, all reads which were called uniquely mapped in hg38 and multi-mapped in HG00621 maternal were extracted from the HG00621 alignment. Chromosomal placement counts for affected reads was generated to determine where reads are multi-mapping to (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). The localization of reads to chr20 was determined and visualized with the WashU Epigenome Browser, showing a large insertion into the HG00621 maternal assembly. The insertion is highly similar to chrM. To confirm the presence of the insertion parental (HG00619 & HG00620) reads were aligned to the HG00621 maternal assembly using BWA-MEM. Alignments were checked for whether parental reads span the break point (Supplementary Fig. 2E–G) using IGV86. Additionally, we confirm the insertion using bionano optical maps.

Characterizing CHM13 rDNA array affecting RNA-seq alignments

Reads which were unmapped when aligning to CHM13 and multi-mapped when aligned to hg38 localized to rDNA arrays. The hg38 chr21 (FP671120.18 clone) rDNA array containing sequence was BLAST49 aligned against each of the individual and CHM13 chr21 assembly sequences (Supplementary Fig. 4). Sequence dot plots were generated using genome workbench (v3.8.2). In order to determine whether the full resolution of rDNA arrays in CHM13 breaks STAR57 alignment to rRNA genes alternative, Subjunc58 and BBMap59 were used to align RNA-seq data against CHM13 and hg38. STAR uses a seed-and-extend strategy for alignment, whereas Subjunc and BBMap use a seed-and-vote strategy. The composition of %uniquely mapped, %multi-mapped, an %unmapped were calculated for the three strategies. To confirm that it is specifically the copy number of the rDNA array, which is breaking STAR alignments, the hg38 rDNA was expanded to the same number of copies as seen in CHM13. This expanded-hg38 assembly was then aligned against as before to show that expanding the copy number disrupts the alignment and reproduced the CHM13 pattern.

Evaluating genome choice effects on functional outputs

Functional outputs are categorized into annotations and continuous values. For functional annotations as in Hi-C and ATAC-seq, the number of annotations was directly compared. For continuous value functional estimates, dimensionality reduction (with scaling) was performed by principle components analysis to visualize and quantify the global effects of genome reference choice. This was done using R and the factoextra package. To get a global measure of chromatin accessibility across references, peaks were lifted over to hg38 coordinates, the peaks were then clustered. This was done requiring peaks to overlap by >50% and for the summit of both peaks to fall within the overlap. Each peak falling within a cluster was assigned a cluster id. The peak clusters were columns, sample:replicate:genome runs were rows and the values of elements were signal value calculated by Macs2. The corresponding matrix was filtered removing clusters for which did not have a representative value for every run and to remove clusters with a variance in signal value of zero. To get a global measure of gene expression across references, the set of shared transcript IDs across assemblies was generated. The columns corresponded to transcripts, the rows corresponded to sample:replicate:genome runs, and the elements were the TPM values calculated by RSEM or RPVG. The corresponding matrix was filtered removing with no variance in TPM value. To get a global measure of DNA methylation across references, CpG sites were lifted to hg38 coordinates using paftools. Sites were sorted and then binned taking the mean methylation of all CpGs within 3Kb intervals generated by bedops (chop = 3000, stagger = 3001)87. The data were combined with the columns corresponding to genomic bins, the rows corresponded to sample:replicate:genome runs, and the elements were the mean CpG methylation values derived from bismark. The corresponding matrix was filtered to remove any bins for which all samples did not have a value and then removing genomic intervals with methylation variance of zero.

A MANOVA was conducted using R88 to assess the impact of Sample, Genome, Sample:Genome, and Replicate on variation within principal component space. Additionally, for each principal component, variances were partitioned into the same categories (Supplementary Fig. 2). This was done by dividing the sum of squares for each term by the total sum of squares for the entire model, resulting in a partial eta squared estimate.

The functional outputs of each assay were evaluated for feature specific effects of genome reference choice. When evaluating chromatin accessibility, the specificity of peak calls relative to genome references was determined and visualized using the ComplexUpsetR package89. This was done for every cluster of peaks (generated as described above). When evaluating gene expression, the variation attributable to genome, sample, sample:genome, and abundance caller was determined for each transcript annotation by performing analysis of variance and calculating per term partial eta squared estimates. This was done by dividing the sum of squares for each term by the total sum of squares for the entire model. The abundance caller term corresponds to the RPVG pangenome abundance caller. When evaluating DNA methylation, CpG sites were lifted over from each non-hg38 assembly to hg38 coordinates using PAFtools. DSS was then used to identify differentially methylated regions for each individual assembly and CHM13 relative to hg38 using the DSS R package60. A minimum absolute value difference in methylation greater than 0.25 was required to consider a region to be differentially methylated.

In order to visualize functional genomics data across assemblies without explicitly performing liftover. The WashU comparative epigenome browser was used to visualized and compare these data48,90.

Hi-C loop anchor clustering and cross-assembly comparison

Hi-C data from the five benchmark individuals were processed using the ENCODE Hi-C pipeline with identical parameters across all assemblies (GRCh38, CHM13, and each individual’s maternal and paternal haplotypes). Loop anchor coordinates from all assemblies were converted to a common coordinate system by liftover to hg38.

To identify recurrent anchor loci, lifted anchor intervals were merged by chromosome to define clusters of overlapping anchors. Merging was performed without any distance extension, such that only directly overlapping intervals were grouped together. Each merged interval was assigned a unique cluster identifier, and non-overlapping anchors formed singleton clusters.

Clusters containing more than twenty anchors were removed prior to downstream analyses to avoid potential artifacts arising from over-merging. The genomic span of each cluster was calculated as the merged interval width, and the distance between paired loop anchors was measured within each native assembly to evaluate loop separation.

Cluster membership across individuals and assemblies was summarized as binary presence–absence matrices. Overlap and intersection analyses were performed within each assembly to evaluate inter-individual reproducibility, and within each individual to assess consistency across assemblies. Intersection sets were visualized as UpSet plots to represent shared and unique clusters across comparison groups.

Identifying genes proximal to features affected by genome choice

In order to contextualize the biological implications of the effects of genome reference choice, genes showing or proximal to features showing genome choice effects. Genes showing genome choice effects on transcript expression as defined by greater than 50% of variance attributable to sample:genome were identified. For chromatin accessibility peaks the nearest gene to each peak whose detection is specific to the use of an individual assembly were identified. For CpG methylation, the nearest gene to each DMR between the individual assemblies and hg38 were identified. The intersection of gene sets was evaluated and identified using the R complexUpsetR package. Further locations of gene sets in hg38 coordinates were visualized using Chromomap with 1 Mb sized bins.

Functional overrepresentation analysis of gene sets

Gene set over-representation analysis was performed on each of the above gene sets using WebGestalt using the GO:Biological process, GO:Cellular component, GO:Molecular function, KEGG:Pathways, and Human Phenotype ontology sets (Supplementary Data 7)62. The background for ATAC-seq ORA analysis was generated by taking the nearest gene to all peak calls. The background for RNA-seq was taken as the list of all genes with a transcript with non-zero expression. The background for WGBS was taken as the list of all genes in the genome.

Identifying regions with elevated rates of variation

Regions of the genome with significantly elevated rates of variation were identified separately for SNPs, Indels, and SVs. Variant call sets were generated by the HPRC for all samples in the first human pangenome described using Pacbio HiFi long reads [https://s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/human-pangenomics/index.html?prefix=submissions/ADF1EDBC-1463-482D-808E-C7EBED5C1161--WUSTL_Variant_Calls/]. SNPs and small Indels were called for each of the 47 samples using DeepVariant91. SV’s were called using an integration of sniffles92, SVIM93, and PBSV94 variant calls. The genome was split into 24 Kb bins, the approximate median gene size. The number of unique variants for each class of variation was calculated per bin. Permutation tests were performed by shuffling variant positions within each chromosome using bedtools shuffle for 1000 iterations. The significance of per bin enrichment of variation was estimated using the shuffles as a null model of variant counts for each chromosome. This was performed as a one-tailed test. Multiple tests corrections were performed by the benjamini-hochberg procedure. To be considered to have elevated variation, a region was required to have FDR ≤ 0.005 and with a variant count in the upper 10% of real variant count values. Genome wide localization of bins with elevated rates of variation were visualized using R (Supplementary Fig. 6)95. To determine the relationship between these variant calls and the structural complexity of the draft pangenome, the sum of per-interval variant counts (across all variant classes) was calculated and compared to the corresponding per-interval number of non-reference alleles from the decomposed graph VCF. Pearson’s rho was calculated comparing per-interval variant counts against per-interval number of non-reference alleles from the pangenome using the R statistical package.

Estimating genome choice features enrichment with elevated variation

The enrichment of features showing genome choice effects in regions with elevated rates of variation was estimated by testing for whether localization of gene from/proximal to assayed features (ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and WGBS) was statistically independent of whether they fall in regions with elevated rates of variation. This was performed using fisher’s exact test using the R statistical package88.

Identification and harmonization of transposable elements

Haplotype-resolved contig assemblies were used for all analyses [https://data.humanpangenome.org/assemblies]. Each contig assembly was aligned to GRCh38 using minimap2 to enable transfer of non-reference transposable element (TE) annotations that were originally defined in GRCh38 coordinates described by Zhuo et al. [10.1101/2024.11.22.623804]. Liftovers of non-reference TE annotations were performed with rustybam [https://github.com/mrvollger/rustybam], transferring coordinates from GRCh38 to the corresponding contig-level assembly coordinates. Each lifted annotation was extended by ±25 bp to include proximal flanking sequence.

Publicly available RepeatMasker96 annotations for each individual’s contig-level assemblies were used [https://data.humanpangenome.org/annotations]. Lifted non-reference annotations were intersected with the per-individual RepeatMasker tracks using bedtools intersect to identify corresponding sequence-level matches.

Pangenome methylation analysis and coordinate conversion

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) reads from the matched individuals were processed using MethylGrapher64, which aligns bisulfite-converted reads to the HPRC version 1.0 minigraph-cactus graph. Multi-mapped reads were permitted, but each read was assigned a single best alignment. Graph-space CpG methylation calls were linearized by haplotype-specific surjection onto each individual’s maternal and paternal paths, producing contig-level methylation data. A task facilitated by the presence of these individuals in the pangenome graph. Contig coordinates were subsequently converted to chromosomal coordinates using the.agp files generated by RagTag during the chromosomal scaffolding process.

Intersection of TE annotations with methylation data

Non-reference TE annotations in contig coordinates were intersected with the per-haplotype methylation calls. Repeats were filtered to retain only full-length elements with family annotations consistent with comparison set and sufficient CpG coverage (≥4 reads per CpG). Orientations were standardized across loci. To generate a comparison set of reference TEs, the intersection was inverted to select all other annotated TEs. These were subjected to identical filtering criteria, ensuring that both reference and non-reference elements were processed using identical methylation data and quality thresholds.

TE methylation quantification and statistical analysis

Mean CpG methylation across the body of each element was computed per locus and summarized by repeat family (Alu and L1) and by individual. Comparisons between reference and non-reference elements were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with bootstrapped downsampling of the larger reference group to match the non-reference sample size across K = 1000 iterations. p-values across resamples were aggregated using the Cauchy combination test, and multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method. To assess within-element methylation profiles, CpG positions were converted to relative coordinates spanning 0–100% of the element length, and methylation values were binned in 5% increments to compute mean methylation across the element body.

Visualization of a candidate Alu insertion

A representative non-reference AluYa5 insertion within an intron of TNKS on chromosome 8 (chr8:9.59–9.62 Mb) in the HG00621 maternal assembly was visualized using the WashU Epigenome Browser. Comparative genome-alignment tracks illustrated the presence of the insertion and corresponding methylation patterns, alongside ENCODE candidate cis-regulatory element annotations (ENCFF420VPZ) To visualize local pangenome topology, the graph in.gfa format was converted to a packed-graph format, and vg chunk was used (with the --components flag) to extract the chromosome 8 subgraph. This is essential to preserving haplotype paths in the graph structure. Then the local subgraph encompassing the AluYa5 locus was extracted using vg chunk. The resulting subgraph was visualized in BandageNG97. The canonical AluYa5 consensus sequence was aligned to the maternal sequence to confirm target-site duplications and the poly-A tail, verifying the completeness of the insertion.
