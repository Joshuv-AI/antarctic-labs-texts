CDKN2A alterations in oral squamous cell carcinoma: A systematic review of diagnostic and prognostic significance
Authors: Sivakumar Gopalakrishnan, Vijayashree P. Jayaseelan, Raghini Ramamurthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains a significant global health burden with limited improvement in survival rates. CDKN2A, a tumour suppressor gene encoding the p16^INK4a protein, is frequently altered in OSCC through promoter methylation, gene deletion, or expression downregulation. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic value of CDKN2A alterations in OSCC. A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE identified case-control studies published between 2015 and 2025. Only human tumour–normal comparative studies (including primary case–control and in silico comparative transcriptomic analyses) analyzing human-derived tissue or blood samples for CDKN2A alterations (methylation, expression, or copy number changes) and reporting clinical outcomes were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. No quantitative meta-analysis was performed due to heterogeneity in effect measures (hazard ratio [HR] vs odds ratio [OR]), alteration types/assays, and the limited number of studies reporting extractable survival data. Five case-control studies (≈500 participants) met eligibility criteria, spanning diverse regions, methods, and sample sizes. In four studies, CDKN2A disruption—via promoter methylation, copy-number loss, or reduced expression—aligned with worse endpoints (recurrence, metastasis, or survival), while one polymorphism-focused study was null. Because effect metrics were incompatible (HR vs OR), assays varied, and few studies reported extractable survival data, results could not be pooled. However, qualitative synthesis showed convergent adverse associations in four of five studies, with overall moderate–high quality on the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Unlike previous reviews that combined heterogeneous study designs, this analysis focused on human tumour–normal comparative studies (primary case–control and in silico analyses), providing a more direct evaluation of CDKN2A status in clinically matched populations. The findings confirm that CDKN2A alterations are strongly associated with poor prognosis in OSCC and highlight their value as one of the most consistent biomarkers for prognostic assessment. This case-control–focused appraisal offers unique evidence to guide risk stratification and supports further prospective validation.

INTRODUCTION

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for the vast majority of malignancies arising in the oral cavity and continues to impose a substantial global disease burden.[1] Despite improvements in surgical and adjuvant therapies, survival rates have shown minimal progress and remain approximately 50–60%, primarily due to delayed diagnosis, high rates of recurrence, and a persistent lack of reliable prognostic biomarkers.[23] These limitations have driven increasing interest in tumour-specific molecular alterations that may enhance early detection and risk stratification.

Among the genes investigated, CDKN2A has emerged as one of the most consistently altered loci in OSCC and is frequently evaluated for its potential diagnostic and prognostic relevance.[3] Although CDKN2A dysregulation is recognized across several cancer types, its significance in OSCC may be particularly pronounced because of the disease’s heterogeneous aetiology. In many regions, carcinogenesis is driven predominantly by tobacco, alcohol, and areca-nut exposure, while in specific populations, human papillomavirus (HPV)–associated disease represents a smaller biologically distinct subset.[456] Evidence indicates that these risk factors exert differential effects on CDKN2A regulation, including promoter hypermethylation, reduced expression, or paradoxical p16 overexpression in HPV-positive disease.[6789]

A growing body of research has evaluated CDKN2A alterations—including methylation, expression loss, and copy-number changes—as predictors of tumour aggressiveness, recurrence, and survival. Several studies have linked p16 promoter methylation with higher stage, poorer differentiation, and unfavourable outcomes,[1011] whereas transcriptomic investigations have incorporated CDKN2A into multigene prognostic signatures capable of stratifying survival across independent cohorts.[12] However, differences in study design and analytic methods across the literature have contributed to variability in reported associations.[13]

There is increasing clinical demand for biomarkers that distinguish aggressive from indolent OSCC, guide treatment decisions, and support surveillance, and CDKN2A is a leading candidate for this purpose, although its utility still requires validation through consistent, high-quality evidence.[14] Recognizing these gaps, this systematic review was designed to appraise the diagnostic and prognostic value of CDKN2A alterations in OSCC using strict inclusion criteria. Only human tumour–normal comparative studies with histologically confirmed OSCC were included. Studies embedded within broader HNSCC cohorts without OSCC-specific stratification were excluded, given the biological heterogeneity across head and neck subsites.[15] All selected studies used human tissue or blood samples, ensuring clinical applicability, and methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.[16]

To isolate the standalone contribution of CDKN2A, this review focused on single-gene analyses rather than multigene signatures, which can obscure individual gene effects.[17] Recognizing the biological complexity of CDKN2A, all major alteration types—genetic, epigenetic, and transcriptomic—were considered, as each affects biomarker detectability and clinical interpretation differently. For example, promoter methylation can be captured through non-invasive blood or saliva assays, whereas loss of protein expression requires tissue-based immunohistochemistry.[18]

By synthesizing the most recent human tumour–normal comparative evidence, this review aims to clarify the diagnostic and prognostic role of CDKN2A and to strengthen the foundation for its integration into future clinical, risk-stratification, and therapeutic algorithms.

Search strategy

A systematic search was carried out to identify studies that examined the diagnostic or prognostic value of CDKN2A alterations in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). The search spanned from January 2015 to June 2025, targeting the most current decade of molecular research relevant to this biomarker. Databases searched included PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science. Search terms included combinations such as “CDKN2A,” “p16,” “INK4a,” “oral squamous cell carcinoma,” “OSCC,” “methylation,” “expression,” “alteration,” and “biomarker.” Filters were applied to restrict the results to human studies, English language, and full-text availability. An initial yield of 499 articles was obtained. After removing duplicates, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies that appeared to meet the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies during the screening process were resolved by discussion or with input from a third reviewer. The selection and screening process followed PRISMA guidelines,[19] and the filtration pathway is documented in a supplementary PRISMA flowchart [Figure 1].

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met several strict criteria aimed at ensuring scientific and clinical relevance. Eligible studies had to be published between 2015 and 2025, be written in English, and focus specifically on OSCC rather than general head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Only studies that directly analyzed CDKN2A or its protein product p16^INK4a were included. Furthermore, they were required to use human-derived tissue or blood samples for molecular analyses and report on patient outcomes.

Eligible study designs were restricted to human tumour–normal comparative studies (primary case–control or in silico analyses using public datasets such as GEO/TCGA), in line with the scope of this review. All included studies had to involve human subjects and provide sufficient clinical context, such as associations with survival, tumour progression, or recurrence. The goal was to capture meaningful, patient-centred insights into the clinical utility of CDKN2A as a biomarker.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they failed to distinguish OSCC from broader HNSCC, did not include analysis of CDKN2A or p16, or lacked primary biological sample testing. Reviews, editorials, dissertations, and other forms of non-original research were also excluded. Articles unavailable in full text, or published in languages other than English, were removed from consideration. Additionally, in vitro-only studies or those involving animal models without human clinical correlation were excluded. These criteria refined the dataset to studies with direct clinical relevance to OSCC.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from each included study using a standardized template to ensure consistency and accuracy. The following information was collected: study title, authors, year of publication, country of origin, study design, and sample size. Population characteristics such as age, sex, and tumour stage were recorded. Details about CDKN2A assessment included the type of alteration (e.g. promoter methylation, gene expression, copy number variation), sample type (tissue or blood), and molecular techniques employed, such as methylation-specific PCR (MSP), qPCR, or MLPA.

Statistical methods used in each study were noted, including Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, Cox regression, ANOVA, and Chi-square tests. Clinical outcomes such as overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), recurrence, and metastasis were recorded. The key findings of each study were summarized to capture the strength and direction of associations between CDKN2A alterations and clinical parameters. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third party.

Risk of bias assessment

To assess the quality of the included studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied.[16] This tool evaluates non-randomized studies based on three main domains: selection of participants, comparability of study groups, and ascertainment of exposure or outcome. Each study could score a maximum of nine stars, reflecting low risk of bias. The quality assessments focused on factors such as case definition, sample representativeness, follow-up duration, and control for confounding variables. NOS was applied only to clinical case–control studies; the TCGA/GEO bioinformatics study[20] was appraised narratively with domains adapted for secondary-dataset analyses.

Data synthesis

A quantitative meta-analysis was not performed because the included studies used non-comparable effect measures (time-to-event HRs vs case–control ORs), evaluated different types of CDKN2A alterations with non-uniform assays and thresholds, and only two studies reported extractable survival HRs, precluding a meaningful pooled estimate.

RESULTS

From an initial set of 499 studies identified through database searches, only five met the inclusion criteria after thorough screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts. These five studies, published between 2015 and 2025, included research conducted in Iran, China, India, Portugal, and Poland. The included studies varied in methodology and sample size, ranging from localized institutional cohorts with fewer than 100 participants to large-scale transcriptomic analyses using publicly available datasets such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). All studies investigated the role of CDKN2A gene alterations in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), with a focus on their diagnostic or prognostic potential.

Sample types included formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues, fresh-frozen tumour biopsies, and blood-derived DNA.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review evaluated the diagnostic and prognostic significance of CDKN2A gene alterations in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), synthesizing data from five eligible human tumour–normal comparative studies published between 2015 and 2025.[2021222324]

Across the five studies, the direction of effect was largely consistent—four reported that CDKN2A alterations were associated with adverse outcomes, while one polymorphism study was null—but effect sizes were not pooled because the studies reported non-comparable metrics (HRs vs ORs), targeted different alteration types with non-uniform assays, and too few provided extractable survival HRs to support a reliable meta-estimate. From a mechanistic perspective, CDKN2A plays a critical role in regulating the G1–S checkpoint through its two protein products, p16^INK4a and p14^ARF, which arise via alternative splicing from the 9p21 locus. p16^INK4a inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6), while p14^ARF stabilizes p53 signalling by antagonizing MDM2.[2526] Disruption of these functions—whether through promoter hypermethylation, loss of heterozygosity, homozygous deletions, or transcriptional downregulation—leads to unrestrained proliferation and genomic instability.[23] Methylation of the CDKN2A promoter, as observed in the included studies,[222324] silences gene expression and functionally removes this regulatory brake. Similarly, gene deletions and reduced mRNA expression eliminate the gene’s tumour-suppressive activity.[2124] These findings align with established cancer biology and further validate the clinical significance of the CDKN2A pathway in OSCC.

Importantly, this review also demonstrated the feasibility of assessing CDKN2A alterations using accessible molecular techniques, including methylation-specific PCR, qRT-PCR, and copy number assays.[222324] One study even used publicly available transcriptomic datasets and bioinformatics tools to uncover a prognostic gene signature that included CDKN2A.[20] These methods are adaptable to clinical workflows and offer a potential pathway for translating molecular markers into real-world diagnostics.

As shown in our Results, Zhao et al.[20] validated a three-gene model incorporating CDKN2A that stratified survival risk. They also identified enrichment of cancer-promoting pathways such as oncostatin M/IL-6 family signalling, matrix metalloproteinase-mediated extracellular matrix/collagen catabolism, and loss of epithelial tight-junction integrity, all of which may act synergistically with CDKN2A disruption to accelerate invasion and metastasis. Recent clinical tissue–margin work underscores expression-level heterogeneity: CDKN2A mRNA itself does not always differ between tumour and matched margin, and grade-linked signals may instead emerge in adjacent G2 tumours via E2F2, highlighting why alteration type (methylation/deletion vs expression) and assay choice matter for prognostic read-through.[27]

Comparison with prior systematic reviews highlights the unique strength of our approach. Previous reviews often combined heterogeneous study designs—including retrospective cohorts, prospective cohorts, and even cell-line analyses—making it difficult to isolate the specific prognostic contribution of CDKN2A in OSCC.[28] By restricting inclusion to human tumour–normal comparative studies, our review provides a sharper lens on clinically matched populations and reduces confounding from unrelated head and neck subsites. This methodological rigor adds weight to our findings and distinguishes this work from earlier reviews.

While the evidence is compelling, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, sample sizes across individual studies were modest, which may affect the precision of effect estimates. Second, there was methodological heterogeneity in how CDKN2A was assessed, ranging from different methylation detection techniques to varied thresholds for defining gene silencing. Third, not all studies adjusted for potential confounders, such as smoking status, HPV infection, or comorbidities, which could influence both gene expression and prognosis. Notably, p16 IHC positivity does not exclude CDKN2A mutation–driven disease in HPV-negative OSCC, reinforcing the need to interpret p16 with genotype and HPV status together.[2829]

Another limitation relates to geographic and population diversity. Although the studies originated from different regions, including Asia and Europe, larger and more diverse multicentre studies are needed to validate the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, none of the included studies examined longitudinal changes in CDKN2A status during treatment or follow-up, leaving unanswered questions about its utility in monitoring disease progression or response to therapy. Two practical sources of inconsistency are the molecular ‘field effect’—margins can harbour subtle cycle-control changes—and incomplete co-stratification by HPV/p16, both of which can blur CDKN2A expression signals across studies.[30]

Despite these limitations, this review highlights clear opportunities. The consistent association between CDKN2A alterations and adverse outcomes presents a strong case for incorporating CDKN2A testing into diagnostic and prognostic algorithms. It also opens the door for exploring therapeutic targeting of the CDK4/6 pathway, especially in patients with P16 loss. The clinical success of CDK4/6 inhibition in breast cancer, demonstrated by the palbociclib–letrozole trial showing significant survival benefit in advanced disease,[31] underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway in OSCC cases exhibiting functional inactivation of CDKN2A.

In addition to methylation, deletion, and expression loss, CDKN2A mutations warrant attention. Multiple OSCC genomic studies have identified somatic mutations—particularly in exon 2—that disrupt the p16^INK4a interaction with CDK4/6, resulting in complete loss of checkpoint control.[3233]

These mutations have been associated with higher histological grade, early nodal involvement, and poorer survival outcomes. Although none of the eligible tumour–normal case–control studies included in our review focused primarily on mutational analysis, the broader evidence base supports their potential relevance in prognostic refinement.

Furthermore, rare germline CDKN2A mutations have been reported in young, non-smoking OSCC patients, suggesting a hereditary predisposition in a small subset.[34] These individuals may benefit from genetic counselling and broader HNSCC surveillance.

Polymorphisms also contribute to the biological diversity of CDKN2A-related risk. Although Gaździcka et al.[24] found no association between rs11515, rs3088440, or rs769412 and OSCC prognosis, polymorphisms in the 3′UTR and intronic regions have been shown in other populations to influence mRNA stability, transcriptional efficiency, or susceptibility to promoter methylation.[3536] Thus, genetic variants, even when individually weak, may interact with carcinogen exposure or epigenetic mechanisms to modulate risk in a population-dependent manner.

There is parallel enthusiasm for non-invasive detection: saliva/plasma assays for CDKN2A promoter methylation are repeatedly proposed as translational candidates, warranting case-control validation alongside tissue assays.[37] Because CDKN2A dysregulation intersects with cell-cycle and inflammatory circuitry, combining CDK4/6 inhibition with microenvironment-modulating strategies may be rational; multi-omic signatures that include CDKN2A (or co-drivers such as E2F2/MDM2) should be prioritized for model building.[2836]

Moreover, network-based analyses such as those by Zhao et al.[20] (2018) point to the interaction of CDKN2A dysregulation with extracellular matrix remodelling, collagen catabolism,[38] and inflammatory cytokine signalling,[39] suggesting that combined therapeutic strategies may be necessary. Targeting CDK4/6 alongside modulators of the tumour microenvironment could hold promise for more durable responses. Future research should aim to standardize detection techniques, validate findings in larger prospective cohorts, and explore the interaction between CDKN2A and other molecular markers or pathways, such as TP53, EGFR, or the PI3K/AKT axis. Moreover, it would be valuable to study whether combining CDKN2A status with other clinical parameters improves prognostic accuracy or therapeutic decision-making. Future primary case-control work should integrate HPV/p16 status and benchmark tissue findings against saliva/plasma methylation panels to define deployable, clinic-friendly algorithms. Although uncommon, germline CDKN2A carriers can present with early-onset, non-smoker OSCC; in such contexts, genetic counselling/testing and HNSCC surveillance deserve consideration. This systematic review provides robust evidence supporting the clinical relevance of CDKN2A as a biomarker, which could enhance prognostic stratification in patients with oral cancer. Further prospective studies and translational research are warranted to integrate CDKN2A into routine clinical practice and evaluate its potential as a target for personalized therapy.

CONCLUSION

CDKN2A alterations—including promoter hypermethylation, deletion, and transcriptional downregulation—show a consistent association with adverse clinicopathologic features in OSCC. Across the five eligible tumour–normal comparative studies, these alterations reliably marked poorer survival, supporting their relevance for risk stratification. Integrating CDKN2A assessment into diagnostic workflows may refine prognostic evaluation and identify patients who could benefit from pathway-directed therapies. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to determine how best to incorporate CDKN2A testing into routine clinical practice.
