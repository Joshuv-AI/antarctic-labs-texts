Endoscopic intervention for patients of renal insufficiency with upper gastrointestinal bleeding: efficacy evaluation and predictive model construction
Authors: Yongle Huang, Qia Chen, Rirong Chen, Minhu Chen, Xianwen Guo, Zhen Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore disease severity thresholds beyond which endoscopic intervention fails to reduce mortality in the context of renal insufficiency and upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB), and to develop predictive models for mortality.MethodsThis study retrospectively collected data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV). Disease severity thresholds were explored using restricted cubic splines. Patients in our hospital (n = 37) were included as an external validation cohort.Logistic regression was used to identify independent factors for mortality. Predictive models were developed using Logistic regression, LASSO, XGBoost, and random forest algorithms. Models were evaluated in the hospital cohort and the National Inpatient Sample (NIS, n = 18248) database.ResultsIn the context of renal insufficiency and UGIB, compared with the non-endoscopic group (n = 372), patients receiving endoscopic intervention (n = 1177) had lower in-hospital mortality (12.74% vs. 21.77%, p < 0.001). However, no significant difference was observed when creatinine exceeding 354 µmol/L (p = 0.075). In the hospital cohort, no improvement on mortality, advanced respiratory or circulatory support was observed in patients with creatinine over 354 µmol/L (p = 0.335).For patients receiving endoscopic intervention, hemorrhagic stroke, disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute respiratory failure, hemodialysis, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, hypovolemic shock, acute kidney injury, and age emerged as independent risk factors for mortality. The Logistic predictive model was developed, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.851, 0.809, and 1.000 in the test set, the NIS cohort, and the hospital cohort, respectively.ConclusionsIn the context of renal insufficiency and UGIB, endoscopic intervention failed to improve mortality when creatinine > 354 µmol/L, and comprehensive evaluations should be made in such cases. Independent risk factors for mortality in patients receiving endoscopic intervention were revealed, and the Logistic model stratified risk of mortality effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04149-7.

Introduction

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) is one of the most common critical conditions in clinical practice. Patients usually manifest as hematemesis, melena, and hematochezia [1–3]. Pharmacotherapy, endoscopic intervention, radiological embolization, and surgical hemostasis are widely applied to achieve hemostasis [3]. Notably, endoscopic intervention is regarded as the first-line therapeutic strategy for hemodynamically stable patients [2, 4].

Among patients with renal insufficiency, the prevalence of UGIB ranges from 2.2 to 21.2% [5–8]. For those with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB), Garg et al. showed that groups receiving endoscopic intervention had significantly lower in-hospital mortality when compared with those not [7, 9]. However, uremic toxin accumulation, fluid overload, congestive heart failure, and even mechanical ventilation were also frequently observed in patients with renal insufficiency, further exacerbating the risks of cardiopulmonary adverse events during endoscopic procedures [7, 10–12]. Moreover, considering the effect of anticoagulants, and the development of uremia-induced platelet dysfunction, diffuse mucosal erosions with hemorrhage were commonly reported among such population, potentially reducing the benefits of endoscopic hemostasis [8, 9]. To date, there is still no research to explore whether there exists disease severity threshold beyond which endoscopic intervention fails to improve mortality in the context of renal insufficiency and UGIB.

Our study retrospectively collected patients with UGIB complicated by acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), ESRD, hemodialysis, or peritoneal dialysis, aiming to explore thresholds of renal function parameters, and to reveal the specific population among which endoscopic intervention failed to reduce mortality. For those receiving endoscopic intervention, independent risk factors for mortality were identified, and predictive models were built to assist in risk stratification.

Methods

Study design

Our study primarily collected data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV). MIMIC-IV provides information on medication, laboratory parameters, disease severity index, diagnoses, and discharge outcomes, etc. Patients diagnosed as UGIB and renal insufficiency were enrolled using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplementary Table S1).

Two external cohorts were incorporated for validation. The 2016 National Inpatient Sample (NIS) is one of the largest publicly available databases, collecting approximately 20% of annual hospitalizations nationwide and encompassing demographic, diagnostic, procedural, and outcome statistics. In addition, patients diagnosed as UGIB and renal insufficiency at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between Jan 1 st, 2016, and Dec 31 st, 2020 were collected.

Detailed inclusion criteria were as follows. (1) Patients diagnosed as UGIB, including esophageal ulcer, gastric ulcer, duodenal ulcer, compound ulcer, esophagitis, gastro-esophageal reflux disease, gastritis, duodenitis, gastro-duodenitis, upper gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, gastro-duodenal Dieulafoy disease, varicose, Mallory-Weiss syndrome, etc.; (2) Patients diagnosed as renal insufficiency, including CKD, AKI, ESRD, hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, etc [13, 14]. Complete ICD-10 codes are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (e.g., K25.x for gastric ulcer, N185 and N186 for ESRD). Patients under 18 years, and pregnant women were excluded.

Variables

Variables were collected as follows. (1) Demographics: age, sex, and smoking history; (2) GIB phenotypes; (3) Renal insufficiency; (4) Emergency admission; (5) Disease severity: hypovolemic shock, Diagnosis Related Groups (DRG) mortality risk and severity of illness, Charlson comorbidity index, and sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA); (6) Laboratory parameters: creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); (7) Hemostatic strategies: endoscopic, radiological, and surgical intervention; (8) Comorbidities: arrhythmia, acute respiratory failure, cirrhosis, hepatic failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), etc.; (9) Medication history.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in our study was in-hospital mortality. Requirement of advanced respiratory or circulatory support, and composite outcomes were secondary outcomes. Requirement of positive pressure mechanical ventilation, endotracheal intubation, mechanical circulatory support, arterial catheter, arterial pressure monitoring, and central venous pressure monitoring were defined as advanced respiratory or circulatory support [15, 16]. In-hospital mortality, advanced respiratory or circulatory support were classified as composite outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.1). Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage), and compared using the Chi-square test. Continuous variables following normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those not following normal distribution were reported as median (inter-quantile range [IQR]). Independent T-test or Mann-Whitney U test were applied for comparison.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore the associations between laboratory parameters and mortality. For variables with significant nonlinearity (p < 0.05), inflection points were determined based on slope of the curve. Otherwise, variables were categorized by median. Multivariable Logistic regression was used to adjust for confounding variables, including age, sex, hypovolemic shock, Charlson Comorbidity Index, DRG mortality risk, and severity of illness [17].

Baseline variables, including demographics, GIB phenotypes, renal insufficiency, hypovolemic shock, comorbidities, and medication history, were incorporated to explore predictive factors for mortality. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate Logistic regression were included in the multivariate Logistic regression, presenting as odds ratio [OR] (95% confidence interval [CI]).

Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate Logistic regression were further included to develop predictive models. The training set, and the test set were divided in a ratio of 7:3. Those with p < 0.05 in the multivariate Logistic regression were selected to build the Logistic predictive model. Decision curve was used evaluate the clinical utility, calibration curve and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test were used to assess the calibration. Other predictive models were built based on XGBoost, random forest, and LASSO regression algorithm. The Logistic predictive model and the LASSO regression model were further assessed in two external validation cohorts. Area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate predictive efficacy of models in the training set, test set, and external validation cohorts.

Result

Baseline characteristics

One thousand five hundred forty-nine patients with UGIB and renal insufficiency were included in the MIMIC-IV database. 552 (35.64%) were female, and the median age was 66 years. 1177 (75.98%) received endoscopic intervention, and 54 (3.49%) suffered hypovolemic shock. 412 (26.60%) required advanced respiratory or circulatory support, whereas 231 (14.91%) died in hospital (Table 1).

Exploring disease severity thresholds beyond which endoscopic intervention failed to improve mortality

For patients with UGIB and renal insufficiency, the endoscopic intervention group had improved mortality when compared with the non-endoscopic group (150/1177 [12.74%] vs. 81/372 [21.77%], p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounding variables, endoscopic intervention was still associated with reduced mortality (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40–0.80, p = 0.001). In addition, no significant superiority of endoscopic intervention on the requirement of advanced respiratory or circulatory support was observed (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.68–1.27, p = 0.634).

We further explored thresholds of renal function parameters, evaluating the risk-benefit profile of endoscopic intervention under different disease severities. RCS was used to reveal the associations between mortality, and creatinine, BUN, eGFR, and SOFA (Fig. 1). The nonlinearity test of creatinine and mortality yielded a p-value < 0.001, and patients were categorized by the inflection point of 4 mg/dL (i.e., 354 µmol/L). After adjusting for confounding variables including age, sex, hypovolemic shock, Charlson Comorbidity Index, DRG mortality risk, and severity of illness, for patients with creatinine ≤ 354 µmol/L, the endoscopic intervention group demonstrated lower mortality when compared with the non-endoscopic group (OR = 0.54, p < 0.001). However, for those with creatinine > 354 µmol/L, endoscopic intervention did not significantly improve mortality (OR = 0.44, p = 0.075) (Table 2).

In terms of BUN, nonlinearity was significant with a p-value of 0.001. Patients were stratified according to the inflection point of 62 mg/dL (i.e., 22 mmol/L). For those with BUN > 22 mmol/L, the endoscopic intervention group showed notably lower mortality (OR = 0.43, p = 0.009). In addition, the nonlinearity test of eGFR and mortality yielded a p-value of 0.002. The inflection point of 22 mL/min/1.73m2 was applied. Similarly, patients receiving endoscopic intervention had lower mortality even when eGFR ≤ 22 mL/min/1.73m2 (OR = 0.46, p = 0.014). Furthermore, the non-linearity test of SOFA and mortality received a p-value of 0.855. Among patients with SOFA exceeding the median value of 2, endoscopic intervention was still associated with improved mortality (OR = 0.45, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Exploring the applicability of creatinine threshold in different subgroups

To enhance the clinical interpretability of the creatinine threshold, subgroup analyses were conducted in patients with AKI, CKD stages 1–4, and ESRD. Among the AKI subgroup, endoscopic intervention significantly improved in-hospital mortality (OR = 0.50, 95%CI: 0.36–0.69, p < 0.001). For those with creatinine ≤ 354 µmol/L (n = 1056), the endoscopic intervention group showed lower mortality when compared with the non-endoscopic group (OR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.37–0.73, p < 0.001). For those with creatinine over 354 µmol/L (n = 89), there was no obvious advantage of endoscopic intervention (OR = 0.47, 95%CI: 0.16–1.44, p = 0.173).

Similarly, among patients of CKD 1–4 without dialysis, endoscopic intervention reduced mortality risk significantly (OR = 0.41, 95%CI: 0.19–0.94, p = 0.029). For those with creatinine ≤ 354 µmol/L (n = 368), group receiving endoscopic intervention demonstrated improved mortality (OR = 0.43, 95%CI: 0.19–1.03, p = 0.049). However, for patients with creatinine > 354 µmol/L (n = 19), no statistical advantage of endoscopic intervention existed (OR = 0.45, 95%CI: 0.02–73.92, p = 0.672). For patients with ESRD (n = 161), endoscopic intervention did not significantly improve mortality risk (OR = 0.79, 95%CI: 0.23–3.66, p = 0.729).

Exploring predictive factors for mortality in patients receiving endoscopic intervention

In patients receiving endoscopic intervention, Logistic regression was operated to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. Age (OR = 1.02), hypovolemic shock (OR = 2.58), hemodialysis (OR = 2.98), AKI (OR = 1.87), acute respiratory failure (OR = 3.48), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI, OR = 2.84), hemorrhagic stroke (OR = 7.29), DIC (OR = 4.66) were revealed as independent risk factors. On the other hand, patients with hyperlipidemia (OR = 0.52), and upper gastrointestinal angiodysplasia (OR = 0.48) showed improved mortality (Table 3).

Other predictive models were developed based on XGBoost, random forest, and LASSO regression algorithms. In the training set, the XGBoost model worked well with an AUC of 0.918 (95% CI: 0.890–0.947), and a sensitivity of 0.876. In the test set, the AUC was 0.791 (95% CI: 0.732–0.850, p < 0.001), without significant difference with the Logistic predictive model (p = 0.147). In comparison, the random forest model demonstrated an AUC of 0.994 (95% CI: 0.990–0.998), and a sensitivity of 0.966 in the training set. In the test set, the AUC was 0.814 (95% CI: 0.756–0.873, p < 0.001), without obvious difference with the Logistic predictive model (p = 0.372). The LASSO regression model simplified variables including varicose, acute respiratory failure, and hepatic failure. In the training set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.753 (95% CI: 0.702–0.805), with a sensitivity of 0.809. Correspondingly, in the test set, the AUC was 0.783 (95% CI: 0.718–0.847, p = 0.486). There was no significant difference between the Logistic predictive model and the LASSO regression model (p = 0.115) (Fig. 3).

External validation

In the NIS cohort, 18,248 patients were collected. 7568 (41.50%) were females, and the median age was 72 years. Among them, 14,622 patients underwent endoscopic intervention, 1011 (5.54%) developed hypovolemic shock, and 1345 (7.37%) died during hospitalization. Patients receiving endoscopic intervention showed lower mortality compared with the non-endoscopic group (867/14622 [5.93%] vs. 478/3626 [13.18%], p < 0.001). Among the endoscopic intervention group, the Logistic predictive model demonstrated an AUC of 0.809 (95% CI: 0.794–0.824), a sensitivity of 0.730, and a specificity of 0.777. In addition, the LASSO regression model showed an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.760–0.792), a sensitivity of 0.742, and a specificity of 0.777 (Supplementary Figure S1).

In the hospital cohort, there were 37 patients diagnosed as UGIB and renal insufficiency. 10 (27.03%) were females, and the median age was 64 years. 22 patients received endoscopic intervention, whereas 13 reached the composite outcome. For patients with creatinine ≤ 354 µmol/L, compared with the non-endoscopic group, those undergoing endoscopic intervention had lower mortality (0/10 [0%] vs. 3/5 [60%], p = 0.040). However, for patients with creatinine exceeding 354 µmol/L, the endoscopic intervention group showed no remarkably improved mortality (5/12 [41.67%] vs. 5/10 [50%], p = 1). Furthermore, the Logistic predictive model showed an AUC of 1.000, a sensitivity of 1.000, and a specificity of 1.000. In comparison, the LASSO regression model had an AUC of 0.375 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

In the context of UGIB and renal insufficiency, our study revealed that compared with the non-endoscopic group, patients receiving endoscopic intervention had lower in-hospital mortality. However, among those with creatinine exceeding 354 µmol/L, there was no significant advantage of endoscopic intervention. Given that endoscopic procedure would exacerbate respiratory and circulatory burdens, and potentially induce severe cardiopulmonary complications, careful risk-benefit evaluations would be strongly recommended prior to endoscopic intervention. Furthermore, hemorrhagic stroke, DIC, acute respiratory failure, hemodialysis, NSTEMI, hypovolemic shock, AKI, and age were identified as independent risk factors for mortality among patients receiving endoscopic intervention. The Logistic predictive model demonstrated an AUC of 0.851, and a sensitivity of 0.750 in the test set. In external validation cohorts, the model yielded AUC of 0.809 and 1.000.

Among patients with UGIB and renal insufficiency, our study identified the subgroup among whom endoscopic intervention failed to improve mortality. For renal transplant recipients, Fellström et al. reported that baseline creatinine was associated with major adverse cardiac event (Relative risk [RR] = 1.89) [18]. Similarly, Chen X and Bagheri B et al. demonstrated that patients with elevated creatinine had increased risks of long-term cardiovascular adverse events (e.g., coronary heart disease) [19, 20]. For patients with UGIB, higher creatinine indicated impaired renal perfusion secondary to severe hypovolemia and organ dysfunction [21–23]. Moreover, among those with elevated creatinine and severe renal impairment, platelet dysfunction and diffuse mucosal erosions would further reduce hemostatic efficacy of endoscopy [8, 9]. Based on researches above and our results, for patients with creatinine > 354 µmol/L, there were possibly more severe impairment of systemic perfusion, organ dysfunction, and higher risks of cardiovascular complications. Endoscopic intervention might further increase risks of myocardial ischemia, exacerbate cardiorespiratory burden, whereas fail to improve in-hospital mortality [24, 25]. In such cases, cautious risk-benefit evaluations would be strongly recommended.

Our study further explored predictive factors for mortality in patients receiving endoscopic intervention. Hemorrhagic stroke, DIC, NSTEMI, acute respiratory failure, hypovolemic shock, age, AKI, and hemodialysis served as independent risk factors. Akku R et al. reported that compared with general ESRD patients, those complicated by hemorrhagic stroke had higher mortality risk [26]. Patients with hemorrhagic stroke were prone to dysregulation of respiratory and circulatory system, accompanied by poor hemodynamic fragility. Endoscopic intervention might further worsen the hemodynamic stability, thereby increasing risk of mortality. In addition, DIC patients were susceptible to diffuse gastrointestinal erosions. Endoscopic intervention was challenging in such cases due to incomplete hemostatic efficacy, increased risks of gastrointestinal necrosis, leading to poor survival outcomes [27]. Among patients with GIB and myocardial infarction, Al-Ebrahim F et al. demonstrated that 15.91% experienced recurrent acute coronary syndrome after endoscopic intervention [25]. In the context of NSTEMI, endoscopic operation might exacerbate myocardial ischemia, compromise cardiac pump function, and thus induce systemic hypoperfusion. Moreover, as reported by Garlapati et al., for patients with GIB and ESRD, age and acute respiratory failure were associated with worsened in-hospital mortality [28]. Similarly, Parikh et al. showed that age, mechanical ventilation, and hypovolemic shock increased mortality in GIB patients receiving hemodialysis [29]. Patients with acute respiratory failure were likely to receive positive-pressure ventilation, which compromised cardiac output, thereby exacerbating the hypoperfusion status. Researchers also reported that AKI might induce cytokine storms, leading to pulmonary edema and acute respiratory distress syndrome, which in turn exacerbated AKI [30, 31]. Furthermore, as reported by Solanki et al., groups of hemodialysis had higher all-cause mortality than those not in the context of GIB and AKI [32]. Anticoagulants were routinely required during hemodialysis, and patients were susceptible to significant hemodynamic fluctuations, making hemodialysis a risk factor for poor prognosis.

On the other hand, patients with upper gastrointestinal angiodysplasia and hyper-lipidemia showed improved survival outcomes. Cholesterol participated in synthesis and repair of vessel walls, maintaining vascular integrity and elasticity. Low cholesterol might increase vascular fragility and thus the risk of rupture. Hypocholesterolemia was recognized as a risk factor for GIB in elderly populations, associated with poor prognosis [33]. Notably, conditions such as malnutrition, or malignancies were also responsible for hypolipidemia, which further exacerbated risk of mortality [34, 35]. For patients with upper gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, endoscopic argon plasma coagulation achieved great hemostatic efficacy, contributing to favorable clinical prognosis [36, 37].

Among predictive models developed in this study, XGBoost and random forest models exhibited notable overfitting. Additionally, the complexity and relatively poor interpretability limited their clinical applications. In comparison, the Logistic predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.851 in the test set. In external validation cohorts, the AUC reached 0.809 and 1.000. The model showed good interpretability, and reliable identification of high-risk patients. The LASSO regression model further simplified variables while remaining an AUC of 0.783. However, the robustness of LASSO regression model was compromised in validation cohorts.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore characteristics of patients with renal insufficiency and UGIB among whom endoscopic intervention fails to improve mortality significantly. Furthermore, for patients who receive endoscopic intervention, we innovatively identify independent risk factors for mortality. Compared with XGBoost, random forest, and LASSO regression models, the Logistic predictive model demonstrates superior performance, achieving a balance among variable complexity, AUC, and robustness.

There were some limitations in our study. Firstly, there was inevitable information bias since we mainly collected patients from the MIMIC-IV and the NIS database. Reasons for not performing endoscopy, presence of active bleeding, clinical course of UGIB, past medical history (e.g., status of H. pylori infection), and long-term survival outcomes were unavailable [38, 39]. Secondly, limited sample size (n = 37) in our hospital validation cohort was associated with reduced statistical power and possible model overfitting (AUC = 1.000). To balance these limitations, multicenter prospective studies involving larger populations would be needed to further improve the reliability of our conclusions and reduce the risk of overfitting.

In conclusion, for patients with UGIB and renal insufficiency, endoscopic intervention could not significantly reduce mortality when serum creatinine > 354 µmol/L. Considering severe cardiorespiratory complications following endoscopic operations and limited medical resources, endoscopic intervention might not be recommended in such cases. Among those receiving endoscopic intervention, risk factors for mortality were revealed, and the Logistic predictive model was developed to effectively identify high-risk populations. Further multicenter prospective studies involving larger populations would be warranted to confirm our findings and to promote better management of patients with renal insufficiency and UGIB.
