Sex-specific cardiometabolic responses during microgravity simulation in European Space Agency VIVALDI dry immersion
Authors: Adrien Robin, Nastassia Navasiolava, Audrey Bergouignan, Claire Laurens, Lucas Frassin, Dominique Larrouy, Isabelle de Glisezinski, Isabelle Harant-Farrugia, Chantal Simon, Anne Pavy-Le Traon, Marc Kermorgant, Laurence Vico, Peter Fernandez, Marie-Thérèse Linossier, Angèle Chopard, Guillaume Py, Michael Tipton, Anne Tessier, Valérie Moal, David Andrew Green, Alexander Choukér, Dominique Moser, Pierre Denise, Hervé Normand, Elisabeth Rosnet, Marie-Pierre Bareille, Rebecca Billette de Villemeur, Guillemette Gauquelin-Koch, Ines Antunes, Angelique Van Ombergen, Marc-Antoine Custaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Exposure to microgravity is marked by enhanced physical inactivity, reduced gravitational loading and fluid shifts. These characteristics provide a unique model for medical research. Dry immersion is used as a ground-based model to simulate and investigate some of the physiological effects of microgravity experienced by astronauts (body fluid shifts, lack of mechanical support, unloading of the musculoskeletal system and hypokinesia). To date, there is limited knowledge about the effect of sex on cardiovascular and metabolic responses to dry immersion.Methods:This study integrates results from two 5-day dry immersion validation campaigns conducted by the European Space Agency (ESA): VIVALDI-1 (women, n = 18) and VIVALDI-2 (men, n = 19), where a wide range of physiological data was collected before, during, and after immersion. We investigated hormones changes and fluid redistribution, orthostatic tolerance, aerobic capacity, physical activity, muscle force, venous function, body composition, glucose tolerance, metabolic rate, lipid and bone turnover profiles, endothelial responses, with particular attention to sex-based differences.Results:Our findings reveal that dry immersion induces an acute multi-system deconditioning, affecting cardiovascular, metabolic, and fluid-regulation systems, which is largely comparable between men and women, although women show lower orthostatic tolerance, greater elevation of the atherogenic index, and increase in bone resorption biomarker.Conclusions:These results establish dry immersion as a valuable tool for studying the physiological adaptations of astronauts to microgravity and understanding sex-specific responses, while providing a comprehensive control dataset for the development of future countermeasures.Subject terms: Cardiovascular biology, Metabolism

Introduction

Enhanced physical inactivity coupled with the lack of mechanical loading and fluid redistribution defines the microgravity environment, providing a unique and relevant model for medical research.

Exposure to the microgravity environment during spaceflight leads to physiological multi-system deconditioning, affecting the cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal, and immune systems1. Understanding these changes is essential not only for maintaining astronaut health and performance but also for advancing insight into conditions relevant to healthcare on Earth, such as cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, muscle atrophy, bone demineralization, and aging. Microgravity induces continuous physiological remodeling with structural and functional changes in various organs. Actual and modeled microgravity, implemented in healthy subjects without confounding factors such as obesity or hypertension, allows the study of extreme sedentary behavior and the exploration of a fully reversible deconditioning2.

Sex differences might influence physiological responses to microgravity. Despite females representing an increasing proportion of space crew, there is little evidence for widespread sex-specific differences in physiological deconditioning. However, female astronauts have been reported to experience more pronounced hypovolemia and baroreflex impairment3–5, contributing to a greater susceptibility to post-flight orthostatic intolerance. To date, the metabolic adaptation during spaceflight has not been directly compared between men and women. Therefore, the degree of intra-individual compared to sex-related differences is unclear. The recent emergence of private space programs is creating opportunities for civilians to experience and be exposed to microgravity, albeit briefly. Such missions offer the opportunity to test a greater number and diversity of individuals exposed to spaceflight, which may assist elucidation of mechanisms6. Several short-duration private missions, such as Inspiration4 (2021: 3 days, 2 females), Polaris Dawn (2024: 5 days, 2 females) and Fram2 (2025: 3 days, 2 females), have already been conducted in Low Earth Orbit without the traditional crew of highly trained professional astronauts7. In addition, the first woman to go on a lunar mission has been recently selected (Artemis II, 10-day lunar flyby). Therefore, there is a need to characterize the effects of short-term microgravity exposure, particularly in females, where there is a lack of physiological data and direct sex-related comparisons8.

To simulate the effects of microgravity on Earth, the dry immersion model is used to minimize gravitational load and remove structural support from the body through buoyancy9. Acute physical inactivity and fluid centralization in dry immersion have been shown to contribute to hypovolemia (15–20%), orthostatic intolerance, in addition to inducing metabolic changes and musculoskeletal impairments10,11. As a result, dry immersion is recognized as an effective ground-based model for simulating and investigating the physiological effects of microgravity. To date, however, the integrated cardiovascular and metabolic responses in both men and women using a consistent simulation protocol have not been explored.

Between September 2021 and November 2022, with support from the European Space Agency (ESA), two dry immersion campaigns were conducted in women and men (VIVALDI-1 and VIVALDI-2, respectively). The main objectives were (1) to characterize the potential sex-based physiological differences in the response to 5-day dry immersion, and (2) to establish a reference dataset for future studies aiming at testing new countermeasures. Several findings have already been published, including results in women only11,12, and iron metabolism13 and immune biomarkers across both sexes14.

In this comprehensive study, we focus specifically on sex differences in cardiovascular and metabolic responses. We show that 5 days of strict dry immersion in healthy women and men induces pronounced hypovolemia, orthostatic intolerance, reduced aerobic capacity, cardiometabolic alterations, and changes in venous function, bone turnover and endothelial activation. Most of these adaptations are broadly similar between sexes, but women exhibit lower orthostatic tolerance, greater impairment in insulin sensitivity, greater elevation of the atherogenic index and a tendency toward higher bone resorption biomarkers. These findings support the use of short-term dry immersion as a ground-based microgravity analog to provide a reference dataset to guide future countermeasure development.

Methods

The complete methods followed for both VIVALDI-1 and VIVALDI-2 studies have been extensively described in previous publications11–13.

Ethical approval

The experimental protocols conformed to the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the French National Ethics Committee (VIVALDI-1: ID-RCB: 2021 A00705-36; VIVALDI-2: ID-RCB: 2022-A00881-42) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (VIVALDI-1: NCT05043974; VIVALDI-2: NCT05493176). All participants provided their written informed consent.

Participants

The two dry immersion campaigns were conducted at the MEDES space clinic (Toulouse, France) between September 2021 and November 2022, and 20 young healthy female participants for VIVALDI-1 and 20 young healthy male participants for VIVALDI-2 were recruited. These studies were conducted as two separate single-sex campaigns; participants were not randomized or matched between cohorts, and recruitment within each campaign was independent and based on the similar inclusion and exclusion criteria. The call for candidates through advertisements via Internet and media started after regulatory approval. We recruited healthy male and female volunteers aged 20–40 years, with a body mass index between 20 and 26 kg m−2, V̇O₂max between 30 and 55 ml kg−1 min−1 in VIVALDI-1 and between 35 and 55 ml kg−1 min−1 in VIVALDI-2, and height between 158 and 180 cm (VIVALDI-1) or 158 and 185 cm (VIVALDI-2). All participants were informed about the experimental procedures and provided written consent. One female participant left the protocol on the first day of immersion due to a technical issue (technical problem with the bath lifting platform), and another could not be included for regulatory reasons (participant unexpectedly still in the exclusion period following participation in a previous unrelated clinical trial). One male participant withdrew from the study on the third day of immersion due to back pain. They were excluded from analysis. The data analysis finally included 18 healthy females and 19 healthy males.

General protocol

The participants arrived on the evening of B5 (B-before) and left the clinic on the morning of R2 (R-recovery). The protocol included 4 days of ambulatory baseline measurements before dry immersion (B4 to B1), 5 days (120 h) of dry immersion (D1 to D5), and 2 days of ambulatory recovery (R0, R1). Two participants in two separate baths simultaneously underwent strict dry immersion in the same room. The strict protocol did not allow participants to rise and stand up at all, to avoid a return of the GZ axis gravitational gradient during the microgravity simulation period. Only daily hygiene, weighing, and some specific measurements required extraction from the bath, where participants remained at a 6° head-down or supine position. Total out-of-bath time for the 120 h of immersion was mean (SD) 10.4(1.5) h in women and 6.7(0.8) h in men. Water intake was ad libitum in frames of 35–60 mL kg−1 day−1, and recorded. Dietary intake was standardized and adjusted to body weight throughout the study. Daily protein intake was set at 1.0–1.2 g kg−1 day−1, and total energy intake was individualized from basal metabolic rate (BMR), first estimated with the World Health Organization (WHO) equation, using the body weight measured on B4, then measured. Caloric intake was fixed at 160% of BMR during the ambulatory pre-immersion and recovery periods and at 130% of BMR during immersion. Water temperature throughout dry immersion was 32.8 ± 0.09 °C for women and 32.3 ± 0.10 °C for men. Air temperature was 22.2 ± 0.2 °C for women and 23.8 ± 0.3 °C for men at baseline, 24.2 ± 0.3 °C for women and 25.2 ± 0.4 °C for men during dry immersion, 22.1 ± 0.2 °C for women and 23.4 ± 0.4 °C for men at recovery. During dry immersion participants wore light clothing. Standard clinical monitoring was carried out twice daily (morning and evening), with assessment of heart rate, brachial blood pressure (oscillometric method), and tympanic temperature. Sleep efficiency (defined as total sleep time divided by time in bed) at B3, B1, D1, D3, and D5 was assessed using a wrist-worn SOMNOtouchTM NIBP system (SOMNOmedics, Germany). The device was fitted between 08:30 and 09:30 and removed the following morning. Time in bed was fixed at 23:00–07:00, corresponding to an 8-h lights-off period, and participants were instructed to adhere to this sleep schedule. Data were analyzed using Domino Light 1.4 software (SOMNOmedics, Germany), with sleep–wake allocation based on the device’s proprietary actigraphy algorithm.

The dry immersion model is characterized by gravitational unloading due to buoyancy, and by the absence of specific support zones, hydrostatic pressure being distributed across the entire body surface. These important factors to simulate microgravity represent a major difference compared to the head-down bedrest model.

Hydrostatic pressure (a reflection of immersion depth)

Superficial pressure for different parts of the body has been recorded using a PicoPress device (Microlab, Padua, Italy). Superficial pressure has been measured using pneumatic sensors automatically inflated by a micro compressor. Several sensors have been placed on the mid-scapular, thoracic (xiphoid process), lumbar fossa, lower abdomen, and mid-calf level. We performed the measurement at D3 or D4, by placing the sensors on the participants in the supine position out of the bath, and data collection occurred in the bath half an hour after re-immersing them. The values were transformed from mmHg units to cmH2O for immersion depth estimation (1 mmHg ≈ 1.36 cmH2O).

Blood and urine samples

As described in Robin et al.11, antecubital venous blood samples were collected in the morning before (B4, B2, B1, and D1), during (in the evening at D1, in the morning at D2, D3, D5, and R0), and after dry-immersion in the morning (R1 and R2). Morning blood sampling was performed before breakfast. The AIP, a marker of lipoprotein particle size, was calculated from TG and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) as log(TG/HDL).

All 24-h urine samples were weighed and pooled into the storage container kept at +4 °C. For each 24-h pool, aliquots were prepared and frozen at −80 °C. The partial water balance was calculated as the difference between consumed water and urine volume.

Plasma volume evolution

Plasma volume (PV) evolution was estimated using the Dill & Costill method15. We used hemoglobin (Hb; measured with HemoCue Hb-201® system) and hematocrit count (Hct; measured with microcentrifugation) to estimate the percent change in PV on D1 evening (+8 h), D3-morning, D5-morning, R0 morning compared to baseline (D1-morning before the onset of immersion).

With HbB and HctB being baseline Hb and Hct levels, and Hbi and Hcti being Hb and Hct on further days (D1 evening, D3, D5, and R0).

Body mass and composition: bio-impedance and DXA

We estimated body composition with bio-impedance and DXA scan. Bio-impedance measurements were performed by trained operators using a standardized protocol, with electrode positions marked on the skin and reproduced at each visit, and DXA scans were acquired by the medical doctor on duty according to the same positioning and acquisition procedures throughout the campaigns. Bio-impedance measurements by Bodystat QuadScan 4000 (Bodystat Ltd., Isle of Man, United Kingdom) were performed in supine position when out of the bath on the morning of B1 (baseline measurement), in the evening at D1, and on the morning of D3 and D5. An additional measurement was done during the second VIVALDI campaign in men at R1 to have a recovery time point. Whole-body wrist-ankle measurement was used to estimate total body water (TBW), extracellular water (ECW), intracellular water (ICW), fat-free mass (FFM), and fat mass. Segmental impedancemetry (from knee to ankle) was performed to assess volume evolution of the calf segment.

At B4 and D5, DXA assessment of body composition (total FFM and fat mass) and bone mineral density was performed using a QDR 4500 W scanner with software version 11.2 (Hologic, Nassy, France). The DXA scanner was calibrated each morning before measurements using the manufacturer’s standard quality-control procedure.

Whole blood viscosity

The whole blood viscosity was estimated as described by Duyuler et al.16 for low shear rate as (1.89×Hct) + 3.76×(total protein-78.42) where Hct is hematocrit in %, total protein in g L−1 and whole blood viscosity in centipoise (cP). Hematocrit and protein concentration used for this calculation were taken from blood draw measurements and automated lab analysis to have the same data point.

Ultrasonography of the optic nerve sheath

Ocular measurements were performed at B2, D1, D3, D5, and R1 by investigators trained in ocular ultrasonography to assess the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD). Ultrasound measurements were performed with a linear high-frequency probe (Orcheo- Lite, Sonoscanner, Paris, France), and Ondina software (v.1.1.2, Sonoscanner, Paris, France) was used for analysis. The probe was positioned over the closed eyelid and adjusted to optimize the visualization of the ONSD. Measurements were conducted in two-dimensional mode, with the ONSD assessed 3 mm posterior to the ocular globe for both the right and left eyes in the sagittal plane. The final value was calculated as the average of the measurements from both eyes.

Orthostatic tolerance test: lower body negative pressure (LBNP)

Prior to and immediately at the end of the dry immersion period, we performed a Lower Body Negative Pressure test (LBNP test). A familiarization session was done during the BDC period before the test session to familiarize the volunteers with the equipment and the test procedure. The lower part of the volunteer’s body was enclosed in a hermetic chamber, inside which the pressure can be decreased.

The orthostatic tolerance test was conducted in the morning (09:00–10:00) in a temperature-controlled room (range 22–26 °C) on B3 and R0 (first orthostatic challenge after dry immersion, before standing up). After a 5-min supine baseline data collection, negative pressure was applied from −10 mmHg to −60 mmHg with an increment of −10 mmHg every 3 min. The test was considered finished after completing the −60 mmHg step. Participants were considered intolerant and the test was stopped upon the appearance of pre-syncopal signs, a request to stop, or the occurrence of stopping criteria listed in ref. 11. Finger blood pressure (VIVALDI-1: Nexfin, BMeye, U.S.; VIVALDI-2: Finapres NOVA, Finapres Medical Systems Enschede, the Netherlands) and standard electrocardiogram (ECG; Biopac, ECG 100C, United States) were recorded continuously. Tolerance time was assessed, and the cumulative orthostatic tolerance (OT) index was calculated by summing the product of duration and negative pressure (min x mmHg).

Aerobic capacity: V̇O2peak test

Aerobic capacity was assessed by a peak oxygen uptake (V̇O2peak) test. Incremental dynamic exercise was performed on a cycle ergometer (Ergometrics 800 S, Ergoline, Bitz, Germany) in the sitting position, at B2 and R0 in the afternoon (16:00–17:00). ECG and blood pressure were monitored. Breath-by-breath V̇O2 was recorded with an Oxycon Pro metabolic cart (E. Jaeger, Hochberg, Germany) using a face mask. Data were analyzed using LabManager software (v. 5.3.04, Cardinal Health, Germany). Participants cycled at 70 rpm for 3 min at 45 W for women and 50 W for men, followed by a 15 W increment every minute for women and 25 W increment for men until peak exertion was reached (70 rpm no longer maintained), which was considered as the V̇O2peak (V̇O2 average for the last 30 s of maximum load).

Continuous physical activity: actigraphy

To quantify daily leg movements, as an index of physical activity level, participants wore accelerometers (Actigraph GT3X+, LLC, Fort Walton Beach, FL) on the ankle from B4 to R1 continuously (except for showering). Data were processed using ActiLife5 software (Actigraph, FL, USA).

Muscle force: maximal voluntary contraction

The muscle force was assessed with a maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVC) test on the left leg in the sitting position. The strength level of the knee and ankle flexors and extensors was measured at B4 and R1 using a ConTrex® device (Physiomed; Schnaittach, Germany) with Human Kinetics 1.7.5 software. MVC was determined at 80° extension of the knee and 0° extension of the ankle. To determine the MVC, the maximum force level (Nm) reached during three complete sets of extension/flexion contractions was recorded. If the participant was still improving at the third contraction, successive contractions were recorded until no further improvement was observed.

Calf venous functions: venous occlusion plethysmography

Calf venous functions were determined at B1, D4, and R1 in the morning using an Air Plethysmograph APG® 1000 (ACI Corporation, San Marcos, CA, USA). Plethysmography data were collected through a Biopac device with AcqKnowledge 5.0 software (Biopac Systems, CA, USA).

The left calf has been studied in supine position outside of the bath and was slightly elevated to ensure venous emptying and for the measuring air cuff not to touch the bed. During testing, the pressure in the cuff was continuously measured, reflecting variations in calf volume. As performed by Louisy et al.17, a pre-measurement calibration was done by injecting 100 mL of air into the measuring cuff and by assessing the corresponding changes in cuff pressure.

A manual pneumatic cuff was applied on the thigh, just above the knee, and inflated to a 30, 40, and 50 mmHg pressure (below diastolic arterial pressure) to induce venous occlusion. For each venous occlusion step, a pressure curve was obtained with an increase in calf volume followed by a plateau, and a decrease in calf volume returning to pre-occlusion values with thigh cuff deflation. Venous occlusion was applied long enough (3 to 5-min) to reach the plateau of the volume curve.

The following parameters were measured from this curve:

Arterial Flow Index (AFI): A rapid increase in calf volume at the beginning of occlusion is caused by the arterial flow18. The AFI is calculated as the ascending slope of the linear section of the filling part. Expressed in increased volume min−1. The 40 mmHg step values were taken for calculation.

A custom-made measuring tape was used to measure (every 3 cm) the calf volume before and after dry immersion, and has been used for calf volume normalization.

During the VIVALDI-1 campaign in women, the very first participant underwent a plethysmography protocol with slight differences in occlusion pressure steps and device set-up. The protocol was then adjusted and standardized for the rest of the two campaigns. Thus, we decided to exclude this first woman from venous plethysmography analysis, leading to a n = 17 for women, and n = 19 for men.

Calf muscle and skin cross-sectional area with MRI

To estimate calf volume, we assessed calf cross-section area with MRI on a 1.5T machine (Magnetom Sola, Siemens Healthineers) at B3 and D5 in the evening (20:00–21:00) using a T1-weighted Dixon technique. For the CSA measurement, the largest slice of the calf cross-section for both legs was analyzed (the same at B3 and D5, respectively, to the distal apex of the patella). In both VIVALDI-1 and -2, twelve participants performed MRI, as the others participated in a sub-study with core temperature recording using non-MRI-compatible capsules.

Glucose tolerance: OGTT and HOMA-IR

An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed at B2 and D3 (in women, VIVALDI-1) or D5 (in men, VIVALDI-2) in the morning. Plasma glucose and insulin were measured at baseline (fasting) and 30, 60, and 120 min after 75 g of glucose ingestion. Total area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for both glucose and insulin plasma concentrations. The Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (ISI) and the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR, only with fasting glucose and insulin) were calculated as described in ref. 11.

Resting metabolic rate: indirect calorimetry

We measured oxygen consumption (V̇O2) and carbon dioxide excretion (V̇CO2) at arrival (B5), pre-immersion (B1 in women, B2 in men) and end-immersion (D5) while participants were in a fasting state, at rest, and at thermoneutrality. Canopy dilution respirometry measurements (Quark RMR, Cosmed, Italy) using OMNIA 2.0 software (Cosmed, Italy) were performed 2 times during 20 min. A gas tank of 16.15% O2 and 4.0% CO2 was used in a mixing chamber to perform post-measurement simulations to correct V̇O2 and V̇CO2 raw data. The Ferrannini equation was used to calculate the resting metabolic rate19.

Bone remodeling markers

C-terminal crosslinked telopeptide of type I collagen [CTX pmol/L], procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide [P1NP mg/L], and osteocalcin [OC ng/mL] were analysed by automated chemiluminescence immunoassay (IDS-iSYS automated analyzer, Boldon, UK). Undercarboxylated [Glu-OC ng/mL] and carboxylated [Gla-OC ng/mL] were measured by enzyme-immunoassay (EIA) kits. Serum total calcium [Ca mmol/L] was evaluated by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Cobas®8000 modular analyzer, Roche Diagnostics Ltd., Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m for text and dot plots, and as minimum, 25th percentile, median, 75th percentile, and maximum for box plots, if not indicated otherwise. The effects of dry immersion and sex on the variables of interest were tested by a two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Geisser–Greenhouse correction with sex (women vs. men) as the between-subject factor and time (study day or condition) as the within-subject factor, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests to account for multiple comparisons, or with fitting a Geisser–Greenhouse-corrected mixed model (maximum likelihood method) if data points were missing. Statistical significance was set at adjusted p ≤ 0.05, and exact p-values are reported for all tests either directly on the figures for post hoc comparisons or in the figure legends for main effects, rounded to three decimal places to preserve readability. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 10.2.0.

Biological replicates were individual study participants who completed the 5-day dry immersion campaigns (VIVALDI-1: women, n = 18; VIVALDI-2: men, n = 19). For each outcome, one value per participant and per time point entered the statistical analysis, unless otherwise specified.

Results

A total of 18 female (VIVALDI-I) and 19 male (VIVALDI-2) participants completed 5-day dry immersion campaigns (see Fig. 1 for dry immersion model characteristics and vital signs, and Table 1 for anthropometric data). The study protocol consisted in 4 Baseline days (B4 to B1), 5 days of dry immersion (D1 to D5), and 2 Recovery days (R0, R1). The complete protocol is detailed in Robin et al11. During immersion, subjective back pain increased, and sleep quality decreased (Fig. 1c), with back pain inversely associated with sleep quality in women (Supplementary Fig. 1a). However, we observed no significant main effect of sex on sleep quality, back pain, or general discomfort, indicating comparable overall tolerability of dry immersion between women and men. Sleep efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 1c) was within the normal range at baseline (>80%). During immersion, sleep efficiency declined significantly (main immersion effect, p < 0.001), with the greatest reduction occurring on the first night: by 13 ± 5% in women and 19 ± 4% in men, followed by partial recovery thereafter. Sleep efficiency tended to be higher in women than in men (main sex effect, p = 0.054). For the following results, the effects of dry immersion and sex on the variables of interest were tested by a two-way repeated measures ANOVA (or fitting a mixed model with maximum likelihood method if data points were missing).

Fluid shift-induced hypovolemia, hydration, and water distribution changes

Hypovolemia plays an important role in deconditioning related to spaceflight. This hypovolemia is mainly due to the thoraco-cephalic fluid shift when exposed to microgravity. We investigated first the hormonal regulation of volemia during dry immersion. Renin and aldosterone participate in water balance regulation and were decreased 8 h after the onset of immersion (before a rebound effect on D3) concomitant with an increase in Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP), released in response to cardiomyocyte stretch (Fig. 2a), without significant difference between men and women. The partial water balance (difference between water intake and diuresis) decreased in women and men by about 1050 mL and 1600 mL at D1 compared to B1, respectively (Fig. 2b). We assessed the plasma volume evolution by Hb-Hct Dill & Costill formula and observed a rapid decrease on the evening of the first dry immersion day 8 h after the onset of immersion (−11.9 ± 1.6% in women and −13.4 ± 1.4% in men), the peak of plasma volume depletion occurred on the third day with a decrease of −24.0 ± 1.2% in women and −27.6 ± 1.8% in men. The plasma volume remained reduced at the very end of the immersion on the morning of R0, with a decrease of −18.7 ± 1.6% in women and −20.2 ± 1.5% in men. All the plasma volume changes listed above are statistically different from baseline, but without differences between men and women (Fig. 2c). Urinary chemistry and complete blood count assessment are presented in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4. We then measured changes in total body water content and segmental calf volume by bioimpedance, as calf volume change can reflect a direct mechanical consequence of the fluid shift induced by dry immersion. The total body water decreased at D5 in women by 4.0 ± 0.4% and in men by 4.5 ± 0.3% compared to B1 (Fig. 2d), mostly due to a loss of extracellular fluid (Fig. 2e, f). The calf segmental volume significantly decreased at D5 in women by 6.1 ± 1.0% and in men by 7.0 ± 1.1% compared to B1 (Fig. 2g). There were no statistical differences between men and women. We finally quantified the whole blood viscosity evolution (Fig. 2h), calculated from blood hematocrit and protein content. The whole blood viscosity significantly increased during dry immersion from 53.3 ± 2.6 cP at B1 to 87.2 ± 4.1 cP at D5 in men, and from 37.2 ± 3.7 cP at B1 to 68.4 ± 4.1 cP at D5 in women and decreased significantly at R1. The whole blood viscosity was expectedly higher in men, however, without a sex difference in the percentage increase at D5 (+ 71 ± 11% in men vs. +107 ± 25% in women; sex-by-immersion effect: p > 0.05).

Finally, we examined the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) as an indirect marker of intracranial pressure (Fig. 2i). The ONSD increased during dry immersion compared to baseline, starting very early at D1 (+7 ± 2% in women and +4 ± 1% in men, measurement done approximately 4 h after the onset of immersion) and remained increased at R1 (+ 6 ± 2% in women and +5 ± 1% in men), with no statistical differences between men and women. Overall, these data indicate a DI-induced early reduced fluid content associated with hypovolemia, concomitant with an upward fluid shift, closely mimicking what was observed during spaceflights, and without notable sex differences in the response to simulated microgravity.

Dry immersion induces cardiovascular deconditioning

Orthostatic intolerance, increased heart rate, and decreased aerobic capacity are parts of the triad characterizing the cardiovascular deconditioning experienced by astronauts returning to an Earth-like gravity. We quantified the cardiovascular deconditioning after dry immersion with a V̇O2peak test to assess aerobic capacity, and a lower body negative pressure test (LBNP) to assess orthostatic tolerance and associated cardiovascular responses (Fig. 3). V̇O₂peak test results are shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 1. The V̇O2peak decreased in both men and women (from 38.7 ± 1.5 to 35.9 ± 1.3 mL kg−1 min−1 in women, and from 45.3 ± 1.0 to 42.4 ± 0.9 mL kg−1 min−1 in men, loss in peak oxygen uptake being about −6% for both sexes). During the incremental V̇O₂peak tests, pre-exercise sitting HR (average of the last 30 s before cycling at 45 W in women and 50 W in men) increased after dry immersion by 17 ± 3 bpm in women and 7 ± 4 bpm in men (unpaired t-test for ΔHR, p = 0.051). Peak power output (highest power maintained for ≥30 s) decreased by 7 ± 2% in women and 5 ± 1% in men (p = 0.27). Peak HR (average of the last 30 s of exercise) remained unchanged in both sexes, and values were close to the age-predicted maximal HR (“220-age”). Exercise duration (excluding the initial 3-min step at 45 W in women and 50 W in men) decreased by 9 ± 2% in women and 7 ± 1% in men (p = 0.39). These results demonstrate a significant cardiovascular deconditioning after immersion, as observed after spaceflights.

We quantified a significant decrease in the orthostatic tolerance index during the LBNP test (Fig. 3b), calculated as the sum of the product of duration and negative pressure (by 41 ± 6%, from 589 ± 21 to 359 ± 40 min mmHg in women, and by 30 ± 7%, from 571 ± 22 to 407 ± 41 min mmHg in men) (of note, one male subject who tolerated the test until the second minute of the last step of 60 mmHg after immersion, but stopped at the end of 20 mmHg step before immersion, was not taken into account for averaged orthostatic tolerance index). The heart rate at the first LBNP step (Fig. 3c) significantly increased after immersion (+ 16 ± 2 bpm in women, and +16 ± 3 bpm in men), with no significant difference between men and women. To further describe the dry immersion effect on men and women, we focused on the percentage of finishers at every minute during the LBNP test after dry immersion, and at the number of finishers for each LBNP step (Fig. 3d). The median LBNP survival time following dry immersion was 15 min for men and 13 min for women, with no statistically significant difference between sexes. We observed 82% finishers in women (14 out of 17) vs. 89% finishers in men (17 out of 19) for 30 mmHg LBNP step, 60% finishers in women (10 out of 17) vs. 80% finishers in men (15 out of 19) for 40 mmHg LBNP step, 35% finishers in women (6 out of 17) vs. 53% finishers in men (10 out of 19) for 50 mmHg LBNP step, and 6% finishers in women (1 out of 17) vs. 16% finishers in men (3 out of 19) for the last LBNP step of 60 mmHg, suggesting lower post-dry immersion orthostatic tolerance in women.

Heart rate, blood pressure, stroke volume, and total peripheral resistance at each LBNP pressure step are presented in Fig. 3e for group data, and in Supplementary Figs. 4–11 for individual data. The central hemodynamic response to LBNP (Fig. 3e) included an acute increase in heart rate, with no difference between men and women, concomitant with a decrease in stroke volume, values of stroke volume being expectedly lower in women due to a lower circulating blood volume. Together, these results further emphasize the orthostatic intolerance after dry immersion. Behavior of central hemodynamics variables during LBNP in men and women was identical for heart rate. However, women - but not men - showed a progressive rise in total peripheral resistance and diastolic blood pressure, suggesting greater vasoconstrictive effort to complete the test.

Dry immersion induces an acute lower limb deconditioning

In addition to cardiovascular adaptations, astronauts experience complete postural muscle discharge and less demanding physical effort during spaceflight. These changes are especially marked on the lower limbs, subsequent to a suppression of their own body weight. As dry immersion is characterized by buoyancy-induced gravitational unloading, we estimated physical inactivity at the leg level using ankle accelerometers. A significant acute decrease in daily physical activity counts was observed. The summarized counts at the ankle for daytime resulted in a 10-fold decrease in women and a 15-fold decrease in men during the D2–D5 period compared to baseline. The night activity was significantly lower than at daytime, resulting in a 40-fold decrease at baseline, and a 6- to 9-fold decrease during immersion (Fig. 4a). These results demonstrate the ability of the model to closely reproduce acute lower limb physical inactivity, leading to a sedentary deconditioning behavior. To explore the functional consequences of this period of physical inactivity, we performed an isometric contraction test for the knee and ankle main muscle groups. The maximal isometric knee extensor torque (in absolute values) significantly decreased after immersion (from 133 ± 7 to 123 ± 6 Nm, −8 ± 2%, in women and from 201 ± 15 to 187 ± 17 Nm, −8 ± 3%, in men), without significant changes for the other muscle groups. The maximal isometric torque was significantly higher in men compared to women for all muscle groups tested, but the percentage of change was not different between men and women (Fig. 4b).

Dry immersion leads to preserved venous functions

Venous functions are impaired during and after spaceflight, with an increased venous compliance contributing to orthostatic intolerance upon return to Earth20. To assess venous functions, we examined calf venous filling and emptying properties in response to a thigh occlusion (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 2). Baseline arterial flow index (AFI) was about 20–25% lower in women. AFI significantly decreased during dry immersion in men by 40.0 ± 5.9% and in women by 42.5 ± 5.5%, and was restored at R1 compared to baseline in both men and women. Venous filling index dynamics followed that of AFI. The venous half-emptying rate (VER50%) was significantly decreased during dry immersion in women by 12.3 ± 5.1% but not in men. The VER50% was globally significantly higher in men than in women. Finally, the venous distensibility index (VDI; a reflection of venous compliance) significantly decreased during dry immersion only in men, by 17.7 ± 5.1%, and remained decreased at R1 by 16.0 ± 6.6% compared to baseline. There was a significant difference between men and women at all time-points, the VDI being higher in men than in women. Taken together, these data suggest that venous functions are preserved during dry immersion. We further investigated the immersion effect on calf morphology with a calf muscle and skin (including subcutaneous adipose tissue) cross-sectional area (CSA) measurement from MRI. As a reflection of fluid shift and redistribution due to hydrostatic compression on the lower limbs, and, to a lesser extent, muscle atrophy, dry immersion induced a decrease in calf muscle and skin volume (Fig. 5b). The calf muscle and skin CSA respectively significantly decreased in men (−5.3 ± 0.4% and −5.9 ± 0.7%) and women (−6.1 ± 1.0% and −5.2 ± 1.6%) at D5 compared to B1. There was no significant difference between men and women. Collectively, these results demonstrated a significant lower limb deconditioning induced by dry immersion, mainly due to acute sedentary behavior and hydrostatic compression leading to a fluid redistribution.

Dry immersion reduces glucose tolerance

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) allows the characterization of metabolic impairment by assessing insulin response to glucose loading and glucose tolerance. We performed an OGTT at baseline and D3 in women and at baseline and D5 in men (Fig. 6a). The fasting glucose level was below the pre-diabetic threshold (i.e., 6.1 mmol L−1) in all volunteers at baseline (Fig. 6b), and remained unchanged during dry immersion. Two hours after glucose ingestion, blood glucose increased during dry immersion (6.3 ± 0.35 mmol L−1 in women and 6.7 ± 0.24 mmol L−1 in men) compared to B2 (5.6 ± 0.19 mmol L−1 in women and 6.0 ± 0.26 mmol L−1 in men). Blood glucose level after 120 min post-ingestion reached prediabetic levels (i.e., >7.8 mmol L−1) in three women at D3 and one man at D5. We computed the total area under the curve (AUC) for both plasma insulin and glucose (Fig. 6c). The AUC for insulin significantly increased during dry immersion compared to baseline in both groups (+ 71 ± 14% in women and +67 ± 15% in men), whereas AUC for glucose increased in women but not in men ( + 18 ± 4% in women and +7 ± 4% in men, sex-by-immersion effect p = 0.038). From the OGTT values, we calculated the Matsuda index, which reflects whole-body insulin sensitivity (Fig. 6d). The Matsuda index significantly decreased during dry immersion from 7.8 ± 0.7 to 4.6 ± 0.5 at D3 in women and from 8.1 ± 0.5 to 6.3 ± 0.5 at D5 in men, with significantly higher insulin sensitivity reduction in women (-41 ± 4%) than in men (−19 ± 6%) (sex-by-immersion effect p = 0.004). We finally computed the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). It significantly increased at D3 of dry immersion compared to B1: from 1.56 ± 0.13 to 2.20 ± 0.19 (+ 48 ± 10%) in women and from 1.30 ± 0.11 to 1.83 ± 0.19 (+ 53 ± 13%) in men. At D5, HOMA-IR tended to remain increased in women (+ 14.6 ± 6.6%, p = 0.08) but not in men (−0.8 ± 5.5%). Fasting insulin increased at D3 in women and men (by 40 ± 8% and 48 ± 12% vs. B1, respectively), and remained significantly increased at D5 in women (by 16 ± 6%) but not in men (2 ± 5%). Altogether, these results suggest that dry immersion reduced insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance, with slightly greater impairment in women, suggesting sex-related differences in substrate metabolism or muscle insulin signaling under unloading. This pattern is compatible with known modulatory effects of sex steroids on insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle glucose uptake, although our study was not designed to directly isolate these hormonal mechanisms.

Dry immersion further induces a metabolic impairment

Body composition estimated by both whole-body bioimpedance (BIA) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) methods (Fig. 7a, b) revealed a decrease in fat-free mass (FFM) of about 2 kg (DXA: −1.8 ± 0.2 kg in women and −1.5 ± 0.2 kg in men; BIA: −1.7 ± 0.2 kg in women and −2.2 ± 0.1 kg in men), with percentage decrease in FFM significantly more important in women (DXA: −4.3 ± 0.4% in women vs. −2.5 ± 0.3% in men). Decrease in FFM is already set in at D3 (BIA, Fig. 7b). This decrease in FFM likely reflects fluid redistribution rather than structural atrophy (which should also be considered). Finally, FFM decreased at the end of the immersion by 0.38 ± 0.04 kg (3%) in women and by 0.34 ± 0.06 kg (2%) in men. Overall, muscle dehydration indicated by the loss of extracellular water (Fig. 2e) likely contributes to this decrease in FFM. Total fat mass estimated by DXA was not modified by dry immersion. BIA estimation showed a slight increase in fat mass in both groups during dry immersion, which is likely an artefactual result caused by the reduction in FFM due to the decrease in TBW. The DXA, although not perfect, is less influenced by the hydration status.

We assessed resting metabolic rate (RMR) via gas exchange, as measured by indirect calorimetry, to determine basal energy requirements on the first day of baseline measurements (B4), thereby adapting food intake and measuring changes in energy expenditure. RMR is typically adjusted for FFM; however, when FFM changes are largely driven by fluctuations in water content, this adjustment may not accurately reflect true metabolic changes, so we chose not to apply it. No change in RMR was observed in women (Fig. 7c), while it decreased in men by −4 ± 1% (sex-by-immersion effect p = 0.04). The non-protein respiratory quotient remained unchanged (−0.7 ± 1.1% in women and −2.5 ± 1.4% in men, NS), indicating no detectable changes in fasting fuel use.

The increases in urinary nitrogen at D5 were more pronounced in men than women (28 ± 4% vs. 18 ± 4%; sex-by-immersion effect p = 0.02). This greater protein catabolism may be due to the higher FFM in men compared to women at baseline (57 ± 1 kg vs. 42 ± 1 kg, t-test: p < 0.0001).

Additionally, we assessed the metabolic activity and stress state through urinary cortisol, serum thyroxin (T4) and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (Fig. 7d). Daily urinary cortisol significantly increased only in men at D1 and D2 (from 30 ± 3 at B1 to 55 ± 8 μg 24 h−1 at D2) and returned to baseline level at D4, without being different between men and women during the global study period. Serum T4 and TSH increased similarly during dry immersion in both men and women, suggesting a metabolic stress. Fasting plasma triglycerides, total, high- and low-density cholesterol (LDL, HDL), and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) increased during dry immersion in men and women without being different between groups (Fig. 7e, g). To complete the lipid metabolism assessment, we measured fasting plasma adiponectin (adipocytokine involved in lipid and glucose metabolism), which was decreased significantly in women at D5 (−15 ± 3%, p = 0.003) but not in men (−7 ± 6%, p = 0.24). These results collectively suggest the development of a dyslipidemia.

To finally characterize the cardiovascular risk induced by lipid changes, we computed the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) as a predictor of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (Fig. 7f). At the end of dry immersion AIP increased more in women than in men (+ 0.16 ± 0.02 vs. +0.07 ± 0.01 at D5 compared to B1, sex-by-immersion effect p = 0.006), indicating a potential sex-related difference in the progression of vascular risk.

Dry immersion modulates bone turnover

During spaceflights, astronauts experience significant bone loss characterized by a change in bone formation and resorption turnover, mainly due to body unloading21.

We investigated the changes in bone formation and resorption activity by assessing changes in serum bone markers. As biomarkers of bone formation (Fig. 8a), procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) progressively decreased during the immersion period (−18 ± 3% in both men and women at R0 morning) and remained significantly lower than baseline at R2 in both groups. Total intact osteocalcin (OC) decreased during dry immersion (−9 ± 3% in women and −14 ± 3% in men at R0). Its carboxylated form (Gla-OC) decreased in a similar way, with raw levels being higher in men than women. The undercarboxylated form (Glu-OC) level was below baseline only at R2 in both women (−20 ± 3%) and men (−25 ± 4%).

Dry immersion induces an endothelial alteration

Endothelial impairment has been observed in astronauts as early as 1 week of spaceflight22. To assess the vascular endothelial state, we measured glycoprotein E-selectin as a marker of endothelial cell activation, as well as soluble endothelial factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and VEGF-receptor1 (VEGF-R1) (Supplementary Fig. 12). E-selectin increased during dry immersion, reaching +13 ± 2% in women and +19 ± 2% in men at D3. Plasma VEGF increased by +17 ± 5% in women and +56 ± 11% in men at D3, and VEGF-R1 followed the same tendency, with an increase of +34 ± 6% in women and +25 ± 3% in men at D3. There were no differences between men and women.

Discussion

Over the past decades, spaceflight has been shown to induce multi-system physiological deconditioning, with significant implications for astronaut health and performance. As future missions will include both men and women, understanding potential sex-specific responses is critical. Ground-based analogs such as dry immersion offer valuable insight into these adaptations and support the development of countermeasures. In this study, we investigated the physiological effects of short-duration dry immersion in both sexes, collecting comprehensive physiological data before, during, and after 5 days of exposure. To our knowledge, this represents the first direct comparison of male and female responses to an identical dry immersion protocol. Our aims were to (1) identify potential sex-based differences in physiological adaptation and (2) generate a reference dataset to inform future countermeasure development. This work builds on previous results from the VIVALDI-1 study in women11, as well as related investigations of ophthalmic and metabolic changes12,13. Dry immersion induced marked cardiovascular and metabolic deconditioning (Fig. 9) with important inter-individual variability in both groups, globally similar between sexes. However, some sex differences in physiological responses at the end of 5-day dry immersion could be highlighted. Thus, women exhibited more pronounced orthostatic intolerance (−41% decrease in OT-index vs. −30% in men), with seemingly greater vasoconstriction effort to finish LBNP steps. Women showed 1) a greater impairment in insulin sensitivity, as indicated by increase in HOMA-IR (+15% vs. −1% in men) and decrease in fasting adiponectin (−15% vs. −7% in men), 2) a more marked increase in the atherogenic index of plasma (+0.16 vs. +0.07 in men), 3) a slightly greater loss of fat-free mass (−4.3% vs. −2.5% in men), though lesser increase in total urinary nitrogen (+18% vs. +28% in men) and 4) a potentially stronger bone resorption response (increase in CTx +17% vs. +3% in men).

Bedrest studies and spaceflights have shown that lower limb muscle volume and volemia are reduced23–25. This hypovolemia can contribute to altered hemostatic function (viscosity or hematocrit level), possibly leading to cardiovascular events and endothelial damage26. Recently, a venous thrombosis in the internal jugular vein was discovered in an astronaut during a spaceflight onboard the International Space Station27, and this astronaut had the highest blood viscosity among the participants of the retrospective study28. In addition, the spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS) has been observed for long-duration flights and includes ocular structural changes. Elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) can also play a role in the development of SANS, and the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) is one predictor of ICP.

In our study, hypovolemia and the loss of body water reached their peak on the third day of dry immersion, consistent with previous results from dry immersion studies in men9,10,29. This expected hypovolemia (24–27% plasma volume depletion at the end of immersion) is subsequent to a thoraco-cephalic fluid shift and fluid centralization30. Here, the reduction in plasma volume and the negative water balance indicate that fluid loss during 5-day dry immersion originates predominantly from the extracellular compartment, particularly the vascular space, rather than from intracellular water.

Fluid redistribution and loss seems to be an important cause of the observed decrease in calf muscle volume. Indeed, calf volume assessed by measuring tape or estimated by segmental bioimpedance is rapidly restored 24 h post dry immersion, and skin volume decreases by about the same amount as the associated muscle section. Muscle atrophy might also contribute, as suggested by decrease in dry FFM. Moreover, in other dry immersion studies, m. vastus lateralis biopsy revealed a 10% muscle fibers CSA reduction after 3-day dry immersion31, and 22% reduction after 5-day dry immersion32. In our study, the whole blood viscosity, hematocrit, and vascular endothelial biomarker activity increased. Moreover, a prothrombotic platelet phenotype was observed after 3 days of dry immersion33. The space environment can increase cardiovascular risk. Alterations in rheological properties, in endothelial and in platelet functions may contribute to this risk, but it is worth noting that no thrombosis has ever been observed during dry immersion. In our study, as a result of the thoraco-cephalic fluid shift, the ONSD increased during dry immersion, without reaching the intracranial hypertension threshold11,12.

Women tend to demonstrate a greater loss of plasma volume than men during spaceflight34,35. This sex-difference was not observed in our study, either for plasma volume, water compartments, or segmental calf volume. This suggests that, at least over a short exposure, dry immersion induces a largely sex-independent hypovolemia. One possible explanation is that the combination of strong hydrostatic compression of the lower limbs and the rapid, parallel decrease of renin and aldosterone with a transient rise in BNP observed in both sexes in our protocol leads to a similar neuro-hormonal regulation of fluid balance in women and men. The whole blood viscosity was higher in men than in women, as expected, mainly because of a higher hematocrit level in men. Regarding SANS, the female sex appears to be protective, one reason being the smaller body size in women36. However, in our study, the ONSD increased without sex differences.

After spaceflight, impaired orthostatic tolerance has been reported when returning to Earth’s gravity, possibly due to hypovolemia37–40, changes in cardiac and baroreflex autonomic control41,42, and increased overall venous compliance of the lower limbs20. In addition, exposure to actual or simulated microgravity results in a nonlinear decrease in V̇O2peak, at a rate of nearly 1% day−1 for the first month43–45.

In our study, we observed a resting and effort tachycardia, a decreased V̇O2peak, and an orthostatic intolerance as part of the cardiovascular deconditioning triad subsequent to the hypovolemia and acute physical inactivity.

Women have been shown to be more susceptible to orthostatic intolerance after spaceflight. In our study, for orthostatic tolerance assessed by progressive LBNP test, the percentage of finishers for 30, 40, 50, and 60 mmHg LBNP steps was lower in women after dry immersion. Moreover, a progressive rise in total peripheral resistance and diastolic blood pressure in women suggests that women require greater vasoconstrictive effort to complete the test. Decrease in OT-index was more pronounced in women (−41% vs. −30% in men). However, we did not reveal a statistically significant difference between sexes for median survival time at R0 (13 min for women and 15 min for men). Data comparing the aerobic capacity of men and women after spaceflight is very scarce. In this study, the V̇O2peak decreased by about 6%, peak power by 5–7%, and exercise duration by 7–9% in both men and women, without differences in the diminution between groups. Peak heart rate remained unchanged and close to age-predicted maximal values, whereas sitting heart rate before exercise tended to be ~10 bpm higher in women than in men after dry immersion, suggesting slightly greater cardiovascular or autonomic strain in women despite similar relative reductions in peak power and exercise duration.

Maintaining the lower limb muscle volume and force is critical for extravehicular activities of future astronauts landing on the Moon and Mars. Moreover, skeletal muscle deconditioning is also mainly associated with changes in myotypology, an increase in muscle fatigability and metabolic changes46.

The dry immersion model induced a very acute degree of physical inactivity, measured by ankle accelerometry. This acute inactivity, in addition to the global body unloading, contributed to muscle deconditioning, shown by a decrease in knee extensors maximal isometric torque.

Trappe et al. observed a higher decrease in quadriceps volume in women compared to men, but no significant sex-related difference for triceps surae (calf) volume after 1 month of head-down bedrest47, consistent with our findings. We observed a global higher muscle force in men in the lower limbs, as expected, without sex-related difference in muscle force changes. The human musculoskeletal response to unloading is highly individual-dependent34.

Returning to the Moon and having Mars in sight is the goal of the present decade. Thus, investigating astronauts’ venous function and its contribution to orthostatic intolerance and venous thrombosis remains crucial. Altered venous filling and increased compliance have been observed during simulated48 and real short-term17 and long-term spaceflight20 using lower limb plethysmography.

In the present study, however, the venous compliance was not increased. We hypothesized this major difference in response is mainly due to a sustained hydrostatic compression of the lower limbs during dry immersion, whereas a partial and total calf tissue decompression occurs during bedrest and spaceflight, respectively. The same decrease in venous filling rate, related to drastically slower arterial inflow during dry immersion and bedrest has, however, been observed18, and is hypothesized to be caused by plasma volume depletion concomitant with higher blood viscosity during dry immersion.

In our study, baseline arterial filling rate was about 25% slower in women, presumably related to lesser blood volume per body weight. Baseline fast venous emptying rate also was slightly slower in women. Surprisingly, baseline venous distensibility was lower in women, while greater venous compliance in the lower limbs was expected predisposing to orthostatic intolerance. This reduced lower limb venous compliance in women is, however, in line with findings of Lindenberger et al.49, suggesting yet unknown structural differences in venous wall, perhaps linked to sex hormones effect on collagen-elastin ratio. In response to dry immersion, women, unlike men, further decreased venous emptying rate, but not venous compliance.

Reduced glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity along with metabolic stress are frequent complications during spaceflight50 and acute physical inactivity51. An increase in HOMA-IR52 and a shift in substrate use towards a greater reliance on carbohydrates over fat53 have been reported in astronauts after more than 3 months in space.

After 5 days of dry immersion, we observed a pronounced increase in fasting plasma TG, LDL, NEFA, and atherogenic index of plasma, suggesting a metabolic syndrome-like shift. Collectively, we observed the development of metabolic alterations, including the onset of metabolic stress, dyslipidemia, and decreases in insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance. However, no change in substrate use was noted.

The metabolic impairment reported during spaceflight was associated with a spaceflight-by-sex term, suggesting greater change in men, but in a small sample of 4 males and 4 females52. In our study, slight differences were observed in the response to dry immersion between men and women. At the end of dry immersion (day 5), women still showed a modest increase in fasting insulin (+16% vs. +2% in men), together with decrease in fasting adiponectin (−15% vs. −7% in men), higher HOMA-IR (+15% vs. −1% in men), and greater rise in AIP (+0.16 vs. +0.07 in men). Women lost slightly more FFM (−4.3% vs. −2.5% in men), though men had a more notable increase in urinary nitrogen excretion (+18% in women vs. +28% in men). Sleep quality and duration are well-known modulators of glucose sensitivity; short sleep (<6 h per night) is associated with higher fasting glucose and reduced insulin sensitivity, whereas adequate sleep helps maintain glycemic control54. In our study, sleep disruption was most pronounced during the first half of immersion, as shown by both questionnaire-derived sleep quality and reduced actigraphy-based sleep efficiency, which may partly contribute to the stronger metabolic impairment observed on D3. However, because women did not sleep worse than men (and in fact tended to have slightly higher sleep efficiency), the larger deterioration in glucose metabolism observed in women is unlikely to be attributable solely to differences in sleep quality.

Results on glucose tolerance are to interpret with caution as OGTT test was performed in different days – day 3 in women and day 5 in men. Women showed a higher proportion of prediabetic glucose levels 2 h post-ingestion (3/18 women vs. 1/19 men), a more than doubled increase in AUC for glucose ( + 18% women vs. +7% men), and a twice higher reduction in Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (−41% women vs. −19% men). From literature, 3-day dry immersion in men decreased Matsuda ISI by −31 ± 7%11,29. Overall, these data suggest that the dry immersion impact on OGTT outcomes is influenced by both sex and day of measurement, with impairment being more pronounced at day 3 exposure to dry immersion, than at day 5.

Bone adaptation to spaceflight and bedrest of several weeks leads to bone loss at the weight bearing sites of the skeleton because they are no longer under regular mechanical load. Bone turnover markers indicate unbalanced bone remodeling that results in more bone resorbed than deposited.

Our results during dry immersion suggest that acute unloading and physical inactivity resulted in a progressive decrease in bone formation markers that did not recover at R + 2. Bone resorption markers are less affected; however, serum calcium levels increase over the course of the dry immersion period, along with a marker of bone collagen degradation (CTx), the latter showing a significant rise in women and a trend in men by the end of the period.

Astronauts in space experience bone loss, and women on Earth are generally more prone to osteoporosis. It has been observed during long-term spaceflight that biochemical markers of bone formation and resorption respond similarly in 33 men and 9 women (after 49 to 215 days of flight), suggesting that sex does not significantly impact the rate of bone loss to unloading55. This observation is consistent with the present study showing that there is no major sex-difference in the changes for most bone markers levels during dry immersion. However, women exhibited a trend toward a greater increase in CTx by the end of the dry immersion (+17% in women vs. +3% in men), which implies a potentially higher bone resorption response in women.

We acknowledge several limitations. Some are inherent to the dry immersion model itself and have been detailed previously, including stress during the initial phase, boredom, and the lack of alignment between the onset of immersion and the menstrual cycle in women11. The menstrual cycle was not controlled for female participants, and the cycle phase was deduced from blood progesterone and estradiol dynamics. In comparing the VIVALDI-1 and VIVALDI-2 studies, it is important to note that OGTTs were performed on different immersion days (Day 3 for women and Day 5 for men), which may limit the direct comparability of glucose tolerance responses. However, HOMA-IR was assessed on the same days in both groups, allowing for consistent evaluation of insulin sensitivity. Moreover, although previous analyses in women11 did not reveal a significant influence of menstrual cycle phase on dry immersion–induced changes, a modest contribution of cycle-related variability in OGTT responses observed here cannot be fully excluded and should be addressed in future studies.

Additionally, the 5-day dry immersion period may have been too short to elicit significant sex-specific differences in physiological deconditioning, particularly in parameters such as muscle volume or bone density. These structural adaptations typically require a longer duration of microgravity or analog exposure to become detectable.

Conclusions

There are some particularities in cardiovascular and metabolic responses to 5-day dry immersion in women; however, responses are generally similar between men and women, supporting the inclusion of mixed-sex groups in future studies aimed at developing and validating countermeasures.

To further describe and characterize this dry immersion model, ESA’s Vivaldi program will continue with a new 10-day study comparing microgravity ground analogs. Dry immersion and head-down bed rest both mimic microgravity’s effects, but they likely differ in the speed and magnitude of those changes. Pathophysiological mechanisms may also vary between the two models in some specific aspects.
