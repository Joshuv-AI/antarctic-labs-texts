Development of Potency Assays for Therapeutic EVs: The Art of Congruence
Authors: Mario Gimona, Marca H. M. Wauben, Sun Young Lee, Marta Monguió‐Tortajada, Eva Rohde
Published: 2026

Introduction

Potency assays are essential to assess the biological activity of therapeutic candidates such as extracellular vesicles (EVs), as this activity reflects their therapeutic potential. These assays establish quantifiable functional measures that correlate with the Mechanism of Action (MoA), thereby ensuring both scientific validity and regulatory compliance. In addition, such assays help to ensure consistency in the manufactured product, including batch‐to‐batch reproducibility, shelf life, and overall stability. Beyond quality control, potency assays guide the determination of therapeutic dose in clinical applications, and provide a measurable link between EVs and their biological effect.

Potency assay readouts should not only demonstrate biological activity but also reflect the candidate drugs’ ability to elicit a specific and clinically meaningful effect. Additionally, potency testing should assist in establishing a cause‐and‐effect relationship between the molecular signaling cascades that drive the measured biological activity and clinical efficacy. Several publications have addressed this issue in the past (e.g., Papait et al. 2022).

Why and How to Develop Potency Assays for Native EV‐Based Drug Candidates?

The EV field is increasingly aware of the necessity to develop robust and correlative potency assays that can bridge EV composition, MoA, and therapeutic efficacy, despite the challenges of developing such assays for non‐engineered EV‐based therapies (Giebel and Lim 2025). Unlike small molecules or single‐agent biologics, native (unmodified) EV preparations are intrinsically heterogeneous and carry a complex mixture of bioactive cargo (proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites), making it difficult to pinpoint which component(s) are most relevant to the observed therapeutic effect. The composition is highly sensitive to manufacturing variables such as cell source, passage number, culture conditions, and purification methods. Even minor process changes can alter the EV cargo profile and surface protein repertoire in the final product. This process‐linked heterogeneity further complicates the design of assays that both capture the global biological activity of an EV‐based therapeutic and identify elements that are mechanistically responsible.

One function of a potency assay would be to correlate the biological activity of the product with the effective dose range in clinical applications (see also Bravery et al. 2013; Porat et al. 2015; Aries et al., 2023). Orthogonal measurement strategies to identify different properties of non‐engineered EVs with various physicochemical methods, with the goal of detecting method‐specific bias and interferences, should be ideally suited to address this issue. In preclinical and clinical development, independent from EV source or assay system, the ‘validation of analytical procedures’ is crucial for monitoring consistent EV isolation, storage conditions, batch‐to‐batch variations, enzymatic functionality or other potency assays (Lai et al., 2010; Adamo et al. 2025).

Multi‐omics, machine learning and AI could improve readout analysis and cut time and labor of potency assay development. Standards, controls and reference materials are highly relevant for the development of potency assays for EV‐based as much as for cell‐based therapeutic products (see Simon et al. 2024, 2025). However, despite existing knowledge (Gimona et al. 2021), the EV field pays surprisingly little attention to the availability and suitability of reference standards. Until now, mostly a reductionist approach has been used to elucidate the MoA of EV preparations by analyzing the cargo for candidate bioactive molecules (or attributes) that might potentially mediate the biological or therapeutic effect (e.g., Martin‐Rufino et al. 2019). Their relevance for mediating a biological response could then be functionally evaluated and validated in biological activity or potency assays.

From a purely linguistic standpoint, potency describes the power, strength, or effectiveness to influence something. In pharmacology, potency refers to the amount of drug needed to produce a specific effect, while the MoA describes how a drug achieves that effect. Thus, potency indicates the strength of a drug, while the MoA explains its mode of operation at a molecular or cellular level. Without a good understanding of the MoA, it is hard to imagine there will be a good potency test.

For native EVs, however, it may be necessary to consider multiple MoAs, each requiring distinct product attributes to serve as relevant potency measures. While having clear insight into all the MoA(s) is desirable, it is neither essential nor necessary for having an effective therapeutic agent (see examples in Simon et al. 2024). Establishment of at least one MoA for an EV‐based therapeutic should thus be a goal for developmental teams.

Another important consideration is that potency and efficacy are not the same. They are distinct pharmacological terms that describe different properties of a drug. A drug's effect can be evaluated in terms of potency and efficacy. Potency (strength) refers to the amount of drug needed to produce an effect (e.g., relief of pain or reduction of blood pressure). Efficacy is a drug's capacity to produce an effect (such as pain relief or lowering blood pressure). Potency is measured in laboratory tests while efficacy is measured by the outcomes from a preclinical study or clinical trial (Simon et al. 2024).

From a regulatory standpoint, potency does not necessarily need to be fully defined at the beginning of product development. However, potency should be proactively considered and addressed before regulatory authorities require it during the clinical trial application. At its core, the goal is to demonstrate congruence between laboratory findings and test results (in other words between the product profile and the preclinical data) with the clinical outcome in context with the target disease. Several recent manuscripts (Nguyen et al. 2020; Gimona et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2023; Phinney et al. 2023; Simon et al. 2025, Simon et al. 2025; Vignon et al. 2025) provide excellent guidance for the stepwise coordinated approach towards potency assay development and validation. In particular, Simon and colleagues (2024) present conceptual frameworks and process charts that clarify the relationship between MoA, potency and efficacy. For example, a product can be “potent but not efficacious” due to the choice of a wrong patient population or incorrect hypothesis regarding the disease mechanism (e.g., a potent anti‐cancer drug is not efficacious to treat headache). A product can also be “efficacious but not potent” due to an inadequate potency test or an alternate MoA, depending on the biological context. In congruence with the considerations in this statement, the examples in Simon et al. 2024, ‘are meant to illustrate concepts and are not intended as recommendations for potency tests or efficacy endpoint tests.’ But such guidance can assist in (a) setting practical goals for understanding the MoA of EVs, (b) establishing correlations between potency tests and clinical efficacy and (c) avoiding being led by hidden assumptions that result when concepts such as potency and efficacy are mixed up. Importantly, MoA, potency, potency tests, efficacy, efficacy endpoints and efficacy endpoint tests should be independently defined for any given EV‐based therapeutics to achieve clarity during discussions concerning correlations between potency test results and clinical outcomes (Simon et al. 2024).

Jump the Hurdle—Define Your EVs’ Potency!

With this ISEV Task Force statement on potency testing, we aim to encourage critical thinking about potency assay development for native EV‐preparations. Stringent application of this methodology and strategy to our selected EV‐resident or associated molecule(s) to critically probe the relevance and robustness of our assumptions for MoA, potency and efficacy will prove beneficial—no matter how painful the exercise and how frighteningly sobering the outcome might be.
