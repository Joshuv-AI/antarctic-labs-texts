Resveratrol‒loaded self‒assembled tetrahedral framework nucleic acids reshape the epileptic microenvironment by regulating oxidative stress and neuroinflammation via the SIRT3/SOD2 pathway
Authors: Jingwen Cui, Qilong Zhai, Zixiao Tan, Zhiru Zou, Mingyuan Zhang, Nana Gao, Jiahang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpilepsy management remains a significant clinical challenge, as conventional antiseizure medications primarily mitigate symptoms without addressing the core pathological drivers, specifically the vicious cycle formed by neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of potential neuroprotective agents is severely compromised by the blood‒brain barrier (BBB), poor stability, and insufficient accumulation at epileptic lesions. Therefore, engineering a BBB‒penetrating delivery strategy that simultaneously disrupts the vicious cycle of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress is critical for achieving disease‒modifying effects in epilepsy treatment.ResultsHere, we developed a biomimetic, brain‒targeting nanosystem (R‒tFNAs@PB) by anchoring resveratrol‒loaded tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) onto a Prussian blue (PB) core. This nanosystem effectively traversed the BBB and exhibited precise accumulation within hippocampal epileptic foci. Mechanistically, R‒tFNAs@PB acted as a dual‒function modulator. The PB core and resveratrol synergistically scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activated the SIRT3/SOD2 signaling pathway, thereby increasing the mitochondrial antioxidant capacity. This cascade effectively inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation and promoted the polarization of microglia from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti‒inflammatory M2 phenotype. In a mouse model of kainic acid‒induced epilepsy, the nanosystem significantly reduced neuronal damage, reduced the seizure frequency and severity, and ameliorated cognitive deficits.ConclusionsThis study presents a novel nanotherapeutic strategy that integrates microenvironment remodeling with neural repair. By leveraging the specific brain‒targeting capability of tFNAs and the synergistic antioxidant properties of the core‒shell structure, R‒tFNAs@PB represent a promising approach for treating refractory epilepsy through the precise regulation of the oxidative stress‒neuroinflammation axis via the SIRT3/SOD2 pathway.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04145-3.Keywords: Tetrahedral framework nucleic acid, Resveratrol, Epilepsy, Oxidative stress, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unpredictable seizures, and affects millions of patients worldwide. Historically, treatment has relied on antiseizure medications (ASMs) that manage symptoms by modulating ion channels or synaptic transmission [1–7]. However, the development of drug resistance in approximately 30% of patients underscores a critical limitation: current therapies primarily suppress the downstream symptom of “abnormal discharge” without addressing the underlying epileptogenic cascade [8, 9].

Recent advances have shifted the pathophysiological paradigm from a singular “excitatory‒inhibitory imbalance” to a complex triad involving neural, immune, and oxidative pathways. Specifically, the vicious cycle between neuroinflammation and oxidative stress is now recognized as a key driver of epileptogenesis [8]. Seizure‒induced excitotoxicity triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and massive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. This oxidative burden acts as a potent signal to prime microglia toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, promoting the release of cytokines (IL‒1β, TNF‒α) and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. This cascade disrupts homeostasis, amplifies oxidative stress in a positive feedback loop, and ultimately precipitates neuronal death and cognitive decline [9, 10]. Consequently, dual‒targeting strategies that simultaneously disrupt neuroinflammation and oxidative stress represent a frontier in epilepsy therapy. Resveratrol (Res), a natural polyphenol, has emerged as a promising candidate due to its potent antioxidant and anti‒inflammatory properties. However, its clinical translation is severely hindered by poor water solubility, chemical instability, and rapid metabolism, which prevent it from reaching therapeutic concentrations at the epileptic focus, particularly across the blood‒brain barrier (BBB) [11, 12]. Therefore, an advanced brain‒targeting delivery system is essential to harness the therapeutic potential of Resveratrol. Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) offer a robust solution to these delivery challenges. As a programmable DNA nanostructure, tFNAs possesses intrinsic advantages, including excellent biocompatibility, structural stability, and crucially, the ability to penetrate the BBB and facilitate cellular uptake [13–19]. Moreover, DNA nanostructures have evolved from static scaffolds to functional devices, with recent studies highlighting their potential in intelligent drug delivery and intrinsic ROS scavenging capabilities [20–22]. Thus, tFNAs are an ideal platform for constructing brain-targeted delivery systems.

Here, we developed a biomimetic, synergistic nanotherapeutic system (R‒tFNAs@PB) to remodel the epileptic microenvironment. In this design, a Prussian blue (PB) nanozyme core—endowed with intrinsic catalytic antioxidative activity—is encapsulated by a Resveratrol‒loaded tFNAs shell. This “core‒shell” architecture is engineered to address the triple challenges of drug stability, BBB traversal, and precise lesion targeting. We hypothesize that R‒tFNAs@PB will efficiently accumulate in epileptic foci, where the PB core and Resveratrol work synergistically to scavenge excess ROS and inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby repolarizing microglia from the proinflammatory M1 to the neuroprotective M2 phenotype. By integrating proteomic analyses, we sought to unravel the molecular mechanisms governing mitochondrial defense systems. This work aims to systematically verify the therapeutic efficacy and mechanism at the molecular, cellular, and whole-animal levels, providing a novel nanostrategy for epilepsy treatment.

Materials and methods

tFNAs

The tFNAs were synthesized via a programmable self‒assembly process using the four specific single‒strand DNA (ssDNA) strands (S1–S4, sequences listed in Table S1). Briefly, the four strands were mixed at an equivalent molar ratio in TM buffer (Tris‒HCl containing 10 mM MgCl₂). The mixture was subjected to a gradient annealing protocol using a thermocycler: the solution was heated to 95 °C for 10 min for complete denaturation, followed by controlled cooling to 4 °C over 60 min (cooling rate of ~1.5 °C/min). This process ensures the precise formation of the tetrahedral scaffold through DNA hybridization.

R‒tFNAs

Resveratrol (Res) was encapsulated into the tFNAs scaffold through molecular intercalation into the DNA double helices. Res was initially dissolved in anhydrous DMSO as a stock solution and subsequently added dropwise to the 250 nM tFNAs solution, ensuring that the final DMSO volume fraction was maintained below 2% (v/v) to preserve the structural integrity of the DNA framework. The mixture was stirred at 4 °C in the dark for 6 h. To remove unencapsulated Res and residual ssDNA, the solution was purified via ultrafiltration (30 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane, Millipore, USA) at 4 °C. The loading efficiency (LE) and entrapment efficiency (EE) were determined using an ultramicrospectrophotometer (NanoPhotometer N60, Implen, Germany). The calculations were performed as follows:

LE (nmol Res/nmol tFNAs) = (total resveratrol ‒free resveratrol)/(total tFNAs).

EE (%) = (total resveratrol ‒free resveratrol)/(total resveratrol) ×100%.

R‒tFNAs@PB

R‒tFNAs assembled with a 5’‒thiol‒modified S1 strand were employed to facilitate covalent anchoring onto the Prussian Blue (PB) core. Briefly, PB NPs were mixed with the thiolated R‒tFNAs at a molar ratio of 1:5000 and incubated overnight at room temperature with gentle agitation, allowing for the formation of stable Fe‒S bonds. To achieve high DNA grafting density and form a robust “Spherical Nucleic Acid (SNA)” architecture, a stepwise salt‒aging process was subsequently performed. The NaCl concentration was incrementally increased from 20 mM to 100 mM (in 20 mM increments every 30 min) to screen the electrostatic repulsion between neighboring DNA strands. After reaching 100 mM NaCl, the solution was incubated overnight at room temperature. The resulting R‒tFNAs@PB was purified by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min) and washed three times with deionized water. The final precipitates were resuspended in 5 mM PBS (pH 7.4) and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of R‒tFNAs@PB

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and high performance capillary electrophoresis were performed. Zeta potential analyses were performed by dynamic light scattering (Nano ZS, Malvern, England). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the R‒tFNAs were acquired using an SPM‒9700 instrument (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Transmission electron microscopy of R‒tFNAs@PB was performed using a transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV (JEM‒2100 F, JEOL, Japan).

Cell culture and treatments

BV2, SH‒SY5Y and bEnd.3 cell lines were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured in basic DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin‒Streptomycin‒Amphotericin B solution. The cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For in vitro experiments, the cells were randomly divided into five groups: (1) Control; (2) LPS; (3) LPS + tFNAs; (4) LPS + R‒tFNAs; and (5) LPS + R‒tFNAs@PB were established, The cells in Groups B, C, D, and E were treated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells in Groups C, D, and E were treated with tFNAs, R‒tFNAs and R‒tFNAs@PB (100 µg/mL), respectively, for an additional 24 h. The Control group was maintained in complete medium with an equivalent volume of PBS.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells in each group using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, RNA was reverse‒transcribed into cDNA. Quantitative real‒time PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real‒Time PCR System. The reaction mixture (total volume: 20 µL) consisted of 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green Premix, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6.4 µL of nuclease‒free water. The amplification protocol was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes (IL‒6, TNF‒α, IL‒1β, and IL‒10) were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the endogenous control. Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was quantified using an Annexin V–FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells from each experimental group were collected, washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 195 µL binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 µL Annexin V–FITC and 10 µL PI were added to each cell suspension, followed by incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.

Apoptotic cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences) within 1 h after staining. Data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.0). The total apoptotic rate was calculated as the sum of early apoptotic cells (Annexin V–FITC⁺/PI⁻) and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V–FITC⁺/PI⁺).

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of R-tFNAs@PB toward BV2, SH-SY5Y, and bEnd.3 cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 10³ cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.After cell attachment, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing R-tFNAs@PB at different concentrations (10, 30, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL). Following 24 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were further incubated for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

In vitro assessment of transport across the BBB

An in vitro BBB model was established using a Transwell system. bEnd.3 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a 12‒well insert at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After 8 days of culture to allow monolayer formation, BV2 cells were seeded in the lower chamber at the same density. To mimic neuroinflammation, BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL). Subsequently, the upper chamber was treated with the indicated formulations. After 24 h of incubation, BV2 cells in the lower chamber were collected and lysed via ultrasonication. The lysate was homogenized in 2 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. For extraction, 200 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of methyl tert‒butyl ether (MTBE) and vortexed for 5 min. The mixture was centrifuged again at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The organic supernatant was collected and dried under vacuum. The residue was reconstituted in 3 mL of methanol and analyzed using high‒performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Quantification of BBB penetration via ICP‒MS

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‒MS) was employed to detect the levels of iron (Fe) derived from the Prussian blue core, strictly quantify the transmembrane transport efficiency of the nanocarriers, and exclude the interference of drug leakage. Briefly, the in vitro BBB model was established using bEnd.3 cells as described above. Once the endothelial monolayer was formed (confirmed by TEER measurement), the medium in the upper chamber was replaced with fresh medium containing PB NPs, tFNAs@PB, or R‒tFNAs@PB at an equivalent particle concentration of 100 µg/mL. After an incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the medium from the basolateral chamber was collected. The samples, along with the initial dosing solutions (to determine C0), were digested with concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 90 °C to ensure complete dissolution. Cellular uptake assay

The ability of R‒tFNAs@PB to promote the uptake of M1 microglia after reacting with ROS was evaluated. First, BV2 cells were seeded into 35‒mm glass‒bottom confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells per dish. BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h to induce pro‒inflammatory polarization (M1 phenotype). Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing FITC‒labeled tFNAs, R‒tFNAs, or R‒tFNAs@PB (100 µg/mL).

After incubation for 2 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles. For visualization, cell membranes were stained with DiI (10 µM) and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 µg/mL) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the cells were washed again with PBS, and fluorescence images were captured using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM).

Measurement of ROS levels

Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) generation was detected using the oxidation‒sensitive fluorescent probe 2’,7’‒dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‒DA). Following the indicated treatments, the culture medium was removed, and BV2 cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, BV2 cells were incubated with serum‒free medium containing 10 µM DCFH‒DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, BV2 cells were washed three times with serum‒free medium to remove the extracellular residual probe. Fluorescence images were immediately captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 525 nm) was quantified using ImageJ software, and the results were expressed as relative fluorescence intensity normalized to the control group.

Analysis of the mitochondrial membrane potential (JC‒1 staining)

Changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) were assessed using a JC‒1 Fluorescent Probe Kit. Briefly, BV2 cells were seeded in 6‒well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. Following LPS stimulation (24 h) and subsequent sample treatments (24 h), the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with JC‒1 working solution (5 µg/mL) in the culture medium at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with cold JC‒1 staining buffer. Fluorescence images were immediately acquired using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope. The red fluorescence (JC‒1 aggregates, indicating high potential; Ex/Em: 525/590 nm) and green fluorescence (JC‒1 monomers, indicating low potential; Ex/Em: 490/530 nm) were detected. The ΔΨm was quantified by calculating the ratio of Red/Green fluorescence intensity using ImageJ software.

Analysis of mitochondrial superoxide levels

Mitochondrial superoxide levels were quantified using the MitoSOX™ Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). BV2 cells were seeded in 96‒well black plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Following LPS stimulation and drug treatments, cells were washed with warm HBSS and incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX™ Red working solution in the dark at 37 °C for 10 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with warm HBSS. The fluorescence intensity was immediately measured using a fluorescence microplate reader with excitation/emission wavelengths of 510/580 nm. The results were expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU) normalized to the Control group.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J wild‒type mice (6‒8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., td. (Liaoning, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen‒free (SPF) facility under controlled environmental conditions, with a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, a relative humidity of 55 ± 10%, and a standard 12 h light/dark cycle. The animals were provided with standard rodent chow and water ad libitum. Prior to the experiments, the mice were allowed to acclimatize to the laboratory environment for at least 7 days. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval Number: YJSDW2025.328).

In vivo imaging

The biodistribution of intravenously injected tFNAs and R‒tFNAs@PB was monitored using an in vivo imaging system. Following administration, mice were euthanized at 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 h with an overdose of 3% pentobarbital sodium. A set of organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain) was subsequently collected from each animal for ex vivo imaging. The excitation wavelength for the GFP signal was set at 420 nm, with an emission wavelength of 510 nm.

Hemolysis test

Hemolysis tests were conducted to evaluate the blood compatibility of the materials. Blood samples were collected from the eyes of healthy C57BL/6J male mice and subsequently transferred into heparin‒containing anticoagulant tubes. The blood was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, after which the resulting red blood cells were washed twice with phosphate‒buffered saline (PBS). After the supernatant was discarded, the red blood cells were resuspended in PBS to prepare a 2% cell suspension. Afterward, 200 µL of the 2% red blood cell suspension was mixed with 200 µL of solutions of the materials at various concentrations (resveratrol: 10, 30, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After the incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer. PBS and distilled water(ddH2O) served as negative and positive controls, respectively. .

KA model

Male C57BL/6J mice were housed under SPF conditions and acclimatized for one week. One day prior to surgery, mice were weighed and marked. Anesthesia was induced with 3%–4% isoflurane and maintained at 1.5%–2% via a nose cone. The mice were then immobilized in a stereotaxic apparatus. Following scalp incision and exposure of the bregma, a microsyringe was positioned at the stereotaxic coordinates targeting the right lateral ventricle (AP: ‒ [0.65] mm; ML: + [1.00] mm; DV: ‒ [1.92] mm). Kainic acid (KA) dissolved in PBS was infused at a constant rate of 0.2 µL/min. For the intracerebroventricular (ICV) model, 1 µL of KA (0.25 mg/mL) was infused. The needle was left in place for an additional 5 min post‒injection to prevent backflow and then slowly withdrawn. The incision was sutured, and buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) was administered for analgesia. Post‒surgery, mice were housed individually and monitored on a 12/12 h light‒dark cycle.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) recording

Adult C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with 1.5‒2% isoflurane and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. Two Teflon‒coated silver wire electrodes (diameter: 0.1 mm) were used. One electrode, serving as the working electrode, was stereotaxically implanted into the CA1 region of the left hippocampus. The other electrode, serving as the reference, was placed epidurally over the cerebellum. The exposed tips of the electrodes were insulated from each other and surrounding tissue by a coating of epoxy resin. The coordinates for the hippocampal electrode relative to bregma were as follows: anterior‒posterior, ‒2.0 mm; medial‒lateral, ‒1.5 mm; and dorsal‒ventral, ‒2.0 mm. The reference electrode was secured over the cerebellum with dental acrylic cement. The entire electrode assembly was then securely anchored to the skull using dental acrylic cement (GC Dental Industrial Corp.). After a 3‒day postoperative recovery period, EEG signals were acquired using a data acquisition system (Unique Medical Co., Ltd.). Electrographic seizures were defined as signals exceeding 3 Hz in frequency, with a high amplitude twice the baseline and a duration of at least 10 s. For interictal‒like discharge detection, signals were denoised, filtered (1–40 Hz) and normalized. Events exceeding a threshold of the mean baseline plus four times the standard deviation were defined as positive signals.

Barnes circular maze

The Barnes maze platform (91 cm in diameter, elevated 90 cm from the floor) consisted of 20 holes (each 5 cm in diameter). All the holes were blocked except for one target hole, which led to a recessed escape box. Spatial cues, a bright light (500 lx), and white noise were used to motivate the mice to find the escape box during each session. In the adaptation phase, each mouse explored the platform for 60 s. Any mouse that did not find the escape box was guided to it and held there for 90 s. In the acquisition phase, each trial followed the same protocol, with the goal of training each mouse to find the target and enter the escape box within 180 s. Two trials were performed approximately 20 min apart each day for 4 consecutive days. The movement of the mouse, the number of errors made, and the latency to find the target hole were recorded during the training trials by video tracking software (BMT‒100, Chengdu Techman Software, China). In the probe test (Day 5), each mouse performed one 90 s trial. The target was located in the same position, but the escape box was blocked. The latency and errors in reaching the target, as well as the number of mouse pokes to each of the holes (target preference), were measured.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cellular staining, SH‒SY5Y and BV2 cells cultured on confocal plates were subjected to treatment with various preparations for 24 h. After fixation, the cells were processed using the same immunostaining procedure described above. The primary antibodies used included: anti‒Caspase3 (1:100, Affinity, AF6311), anti‒CD86 (1:100, Abcam, #ab239075), anti‒CD206 (1:100, Abcam, #ab324515), anti‒Arg1 (1:100, Affinity, DF6657), anti‒IBA‒1 (1:300, Wako, #011‒27991), iNOS (1:100, Abcam, #ab728115) and β‒tubulin (1:500, Affinity, #AF7011). All the images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

For tissue staining, frozen brain sections were air‒dried, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X‒100 at 37 °C for 18 min, and blocked with 5% goat serum for 2 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies (anti‒Iba1 antibody, 1:200, YP‒Ab‒17998, Abcam; anti‒CD206 antibody, 1:200, YP‒Ab‒14084, Abcam; anti‒CD86 antibody, 1:200, YP‒Ab‒14107, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After the sections were washed, they were incubated with secondary antibodies (donkey anti‒goat IgG Alexa Fluor 488, ab150129, Abcam; donkey anti‒rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594, ab150062, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature for scanning fluorescence microscopy imaging (Olympus). Moreover, DAPI(Beyotime) was used to stain the nuclei. All images were captured via confocal microscopy and analyzed using ImageJ, including the generation of 2.5D surface plots to show fluorescence intensity topography.

Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining and Nissl staining

After the mice were anesthetized, they were transcardially perfused with ice‒cold PBS (pH 7.4) to remove residual blood, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The excised tissues (including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and brain) were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. Subsequently, the brain sections were subjected to gradient dewaxing and rehydration. For morphological assessment, sections were stained with Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE). For evaluation of neuronal functional integrity, sections were stained with toluidine blue/cresyl violet to visualize Nissl bodies. After staining, the sections were differentiated, dehydrated with absolute ethanol, cleared with xylene, and mounted.Observations and imaging were performed under a light microscope. The number of viable neurons (defined by visible nucleoli and abundant Nissl bodies) and relative neuronal density in the hippocampal CA3 region were quantified using ImageJ software in a blinded manner.

PTZ kindling test

Adult C57BL/6J mice (approximately 23 g) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 mice per group) and received pretreatment with 0.9% NaCl, tFNAs, or R‒tFNAs@PB via a tail vein injection on Day ‒3 and Day ‒1 (Fig. 9 A). On Day 0, each mouse received an initial intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 10 mg/kg PTZ. Subsequently, booster dosage of 10 mg/kg were administered at 10‒min intervals until a Stage 5 seizure was observed. After each injection, mice were monitored for 10 min by an observer blinded to the groups. Seizure severity was scored using a modified Racine scale: Stage 1, facial movement; Stage 2, head nodding; Stage 3, unilateral forelimb clonus; Stage 4, rearing with bilateral forelimb clonus; and Stage 5, generalized tonic‒clonic seizures with falling.

Western blotting (WB)

Protein lysates were extracted from hippocampal tissues or BV2 cells with cold RIPA buffer, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the protein concentrations in the supernatant were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Boster, China). After separation by SDS‒PAGE and transfer to PVDF membranes, the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 90 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following specific primary antibodies: anti‒Sirt3 (1:1000, CST, #5490), anti‒Ac SOD2 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab137037), anti‒SOD2 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab68155), anti‒NLRP3 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab263899), anti‒Caspase1 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab207802, to detect pro‒ and cleaved‒forms), anti‒GSDMD (1:1000, Abcam, #ab219800,detecting both full‒length and N‒terminal fragments), anti‒IL‒6 (1:1000, Affinity, DF6087), anti‒IL‒1β (1:1000, Affinity, BF8021), anti‒IL‒10 (1:1000, Affinity, DF6894), anti‒TNF‒α (1:1000, Affinity, AF7014) and anti‒GAPDH (1:1000, Affinity, AF7021). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‒conjugated secondary antibodies—goat anti‒rabbit IgG (1:5000, ABclonal, AS014) or rabbit anti‒goat IgG (1:10000, ABclonal, AS029)—for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate (Boster, China), captured with a Bio‒Rad imaging system, and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software, using GAPDH serving as an internal control.

In vivo immunogenicity and biosafety evaluation

The levels of key immunoglobulins and inflammatory cytokines in mouse serum were determined by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) to comprehensively evaluate the in vivo biosafety of R-tFNAs@PB. Serum Collection: At the endpoint of the experiment, whole blood samples were collected from mice in each group. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min to separate the serum. Samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. ELISA Assay: The serum concentrations of IgG and IgM (indicators of humoral immune response), as well as TNF-α and IFN-γ (indicators of acute inflammation), were quantified using commercial mouse-specific ELISA kits (Wuhan Gene Mei Technology Co., Ltd.). All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols. Briefly, serum samples and standards were added to the antibody-coated microplates, followed by sequential incubation with detection antibodies and substrates. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Statistical Analysis: Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD, n = 6). Statistical comparisons between the Control and R-tFNAs@PB groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test via GraphPad Prism software. Differences were considered not statistically significant (ns) when P > 0.05.

Proteomic analysis

A proteomic analysis of BV2 cells from the LPS group and R-tFNAs@PB group (n = 3 per group) was performed using LC‒MS/MS technology (provided by Oebiotech Co., Ltd.). The raw data were processed, and missing values were estimated using the minimum value imputation method. Prior to downstream analysis, the data underwent median normalization and log₂ transformation. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were screened based on the criteria of |log₂ (fold change) | > 0.26 (FC > 1.2 or < 0.83) and p‒value < 0.05. The distribution and expression profiles of DEPs were visualized via Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps. Functional enrichment, including Gene Ontology (GO) functional and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, were conducted using TopGO software and the KEGG database to identify key regulatory pathways, specifically focusing on mitochondrial function and inflammatory signaling.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed at least three independent times. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 16.0 or GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as the mean±SD or mean±SEM, as specified in the figure legends. For comparisons between multiple groups, one‒way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post‒hoc test was employed.

Probability values of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001 were considered indicate statistical significance. In all graphs, asterisks (*) denote significance compared to the control group, while pound signs (#) indicate significance relative to the model group (LPS or EP).

Synthesis and characterization of R‒tFNAs@PB

DNA nanostructures have emerged as ideal drug delivery carriers because of their extremely low immunogenicity, inherent biocompatibility, excellent structural stability, and high programmability [23, 24]. Based on this information, we constructed an R‒tFNAs@PB nanocomposite system using tFNAs as the matrix, and its preparation process is shown in Fig. 1A. First, the tFNAs base structure was formed by self‒assembly of four specific single‒stranded DNAs (Table S1). Different concentrations (80 µM and 160 µM) of resveratrol were subsequently coincubated with 250 nM tFNAs at 4 °C for drug loading. Notably, resveratrol can form a stable complex by intercalating into the DNA double helix and interacting specifically with purine and thymine bases via hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces [25, 26]. The calculation of the drug loading efficiency (LE) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) using a resveratrol standard curve showed that the LE increased with rising resveratrol concentration, whereas the EE decreased (Fig. S1, S2). The optimal resveratrol concentration was selected as 80 µM for subsequent experiments to balance drug loading and encapsulation efficiency. Compared with the 1:10 Res/PLGA ratio used by Nikita Katila et al. to prepare Res‒PLGA‒NPs, this formulation displayed a greater drug loading capacity [27]. The successful self‒assembly of the tetrahedral framework and the subsequent loading of resveratrol were validated via native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Native PAGE). As shown in Figure S3, the transition from individual single‒stranded DNA (ssDNA) to self‒assembled tFNAs was marked by a significant decrease in electrophoretic mobility, yielding a single, sharp characteristic band. Upon incubation with resveratrol, a distinct upward shift in the R‒tFNAs band was observed compared with that of the bare tFNAs. This retardation confirms the successful intercalation of resveratrol molecules into the DNA framework without compromising its structural stability. The fluorescence intensity analysis further confirmed successful loading, as the fluorescence signal significantly increased with increasing resveratrol concentration (Fig. 1B), indicating the successful synthesis of R‒tFNAs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterization consistently showed that R‒tFNAs exhibited the expected triangular framework morphology and uniform size distribution (Fig. 1 C and D). For a clear visualization, pure tFNAs (Control) were also characterized, showing well‒defined triangular structures with dimensions consistent with those of theoretical designs (Fig. S4). The height profile analysis (Fig. S5) revealed that R‒tFNAs possess an average vertical height of approximately 7–8 nm. This dimension is consistent with the theoretical size of the tetrahedral framework, confirming the structural integrity of the nanoparticles after resveratrol loading. Previous studies have confirmed that self‒assembled tFNAs have good BBB penetration ability, particularly in epilepsy models where their accumulation in the brain is more pronounced because of impaired BBB integrity [28]. We developed a biomimetic framework strategy based on R-tFNAs to further improve the performance of the system. R‒tFNAs@PB, by anchoring self‒assembled R‒tFNAs units onto a Prussian blue (PB) core (~50 nm). In this process, the newly introduced thiolated tFNAs were covalently conjugated to the PB surface via Fe‒S bonds, creating a stable composite structure (Fig. 1E). Unlike the well-known covalent bonding between gold and thiols, the conjugation of thiolated tFNAs onto the Prussian Blue (PB) surface is primarily driven by coordination chemistry. The PB nanoparticles possess abundant coordinatively unsaturated iron sites (Fe3+) on their surface/defects. The thiol group (-SH) at the 5’-end of the tFNAs acts as a ligand (electron donor), forming stable Fe‒S coordinate bonds with the surface iron ions by replacing surface-adsorbed water molecules or hydroxyl groups. This mechanism is further substantiated by the High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. S6), where a distinct negative shift in the Fe 2p binding energy was observed for R-tFNAs@PB compared to bare PB, indicative of the increased electron density on iron atoms due to electron donation from the sulfur ligand [29, 30]. Furthermore, the dense modification of tFNAs provides steric stabilization, preventing the aggregation of the PB core. The successful synthesis was confirmed by UV‒Vis absorption spectroscopy, X‒ray diffraction (XRD), energy‒dispersive X‒ray spectroscopy (EDS), and Fourier‒transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)(Fig. S7‒S10). Furthermore, the morphology of R-tFNAs@PB was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to confirm the successful construction. As shown in Fig.S11, the large-area TEM image reveals that R-tFNAs@PB possesses a uniform spherical shape with an average diameter of approximately 70 nm and excellent monodispersity. T1he core-shell architecture was rigorously verified through morphological and elemental analyses. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed a distinct structural contrast (Fig. 1 F), where the electron-dense inorganic PB core is enveloped by a relatively translucent organic tFNAs corona. This morphology is particularly evident in the high-magnification inset, which clearly displays the encapsulation layer. Consistent with these visual findings, high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) coupled with EDS mapping (Fig. 1G) confirmed the elemental composition. The specific spatial distribution of Phosphorus (P)—the signature element of the DNA backbone—was observed co-localized with the Carbon (C) and Nitrogen (N) signals, definitively substantiating the successful anchoring of the tFNAs shell onto the PB surface.

Schematic diagram of the mechanism of action of R‒tFNAs@PB

Physiological stability is a prerequisite for in vivo efficacy. As shown in Fig. S12, while free R‒tFNAs degraded gradually, R‒tFNAs@PB maintained exceptional structural integrity in serum‒containing medium for at least 7 days, without aggregation or a disappearance of bands. This enhanced stability is attributed to the dense surface packing of tFNAs on the PB core. Analogous to the “spherical nucleic acid (SNA)” effect, this dense immobilization causes steric hindrance that blocks nuclease access [31]. While the rigid PB core limits conformational flexibility, shielding the DNA backbone from enzymatic degradation. Having established its stability, we further evaluated the ROS scavenging capacity of R‒tFNAs@PB against three typical ROS types: hydrogen peroxide, superoxide anion (O₂•⁻), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). As shown in Figs. S13–S15, R‒tFNAs@PB exhibited significant ROS scavenging activity: at 30 µg/mL, approximately 90% of H₂O₂, ~ 80% of O₂•⁻, and over 80% of •OH were decomposed. Under the same dosage and concentration, R‒tFNAs@PB exhibited far superior ROS scavenging ability to the KCaHNPs reported by Feng et al. [32]. We compared the SOD‒like activity of R‒tFNAs@PB with bare PB and other representative metal oxide nanozymes to further determine the influence of the tFNA shell on the catalytic performance of the PB core. As shown in Figure S16, R‒tFNAs@PB achieved the highest SOD‒like inhibition rate (~60%), significantly outperforming bare PB (~31%) and other nanozymes such as CuO, Co3O4, CeO2, Prussian blue (PB), MnO2, and V2O5. Importantly, the tFNAs shell does not compromise the PB core’s intrinsic catalytic function but rather enhances its overall efficiency. This improvement is primarily driven by the tFNAs framework’s programmable 3D structure, which ensures excellent colloidal stability and dispersibility. By preventing PB aggregation, the shell maximizes the specific surface area and the exposure of active sites for ROS interaction. Beyond SOD‒like performance, R‒tFNAs@PB exhibits versatile multienzyme‒mimetic capabilities, including catalase (CAT)‒like and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)‒like activities, with peak efficiency observed at 30 µg/mL. This comprehensive catalytic profile highlights the synergy of the core‒shell design: it maintains the PB core in a monodispersed, highly active state while integrating the inherent antioxidant properties of tFNAs and the pharmacological bioactivity of Resveratrol. Consequently, the nanoplatform provides an “orchestrated” antioxidant defense significantly superior to individual components, effectively neutralizing broad‒spectrum ROS within the epileptic microenvironment.

We investigated the cumulative resveratrol release profile in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) at different pH values to evaluate the potential of R-tFNAs@PB for targeted drug delivery (Fig. S17). At physiological pH (7.4), R-tFNAs@PB exhibited excellent stability with minimal leakage (< 30% over 24 h), ensuring drug retention during circulation. In stark contrast, at pH 5.5 simulating the acidic endolysosomal compartment and inflammatory microenvironment—rapid burst release occurred, reaching 85% within 24 h. This pH‒responsive behavior is presumably triggered by the protonation of nucleobases, which disrupts the noncovalent interactions between resveratrol and the DNA framework. Such a ‘smart’ mechanism ensures selective drug liberation upon cellular internalization, maximizing therapeutic efficacy. Having established the functional release profile, we next verified the biocompatibility of the nanosystem as a prerequisite for biological application. CCK‒8 assays were performed on SH‒SY5Y neurons, BV2 cells, and bEnd.3 endothelial cells. The results demonstrated that R-tFNAs@PB maintains high cell viability across the tested range. Specifically, bEnd.3 cells exhibited negligible cytotoxicity (> 90% viability) even at the highest concentration of 500 µg/mL, indicating excellent safety towards the blood-brain barrier (BBB). BV2 and SH-SY5Y cells also showed high survival rates (> 80%) throughout the concentration range (Fig. S18). Based on this comprehensive biocompatibility profile, 100 µg/mL was selected as the optimal working concentration for subsequent functional experiments to ensure maximum safety.

Collectively, we have successfully engineered R‒tFNAs@PB as a robust core‒shell nanoplatform. This system integrates exceptional physiological stability with intelligent pH‒responsive drug release characteristics. Furthermore, it combines high drug loading capacity with potent multi‒enzyme‒mimetic antioxidant activity and a favorable safety profile, laying a solid foundation for subsequent in vivo epilepsy therapy.

Trans‒barrier transport, targeted microglial uptake, and spatiotemporal biodistribution of R‒tFNAs@PB

Efficient cellular internalization and precise tissue accumulation are prerequisites for therapeutic efficacy. We sequentially investigated the transbarrier transport and cellular uptake using in vitro BBB models and microglial assays, followed by validation in a temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) mouse model to systematically assess their biological performance. First, we quantified the relative cellular uptake by downstream BV2 cells after the nanocarriers traversed the bEnd.3 endothelial barrier to assess the functional trans‒monolayer delivery. The results confirmed that all formulations could successfully cross the BBB, consistent with findings by Zhu et al. [28]. Notably, R‒tFNAs@PB exhibited superior trans‒monolayer uptake efficiency, reaching approximately 4.3‒fold that of the tFNAs group (Fig. 2B). We quantified iron (Fe) transport using ICP‒MS to rigorously validate this and exclude interference from drug leakage. As shown in Table S2, naked PB NPs showed negligible passive diffusion(Papp=6.24 ± 0.33 × 10− 8cm/s). In stark contrast, tFNAs@PB (without resveratrol) displayed approximately 7‒fold enhancement in permeability(4.41 ± 0.19 × 10− 7cm/s, ****p < 0.0001), confirming that the tFNAs shell drives active transcytosis. Importantly, resveratrol loading did not compromise this penetrating ability(4.30 ± 0.15 × 10− 7cm/s). This enhanced traversal is likely attributed to the multivalent display of tFNAs on the PB core, which increases binding avidity to endothelial receptors. Following BBB translocation, the nanosystem must be internalized by target microglia. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) of BV2 microglial cells treated with FITC‒labeled nanocarriers for 2 h showed that the intracellular fluorescence signal in the R‒tFNAs@PB group was significantly increased compared with the signals in the tFNAs and R-tFNAs groups (Fig. 2 C, D). As illustrated in Scheme 1, this efficient internalization is mechanistically rooted in thiol‒mediated uptake (TMU). Although most thiols are used for anchoring to the PB core, residual surface‒accessible thiols on the tFNAs shell facilitate dynamic disulfide exchange reactions. Specifically, these thiols can covalently ‘dock’ onto the transferrin receptor (TFRC) on BBB and microglial membranes, significantly lowering the energy barrier for membrane translocation and synergistically promoting lipid raft/caveolae‒mediated endocytosis [33–36]. Furthermore, this interaction plays a pivotal role in endosomal escape, allowing R‒tFNAs@PB to bypass lysosomal degradation and deliver the payload into the cytosol and mitochondria [37]. Before proceeding to in vivo studies, we evaluated the hemocompatibility of the materials by performing a hemolysis assay. Even at a high concentration of 500 µg/mL, the hemolysis rates of all groups were less than 5% (Fig. S19), indicating suitability for intravenous administration. Subsequently, we evaluated brain‒targeting efficiency in a kainic acid (KA)‒induced TLE mouse model. Seventy‒two hours post‒modeling, FITC‒labeled nanocarriers were injected intravenously (standardized to 10 mg/kg equivalent tFNAs). Dynamic monitoring revealed that brain accumulation in the R‒tFNAs@PB group peaked at 9 h post‒injection and was significantly higher than that of free tFNAs (Fig. 2E, F). This confirms that the core‒shell structure not only enhances BBB crossing via multivalency but also protects the DNA framework from premature systemic degradation. To verify “lesion‒targeting” capability beyond general BBB penetration, we examined fluorescence distribution in specific brain subregions. High‒resolution imaging (Fig. S20) revealed that R‒tFNAs@PB signals were predominantly concentrated in the inflamed hippocampal CA3 region—the primary site of KA‒induced damage. In contrast, the fluorescence signals in the CA1, Dentate Gyrus (DG), and Isocortex regions were negligible signals. This precise accumulation suggests that R‒tFNAs@PB can actively distinguish between diseased and healthy tissue, which is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target side effects. Regarding biodistribution, while accumulation in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were comparable between groups at 24 h (Fig. S21), the preferential accumulation in the injured brain hemisphere confirms effective targeting. In parallel, we evaluated the major organs across different treatment groups to ensure safety during the acute phase. Histological and functional assessments revealed no significant tissue damage or acute toxicity in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney shortly after administration, confirming the initial biocompatibility of the materials (Fig. S22). Subsequently, we assessed long‒term safety and metabolic clearance over 28 days. Mice treated with R‒tFNAs@PB showed steady body weight gain (Fig. S24) and normal liver/kidney function markers (Fig. S23). Histological analysis (H&E) revealed no pathological damage or immune cell infiltration in major organs (Fig. S25). Furthermore, quantitative tracking of iron (Fe) using ICP‒MS demonstrated a time‒dependent clearance profile (Fig. S26). While Fe content initially increased in the brain (confirming targeting) and reticuloendothelial organs (liver/spleen) on day 1, it significantly decreased by day 14 and returned to baseline levels by day 28. Furthermore, we evaluated the systemic inflammatory response and adaptive immune activation to consider the potential immunogenicity of tFNAs as exogenous DNA and the long-term stability of the PB core. ELISA analysis revealed that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ and TNF-α) at 24 h post-injection remained within the normal range, with no significant difference compared to the Control group (Fig. S27A, B). Besides, we monitored serum immunoglobulin levels (IgM and IgG) over a 28-day period to assess long-term immunogenicity. As shown in Fig. S27C and D, no significant elevation of IgM or IgG was observed in R-tFNAs@PB treated mice at day 28, indicating that the nanosystem does not trigger a significant adaptive immune response. These findings, coupled with the time-dependent clearance profile of iron (Fig. S26), confirm the excellent systemic biosafety and low immunogenicity of R-tFNAs@PB for long-term therapeutic use.This confirms that the R‒tFNAs@PB nanosystem is biodegradable, with components effectively metabolized and excreted, thereby avoiding risks of heavy metal accumulation or cyanide toxicity.

R-tFNAs@PB modulates microglial polarization from a proinflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype in vitro

Neuroinflammation plays a critical role in epileptogenesis, with aberrant microglial activation representing a central pathological feature. Specifically, M1-polarized microglia exacerbate neuronal damage by secreting neurotoxic pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), whereas M2-polarized microglia exert neuroprotective effects via anti-inflammatory mediators like IL-10 [38, 39]. We established an M1 proinflammatory activation model by stimulating BV2 microglia with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to investigate the effect of R-tFNAs@PB on microglial polarization. Immunofluorescence staining showed that compared with the control treatment, LPS stimulation significantly increased the fluorescence intensity of the M1 marker CD86 by approximately 16.53-fold. Although the R-tFNA treatment group showed a slight reduction in CD86 expression, R-tFNAs@PB treatment significantly suppressed CD86 expression, reducing it to approximately 20% of that in the LPS group, while increasing the expression of the M2 marker CD206 by approximately 10.7-fold (Fig. 3A–D). We further verified this effect by evaluating changes in the expression of the M1 marker inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and the M2 marker arginase-1 (Arg-1). Compared with LPS treatment, R-tFNAs@PB treatment decreased iNOS expression to 27% and increased Arg-1 expression by approximately 3.4-fold (Fig. 3E–G). These results indicate that R-tFNAs@PB can effectively promote the transition of microglia from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in vitro. We detected the mRNA expression levels of key inflammatory cytokines using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) to further explore the regulatory role of R-tFNAs@PB in neuroinflammation. Compared with the control treatment, LPS treatment upregulated the expression of the proinflammatory factors IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β by 5.2-fold, 6.1-fold, and 19.4-fold, respectively, and downregulated the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 by approximately 45%. R-tFNA treatment only slightly inhibited the expression of proinflammatory factors, whereas R-tFNAs@PB significantly reduced the transcript levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β to approximately 30.1%, 17.7%, and 12% of those in the LPS group, respectively, and increased the expression of IL-10 by approximately 3.3-fold. These data further confirm that R-tFNAs@PB effectively inhibit the LPS-induced proinflammatory activation of microglia. While tFNAs alone exerted only modest regulatory effects, R-tFNAs@PB displayed the most pronounced efficacy in suppressing inflammation.

R-tFNAs@PB alleviate oxidative stress and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation via a SIRT3/SOD2-dependent mechanism

We elucidated the molecular mechanism by which R-tFNAs@PB regulate microglial polarization in depth by performing a proteomic analysis to systematically analyze the protein expression profiles of BV2 microglia treated with R-tFNAs@PB. A comparison of the LPS-induced injury group with the R-tFNAs@PB treatment group revealed 118 differentially expressed proteins, with 87 downregulated proteins and 31 upregulated proteins. The volcano plot shows the significant downregulation of key inflammation-related proteins, such as IL-1β, CASP1, and NLRP3, while the mitochondrial function-related proteins SIRT3 and SOD2 were markedly upregulated (Fig. 4 A). We performed a hierarchical clustering analysis of the 12 most significantly upregulated and downregulated differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) to identify the core regulatory nodes affected by R-tFNAs@PB (Fig. 4B). The refined heatmap clearly shows a robust reversal of the LPS-induced proteomic signature. Among the top upregulated proteins, key antioxidant enzymes and regulatory factors, including SIRT3, SOD2, and CAT, showed the most prominent recovery. Conversely, the top 12 downregulated proteins were predominantly enriched in proinflammatory and pyroptotic pathways, such as the NLRP3, CASP1, and IL-1β pathways. This highly filtered protein profile suggests that R-tFNAs@PB treatment is associated with coordinated changes in mitochondrial antioxidant defense–related proteins and inflammasome-associated inflammatory pathways. The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further indicated that the differentially expressed proteins were primarily localized to mitochondria and their inner membrane system (Fig. 4 C), suggesting that R-tFNAs@PB might exert their regulatory effects by targeting mitochondrial protein complexes. Furthermore, these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to oxidative stress and the inflammatory response (Fig. 4 C, D), and R-tFNAs@PB treatment effectively reversed the abnormal activation of these pathways. SIRT3 is a mitochondrial NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase known to regulate mitochondrial redox homeostasis by modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2. This enzyme promotes ATP production and maintains energy stability by regulating respiratory chain complex activity and metabolic enzyme function [15, 40]. More importantly, SIRT3 specifically regulates the activity of the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme SOD2 via deacetylation, thereby increasing the superoxide anion scavenging capacity and maintaining redox balance [41]. This protective mechanism has been validated in various disease models [42–44]. Therefore, the SIRT3/SOD2 axis is considered a key component of the mitochondrial antioxidant defense system. Its dysfunction leads to reduced SOD2 activity, the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS, the subsequent activation of downstream inflammatory pathways, the promotion of the release of proinflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), and ultimately the exacerbation of neuroinflammatory damage. Based on the proteomic results, we validated the impact of R-tFNAs@PB on the SIRT3/SOD2 pathway via Western blot. The results showed that LPS stimulation significantly reduced the expression of the SIRT3 and SOD2 proteins while increasing the acetylation of SOD2 (Ac-SOD2). R-tFNAs@PB treatment effectively reversed these changes (Fig. 4E–H). We further clarified the mechanistic hierarchy, specifically, whether the upregulation of SIRT3 is a direct effect of our nanosystem or a secondary outcome of general ROS scavenging, by performing a decoupling experiment using different components of the nanocomposite (Fig. S28). As expected, compared with the full R-tFNAs@PB system, the groups containing the Prussian blue core (PB and tFNAs@PB) displayed excellent ROS scavenging abilities because of the intrinsic catalase/SOD-like activity of PB. However, Western blot analysis revealed a crucial distinction: simply scavenging ROS (in the PB and tFNAs@PB groups) did not result in the restoration of SIRT3 protein levels. In contrast, R-tFNAs@PB significantly upregulated SIRT3 expression. These findings suggest that the PB core and resveratrol may contribute to the observed protective effects through distinct but complementary mechanisms. The PB core acts as an environmental “sweeper” to directly eliminate excess ROS, creating a favorable cellular environment. Simultaneously, the delivered resveratrol functions as a specific “activator” to directly upregulate SIRT3 signaling. Therefore, SIRT3 activation is a primary pharmacological effect of the loaded drug rather than a passive secondary effect of ROS reduction. We detected intracellular ROS levels and the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) to further investigate the function of this pathway. As shown in Fig. 5A–C, LPS stimulation led to significant increases in total ROS and mitochondrial ROS levels, whereas R-tFNAs@PB treatment restored these levels to near-normal levels. The results of JC-1 staining revealed a significant decrease in the ΔΨm in the LPS group (increased JC-1 monomer ratio), indicating severe mitochondrial dysfunction. In contrast, R-tFNAs@PB pretreatment effectively maintained ΔΨm stability. Additionally, the LPS-induced decrease in ATP production was significantly ameliorated by R-tFNAs@PB (Fig. 5E). Notably, when the SIRT3-specific inhibitor 3-TYP was used, the protective effects of R-tFNAs@PB were completely abolished, further supporting the involvement of SIRT3 in this pathway. While resveratrol is a well-established activator of SIRT1, recent studies have highlighted the critical role of the mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT3 in mediating the protective effects of resveratrol on oxidative stress in various models, including stressed macrophages [45], myocardial ischemic injury [46], and intestinal ischemia‒reperfusion [47] models. We evaluated SIRT1 expression to clarify the specific pathway involved in our R-tFNAs@PB system. Western blot analysis revealed that R-tFNAs@PB failed to significantly restore SIRT1 levels (Fig. S29), in sharp contrast to the robust upregulation of SIRT3. This specificity aligns with the findings of Wang et al. [47] and Zhu et al. [46], who reported that resveratrol restores redox homeostasis primarily by activating the SIRT3-mediated antioxidant defense system (e.g., SOD2 and catalase). Furthermore, our proteomic data revealed the preferential enrichment of differentially expressed proteins in the mitochondrial inner membrane. Collectively, these results confirm that R-tFNAs@PB exert neuroprotective effects by specifically targeting the mitochondrial SIRT3/SOD2 axis.

Recent studies have indicated a close interaction between oxidative stress and neuroinflammation during epilepsy progression, with NLRP3 inflammasome activation being particularly crucial [48–50]. The inflammasome can recognize intracellular pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), promoting the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 via caspase-1 activation, thereby triggering neuroinflammation and pyroptosis [51]. NLRP3, one of the most extensively studied inflammasomes, plays important roles in various neurological disorders [52, 53]. Clinical studies have shown the significant upregulation of the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes in the microglia of patients with new-onset refractory status epilepticus (NORSE) and TLE, suggesting that NLRP3 plays an important role in the pathology of refractory epilepsy [54]. Based on previous studies, we examined whether mitochondrial ROS might contribute to NLRP3 inflammasome activation [55, 56] and that the SIRT3/SOD2 axis might inhibit NLRP3 activation by scavenging mitochondrial ROS. The results showed that R-tFNAs@PB treatment significantly reduced LPS-induced mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD, and its cleaved form N-GSDMD, whereas R-tFNAs@PB effectively decreased the levels of these proteins. Importantly, when the SIRT3 inhibitor 3-TYP was used, the inhibitory effect of R-tFNAs@PB was significantly attenuated (Fig. 5F–J).

In summary, these results indicate that R-tFNAs@PB modulates mitochondrial redox homeostasis and attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activation in BV2 microglia.

R-tFNAs@PB attenuates microglia-induced neuronal oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction

The hippocampus, a highly metabolic region in the CNS with dense neurons and abundant mitochondria, is highly vulnerable to oxidative stress damage during TLE pathology, leading to neuronal dysfunction and even death. This vicious cycle between neuroinflammation and seizures further exacerbates this pathological process [57, 58]. Our previous studies confirmed that R-tFNAs@PB can effectively inhibit ROS generation, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and the proinflammatory polarization of microglia by regulating the SIRT3/SOD2 signaling axis. However, activated microglia can also severely impair mitochondrial function through various pathways, including reducing ATP levels, increasing ROS production, and disrupting ΔΨm, ultimately leading to mitochondria-mediated neuronal apoptosis [59]. We established a Transwell coculture system of BV2 microglia and SH-SY5Y neurons to investigate the direct protective effect of R-tFNAs@PB on neurons (Fig. 6B). In this system, BV2 cells in the upper chamber were stimulated with LPS and treated with R-tFNAs@PB, while SH-SY5Y neurons in the lower chamber were not directly exposed to either LPS or R-tFNAs@PB The fluorescence detection results revealed that compared with the control treatment, the LPS treatment increased the ROS fluorescence intensity by approximately 6.7-fold. R-tFNAs@PB significantly reduced the LPS-induced increase in intracellular ROS levels in SH-SY5Y cells to approximately 46.2% of those in the LPS group (Fig. 6 C, D). The results of JC-1 staining indicated that LPS treatment caused a significant decrease in the neuronal mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), indicating a dysfunctional state. In contrast, pretreatment with R-tFNAs@PB markedly restored the ΔΨm, indicating improved mitochondrial membrane potential stability (Fig. 6E). We detected the status of caspase-3 activation to further further characterize the apoptotic component of neuronal cell death. Caspase-3 typically exists as an inactive zymogen and requires specific cleavage via the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway for activation. Immunofluorescence staining showed that compared with the control treatment, LPS treatment significantly promoted caspase-3 activation, increasing the fluorescence intensity by 4.6-fold, whereas R-tFNAs@PB treatment effectively reversed this trend, reducing the activity to approximately 21.9% (Fig. 6 F, G). Concurrently, a flow cytometry analysis confirmed that R-tFNAs@PB significantly reduced the LPS-induced apoptosis of SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 6G).In summary, R‒tFNAs@PB exerts a potent neuroprotective effect not only by directly scavenging ROS but, crucially, by modulating the microglial activation state. This stops the paracrine oxidative attack, restores neuronal ΔΨm stability, and significantly inhibits the apoptotic cascade (Fig. 6 A).

Lesion‒specific immunomodulation and microglial reprogramming by R‒tFNAs@PB

In neurological disorders such as epilepsy, aberrant microglial activation is a central aspect of neuroinflammation. Activated microglia not only undergo significant morphological changes but also participate in inflammatory cascades by releasing large amounts of proinflammatory factors, thereby increasing neuronal excitability and exacerbating neural damage [60]. Notably, seizure‒induced neuronal hyperexcitability leads to excessive ROS production and mitochondrial dysfunction, which can further activate microglia, resulting in a vicious cycle [38, 61]. Therefore, the interaction between oxidative stress and neuroinflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy. Our previous in vitro experiments confirmed that R‒tFNAs@PB effectively regulates oxidative stress levels, induces polarization of BV2 microglia from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti‒inflammatory M2 phenotype, and exerts protective effects on neurons. To validate this therapeutic potential in vivo, we evaluated the neuroinflammatory landscape in the hippocampus of a Kainic Acid (KA)‒induced TLE mouse model. First, we assessed the spatial heterogeneity of microglial activation to confirm lesion specificity. As shown in Fig. S29, immunofluorescence imaging of the non‒lesion areas (DG and CA1 regions) revealed that microglia largely retained a resting state. In both Control and EP groups, these regions displayed dominant blue fluorescence (DAPI) with negligible red (IBA‒1) or green (CD86) signals. The corresponding 2.5D surface plots (bottom panels of Fig. S30) further corroborated this, showing a flat landscape populated only by blue nuclear spikes, confirming that the inflammatory damage in this model is specifically concentrated in the CA3 region. In stark contrast, the CA3 region exhibited severe neuroinflammation (Fig. 7). In the EP group, Fig. 7 A reveals a dense accumulation of activated microglia, characterized by the intense co‒localization of red fluorescence (IBA‒1) and green fluorescence (CD86). The 2.5D surface plots visually quantify this “inflammatory storm”, displaying chaotic, high‒amplitude peaks of mixed red and green signals. Treatment with tFNAs alone failed to mitigate this intensity. However, R‒tFNAs@PB intervention dramatically suppressed the M1 phenotype, effectively “flattening” the fluorescence peaks in the 2.5D plots and reducing the green CD86 signal to near‒control levels. Conversely, we evaluated the reparative M2 phenotype (Fig. 7B). While the EP group showed almost no green fluorescence for the M2 marker CD206, the R‒tFNAs@PB group exhibited distinct, punctate green signals overlapping with the red microglia. The 2.5D plots highlighted this upregulation as the emergence of new green peaks absent in other groups, visually confirming that R‒tFNAs@PB promotes a phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 specifically at the epileptic focus. We further corroborated these histological findings with protein analysis via Western blot (Fig. 7 C). The hippocampal tissues from the EP group displayed dense, thickened bands for the proinflammatory cytokines (IL‒6, TNF‒α, and IL‒1β), reflecting a significant upregulation compared to controls. Simultaneously, the band for the anti‒inflammatory cytokine IL‒10 appeared faint and thin. R‒tFNAs@PB treatment effectively attenuated the density of the proinflammatory bands while restoring the intensity of the IL‒10 band. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 7D‒G) confirmed that R‒tFNAs@PB significantly reversed the abnormal expression patterns of these inflammatory factors.

Neuroprotective efficacy of R‒tFNAs@PB in the TLE hippocampus

Current research indicates that TLE pathology is characterized by a “triad” of glial overactivation, cytokine storms, and accelerated neuronal apoptosis, leading to severe hippocampal restructuring [62–66]. Having confirmed that R‒tFNAs@PB effectively quells neuroinflammation in the CA3 region, we next evaluated its neuroprotective efficacy. We administered R‒tFNAs@PB (10 mg/kg) via tail vein injection on Days 1 and 3 post‒modeling and harvested hippocampal tissues on Day 7 for systematic pathological analysis (Fig. 8 A). Histological assessment via H&E staining revealed the extent of neuronal rescue (Fig. 8B). In the healthy Control group, neurons in the CA3 region presented a tightly packed, multi‒layered arrangement. Their nuclei were round and pale blue with clearly visible nucleoli, and the cytoplasm was uniform. In stark contrast, the EP group exhibited classic signs of neuronal necrosis and degeneration: the pyramidal cell layer was loosely arranged and disordered, with significantly reduced cell numbers (dropping to ~65%). Crucially, many remaining neurons displayed nuclear pyknosis (shrunken, deeply stained, hyperchromatic nuclei) and eosinophilic cytoplasm (bright pink), indicating irreversible cell death. R‒tFNAs@PB treatment remarkably reversed this damage. The number of surviving neurons significantly increased (1.4‒fold vs. EP), and the majority retained healthy, plump nuclei with orderly alignment. Consistent with our previous findings, no significant pathological changes were observed in the non‒lesion CA1 or DG regions (Fig. S31), confirming the lesion‒specific protection. Nissl staining provided further insight into neuronal functional integrity (Fig. 8C‒D). In the Control group, neurons were densely distributed (~277 cells/mm²) and rich in deep blue Nissl bodies (tigroid substance) within the cytoplasm, marking robust protein synthesis activity. The EP group, however, showed severe chromatolysis: the cytoplasm appeared pale and ghost‒like due to the loss of Nissl bodies, accompanied by extensive vacuolar degeneration (empty white spaces in the tissue). This loss resulted in blurred boundaries of the pyramidal layer and a sharp decline in neuron density (~156 cells/mm²). R‒tFNAs@PB treatment effectively preserved the integrity of Nissl bodies, reducing vacuolization and restoring the neuronal density to ~223 cells/mm². Finally, we utilized NeuN immunofluorescence to specifically quantify surviving neurons (Fig. 8E). The Control group displayed a continuous, dense band of bright green fluorescence, representing intact neuronal nuclei. In the EP group, this fluorescent band was fragmented and discontinuous, with large dark gaps indicating massive neuronal loss. Following R‒tFNAs@PB intervention, the continuity and intensity of the green fluorescence were largely restored, and the vacuolar spaces were markedly reduced. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant preservation of NeuN‒positive cells, corroborating the H&E and Nissl results.

In summary, these multi‒modal histological analyses demonstrate that R‒tFNAs@PB actively targets the lesion site to inhibit neuronal apoptosis and promote repair, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the hippocampal CA3 region.

R‒tFNAs@PB attenuates seizure severity, elevates seizure threshold, and ameliorates cognitive deficits

R‒tFNAs@PB significantly ameliorates oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in the hippocampi of TLE mice by activating the SIRT3/SOD2 pathway, reducing mitochondrial ROS levels, and inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Research has shown that targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome is an effective strategy for controlling epilepsy progression. For example, the specific inhibitor MCC950, which blocks NLRP3 activation and reduces IL‒1β maturation/release, not only significantly decreases seizure frequency and severity but also improves BBB function and cognitive ability [52, 67]. CY‒09 effectively alleviates pentylenetetrazol‒induced seizures and neuronal damage by inhibiting NLRP3‒mediated neuroinflammation and astrocyte activation [68]. Additionally, drugs such as ulinastatin and semaglutide also demonstrate anti‒seizure and neuroprotective effects on epilepsy models by modulating the NLRP3 signaling pathway [69, 70]. Given the close association between hippocampal neuronal damage and cognitive dysfunction, and the crucial role of neuroinflammation regulation in improving TLE prognosis [71], we benchmarked the in vivo efficacy of R‒tFNAs@PB against carbamazepine (CBZ), a first‒line clinical anti‒epileptic drug, assessing both seizure phenotypes and cognitive comorbidities. First, we evaluated seizure intensity using Video‒EEG monitoring in the KA‒induced TLE model (Fig. 9B‒D). Visual inspection of the EEG traces (Fig. 9B) revealed that the EP group exhibited chaotic, high‒amplitude polyspike discharges, indicated severe epileptic seizures. The corresponding frequency‒power spectrograms (heatmaps in Fig. 9 C) displayed intense red and yellow signals, reflecting high‒energy neuronal hypersynchrony. While CBZ treatment partially reduced the spike frequency, R‒tFNAs@PB resulted in a profound suppression of epileptiform activity. The EEG traces in the R‒tFNAs@PB group appeared remarkably flat and stable, similar to the control group, and the spectrograms were dominated by a deep blue background, signifying a return to a quiescent, low‒energy state. Quantitative analysis confirmed that R‒tFNAs@PB was statistically superior to CBZ in reducing average seizure intensity (#p < 0.05, Fig. 9D), suggesting that remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment offers better seizure control than ion channel modulation alone. Next, to investigate the effect on seizure susceptibility, we utilized a PTZ‒induced kindling model (Timeline in Fig. 9 A). The progression of seizure severity was visualized using a heat map (Fig. 9E), where “blue” represents low‒stage seizures and “red” represents fully kindled generalized seizures (Stage 4‒5). In the Control and tFNAs groups, the heat map rapidly shifted from blue to red by Day 10, indicating successful kindling. In striking contrast, the R‒tFNAs@PB group maintained a predominantly blue/grey profile (Stages 0‒2) for most of the experiment, only approaching higher stages by the 13th injection. This significant delay in kindling progression (Fig. 9 F) demonstrates that the nanosystem effectively raises the seizure threshold. Finally, we assessed spatial learning and memory via the Barnes maze test (Fig. 9G‒J). The motion tracks in Fig. 9G provide direct evidence of cognitive recovery. Mice in the EP group displayed disordered, aimless wandering trajectories covering the entire arena, characteristic of severe spatial disorientation. While CBZ‒treated mice showed a slightly improved but still circuitous path, R‒tFNAs@PB‒treated mice exhibited a focused, linear trajectory directly towards the target hole, closely mimicking the healthy Control group. Quantitative results showed that R‒tFNAs@PB treatment significantly shortened the latency to find the target and reduced error rates compared to both the EP and CBZ groups (Fig. 9H‒J). This highlights a distinct advantage of our nanosystem: unlike traditional antiepileptic drugs which may not address (or even exacerbate) cognitive deficits, R‒tFNAs@PB simultaneously suppresses seizures and rescues cognitive function.

In summary, R‒tFNAs@PB not only exerts potent antiseizure effects superior to CBZ in specific metrics but also confers significant neuroprotection against cognitive comorbidities, driven by its dual regulation of oxidative stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have successfully engineered a brain‒targeted nanoplatform, R‒tFNAs@PB, designed to remodel the pathological microenvironment of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). Our findings demonstrate that this nanosystem effectively traverses the blood‒brain barrier and accumulates at epileptic foci, where it upregulates the SIRT3/SOD2 signaling axis to enhance mitochondrial antioxidant capacity and alleviate oxidative stress. This molecular cascade subsequently inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reprograms microglia from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward a neuroprotective M2 state. Consequently, R‒tFNAs@PB intervention exerts potent anti‒seizure effects, attenuates neuronal damage, and rescues cognitive deficits both in vitro and in vivo. Overall, this study establishes a promising biomimetic nanotherapeutic strategy for refractory epilepsy and provides critical mechanistic insights into the synergistic regulation of the oxidative stress‒neuroinflammation axis.
