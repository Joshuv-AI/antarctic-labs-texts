Experimental Study on Spontaneous Imbibition of Coal Samples of Different Ranks Based on the NMR Relaxation Spectrum
Authors: Ning Wang, Yi Du, Changqing Fu, Xicong Ma, Xin Zhang, Jiayi Wang, Ning Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe comprehension of the mechanisms governing spontaneous water imbibition in gas–water systems plays a significant role in the operation of hydraulic fracturing and the development of coalbed methane (CBM). In this study, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) techniques were used to investigate the pore structure and fluid behavior of different rank coal samples during spontaneous imbibition. Analyses were conducted on the 1D NMR T2 spectrum, 2D NMR T1–T2 spectrum, and layer division T2 spectra to achieve accurate and detailed information about the internal pore structure of coal and the characteristics of fluid transport during spontaneous imbibition. Low-rank coal exhibits good pore connectivity and favorable pore sorting characteristics, indicating favorable reservoir conditions. High-rank coal has larger pore spaces and highly developed micropores, which are highly beneficial for gas adsorption. However, its poor pore sorting characteristics and connectivity limit the migration and diffusion of fluids within the reservoir. The imbibition capacity follows a specific order of contribution, with small pores (10–50 nm) having the most significant role, followed by micropores (2–10 nm), ultramicropores (r < 2 nm), mesopores (50–1000 nm), macropores (1000–10,000 nm), and microfractures (r ≥ 10,000 nm). Low-rank coal stands out due to the restricted development of ultramicropores and small pores, leading to a different contribution of imbibition capacity compared to other samples, where macropores and microfractures dominate over all pore types. The coal reservoirs with favorable pore sorting characteristics and pore connectivity tend to exhibit a tendency toward rapid saturation and attainment of a prompt stable state during the hydraulic fracturing process. Finally, the mechanism of the late retreat effect of imbibition and the laws governing different coal ranks, pore structures, and fluid transport were discussed. This study offers comprehensive analyses of the mechanism of coal spontaneous imbibition and the characteristic laws of fluid seepage, providing insights into the optimization of CBM recovery and reservoir management.

Introduction

With the burgeoning development of oil and gas exploration, unconventional oil and gas resources, such as coalbed methane (CBM), present a significant potential for development and utilization.1,2 Considering that deep CBM reservoirs have low permeability and complex pore structures, hydraulic fracturing technology is often used to increase the permeability of low-permeability coal seams and improve CBM production.3,4 During hydraulic fracturing, water enters the fractures under injection pressure. Once the residual pressure is removed, water enters the pores through spontaneous imbibition under the action of capillary forces.5,6 When the direction of imbibition is consistent with the direction of CBM production, the capillary force is the driving force of CBM migration. Otherwise, it is resistance.7,8 This resistance causes the retention of fracturing fluid, known as the water-blocking effect.9,10 Therefore, water imbibition in coal seams is considered a key factor affecting CBM recovery, and understanding the transport mechanism of the water imbibition process in coal bodies is of great importance for CBM production.11−13

Spontaneous imbibition refers to the spontaneous interaction between wetting fluid and matrix in porous media under the combined action of capillary force and gravity.14,15 When water enters the pore space, the water molecules in the pore space migrate to the coal surface and form a water film. If the relative humidity of the coal surface is lower than the saturation vapor pressure of the water film, the water molecules spontaneously migrate to the air.16 The characteristics of spontaneous imbibition are significantly influenced by the pore structure, wettability, mineral composition, initial saturation, gas fraction, and distribution state of the coal.17,18Capillary force is the main driving force of imbibition, and numerous micropores will provide superior imbibition effect. Different types of pores have different effects on imbibition recovery. The process of imbibition is mainly affected by nanopores.19,20

Typically, spontaneous imbibition volume and fluid flow behavior can be acquired with multiple techniques, including the section method and the water-sensitive indicator method, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), neutron imaging technology, scanning electron microscopy, and numerical simulation.21−26

NMR analysis provides a significant advantage in the study of pore size distribution, porosity, and pore connectivity, such as practical simplicity, sensitivity, rapidity, nondestructive detection, and wide characterization range.27−29 The precision of NMR testing is influenced by the signal-to-noise ratio. Due to the large magnetic moment of hydrogen nuclei, the resulting nuclear magnetic signals are stronger. Therefore, NMR is suitable for detecting the water content during spontaneous imbibition processes and analyzing the contribution degree of pores at different scales to spontaneous imbibition capacity, as well as fluid transport mechanisms.

Numerous scholars have conducted extensive research on the impact of factors such as interfacial tension, surfactants, permeability, fracturing fluid, retention fluid, and other parameters on the spontaneous seepage process in reservoirs.30−32 Moreover, a considerable number of experiments on spontaneous seepage reservoir drives have been conducted.33,34 Currently, the NMR method primarily obtains information on rock water absorption and pore water distribution through spectral analysis. However, there is limited research on the direct monitoring of water migration processes. Meanwhile, it has been observed that most of the studies tend to focus on cores in the field of petroleum engineering, with very few investigations into coal cores.13,35

In this study, samples from the Datong, Duanshi, and Sihe coal mines in the Qinshui Basin and the Zhangjiamao coal mine in the Ordos Basin are selected as research objects. The samples were made into 25 mm diameter columns for spontaneous imbibition experiments at room temperature. Furthermore, low-field NMR analysis was performed to evaluate the total T2 spectrum characteristics, T1–T2, and the T2 spectrum corresponding to different layers of the samples. NMR component profiles of the coal samples in one dimension at different imbibition times. The capillary force-based water imbibition pattern at a time scale was studied, and the pore characteristics distribution of the coal samples was visually obtained. The aim of this study is to provide more accurate methods for characterizing the pore structure of coal and to gain a deeper understanding of the microscale transport mechanisms of water in coal samples. By revealing the contributions of different-scale pores to the water imbibition capacity during spontaneous imbibition, a better comprehension of fluid migration patterns in coal reservoirs can be achieved. This research holds significant practical implications for the fields of coal mining engineering and petroleum engineering, contributing to the optimization of coalbed methane development and the enhancement of energy extraction efficiency and reservoir management.

Experimental Equipment, Parameters, and Principles

The analysis was conducted using a low-field NMR analyzer (model MacroMR12-150HTHP-I) equipped with a 25 mm magnetic needle probe. The analyzer was operated at a magnetic field strength of 12 MHz and a sampling frequency of 200 kHz under controlled conditions (25 °C experimental environment).

Given the unique pore characteristics of coal samples compared to conventional rock samples, careful consideration was given to the selection of measurement parameters to ensure the comprehensive acquisition of pore–fracture information while avoiding the omission of nanoscale pore details within the instrument’s measurement accuracy. Prior to the actual testing, a standardized sample was employed as the substrate for preliminary testing. Systematic variations were introduced to parameters such as pulse width, scan time, or pulse interval while monitoring changes in signal intensity, resolution, and peak shape. By comparing the spectra obtained under different parameter settings, the optimal combination of parameters could be determined. Subsequently, a series of repeated tests were conducted to validate the reproducibility and stability of the results after finalizing the optimal parameters.

The T2 spectrum was determined using specific parameters, including 16 scans, a 2000 ms waiting time, a 7.4 90-degree pulse width, a 15.2 180-degree pulse width, 3 digital gain and radius, 10,000 echoes, and a 0.1 echo time. Additionally, the T1–T2 spectrum measurement sequence inversion time was set to 21 to optimize the accuracy and precision of the measurements.

The phenomenon of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is based on the interaction between spin nuclei, magnetic fields, and energy requirements. Specifically, when the hydrogen nucleus meets the Larmor frequency conditions under an applied magnetic field, the proton absorbs energy and resonates from the ground state to the excited state. Subsequently, as the energy is released, the proton returns to the ground state, leading to relaxation.

Experimental Procedure

The drilled short cylindrical core samples underwent a 24-h drying process at 105 °C before the experiments. Following this, the samples were removed and allowed to cool to room temperature before being weighed. The subsequent measurement of NMR T2 and T1–T2 spectra of the dried samples ensures a thorough understanding of the sample’s relaxation properties and fluid behavior in the dry state. These measurements serve as a substrate for comparison with the results obtained before and after spontaneous imbibition experiments. Subsequently, the dried coal samples were placed in a vacuum saturation device for negative pressure saturated with water for 60 h. Once the saturation process was complete, the samples were subjected to further T2 and T1–T2 spectrum testing, followed by drying and cooling.

To conduct the spontaneous imbibition experiments as shown in Figure 2, distilled water was added to a water vessel to immerse the bottom of the sample to 5 mm. The experiments were carried out at atmospheric pressure (0.1 MPa) and room temperature (26 °C).

Spontaneous imbibition experimental device and NMR analyzer.

NMR tests were carried out at specific intervals after the bottom of the coal sample was soaked, which was counted as the initial time. The test times were 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 360, 720, 1440, 2880, 4320, 5760, 7200, 8640, and 10,080 min, respectively. When the corresponding set time was reached, the sample was taken out, the residual water on the surface was wiped, and then put into the NMR analyzer for testing T2 and T1–T2. Once the measurements were completed, the sample was put back into the water for immersion.

It is important to note that to minimize systematic errors, calibration of the instrument was carried out prior to the experiment. Standard samples with a standard volume of 30 mL and porosity values of 1, 5, 10, and 20% were calibrated individually. The data obtained after inversion revealed a significant linear relationship between the cumulative signal amplitude and incremental pore volume, with negligible instrument error. To mitigate the impact of magnetic field inhomogeneity on the intrinsic relaxation characteristics of protons, testing was performed using the echo-based Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. Data inversion was subsequently employed to extract and deduce the relevant parameters and properties of the sample from the collected signals, thus characterizing the relaxation times under different time intervals.

Results

Analysis of Pore Structure

By utilizing the relationship equations established in eq 6, the pores of the coal samples were simplified to spherical pores, and the law of relaxation time and pore size was further derived based on the pore-specific surface area and relaxation strength at relaxation.40,41

Here, Fs was a geometric shape factor, with a value of 3 for simplified spherical pores. The constant terms were combined and simplified to obtain the conversion factor C = Fsρ2. Where ρ2 is the bulk density of the sample. Thus, the conversion factor could be determined, and the relaxation time could be converted into pore size by multiplying with the conversion factor. This approach has been previously described in the literature.37

Since the mercury intrusion capillary pressure method would inevitably destroy the microstructure of the sample, the conversion coefficient was obtained by the low-temperature liquid nitrogen adsorption method.42,43 The values obtained are 1.62 × 10–8 for high-rank coal, 3.54 × 10–8 for medium-rank coal, and 3.54 × 10–8 for low-rank coal.42,43 After obtaining the conversion factor, the T2 distribution curve is converted into the pore size distribution curve.

Based on the pore size distribution data measured by the NMR T2 spectrum, combined with the principle of pore classification by many scholars,44 the pores are divided into the following categories: ultramicropores, micropores, small pores, mesopores, macropores, and microfractures. The ultramicropores category includes pores with a size of less than 2 nm, while micropores range in size from 2 to 10 nm. Small pores have sizes ranging from 10 to 50 nm, while mesopores range from 50 to 1000 nm. Macropores are defined as having sizes ranging from 1000 to 10,000 nm, and microfractures are pores with a size greater than 10,000 nm. This classification scheme allows for the characterization of the coal reservoir pore–fracture network system and provides insight into the transport properties of water and other fluids in coal.44

The T2 spectrum comprises a horizontal axis that denotes the relaxation time and a vertical axis that corresponds to the amplitude. A larger peak area toward the left of the spectrum indicates the presence of small nanopores in the sample, with the fluid inside them mostly immobilized in a bound state, as evidenced by a short relaxation time and a fast relaxation rate. Conversely, larger peak areas toward the right side of the spectrum signal the presence of medium and large pores and fractures, as well as a dynamic fluid state, due to high relaxation times.

The T2 spectrum of fully saturated water samples indicates that the four tested samples exhibit different pore size distribution patterns. This is attributed to the fact that each coal sample has undergone distinct coalification processes and formation conditions, resulting in variations in their individual physical properties (Figure 3). The T2 spectral peaks of low-rank and medium-rank coals can be roughly distinguished in sequence as micropores, intermediate pores, and macropores–fractures as the relaxation time increases. The area under each peak represents the water content within different-sized pores after complete saturation. High-rank coal, with poor connectivity between macropores and microfractures, exhibits four distinct peak shapes.

T2 distribution of water-saturated coal samples.

According to the similarity of the T2 spectra, the four samples can be divided into two groups: low-rank to medium-rank coal and high-rank coal. In the low-rank to medium-rank coal group, both the ZJM and DT samples exhibit well-developed micropores and small pores, displaying good connectivity. The difference lies in the significant development of large pore sizes in the ZJM sample and strong connectivity between pore peaks at different scales. This greatly enhances the transport and diffusion of gases and liquids between pores of different sizes. In contrast, the DT sample has relatively weak pore development and poor connectivity between micropores and mesopores, to some extent hindering the free transport of water. In the high-rank coal group, the DS and SH samples show high similarity, indicating the presence of developed ultramicropores and highly developed small pores. However, the connectivity between the pores is poor, greatly limiting the movement of water.

Spontaneous Imbibition Characteristics Based on T2 Distribution

The coal samples were analyzed while in a desiccated state; however, it should be noted that complete desiccation was not achieved at the designated temperature over a 24-h period. As a result, a portion of bound water may have remained adhered to the walls of the micropores or supermicropores within the samples.

The experimental results, as illustrated in Figure 5, demonstrate that the samples exhibit bimodal pore size distributions after the drying process, with a highly prominent peak corresponding to micropores and a markedly less significant peak corresponding to intermediate pores. Notably, these two peaks appear to be disconnected from each other. Upon initiation of the imbibition experiment, the previously separate peaks become concurrently absorbed, resulting in pronounced variations in sample behavior.

T2 distribution of coal samples during spontaneous imbibition experiment.

The ZJM coal sample exhibited three distinct peaks within a brief period after the initiation of the experiment, with all peaks exhibiting strong connectivity, indicative of a substantial number of interconnected pores within the coal sample and simultaneous water capillary absorption (Figure 5a). Over time, the peak values of each peak increased at varying rates, with the integral area of both micro- and intermediate-sized pores expanding considerably. The results also highlighted the role of pore diameter in water capillary absorption during spontaneous imbibition, with capillary force acting as the primary driving force. The observed trend showed that smaller pore sizes corresponded to stronger capillary forces and, thus, facilitated capillary absorption. Therefore, pores with smaller diameters played a dominant role in the process. Notably, the peak of water capillary absorption gradually shifted toward higher pore sizes over time, indicating that smaller pores were filled first due to their relatively stronger capillary forces. As time progressed, capillary forces diminished, leading to a shift in water capillary absorption toward larger pores.

Figure 5b illustrates that the DT coal sample exhibited an imbibition pattern similar to the ZJM coal sample, with the three-peak pattern and right-shifted peak appearing within a short period of time. However, the DT coal sample differed from the ZJM sample in terms of weak connectivity, where all peaks remained independent from the onset of imbibition. Specifically, the micropore capillary absorption peak and intermediate pore peak exhibited strong independence until 360 min when the intermediate pore capillary absorption peak and macropore–microfracture water peak showed a partial connection. The degree of connectivity gradually increased and ultimately peaked at approximately 7220 min.

Figure 5c,d highlights the strong similarities in the evolution of imbibition phenomena between the DS and SH samples. As experimental time increased, the samples exhibited a shift from single-peak to double-peak behavior, with excellent connectivity between the two types of pores leading to gradual depletion of residual water in the ultramicropores and biased water uptake in the microfracture overload. Notably, the third water peak in medium and large pores appeared at 30 min. The SH sample exhibited closure between the two pores at 1440 min, and water exchange reached a stable state, while the water inlet in the microfracture at 2880 min was relatively insignificant, resulting in a fourth peak that appeared early in the experiment but was not significant. These results suggest that water uptake of the sample in the macropore–microfracture was relatively low. Overall, the differences between the DS and SH samples in T2 patterns during spontaneous imbibition evolution were minor, with only slight variations observed in some pore sizes.

This observation is consistent with the general understanding of spontaneous imbibition in porous media, where capillary force is the main driving force. As the pore size decreases, capillary force strengthens, facilitating water capillary absorption. Thus, the pore size distribution plays a dominant role in spontaneous imbibition, and the impact of larger pores on water uptake is relatively insignificant when compared to smaller pores. The smaller pores have a larger specific surface area and a higher capillary pressure, leading to a more efficient water uptake process. The proportion of small-scale pores in each sample is a key factor determining the overall water uptake performance, which is reflected in the observed differences in water intake in different pore sizes among the four coal samples.

Imbibition Capacity of Different Pore Sizes

The parameter ω is commonly utilized to assess the water uptake capacity of a particular sample and is mathematically defined as the ratio of the volume of water absorbed (v) to the maximum volume of water that can be absorbed (vw). This metric serves as a useful indicator of the efficiency of the sample’s pore structure in absorbing and retaining fluids.

A total of 10 time points were selected from the experiment to calculate the contribution and create a pie chart. The time axis, from inner to outer, represents 0, 5, 30, 60, 120, 360, 720, 1440, 2880, and 4320 min.

As depicted in Figure 8, the time-dependent contribution of different pore sizes in various samples is illustrated. The findings demonstrate that the distribution of pore sizes in the ZJM sample is relatively even, with the contribution remaining constant around micropores (30%), small pores (40%), mesopores (20%), and macropores (5%). The contribution of micropores decreases slowly over time, while small pores continue to increase. The contribution of ultramicropores, mesopores, and macropores remains basically unchanged throughout the experiment. The DT sample, on the other hand, possesses a unique pore structure with a predominant percentage of micropores and small pores, contributing to around 90% of the total pore volume. As the experiment proceeds, the contribution of micropores decreases and that of small pores increases. In the steady-state condition, the micropores contribute around 37%, while the small pores contribute 56% to the total water absorption.

Contribution of water content of different pore sizes.

For SH and DS samples, the increase in the contribution of small pores is significant, rising from 24 to 84% and 18 to 78%, respectively. This reflects a considerable increase in the contribution of small pores over time and their absolute dominance at stabilization. This is because the percentage of small pores in the pore structure of SH and DS is greater than 80%, resulting in concentrated water transport, as shown in Figure 4.

It is worth noting that although the ultramicropores account for a relatively small proportion of the total pore volume, they play a significant role in the prewetting period for both DS and SH samples, contributing approximately 20% to the water absorption. This is mainly attributed to the strong capillary force of the ultramicropores during the pre-spontaneous imbibition period, making them easier to absorb water despite their small size, and the degree of development of ultramicropores in these samples.

Overall, the results suggest that the water absorption contribution of micropores and small pores dominates, with a gradual decrease in the contribution of supermicropores and micropores, while the contribution of mesopores and macropores remains stable. The observed trends are largely attributable to the weakening of the capillary force and the progressive shift in water transport toward small pores in the later stages of the experiment.

Imbibition Rate

Figure 9 presents a clear demarcation of the imbibition phase into two stages: the initial rapid rising stage, which persisted for 240 min, and the subsequent stable stage. The pore sizes were initially unsynchronized and exhibited no distinct pattern. In the later stage of the experiment, a gradual occurrence of minor desaturation in the larger pores can also be referred to as reverse imbibition (this phenomenon was particularly pronounced in DT).

Water capillary absorption rate of different pore sizes.

The results presented in Figures 4 and 6 exhibit a trend of ZJM < DT < DS < SH for the time required to reach a stable stage. It is thus hypothesized that the water saturation time of coal is reliant on pore network sorting characteristics and connectivity. If the pore structure exhibits well-defined sorting characteristics and is well-connected, then it is more favorable for rapid saturation and stabilization. Conversely, if a certain pore size is overdeveloped while other pore sizes remain underdeveloped, it may result in a slower rate of water imbibition.

The interaction between fluids and formations varies across different coal types. In hydraulic fracturing, coal seams with high levels of pore sorting characteristics and good connectivity exhibit rapid permeability. On the other hand, coal seams with a low degree of sorting characteristic of pores and poor connectivity result in slow imbibition, leading to minimal reservoir damage.

Two-dimensional Spectrum of Polymorphic Water Conversion

The analysis of reservoir fluids is crucial for predicting the behavior of hydrocarbon reservoirs. However, the complex signal overlap problems of various hydrogen-containing materials in the 1D NMR T2 spectrum alone have limited the accuracy of identifying different fluid components. In contrast, the 2D NMR T1–T2 spectrum provides two-dimensional information, such as the internal rotational magnetic field G, diffusion coefficient D, and longitudinal relaxation time T1, which enhances the sensitivity to measured components, resulting in accurate and quantitative identification of different fluid components in the reservoir.37,39,45

The relaxation time of different forms of water varies, leading to shorter relaxation times and smaller diffusion coefficients for components with restricted motion, while free-moving water exhibits relatively longer relaxation times and higher diffusion coefficients. 2D spectroscopy can characterize different phases of the same hydrogen-containing fraction and the intrinsic connection between different hydrogen-containing fractions, surpassing single-dimensional NMR in accurately studying fluid transport processes of the fractions.

By measuring the two-dimensional spectrum characteristics of various water-saturated coal samples, the obtained signals distinguished multiple phase water fractions, classified into four types according to Sun et al.38 These were hydrogen-bearing matters in the matrix, bound water in small-sized, poorly connected inorganic pores and some organic pores, free water in microcracks and large-sized, well-connected inorganic pores, and structural water in the crystal lattice of structural water. Based on the imbibition experimental data, the selected four samples were monitored at 0, 5, 60, 360, 720, and 1440 min time points, and the corresponding two-dimensional profiles were plotted, as depicted in Figure 11. It is evident from the figure that each dried sample’s hydrogen-containing materials in the matrix exhibited remarkably high signal values before the experiment’s commencement. Furthermore, the structural water within the lattice did not completely evaporate during the drying process at high temperatures. Due to the peculiar pore structure, some of the samples, namely, ZJM and DT, revealed the presence of trace amounts of bound and free water attached to the micropores’ walls or remaining in the ultramicropores. However, the other two samples did not display signals indicating that the pores contained almost no residual water component, except for the water present in the lattice.

T1–T2 spectrum during the spontaneous imbibition of coal samples.

As the imbibition time progresses, the ingress of water results in a rapid decline in the signal intensity of hydrogen-containing substances in the matrix while characterizing the water component with differing transport and phase transition patterns observed among the various samples. The ZJM samples displayed a marked proclivity toward rapid conversion of their components to bound water during the initial moments of imbibition, accompanied by noticeable interference from the matrix’s hydrogen-containing components. Subsequently, a gradual diffusion to free water was observed, with bound water occupying the dominant position of phase water after 60 min. Conversely, the DT sample exhibited two-dimensional spectrum characteristics comparable to those observed in the previous sample’s dry state but with discernible variations in the moisture migration direction upon the moisture entering the sample, as indicated by the white arrow in the figure. Meanwhile, the migration of free water was negligible, with a nonobvious transformation relationship, as the instrument did not detect any signal value at 720 min. This suggests that free water migrated in trace amounts before the start of imbibition and gradually transformed into bound water over time, eventually stabilizing at 360 min with almost no discernible change.

In contrast, the DS and SH samples converged in their dry state, with no free or bound phase water detected, indicating a complete drying process with little to no residual water present. The 2D water peak at 60 min revealed that the bound water had already assumed an absolute position, with an elliptical morphology and no free water detected in the large pores, which only showed a transition to the bound state.

Spatial Evolution of Imbibition Based on Layer Division T2 Spectra

The total T2 spectrum-based test provided an overall understanding of the fluid changes in individual coal samples during the imbibition process. However, it was unable to show the evolution pattern of each layer in the sample during imbibition. To address this issue, the SH sample was selected, and layer division scans of samples with different imbibition times were performed. Upon inversion, a layer division spectrum was obtained, as illustrated in Figure 12. The layer division spectrum visualized the signal intensity of different layers from the bottom to the top along the long axis of the coal sample, enabling observation of the real-time evolution pattern of each layer.

Layer division T2 spectrum for SH samples at different experimental times.

In general, the permeability of coal is primarily influenced by cleat and fracture contributions compared to the coal matrix. Therefore, exploring the relationship between fractures and self-imbibition behavior can provide a basis for the preferential selection of favorable areas in coalbed methane reservoirs.

Previous studies have examined the impact of fractures on self-imbibition in coal and have found that in the initial stages of the experiment, imbibition fractures impede the flow of water within the coal. However, in the later stages of imbibition, fractures play an increasingly important role in spontaneous imbibition.46 Under the influence of long-range van der Waals cohesive forces, a thin water film is formed on the fracture walls due to capillary pressure, enhancing the wetting of the fracture surfaces and increasing capillary pressure.

It was indicated that the NMR signal intensity at the bottom of the sample was significantly higher than that at the top at the same time, and the peak of the micropore was higher than that of the microfracture. When the water capillary absorption in the microfractures of each layer was basically in the saturated state at 5 min, the signal intensity was stable. However, the peak of the micropore appeared at the top, and the phenomenon is believed to occur because water initially advances along the face cleats and butt cleats, progressing along the vertical pathways into the top section. Subsequently, it diffuses laterally into micropores, as illustrated in Figure 13. As time passed, the longitudinal coordinate of the stratification spectrum increased sharply, resulting in the micropore peak being far from the microfracture saturation peak. In the later stages of imbibition, the internal pores of the coal become essentially saturated with water, and fractures become the main pathway for swelling. Capillary forces gradually strengthen, facilitating the imbibition process.

Discussion

Mechanism of Spontaneous Imbibition and Regression Effect Produced at the Later Stage of the Experiment

By utilizing NMR pore size distributions instead of individual capillary radii, this study extends its analysis from single capillaries to complex multiscale pore systems. Previous studies have established a mathematical model that takes into account several factors, including the surface relaxation rate of low-order coal, the surface tension of water on coal, and pore curvature, while also identifying gravity as a non-negligible factor in vertical well flow.47

The capillary force plays a crucial role during the rapid rising stage, where smaller pore sizes exhibit stronger capillary forces, facilitating the capillary absorption of water. During the rapid wicking stage, water rapidly percolates into ultramicropores and then transfers rapidly into micropores and small pores. The growth rate of ultramicropores is the fastest, although their contribution gradually decreases over time. In contrast, the contributions of micropores and small pores continue to increase, while the contribution of macropore–microfractures remains relatively constant. In the subsequent stable stage, the water volume remains mostly unchanged, with fluid exchange occurring across different pores. There is a gradual transport trend from ultramicropores and micropores into small pores.

However, the results from Figures 5, 7, and 8 reveal that at the end of the imbibition experiment, there is a slight decrease in the peak value during the high relaxation stage, accompanied by a slow increase in water content. The desaturation phenomenon is most pronounced in larger pore sizes. Larger pore sizes lead to reduced water adhesion to the pore surfaces and a greater influence of gravity, thereby increasing the extent of reverse imbibition. Due to their relatively low proportion among all pore sizes, the desaturation level in mesopores and large pores is not significant for the DT samples. Additionally, the unique development characteristics of DS and SH ultramicropores cause a leftward shift of the water peak for this pore size, resulting in a reduced integrated area of the peak in the imbibition experiment. This reduction indicates the occurrence of water loss and imbibition in small pore sizes as well. The research results suggest that the strong connectivity between intermediate pores and macropore–microfractures in coal restricts the diffusion of water, making it difficult for water in the macropore–microfracture network to continuously imbibe and maintain its original volume in the pore space. Figure 5 demonstrates that the highest proportion of pores smaller than 50 nm in the coal samples plays a decisive role, accounting for over ninety percent of the total pore volume in the coal samples and exerting a significant influence during the imbibition process.

The results demonstrate that the effective combination of gravity and viscous resistance of free water leads directly to the decrease of slope at the end of the spontaneous imbibition experiment. This represents a balance between the dominant capillary management in the prediffusion period and the combined effect of gravity and viscous drag. The reverse imbibition of the experimental samples becomes more pronounced approximately 8500 min after the start of the experiment.

Relationship between Pore Structure and Imbibition Properties and Coal Rank

The research results indicate a significant correlation between the internal pore network and imbibition characteristics of the coal samples with respect to Ro. As Ro increases, the pore structure transitions from well-ordered pores to highly developed small pores and less developed large pores (Figure 4). The two studied coal samples, ZJM and DT, demonstrate substantial development of micropores, constituting 23.905 and 35.046% of the total porosity, respectively. This significant micropore development sets them apart from higher-rank coals, which exhibit minimal micropore development. Apart from the moderately developed mesopores in the ZJM sample with the lowest Ro, the other samples show extremely poor development of mesopores.

The variations in the pore structure development of raw coal result in different interactions between fluids and coal seams. Figures 6, 8, and 9 demonstrate that lower-rank coal samples are more sensitive to hydraulic conditions, with a more even distribution of contributions from different pore sizes and a shorter time to reach a stable imbibition state. The imbibition contributions for medium- and high-rank coals are limited to micropores and small pores, and the overall imbibition rate and the rate of imbibition in small pores tend to align. This indicates that small pores dominate the free imbibition rate in the hydraulic fracturing process of higher-rank coals. Therefore, different fracturing methods and wetting techniques should be employed for coals of different ranks to enhance coalbed methane recovery efficiency and mitigate the impact of hydraulic measures on reservoir permeability.

Conclusions

This study utilized spontaneous imbibition experiments on different rank coal samples to investigate the effect of spontaneous water imbibition from coal seams during hydraulic fracturing on CBM production. To analyze the pore development and distribution in coal and the state of pore water distribution, the 1D NMR T2 spectrum, 2D NMR T1–T2 spectrum, and layer division T2 spectra were utilized to analyze peak shapes, peak area ratios, maximum peak values, and peak movement patterns of the fully saturated and spontaneous imbibition relaxation spectrum. The transport and transformation of multiphase water were also investigated to obtain an intrinsic relationship between pore development and distribution in coal and pore water storage state, imbibition rate of different pore sizes, imbibition capacity, and transport and transformation of multiphase water. Consequently, the following conclusions can be drawn from this analysis:

(1)The nature of the reservoir is affected by the coal rank. Low-rank coal has good pore space connectivity, significant cut fracture development, strong permeability, and fractures that are mostly filled with free water, indicating good physical properties of the reservoir. High-rank coal, on the other hand, has large pore space but shows poor connectivity, poor fracture development, weak permeability, and poor reservoir.

The general rule of contribution generally follows that of small pores (10–50 nm) ≥ micropores (2–10 nm) ≥ ultramicropores (<2 nm) ≥ mesopores (50–1000 nm) ≥ macropores (1000–10,000 nm) ≥ microfractures (≥10,000 nm). A more balanced pore size development and better connectivity of the coal reservoir may lead to rapid saturation and stabilization during hydraulic fracturing.

The general trend of the phase state is to convert to the bound water state.
