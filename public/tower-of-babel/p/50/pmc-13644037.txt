Timing of florfenicol administration determines the protective efficacy of an Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae live attenuated vaccine in pigs
Authors: Song Qi, Qi-yun He, Peng Chen, Yi-qing Li, Rong-chen Jiang, Zhong-en Teng, Jin-fu Huang, Wei-cheng Bei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActinobacillus pleuropneumoniae (APP) is a major cause of severe respiratory disease in pigs and is associated with substantial economic losses. During outbreaks, the urgent use of antibiotics in infected herds often conflicts with emergency vaccination of at-risk animals, as antibiotics may impair the effectiveness of live vaccines. This study investigated how the timing of florfenicol administration affects the protective immunity induced by a live attenuated APP vaccine.MethodsThirty pigs were immunized with a live attenuated APP vaccine (HB04M strain). Animals were divided into six groups (n = 5 per group). Four groups received a single therapeutic dose of florfenicol at the time of immunization (0 dpi), or at 1, 3, and 5 days post-immunization (dpi). The remaining groups served as controls, including a vaccine-only group without florfenicol treatment and an unvaccinated blank control group. Nasal colonization by the vaccine strain was monitored by qPCR. Humoral immune responses (ApxII/III antibodies, IL-4) and cellular immune responses (IFN-γ, IL-6) were measured by ELISA. At 7 dpi, all pigs were challenged intranasally with a lethal dose of mixed heterologous APP serotypes 1 and 5. Clinical signs, survival, and lung pathology were evaluated for 7 days after challenge.ResultsFlorfenicol significantly reduced nasal colonization by the vaccine strain, and this reduction was associated with a marked, time-dependent inhibition of both humoral and cellular immune responses. In the G1 (florfenicol at 0 dpi), seroconversion was almost completely abolished, and protective efficacy was lowest, with a survival rate of 20%. Clinical scores and lung lesions in this group were comparable to those in the blank control group, in which survival was 0%. Protection improved as antibiotic treatment was delayed. Survival rates were 40% in G2 (1 dpi/florfenicol) and G3 (3 dpi/florfenicol) groups, and 60% in G4 (5 dpi/florfenicol). The G4 group also exhibited milder clinical signs and significantly less severe lung pathology than the early-treatment groups (P < 0.05). The vaccine-only group (G5) showed the highest level of protection, with an 80% survival rate.ConclusionsThe inhibitory effect of florfenicol on the protective efficacy of a live attenuated APP vaccine is strongly dependent on the timing of administration. To preserve vaccine-induced immunity, florfenicol should be avoided during the first 3 days after vaccination. Delaying treatment until at least 5 days post-vaccination may provide a better balance between necessary antibiotic therapy and the developmen of protective immunity, offering a practical approach to APP outbreak control.Keywords: Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae (APP), Florfenicol, Live attenuated vaccine (LAV), Immune response

Introduction

Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae (APP) is a Gram-negative bacterium in the family Pasteurellaceae and one of the main causes of swine respiratory disease worldwide. It causes acute fibrinohemorrhagic and necrotizing pleuropneumonia, and is associated with high morbidity and mortality in susceptible herds [1]. Chronic infection can also reduce feed intake and slow weight gain, which seriously affects the productivity of the swine industry [2]. Currently, 19 serotypes (1–19) and two biotypes (NAD-dependent and NAD-independent) have been identified, with differences in virulence and geographic distribution [2, 3]. Effective control of APP is therefore important for both swine health and the economic sustainability of pork production.

Current control of APP mainly depends on biosecurity, vaccination, and antibiotic therapy. Among commercially available vaccine types, three main categories are used for APP control: bacterins (inactivated whole-cell vaccines), subunit vaccines (based on Apx toxins and outer membrane proteins), and gene-deleted live attenuated vaccines (LAVs) [4]. As we have previously reviewed and compared [5, 6], bacterins are the most widely used products in the field. However, they are serotype‑specific and confer only limited cross‑protection against heterologous serotypes; even within the same capsular serotype, antigenic variation can lead to vaccination failure. Subunit vaccines can provide broader protection regardless of APP serotype, yet they lack whole‑cell components and thus have limited bactericidal effects—moreover, vaccinated pigs may still become asymptomatic carriers. Given these limitations, gene‑deleted LAVs have attracted considerable research attention. Unlike bacterins and subunit vaccines, LAVs can mimic natural infection and in vivo replication, effectively stimulating both cellular and humoral immune responses [4, 7]. Indeed, in our previous study, the APP gene-deleted LAV (HB04M strain) provided nearly complete protection against heterologous virulent challenge as early as 7 days after immunization, further supporting its strong potential for emergency application during APP outbreaks [6].

However, in clinical farm settings, APP often has a short incubation period and can spread rapidly through a herd. Outbreaks may develop quickly, with high morbidity and mortality in a short time, leaving little opportunity to begin vaccination early enough. In these situations, emergency antibiotic treatment is often necessary. Previous studies have shown that timely antimicrobial treatment after the onset of clinical symptoms is critical for reducing mortality and slowing transmission [1, 2]. Nevertheless, antibiotic use may interfere with the development of protective immunity. Extensive or early antimicrobial treatment can weaken immune responses and may increase susceptibility to later infections [8]. Evidence from mouse models has shown that early antibiotic treatment impairs acquired immunity against secondary challenge with pathogens such as Listeria monocytogenes and Chlamydia trachomatis [9, 10]. Similar findings have also been reported in swine, where early antibiotic use following APP infection negatively affected the development of adaptive immune responses [1]. Antibiotics have also been reported to alter vaccine-induced immune responses in several animal species. For instance, Hu et al. reported that therapeutic doses of florfenicol partially induced transient toxicity in the hematopoietic and lymphoid organs of piglets, thereby impacting their hematopoiesis and immune function [11]. These findings raise the possibility that antibiotics may also interfere with the colonization, replication, antigen processing, or antigen presentation of APP gene-deleted LAV in vivo, which could in turn affect the development of both humoral and cellular immunity.

Florfenicol is a broad‑spectrum amphenicolic antibiotic that inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 50 S subunit of the 70 S ribosome [12]. Because of its good tissue penetration, rapid absorption, relatively low toxicity, and broad antibacterial activity, it is widely used in swine production to prevent and treat APP infections [13]. Previous studies have shown that florfenicol can suppress T‑lymphocyte proliferation and reduce B‑cell antibody secretion, which may weaken immune responses to vaccination [14, 15]. However, whether antibiotic treatment affects the protective efficacy of the APP LAV remains unclear, as much less is known about their effects on bacterial LAVs compared with viral vaccines [16–18]. Early antibiotic treatment after vaccination may inhibit colonization and antigen presentation of the vaccine strain [19, 20], while delaying treatment may increase clinical losses by allowing further spread of the pathogen. In practice, this uncertainty often leads to empirical treatment decisions, which may contribute to vaccine failure and disease recurrence. For this reason, it is important to clarify how florfenicol affects the immunogenicity of APP LAVs and to determine a rational interval between vaccination and antibiotic administration for integrated APP control.

In this study, we evaluated the effect of therapeutic florfenicol treatment on the protective immune responses induced by an APP gene-deleted LAV. By administering florfenicol at various time points after immunization, we aimed to identify the shortest safe interval that would not impair vaccine-induced protective immunity and to preliminarily explore the underlying mechanism. To mimic an outbreak situation, pigs were challenged on day 7 after immunization with a heterologous virulent APP mixture of serotypes 1 and 5. This design allowed us to assess both the emergency protective efficacy of the vaccine and the effect of antibiotic timing on that protection. The results are expected to offer practical guidance for balancing antibiotic use with emergency vaccination during APP outbreaks in swine farms, and to support the more rational combined use of antibiotics and bacterial LAVs in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The strains utilized for challenge-protection efficacy evaluation included APP serotype 1 strain 4074 (S4074) and serotype 5 strain JN. Strain S4074 was generously provided by Dr. Pat Blackall at the Queensland Animal Health Laboratory, Australia. Strain JN was isolated from clinically diseased pigs on a local farm and is preserved at the National Key Laboratory of Agricultural Microbiology Resources Exploration and Utilization (Huazhong Agricultural University). The virulence of both strains has been verified in previous studies, in which a challenge dose of 1 × 10⁸ CFU resulted in 100% mortality within 5 days [21].

The vaccine strain was an APP gene-deleted LAV strain, HB04M (serotype 7), derived from the parental strain HB04C⁻ (serotype 7). Specific details of the genetic modifications are not provided here due to intellectual property restrictions, but the construction procedure and related data have been reported previously [22]. The HB04M strain is preserved at the National Key Laboratory of Agricultural Microbiology Resources Exploration and Utilization, Huazhong Agricultural University, and has been commercially produced by several domestic vaccine manufacturers. All strains were cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) or on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) supplemented with 10 µg/mL nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and 10% inactivated newborn bovine serum at 37 °C.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the vaccine strain

Prior to evaluating the protective efficacy, we determined the susceptibility of the APP LAV strain to florfenicol. Florfenicol injection (30%, w/v) was purchased from Zhongnong Huawei Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China), a product commonly used for the treatment of APP infections in pigs. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the recommended dose for APP and Pasteurella multocida (PM) infections is 0.05–0.067 mL/kg body weight, and the formulation has a half-life of approximately 48 h. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of florfenicol against the LAV strain was determined using the microbroth dilution method. The strain was inoculated in TSB containing 10% inactivated newborn calf serum and NAD, and co-cultured with serial concentrations of florfenicol (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 µg/mL). OD₆₀₀ was measured every 30 min over a 12 h incubation period using a Bioscreen C automated microbial growth curve analysis system. The experiment was performed in triplicate, and data from a representative experiment are shown. Susceptibility was interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI, 2018).

Preparation of the live vaccine

The LAV strain was cultivated in 100 mL TSB supplemented with 10% newborn calf serum and 10 µg/mL NAD at 37 °C for 12 h. The culture was then diluted 1:10 into fresh prewarmed TSB and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 12 h until the bacteria reached logarithmic phase. Bacteria were then harvested and washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS: 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na₂HPO₄, 2.8 mM KH₂PO₄, pH 7.4), and mixed with gelatin to a final concentration of 1.5% (w/v) before lyophilization. For immunization, the lyophilized vaccine was reconstituted in PBS and adjusted to a final concentration of approximately 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Each pig received 2 mL of vaccine.

Animals and housing

All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the China Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (1988) and the Hubei Provincial Regulations (2005). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (Permit Number: HZAUSW-2024-0004). Animals that reached predetermined humane endpoints during the study, and all surviving animals at the end of the experiment, were euthanized in accordance with approved ethical guidelines.

Thirty healthy 8-week-old pigs were obtained from a high-health-status closed herd in Chongzuo, China. Prior to experimental enrollment, oronasal swabs and whole blood samples were subjected to qPCR analysis to ascertain the absence of key porcine pathogens, including APP, PM, porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (Mhp), porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV-2), pseudorabies virus (PRV), porcine parvovirus (PPV), and classical swine fever virus (CSFV). Total nucleic acids were extracted from the samples using an automated nucleic acid extraction system (Guangzhou Weiboxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). qPCR was performed on a real-time PCR system (Xi’an Tianlong Technology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China) using commercial detection kits (Biostone Animal Health LLC, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Furthermore, the herd had not been vaccinated against PRRSV for an extended period, and consistent seronegativity for PRRSV across different production stages was verified using a commercial ELISA kit (PRRS X3 Ab Test, IDEXX Laboratories, USA). After arrival, all pigs were tested and confirmed to be seronegative for ApxII/III antibodies using a commercial ELISA kit (Biostone Animal Health LLC, USA), indicating no evidence of maternal antibodies and previous natural APP infection.

The pigs were identified with ear tags and randomly assigned to six experimental groups (n = 5 per group). Group assignments were balanced as far as possible for sex, body weight, and litter origin. The six groups were labeled as G1 (0 dpi/florfenicol), G2 (1 dpi/florfenicol), G3 (3 dpi/florfenicol), G4 (5 dpi/florfenicol), G5 (vaccine-only), and G6 (blank control). Each group was housed separately in an isolation pen equipped with individual drinking nipple and a semicircular cement feeder. All pigs were managed according to institutional guidelines for the ethical use of animals in research. Upon arrival at the research facility, pigs received oral Flunixin Meglumine (10%, w/w, Hebei Lihua Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd) three times as a prophylactic anti‑inflammatory measure, together with a 7‑day course of multivitamin and electrolyte supplementation (Bayer Sichuan Animal Health Co., Ltd, Sichuan, China). These treatments were administered to mitigate stress‑associated inflammatory responses induced by transport and regrouping, as our preliminary experiments showed that such stress, and routine procedures like blood collection, could cause non‑specific elevations in inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL‑6, a key parameter for evaluating vaccine‑induced immune responses. To avoid confounding effects on baseline cytokine data, all pharmaceutical agents and nutritional supplements were discontinued for three days prior to the formal experiment.

Experiment design

At the beginning of the study, the mean body weight of the pigs in each group was approximately 25 kg. Except for G6 (blank control, non-immunized and non-florfenicol-treated), all pigs were immunized with 2 mL of LAV suspension (2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL in PBS). Florfenicol (2 mL per pig) was administered intramuscularly at different time points relative to immunization: concurrently with immunization (G1), or at 1 dpi (G2), 3 dpi (G3), or 5 dpi (G4). G5 (vaccine-only) received the vaccine without florfenicol treatment. Details of the experimental groups and vaccination protocols are summarized in Table 1.

At 7 dpi, all pigs were challenged intranasally using a specialized nasal immunization device that connects to a syringe and nebulizes the liquid inoculum into a fine mist. The challenge inoculum consisted of a mixed bacterial suspension containing 3 mL of strain S4074 (> 10⁹ CFU/mL) and 3 mL of strain JN (> 10⁹ CFU/mL), administered as 6 mL per pig (3 mL per nostril). Following challenge, pigs were monitored daily for clinical symptoms for 7 days according to a previously described protocol [6]. Rectal temperatures were recorded at 12 h, and at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post‑challenge.

Preparation of the APP serotype 1 strain 4074 and serotype 5 strain JN for challenge

The challenge suspension was prepared from frozen stocks of APP serotype 1 strain 4074 (S4074) and serotype 5 strain JN. Each strain was streaked onto TSA plates supplemented with 10 µg/mL NAD and 10% newborn bovine serum, and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were then selected and inoculated into 10 mL TSB containing the same supplements, and cultured with shaking at 37 °C for approximately 8 h to reach concentrations of approximately 10⁸ to 10⁹ CFU/mL, as confirmed by plate counting.

At 7 dpi, the bacterial suspension was plated in triplicate for enumeration, transported to the animal facility under cold chain conditions within 15 min, and administered intranasally to each pig (6 mL per pig; 3 mL per nostril). The remaining suspension was returned to the laboratory under cold chain conditions and plated again in triplicate. The final challenge concentration was calculated as the average of the pre- and post-challenge plate counts.

Blood samples collection

Blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava before immunization (0 dpi) and at 3, 5, and 7 dpi. For each pig, 4 mL of blood was collected into EDTA-K₂ anticoagulant tubes. The tubes were gently inverted several times immediately after collection to ensure proper mixing with the anticoagulant. Samples were transported to the laboratory on ice, and complete blood count analysis was performed within 12 h. After blood cell analysis, the remaining samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 6,000×g for 5 min to separate plasma. The plasma was aliquoted into sterile cryotubes and stored at -20 °C for subsequent cytokine and antibody analysis.

Nasal swab samples collection and qPCR test

Nasal swab samples were collected at 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 dpi. During sampling, pigs were properly restrained, and sterile flocked swabs were inserted approximately 1–2 cm into the ventral nasal passage of each nostril, gently rotated, and removed. Swab tips were immediately placed in centrifuge tubes containing 1 mL sterile PBS and maintained under cold chain conditions. All samples were transported to the laboratory and processed for nucleic acid extraction and qPCR within 12 h. To minimize cross‑contamination, G6 (blank control) was sampled first, followed by the immunized groups. After each pig was sampled, the restrainer disinfected their hands, and the sampler changed their disposable latex gloves.

To assess colonization of the APP LAV strain in the nasal cavity, an ApxⅡA‑based qPCR method was used. The HB04M vaccine strain carries an ApxIV gene deletion; therefore, the commonly used ApxIV‑based qPCR for wild‑type APP detection cannot recognize the vaccine strain. The ApxⅡA gene is retained in the vaccine strain and serves as a specific marker for tracking its nasal colonization. This method has been validated in our laboratory for specificity and sensitivity in discriminating the vaccine strain from field isolates. The assay was performed using a commercial ApxⅡA qPCR kit (Biostone Animal Health LLC, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 µL of swab suspension was mixed with 20 µL of proteinase K and incubated at room temperature for 1 min. Total DNA was extracted using an automated nucleic acid extraction system (Guangzhou Weiboxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). qPCR was performed in a 25 µL reaction mixture containing 19 µL of pre‑mixed buffer, 1 µL of enzyme mix, and 5 µL of DNA template, with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 55 °C for 30 s. A cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 37 was considered positive. Bacterial load was calculated from a standard curve generated using recombinant plasmids containing the ApxⅡA gene fragment and expressed as genome copies per swab.

Hematological examinations and antibody, cytokines detection in plasma

Complete blood count analysis was performed on EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood using an automated hematology analyzer (DYSMEX Corporation, Shenzhen). The main parameter analyzed was the total white blood cell (WBC) count. Plasma antibodies against ApxII/III were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Biostone Animal Health LLC, USA). Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm, and the post-positivity (PP) value was calculated as: (sample OD / mean positive control OD) × 100%. Samples with PP < 40% were considered negative, and those with PP ≥ 40% positive.

Plasma concentrations of IL-4, IL-6, and IFN-γ were measured using commercial ELISA kits (UpingBio Technology Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China), with detection limits of 10 pg/mL for IL-4/6 and 30 pg/mL for IFN-γ. All samples were tested in duplicate, and cytokine concentrations were calculated by interpolation from standard curves generated according to the manufacturer’s protocols. For each animal, cytokine concentrations at 7 dpi were normalized to the corresponding baseline value at 0 dpi to calculate the fold change (FC = value at 7 dpi / value at 0 dpi). This normalization was applied to account for inter‑individual variability in baseline cytokine levels. Fold change data were log2‑transformed prior to statistical analysis, and the results are presented as mean fold change ± SD.

Determination of survival rate and clinical symptom scoring after challenge

Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1) based on mortality after challenge. Clinical manifestations in challenged pigs were quantified using a scoring system adapted from Jolie et al. [23]:

Appetite: 0 points (normal feed intake), 1 point (no feed intake). Observations were conducted at fixed 12-hour intervals twice daily, with the total score being the sum of the two observations.

Mental status: 0 points (alert and responsive); 1 point (slightly dull and less alert); 2 points (lethargic and dull); 3 points (prostrate and moribund).

Post-mortem examinations

Following challenge, clinical observations were recorded every 3 h. Pigs that died during the study were promptly necropsied. Pigs exhibiting severe respiratory distress, hypothermia, or a moribund state were humanely euthanized with pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg body weight) and subsequently necropsied. On day 7 post-challenge, all surviving pigs were euthanized and necropsied. The severity of lung lesions was evaluated using the CLP® lung scoring system [24], adapted from the method described by Madec and Kobish [25]. The total lung score for each pig was calculated as the sum of all lesion scores (maximum possible score: 35) and is expressed as mean ± SD.

For histopathology, tissue samples were collected from the border of pneumonic lesions, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (pH 7.2), and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The severity of inflammatory changes, including hemorrhage, vascular thrombosis, edema, necrosis, and fibrinous exudation, was scored semi‑quantitatively on a scale of 0 to 5 [24]. The mean histopathological score for each group is expressed as mean ± SD.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). All data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, with non‑parametric alternatives (Kruskal-Wallis + Dunn’s test) applied when assumptions were violated. For multiple group comparisons at a single time point, one‑way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used. For repeated‑measures across time points, two‑way repeated‑measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post‑hoc test was applied. Binary outcomes (e.g., survival) were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (P < 0.01 for highly significant). The specific tests used for each comparison are indicated in the corresponding figure legends and tables.

Results

Impact of florfenicol on the growth characteristics of the vaccine strain

Florfenicol inhibited growth of the HB04M strain at concentrations ≥ 1 µg/mL, whereas only partial inhibition was observed at 0.5 µg/mL. The MIC of florfenicol against the LAV strain was determined to be below 2.0 µg/mL, indicating that the vaccine strain is susceptible to florfenicol according to CLSI (2018) breakpoints.

Detection of vaccine strain from nasal samples

Florfenicol significantly suppressed nasal colonization by the vaccine strain in a time‑dependent manner (Fig. 1). The strongest suppression was observed in G1 (0 dpi/florfenicol), where bacterial loads at 3 and 5 dpi were significantly lower than those in all other vaccinated groups (P < 0.05). In G3 (3 dpi/florfenicol) and G4 (5 dpi/florfenicol), bacterial loads dropped sharply within 2 days after florfenicol administration, although the vaccine strain was not completely eliminated, as all pigs remained qPCR‑positive thereafter.

Detection of the APP LAV strain copy numbers in nasal swabs at 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 dpi. Detection was performed using a commercial ApxⅡA qPCR kit

At 1 dpi, the vaccine strain was detected in G2, G3, G4, and G5, with no significant differences in bacterial loads or positive rates among these groups. By 3 dpi, G3, G4, and G5 all reached 100% positivity, with substantially higher bacterial loads than G1 and G2. G5 (vaccine‑only) consistently maintained the highest bacterial loads throughout the observation period. Following florfenicol treatment, bacterial loads in G3 (at 5 dpi) and G4 (at 7 dpi) declined significantly from their pre‑treatment peaks, whereas loads in G1 and G2 remained low throughout.

The detailed bacterial load data at each time point, including pairwise statistical comparisons, are presented in Table 2. Overall, these results confirm that therapeutic florfenicol effectively suppresses but does not completely eradicate the vaccine strain, and that the magnitude of this suppression depends on the timing of administration relative to vaccination.

At 3 dpi, WBC counts in G1 and G2 were significantly higher than those in G6 (blank control) (P < 0.05). However, both G1 and G2 had significantly lower counts than G3, G4, and G5 (P < 0.05), none of which had received florfenicol by that time. No significant differences were observed among G3, G4, and G5. At 5 dpi, WBC counts in G1 and G2 increased significantly compared with their 3 dpi levels. In contrast, counts in G3, G4, and G5 decreased significantly, with the most marked decline observed in G3, which was sampled 2 days after florfenicol administration. By 7 dpi, WBC counts had decreased to varying degrees in all groups, and no significant differences were detected among them.

Collectively, these results indicate that early florfenicol administration (G1 and G2) delayed the post‑immunization leukocyte response, whereas later treatment (G3 and G4) caused a rapid decline in WBC counts following drug administration, further supporting the time‑dependent immunomodulatory effect of florfenicol.

Protective efficacy in pigs

To evaluate the impact of florfenicol administration timing on vaccine efficacy, pigs were challenged at 7 dpi with a mixed suspension of highly virulent heterologous APP serotypes 1 (S4074) and 5 (JN). Plate counts confirmed that each pig received 6 mL of inoculum (3 mL/nostril), containing approximately 1 × 10¹⁰ CFU of S4074 and 3 × 10¹¹ CFU of JN, sufficient to cause lethal infection.

The survival curves are shown in Fig. 4, and the 7-day survival rates are summarized in Table 6. In G6 (blank control), three pigs died within the first day post-challenge, and the remaining two died on day 2, resulting in 100% mortality and confirming the lethality of the challenge dose. Among the florfenicol-treated groups, G1 (0 dpi/florfenicol), G2 (1 dpi/florfenicol), and G3 (3 dpi/florfenicol) exhibited 7-day survival rates of 20%, 40%, and 40%, respectively, with all three groups experiencing 60% mortality within the first 2 days after challenge. G4 (5 dpi/florfenicol) showed a 7-day survival rate of 60%, with 40% mortality within 2 days. G5 (vaccine-only) experienced the highest level of protection, with no deaths during the first 2 days and a 7-day survival rate of 80%. Fisher’s exact test revealed that the survival rate of G5 was significantly higher than that of G6 (P = 0.008) and G1 (P = 0.048), while G4 showed a significantly higher survival rate than G6 (P = 0.048). No significant differences were observed among G1, G2, G3, and G6 (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). Collectively, these results show that earlier florfenicol administration was associated with lower vaccine-induced protection.

Histopathological analysis

All pigs were necropsied at 7 days post-challenge. Using the CLP® lung scoring system, the lesion data were consistent with survival, clinical, and immunological findings, further supporting the importance of florfenicol timing in determining vaccine protective efficacy. The blank control group (G6) exhibited the most severe lung lesions, with extensive hemorrhagic, necrotizing, and fibrinous pneumonia. The average lung lesion score was 29.16 ± 1.98, approaching the maximum score, indicating severe pulmonary damage caused by infection with the virulent APP strain. In contrast, G5 (vaccine-only) had a markedly lower lung lesion score of 13.82 ± 6.68. Most pigs in this group showed only focal pneumonia or mild fibrinous exudation, and both the extent and severity of lung damage were clearly reduced compared with the other groups. Discussion

At the early stage of an APP outbreak on a pig farm, the recommended response is to use antibiotics immediately to reduce morbidity and mortality within the infected pigs, while also carrying out emergency vaccination of the naïve pigs with an APP LAV [6, 7]. However, antibiotic use is often unavoidable, and it remains unclear whether this compromises the protective efficacy of emergency vaccination with the APP LAV. In this study, we systematically evaluated the impact of administering a therapeutic dose of florfenicol at various time points post‑vaccination on the vaccine’s protective efficacy. Our results clearly showed that the timing of florfenicol administration is a critical determinant of vaccine efficacy. When florfenicol was given during the early period after vaccination (0–3 dpi), it markedly interfered with the development of the immune response, resulting in reduced antibody production, weaker cellular immunity, and substantially lower protection against challenge. In contrast, delaying administration until 5 days post‑vaccination largely reduced this inhibitory effect and allowed the vaccine to maintain a relatively high level of protection. These findings have important practical importance for the rational combined use of antibiotics and LAVs in swine production.

The effect of florfenicol administration timing on vaccine-induced specific antibody production

The humoral immune response, especially the production of specific antibodies, plays a critical role in host defense against APP infection [2, 4]. In this study, all pigs were seronegative for ApxII/III antibodies before immunization, with baseline PP values ranging from 5.75% to 10.51%. By 7 dpi, both the PP value and seropositive rate in G5 (vaccine‑only) were significantly higher than those in all florfenicol‑injected groups. The strongest inhibition was observed in G1 (0 dpi/florfenicol), in which florfenicol was administered at the time of immunization. In this group, the seropositive rate remained 0%, although the mean PP value increased slightly from baseline to 17.27% ± 4.95%. As the timing of florfenicol administration was delayed, both PP values and seropositive rates gradually increased. In G3 and G4, where florfenicol was given at 3 or 5 dpi, the humoral response was partially restored, although it still remained significantly lower than that in G5.

These results suggest that florfenicol suppresses the vaccine‑induced humoral immune response in a time‑dependent manner. The immunogenicity of the APP LAV relies on the transient colonization and limited proliferation of the vaccine strain in vivo, which are important for antigen presentation and the subsequent induction of adaptive immunity [26, 27]. Florfenicol inhibits bacterial protein synthesis and therefore restricts bacterial growth. The first 3 days after immunization represent a critical window for vaccine strain colonization, proliferation, and initial antigen presentation. When florfenicol is administered during this period, it is likely to reduce survival of the vaccine strain and limit antigen availability, which may then impair B‑cell activation, proliferation, and plasma cell differentiation. This likely explains the weaker antibody response observed in the early‑treatment groups and is consistent with previous reports [20].

When florfenicol administration was delayed, the vaccine strain had more time to establish early colonization and initiate antigen presentation before exposure to the antibiotic. As a result, the inhibitory effect on humoral immunity was less pronounced, and antibody production was partially preserved. Taken together, these results support the view that the effect of florfenicol on the humoral response to the APP gene‑deleted LAV is closely related to its interference with early vaccine strain colonization, proliferation, and antigen presentation in vivo.

The effect of florfenicol administration timing on vaccine-induced Th1/Th2 immune responses

The balance between Th1‑ and Th2‑type immune responses is important for maintaining immune homeostasis and generating effective protective immunity. IFN‑γ is a key Th1‑associated cytokine that promotes macrophage activation and enhances cellular immune responses, which are important for clearance of intracellular APP [28]. In contrast, IL‑4 is a representative Th2‑associated cytokine that supports B cell activation, proliferation, and antibody class switching, and therefore plays an important role in humoral immune responses [29, 30]. In the present study, the cytokine data (presented as fold change relative to baseline) complemented the antibody results and provided further insight into how florfenicol affected vaccine‑induced immune responses.

The fold change analysis of IFN‑γ (Th1 type) and IL‑4 (Th2 type) revealed that both cytokines were substantially upregulated in G5 (vaccine‑only; IFN‑γ: 2.21‑fold; IL‑4: 1.48‑fold) and G4 (5 dpi/florfenicol; IFN‑γ: 1.79‑fold; IL‑4: 1.45‑fold), whereas only limited changes were observed in the groups treated within the first 3 days after immunization (G1-G3; IFN‑γ: 1.02–1.14‑fold; IL‑4: 1.07–1.15‑fold). These findings indicate that the timing of florfenicol administration directly influences the establishment of both Th1‑ and Th2‑type immune responses after vaccination. Protective immunity induced by the APP LAV appears to depend on coordinated cellular and humoral responses: IFN‑γ is associated with activation of macrophages and cellular immunity, while IL‑4 promotes antibody production through its effects on B cells [31]. Early florfenicol treatment therefore seems to suppress not only Th2‑type humoral immunity but also Th1‑type cellular responses. Among the treated groups, G3 (3 dpi/florfenicol) showed the smallest increase in IFN‑γ (1.02‑fold), suggesting that 3 dpi may be particularly important for Th1 response development. By contrast, when florfenicol was administered at 5 dpi, the vaccine strain had already completed the main stages of colonization, proliferation, and antigen presentation in vivo, allowing T‑cell polarization to proceed with less interference. This may explain why immune responses in G4 were better preserved, which is broadly consistent with previous reports [32]. The lack of cytokine elevation in G6 (blank control; IFN‑γ: 0.93‑fold; IL‑4: 0.92‑fold) further supports that the cytokine changes observed in all vaccinated groups were related to vaccination and florfenicol treatment.

IL‑6 is commonly regarded as an important marker of innate immune activation and inflammatory responses, and its expression can reflect the inflammatory changes that occur after LAV immunization [33]. Although reduced inflammation can help limit post‑vaccination reactions, a certain degree of inflammation is necessary for effective activation of innate immunity and the subsequent development of adaptive responses. Excessive suppression of this process may impair antigen‑presenting cell function or disrupt the microenvironment required for lymphocyte activation, thereby indirectly weakening vaccine‑induced immunity. This interpretation is in line with previous studies indicating that IL‑6‑associated moderate inflammation contributes to immune responses induced by LAVs [34] and adjuvants [35]. In the present study, the fold change in IL‑6 at 7 dpi was greater in G5 (vaccine‑only; 2.02‑fold) than in all florfenicol‑treated groups (G1–G4: 1.50–1.65‑fold), while no significant differences were observed among the four florfenicol‑treated groups themselves, indicating that florfenicol reduced vaccine‑induced inflammatory responses in a manner not strongly dependent on the timing of administration. We acknowledge as a limitation that cytokines were only measured at 0 and 7 dpi; the full kinetic profiles during the early phase (e.g., 3 and 5 dpi) were therefore not captured. Nevertheless, the time‑dependent pattern of florfenicol interference with vaccine‑induced immunity is consistently supported by other complementary datasets, including leucocyte counts (3 and 5 dpi), nasal bacterial loads (3 and 5 dpi), and antibody and challenge protection data (7 dpi). Collectively, these multiple lines of evidence robustly support our conclusion. Future studies with more frequent sampling time points would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the early cytokine dynamics following vaccination.

The effect of florfenicol administration timing on vaccine-induced immune protection capacity

Challenge protection is the most direct way to evaluate vaccine efficacy. In this study, pigs were challenged at day 7 post‑immunization with a lethal dose of a heterologous virulent mixture of APP serotypes 1 and 5, which was approximately 10‑fold higher than the advised challenge dose. All pigs in G6 (blank control) died within 48 h after challenge, resulting in 100% mortality, confirming that the challenge model was sufficiently severe for evaluating protective efficacy.

The survival rates and clinical symptom scores demonstrated that earlier florfenicol administration was associated with lower vaccine‑induced protection. G1 (0 dpi/florfenicol) exhibited a survival rate of only 20% after challenge, and its clinical scores were not significantly different from those of the blank control group. This suggests that florfenicol given on the day of vaccination almost completely prevented the establishment of effective protective immunity. As florfenicol administration was delayed, protection gradually improved. Survival rates in G2 and G3 increased to 40%, while G4 achieved a 60% survival rate. The best protection was observed in G5 (vaccine‑only), which showed an 80% survival rate, the mildest clinical symptoms, and the lowest lung lesion scores. These findings were consistent with the antibody and cytokine data. Taken together, the results suggest that early florfenicol administration reduced vaccine efficacy through several related effects, which aligns with conclusions from prior relevant studies [20, 36]. First, it suppressed colonization and proliferation of the vaccine strain in the nasal cavity, which reduced antigen availability. Second, it interfered with the development of balanced Th1/Th2 immune responses, leading to weaker vaccine‑induced immunity. Third, it reduced the inflammatory responses associated with vaccination, which may have limited the immune microenvironment needed for efficient activation of adaptive responses. In contrast, when florfenicol administration was delayed until 5 dpi, the vaccine strain had likely already completed the key early stages of colonization and antigen presentation, and the host had initiated an adaptive immune response. Under these conditions, the inhibitory effect of florfenicol was less pronounced, and protection was better preserved.

It should also be noted that the challenge protocol used in this study differs markedly from the low‑dose, single‑serotype, delayed‑challenge model typically used for vaccine efficacy evaluation. The present design, involving a mixed‑strain, high‑dose challenge at 7 dpi, more closely reflects field conditions during acute APP outbreaks. Under these circumstances, concurrent antibiotic treatment and emergency vaccination are often difficult to avoid. Our findings extend our prior research [7], in which the HB04M vaccine provided nearly complete protection against single‑serotype challenge at 7 dpi. The introduction of florfenicol treatment as an additional variable, combined with a more stringent mixed‑serotype challenge, allowed us to assess vaccine efficacy under more complex and clinically relevant conditions. The observed reduction in protection, even in vaccinated animals, highlights the extent to which florfenicol treatment can affect vaccine efficacy when administered during the early post‑vaccination period.

Optimization of clinical combined application strategy for APP gene-deleted LAV and florfenicol

This study provides critical theoretical and practical guidance for situations in which APP outbreaks create an urgent need for both antibiotic treatment and emergency vaccination. In the field, florfenicol may be required to reduce morbidity and mortality in affected herds, while emergency vaccination is used to protect uninfected pigs and reduce the risk of recurrent outbreaks. Under these conditions, the timing of antibiotic administration in relation to vaccination becomes particularly important.

Our results indicate that the first 3 days after vaccination represent a critical period for the initiation of vaccine‑induced immunity. Administration of florfenicol during this period can markedly reduce protective efficacy by inhibiting replication and proliferation of the live vaccine strain in vivo. Consequently, antibiotic treatment during the first 3 days after vaccination should be avoided whenever possible in production settings. When the clinical status of the pigs permits a short delay in antibiotic intervention, postponing florfenicol administration until at least 5 days post‑vaccination appears to be the most appropriate strategy, as it better preserves vaccine immunogenicity while still allowing effective antimicrobial treatment.

At the same time, early antibiotic treatment may still be unavoidable under some outbreak conditions. In such cases, emergency treatment remains necessary, but its potential effect on vaccine‑induced immunity should be taken into account. In our previous research, when a therapeutic dose of florfenicol was administered on the day of vaccination, the seropositive rate at 21 dpi reached 83.33%, with a mean PP value of 59.21% ± 17.27%. Although this response was significantly lower than that in the vaccine‑only group (100% seropositive; mean PP value: 100.49% ± 16.10%), it suggests that some degree of immune response can still develop over time. It should also be noted that the extent of immune suppression may depend on the dosage and formulation, particularly whether a long‑acting formulation is used, since the duration of effective blood concentration is likely to influence the level of interference with vaccine‑induced immunity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates for the first time that the interference of florfenicol on the protective efficacy of an APP gene‑deleted live vaccine is critically time‑dependent. To preserve vaccine immunogenicity during an outbreak, florfenicol treatment should ideally be delayed until at least 5 days post‑vaccination. When earlier antibiotic treatment cannot be avoided, booster immunization may be needed to maintain adequate protection. These findings provide a crucial scientific basis for optimizing integrated APP control strategies in the field.

Although this study clarified the pattern of florfenicol‑mediated interference with the APP LAV and identified a minimum safe administration interval, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we only measured systemic humoral responses (plasma cytokines and antibodies), without assessing local mucosal immunity. Given that the nasal cavity serves as both the primary entry site for APP and the main site of vaccine strain colonization, the impact of florfenicol on mucosal immune responses remains unknown. Future studies should therefore examine mucosal parameters such as secretory IgA in nasal lavage fluid and immune cell populations in the nasal mucosa or lamina propria, to better define how florfenicol affects local vaccine‑induced immunity. Second, this study only evaluated a single administration of florfenicol and did not investigate repeated dosing, which is more common under field conditions. Because clinical antibiotic treatment often involves multiple consecutive doses, its longer‑term effects on vaccine immunogenicity may differ from those observed here. Further studies using repeated‑treatment regimens are needed to better guide clinical practice. In addition, this study did not investigate florfenicol administration before vaccination, and therefore did not define a rational interval between medication and immunization in that setting. This question is also important for optimizing APP prevention and control programs.
