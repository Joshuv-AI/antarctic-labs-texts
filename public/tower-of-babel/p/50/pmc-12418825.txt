Network analysis of NRG1 variants of uncertain significance (VUSes) in advanced non-small-cell lung cancer and their prognostic role in EGFR-mutant patients treated with first-line osimertinib
Authors: E Vita, A Scala, A Vitale, L Mastrantoni, J Evangelista, F D’Auria, A Stefani, F Monaca, J Russo, G Horn, P Troisi, A Cosmai, S Polidori, M Di Salvatore, E De Paolis, A Minucci, C Nero, R Trisolini, A Cancellieri, G Scambia, G Tortora, E Bria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFusion events involving neuregulin 1 (NRG1), a member of the pan-HER pathway, have been highlighted as a potential therapeutic target; however, little is known about the epidemiological distribution and the prognostic value of NRG1 variants in large real-world datasets.Materials and methodsData from 878 patients with newly diagnosed advanced non-small-cell lung cancer who had undergone DNA- and RNA-based comprehensive genomic profiling from January 2022 to October 2024 were retrospectively collected in the context of the FPG500 program (interventional monocentric prospective study, NCT06020625).ResultsComprehensive genomic profiling analysis reported NRG1 variants of uncertain significance (VUSes) in 70 samples (8.0%), including 41 deletions (58.6%), 21 sequence variants (33.0%), 2 double genetic hits (2.8%) and 2 amplifications (2.8%). Forty NRG1-mutant patients (57.1%) presented one main actionable genomic alteration, particularly EGFR, HER2 and KRAS mutation. NRG1 VUSes occurred along with genes regulating the RTK/KRAS pathway in 59 samples (84%), the TP53 pathway in 47 samples (67%) and the PI3K pathway in 27 samples (39%). In multivariate analysis carried out in a retrospective cohort of EGFR M+ (del19/L858R) patients treated with osimertinib, EGFRM+/NRG1wt patients showed significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with EGFRM+/NRG1 M+ patients {PFS 26.07 versus 12.07 months [hazard ratio (HR) 0.34, 95% confidence interval 0.12-0.95, P = 0.0015]; OS 34.06 versus 20.45 months (HR 0.45, 95% confidence interval 0.22-0.93, P = 0.0230)}.ConclusionsAlthough retrospective, these data support the hypothesis that NRG1 VUSes are frequently associated with poor prognosis and other oncogene alterations. In EGFR M+ patients treated with osimertinib, concurrent NRG1 alterations were an independent poor prognostic factor for both PFS and OS, raising the need of further validation.Key words: NRG1, NSCLC, EGFR, comprehensive genomic profiling

Introduction

Neuregulin 1 (NRG1) is the prominent member of the four proteins constituting the neuregulin family, encoded by the NRG1 gene located on chromosome 8 (8p12 region) in humans.1,2 In normal physiology, NRG1 proteins are structurally related to epidermal growth factor (EGF) and work mainly as a cell–cell interaction mediator in healthy neuronal and non-neuronal cells, encompassing cardiac cells, skeletal muscles and epithelium.3, 4, 5 There are several isoforms of NRG1, generated by activation of specific promoters or alternative exon splicing events, distinguished according to the NH2-terminal portion: type I-NRG1 (neu differentiation factor, heregulin, acetylcholine receptor inducing activity), type II-NRG1 (glial growth factor) and type III-NRG1 (motor neuron-derived factor).6 Irrespective of isoform, NRG1 exerts its biological functions by binding to the extracellular domain of HER3 and HER4 via an EGF-like domain, promoting human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)/HER3 heterodimerization and leading to activation of PI3K–AKT, MAPK and other signaling pathways that translate into cell growth, survival and proliferation.7,8 Recently, specific fusions involving the NRG1 gene (e.g. CD74–NRG1 fusion) have been focused as potential oncogenic drivers in lung adenocarcinomas and other solid tumors, because they generate chimeric type III NRG1 proteins with membrane-tethered EGF-like domains that promote hyperactivation of HER-mediated signaling. Globally, patients with NRG1 fusion non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) seem to have negligible response to standard treatments and very poor prognosis.9, 10, 11, 12 Based on preclinical evidence, several phase I/II trials are currently investigating different targeted strategies for tumors harboring NRG1 fusions, including pan-HER kinase inhibitors, anti-HER3 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), anti-HER2/HER3 bispecific mAbs (e.g. zenocutuzumab) and anti-HER2 antibody–drug conjugates.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Although NRG1 fusions represent a promising field for target therapy in NSCLC, these events remain extremely rare, involving <0.5% of cases.10,12 The spread of next-generation sequencing for molecular characterization of newly diagnosed lung cancers has permitted identification of the more frequent presence of other genomic alterations involving the NRG1 gene (single nucleotide polymorphisms, deletions, amplifications), the biological significance of which is still unknown (tier III). These variants of uncertain significance (VUSes) could play a significant prognostic role for treatments targeting HER family receptors and the MAPK/ERK downstreaming pathway.

To understand the epidemiological landscape of the NRG1 gene VUSes and their potential prognostic implications, we carried out a retrospective analysis in a sizable cohort of advanced NSCLC patients consecutively tested by comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP).

Materials and methods

Study design

We carried out a retrospective analysis of data prospectively collected from patients enrolled in the lung cancer cohort of the FPG500 program (NCT06020625) at Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli IRCCS in Rome, Italy, between January 2022 and October 2024. We considered eligible all patients with histologically confirmed diagnosis of stage IV NSCLC [eighth edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM (tumor–node–metastasis)],18 who received CGP. The study received approval from the institutional Ethics Committee (Comitato Etico Fondazione Policlinico A Gemelli; no. 3837) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study. CGP was carried out in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples using TruSight Oncology 500 High Throughput (TSO500HT, Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA). Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in tumor cells was determined by the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay (Agilent Technologies, Inc, Santa Clara, CA).

Clinical and molecular data were collected using an electronic case report form on the web application RedCap. Clinical and demographic data included sex, age at diagnosis, smoking habits, tumor pathological features and number of metastatic sites. Treatment history, including the date of diagnosis, treatments received, dates of progression and survival status were documented.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined through the consecutive enrolment of patients. Since the study was structured as a descriptive analysis, no formal power calculations were conducted. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize the characteristics of the study population: categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact tests and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. Progression-free survival (PFS) was assessed from treatment initiation until radiologic progression (by RECIST v1.1) or death for any cause. Overall survival (OS) was assessed from treatment initiation through death for any cause. Survival analyses were carried out according to the Kaplan–Meier method, with surviving patients censored at the date of the last follow-up (16 March 2025). To identify the prognostic value of NRG1 VUSes in key subgroups, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to estimate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CIs) [covariate: sex, age ≥65 years, EGFR mutation (del19 versus L858R), brain metastases, liver metastases, NRG1 and TP53 co-mutation]. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL), MedCalc version 14.8 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Boston, MA) or R version 4.4.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Genomic data analysis

Details on the genomic methodologies used in this study have been previously published.19,20 In brief, tissue samples obtained through surgery, core needle biopsy and fine needle aspiration were analyzed to assess tumor cell (TC) fraction, requiring a minimum TC content of 20% for DNA and RNA extraction. Samples with adequate DNA (≥40 ng/μl) and RNA (≥90 ng/μl) quantities underwent CGP using TSO500HT. Raw sequencing data underwent quality control and bioinformatics analysis using Illumina Software TSO500 v2.2 Local App and the Clinical Genomics Workspace software platform from Velsera (Boston, MA) for variant interpretation and reporting. Sequencing was carried out with a mean depth >500×, with a minimum coverage of 100× for 90% of regions and 250× for hotspots. Data were filtered for non-synonymous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) with a variant allelic frequency ≥5%. Variants with a known allele frequency ≥1% were classified as known polymorphisms, excluding those denoted as clinically relevant, used in diagnostic assays, or reported as a mutation in the literature. Nomenclature from the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS)21 and a scoring system approved by the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and College of American Pathologists (CAP)22 were used to classify genomic findings. Copy number variation files were discretized into gains (≥3 copies) and losses (≤1 copy). Fusion with at least 15 supporting reads were considered. Molecular biologists reviewed final reports to ensure quality control. Genomic data were analyzed using the maftools package.23 Pathways were reported according to Sanchez-Vega et al.24 The network analysis was conducted using the igraph package, using the Fruchterman–Reingold algorithm.25 The biological network analysis was conducted using the STRING website (https://string-db.org/, accessed 10 April 2025).

Results

Clinical features and molecular landscape

At data cut-off (31 October 2024), 878 metastatic NSCLC patients were retrospectively included. Overall, the presence of any genomic alteration in the NRG1 gene was found in 70 samples (8.0%). Clinical and pathological features of the NRG1-mutant population are listed in Table 1. Among NRG1-mutant patients, males and females were equally represented (50%), the median age was 69 years, the most prevalent histology was adenocarcinoma (88.7%) and there was a prevalence of patients with a smoking history (67.1%). The presence of NRG1 alterations was associated with a high disease burden, as well as 22 patients (31.4%) having more than 3 metastatic sites (median number of metastatic sites = 2.1).

Among 70 NRG1-mutant patients, we found 68 NRG1 VUSes, including 45 deletions (64.2%), 23 sequence variants (SNVs) (32.7%) and 2 amplifications (2.9%). Anecdotally, two patients (2.9%) harbored a fusion event (both SLC3A2–NRG1 fusion). Four patients (5.8%) showed an NRG1 double genetic hit (one deletion + one sequence variant) (Figure 1A). Considering SNVs, 16 (16/27) were identified as missense substitutions, modifying the amino acid sequence in the neuregulin domain (Figure 1B). The list of NRG1 SNVs identified in the study and their aggregate bioinformatics functional in in silico prediction of pathogenicity are presented in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556. Notably, 40 NRG1-mutant patients (57.1%) presented 1 main actionable genomic alteration (AGA), including 19 EGFR (6 patients with exon 19 deletion and 10 patients with p.L858R mutation; 3 patients with exon 20 insertions), 2 HER2 activating mutation, 12 KRAS mutations (including 7 KRAS G12C) and 6 oncogenic fusions (2 ROS-1, 2 RET, 2 ALK) and 1 MET exon 14 skipping (Figure 1A and Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556). Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was equally distributed between high (>10 mut/Mb) and low (<10 mut/Mb) and PD-L1 was expressed (tumor proportion score ≥ 1%) in 53 tumor samples (75.8%).

In our cohort, NRG1 aberrations occurred along with crucial mutations in genes regulating the RTK/KRAS pathway in 59 samples (84%), the TP53 pathway in 47 samples (67%), the PI3K pathway in 27 samples (39%) and cell cycle regulatory genes in 27 samples (39%) (Figure 1C and D).

In the co-occurrence map of NRG1 VUSes, we observed two main co-occurrence subnetworks: one with EGFR–TP53–PIK3CA and another with KRAS–STK11 (Figure 1E). In the interaction network of the 25 most frequently mutated genes in our cohort, NRG1 showed connection with central hubs such as ERBB2 and EGFR, thereby supporting its hypothesized role as a modulator of RTK signaling pathways (Figure 1F).

Survival analysis in the overall population

Based on clinical evaluation, only 49 patients harboring NRG1 VUSes (70.0%) were eligible for at least first-line treatment due to high disease burden and poor clinical conditions. Globally, 21 patients received first-line target therapy, while 24 patients received immunotherapy-based regimens (8 single-agent immunotherapy, 16 chemoimmunotherapy) or chemotherapy alone (4 patients). Despite being composed of a heterogeneous population, including patients with oncogene-addicted disease, the median first-line PFS was 11.99 months (95% CI 6.35-13.63 months) and the 12-month PFS was 34.6%. The median OS was 20.26 months (95% CI 12.87-29.59 months) and the 18-month OS was 46.9% (Supplementary Table S3 and Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556, and Figure 1G). Explorative subgroup analysis for key molecular variation (PD-L1 expression, NRG1 VUS type, PD-L1 expression, TMB and TP53 status) did not show statistical significance (Supplementary Table S4 and Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556).

Prognostic role of NRG1 co-mutation in EGFR-mutated NSCLC

As NRG1 is biologically involved in ERBB signaling and we have found a significant incidence of NRG1 and EGFR co-mutations, we carried out an explorative univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis about the prognostic role of NRG1 VUSes in our cohort of patients with ‘common’ EGFR mutations (EGFR M+) who received first-line treatment with osimertinib (80 mg per day, orally).

Among the 878 patients who received CGP, we found 107 patients harboring an EGFR mutation (12.2%), including 92 EGFR M+ patients [53 patients with exon 19 deletions (57.6%) and 39 patients with L858R mutation in exon 21 (42.4%)]. Additionally, eight patients harbored EGFR exon20 insertion mutations (EGFR ins20) and seven patients had other uncommon EGFR activating alterations (uEGFR M+). NRG1 co-mutations (NRG1 M+) were detected in 18 samples (15 in EGFR M+ samples and 3 in EGFR ex20ins samples) and included 15 deletions and 2 sequence variants (Figure 2). Overall, TP53 co-mutations were detected in 44 samples (47.8%), including 7 patients with EGFR M+/NRG1 M+ tumors (Figure 3A). Clinical and pathological characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

After a median follow-up of 19.66 months, 43 EGFR M+ patients had experienced disease progression (46.7%) and 34 patients died (34.78%). The median PFS was 25.55 months (95% CI 16.54-29.98 months) and the median OS was 30.71 months (95% CI 24.10-40.34 months).

In subgroup univariate analysis, clinical variables such as smoking history and presence of liver metastases were confirmed to be associated with poor prognosis, while baseline brain metastasis did not show a statistically significant association with survival outcomes (Supplementary Table S3, Figures S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556). Considering molecular variables, patients harboring exon 19 deletions showed favorable prognosis compared with patients with L858R mutations for both PFS [29.98 versus 13.61 months (HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.25-0.88, P = 0.0098)] and OS [40.34 versus 24.85 months (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.17-0.73, P = 0.0035)]. EGFR M+ patients with TP53 co-mutation showed shorter PFS [29.98 versus 18.28 months (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.31-1.08, P = 0.0624)] and OS [not reached versus 24.62 months (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.271-1.12, P = 0.0900)], despite statistical significance not being reached. More interestingly, EGFRM+/NRG1-wt patients showed longer PFS and OS compared with EGFRM+/NRG1 M+ patients [PFS 26.07 versus 12.07 months (HR 0.34, 95% CI 0.12-0.95, P = 0.0015); OS 34.06 versus 20.45 months (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.22-0.93, P = 0.0230)] (Figure 3B and C; Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, the presence of liver metastases confirmed its strong correlation with worst survival outcomes (PFS: P = 0.0042; OS: P = 0.0045). Among molecular variables, EGFR type of mutation failed to reach statistical significance for both PFS (P = 0.1335) and OS (P = 0.0619), despite a trend toward association being confirmed. NRG1 mutational status was the only molecular variable to confirm significant prognostic value for PFS (P = 0.0104) and to reach borderline significant association for OS (P = 0.0719). Results were consistent in sensitivity analysis including key clinical variables and markers of genomic instability (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556).

Discussion

NRG1 fusions have recently emerged as oncogenic drivers in multiple solid tumors, including lung mucinous adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and KRASwt pancreatic cancer. Zenocutuzumab, a bispecific antibody blocking HER2–HER3 dimerization and chimeric NRG1–protein interactions with HER3, showed meaningful clinical activity in the eNRGy trial, a phase II agnostic study that enrolled 94 NSCLC patients (58% of trial population).17 Although NRG1 fusions represent a promising field for target therapy in NSCLC, these events remain extremely rare, involving <0.5% of cases, and sometimes co-occur with other known oncogenic drivers, notably KRAS and EGFR mutations.10,17 To date, a documented oncogenic role could be associated only to the fusion variants of NRG1, while no clear information is reported about other kinds of alterations such as single nucleotide polymorphisms, indels or truncating variants that are associated to a tier III level of evidence. In our retrospective study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of NRG1 VUSes, including SNVs, deletions and amplifications, the biological role of which in cancer is still unexplored. Among 70 NRG1-mutant NSCLC patients, deletions and SNVs accounted for the vast majority of oncogenic events (>90%) and frequently occurred with crucial deregulations of RTK/MAPK and PI3K signaling, disruption of TP53 or cell cycle regulatory genes. Within our co-occurrence network, NRG1 was placed at the intersection of two subnetworks: one centered on EGFR–TP53–PIK3CA and a second with KRAS as the principal hub. This organization suggests that the NRG1 modulatory role on the EGFR signaling cascade could happen at two distinct levels, potentially influencing both receptor-level interactions and downstream transduction pathways. This complex genomic network and high mutational burden can partially explain the underperforming response to standard treatments and poor survival outcomes (shown in Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105556), which are consistent with those reported in NRG1-fusion positive NSCLC.10,11 Importantly, 40 patients (57.1%) presented co-occurrence of NRG1 mutation and one main oncogene driver, including not only 12 KRAS mutations (as similarly reported in the eNRGy trial),17 but also 19 EGFR mutations (6 patients with exon 19 deletion, 10 patients with p.L858R mutation in exon 21 and 3 patients with exon 20 insertions), HER2 activating mutations (1 insertion and 1 point mutation in exon 20) and 6 fusion chimeric receptors (RET, ROS1, ALK). As NRG1 is biologically involved in ERBB downstream signaling, the concurrence of NRG1 VUSes in EGFR M+ or HER2ins patients may significantly affect the outcomes of target treatment. In our retrospective cohort, EGFR M+/NRG1 M+ patients showed significantly shorter PFS (12.07 versus 26.07 months) and OS (20.45 versus 34.06 months) compared with EGFR M+/NRG1wt patients; importantly, the unfavorable prognostic value of the concomitant NRG1 aberrations was confirmed in multivariate analysis, unlike EGFR type mutation and TP53 co-mutation.

We are aware that our study has some limitations. Firstly, the majority of NRG1 VUSes identified in the study did not show an activating effect on the protein, being benign or uncertain; only one splice site variant resulted in a putative loss of function effect on the protein. Loss of function, however, is not a known mechanism of disease related to the oncogene NRG1 and our findings lack biological validation in cell or animal models; consequently, mechanism research remains at the correlation level. Additionally, the limited sample size and the retrospective nature of our study did not allow to exclude that we are actually observing randomly distributed DNA damage within the NRG1 gene, acting as a surrogate of broader genomic instability. Nevertheless, the sensitivity analysis failed to reach statistical correlation with more general sequencing features such as TMB and TP53 mutations, strongly suggesting that NRG1 genomic alterations may independently confer a worse prognosis per se, acting as surrogate markers of genomic instability within the ERBB receptor tyrosine kinase signaling network, leading to a diminished response to EGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). In our analysis, the inclusion of NRG1 status allowed for a deeper prognostic stratification of patients beyond that provided by well-established molecular and clinical prognostic factors26,27 and is deserving for further validation in larger datasets. In the current landscape of first-line treatment of EGFR M+ patients, where no universally accepted clinical or genomic criteria guide the choice between regimens (such as TKI monotherapy, chemotherapy + TKI combinations or TKI + mAbs),27, 28, 29, 30 the identification of additional prognostic and predictive biomarkers, such as NRG1 variants, may offer a more comprehensive understanding of tumor biology. This, in turn, could support more informed and personalized treatment decisions. At the same time, NRG1 alterations may also serve as actionable biomarkers to identify patients who could benefit from intensified inhibition strategies targeting the ERBB2/ERBB3 signaling axis. This approach may help to overcome potential resistance mechanisms mediated by NRG1-driven signaling, either by enhancing first-line therapy or as a salvage strategy in the context of early disease progression.31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39

Conclusion

In conclusion, to date, our report represents the largest series of lung cancer patients harboring NRG1 VUSes, captured by CGP. Despite the fact that their functional and biological significance remains uncertain and incompletely explored, we found that NRG1 VUSes are frequently concomitant with EGFR mutations and emerge as an independent poor prognostic factor for survival outcomes in patients treated with first-line osimertinib. Considering the alternative available strategies in first-line treatment of EGFR M+ LUAD and the potential role of targeting HER2/HER3 to overcome resistance, the biological role of NRG1 variants needs further validation, paving the way for tailoring more refined treatment in ERBB family-dependent NSCLC.
