Spatial distribution and topographic gradient effects of habitat quality in the Chang-Zhu-Tan Urban Agglomeration, China
Authors: Yi Mi, Sheng Li, Zhiyuan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to analyze spatio-temporal changes in habitat quality in Chang-Zhu-Tan Urban Agglomeration during the 2000–2020 period and explore its topographic gradient effects. Using land use data from this period, the InVEST model was employed to assess the spatio-temporal variations in habitat quality. The bivariate spatial autocorrelation model was used to analyze the spatial correlation characteristics between habitat quality and various topographical factors. Additionally, terrain factor analysis was utilized to study the terrain gradient effects on habitat quality in the study area. The results reveal that: (1) The primary land use changes in the study area from 2000 to 2020 predominantly involve substantial arable land and forest conversions into urban development. (2) The average habitat quality indices for 2000, 2010, and 2020 in the Chang-Zhu-Tan Urban Agglomeration stand at 0.651, 0.622, and 0.606, respectively, indicating a consistent declining trend in habitat quality. The distribution of habitat quality grades demonstrates a spatial pattern of “lower in the central surroundings, higher in the surroundings.” (3) The Chang-Zhu-Tan Urban Agglomeration shows significant positive correlations between habitat quality and topographical gradients. Spatial aggregation tendencies between habitat quality and topographical gradients primarily exhibit “high-high” and “low-low” clustering. (4) The habitat quality of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibits a significant topographic gradient effect, primarily characterized by an increase in habitat quality with the rise of the topographic gradient. The study outcomes contribute to unveiling the spatiotemporal variations in habitat quality within the Chang-Zhu-Tan Urban Agglomeration. Moreover, leveraging different habitat types’ distinctive terrain distribution characteristics, it proposes targeted habitat conservation measures, thereby offering theoretical support for biodiversity conservation and territorial spatial planning in the study area.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Conservation biology, Ecosystem ecology, Ecosystem services

Introduction

Habitat quality(HQ) is a pivotal evaluation factor for the integrity of ecosystem functionality1, representing the capacity of the environment to provide suitable conditions for species’ survival and reproduction2,3. A stable HQ provides a vital foundation for maintaining biodiversity4 and, to a certain extent, offers insights into the regional biodiversity5,6. The rapid social and economic development and the increased human alteration of the natural world have led to functional and structural changes in the regional ecosystem7,8. These changes have accelerated the rate of species extinction9, leading to considerable biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation10, thus posing significant risks to human survival and sustainable development11,12. As a critical component of global change, land use alteration is a crucial indicator threatening regional HQ and biodiversity13–15. Changes in land use directly or indirectly alter the spatial and temporal distribution of resources and biological habitats16,17, consequently affecting the productivity and service capabilities in these habitats, leading to a decline in biodiversity18,19. As a fundamental indicator of the geographical environment, topography significantly influences material migration and energy transformation20,21. Research indicates that variations in topography influence land use patterns22,23, resulting in spatial changes in HQ and subsequently affecting biodiversity 24,25. Understanding the relationship between topographic gradients and HQ changes26,27, mitigating the impacts of these changes on HQ, improving regional ecological security, and enhancing human well-being have become central concerns for policymakers and researchers alike28,29.

Early evaluations of HQ, originated from studies on economic development and human societal activities. Those studies relied on field investigations or constructing indicator systems to research the impact of human behavior on HQ30,31. While this method yields relatively high accuracy, it is time-consuming and labor-intensive, suitable only for small-scale surveys such as sampling points or bands, making it challenging to implement at regional scales and over longer periods 32,33. At medium to large scales, ecological models such as the multiscale integrated earth systems (MIMES) model34, the social values for ecosystem services (SolVES) model35, and, notably, the integrated valuation of ecosystem services and trade-offs (InVEST) model36 based on land use have been widely used due to their advantages in accessibility and simplicity37. In recent years, scholars have utilized the InVEST model for HQ assessments at different scales, including regions38, provinces39, and cities40, providing crucial references for regional ecology and conservation planning. However, most of the related research has focused on the relationship between HQ and urbanization41, land use42, or landscape patterns43, with limited exploration of how HQ changes with topographical gradients. As a crucial element of physical geography, topography is intrinsically related to habitat quality. It crucially influences the spatial distribution of ecosystem services44,45. Despite existing studies about the topographical gradient effects on HQ, they remain confined to specific analyses of individual topographic factors, unable to encapsulate the comprehensive process of these factors combined. Furthermore, in regions with complex terrain, the influence of topography on biodiversity becomes notably evident. Conducting HQ assessments from a topographical perspective contributes to understanding the spatiotemporal mechanisms of HQ differentiation and facilitates effective ecosystem management. This study aims to identify the relationships between topographical gradient changes and HQ changes and propose land use optimization strategies to enhance the ecological environment. These efforts are intended to alleviate the impacts of land use changes on HQ within urban agglomerations.

The Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration is situated in the midstream of the Yangtze River region, featuring a diverse and complex terrain that includes basins, hills, and mountains with considerable variations in elevation. Both natural and human-induced factors have led to significant ecological challenges in this region, including soil erosion, biodiversity loss, and the reduction of wetland areas, all of which exert substantial pressure on the HQ. As a crucial constituent of the middle reaches of the Yangtze River urban agglomeration, it serves as the strategic core area for national economic development and the principal developmental area for new urbanization. However, rapid economic growth may lead to adverse effects on maintaining biodiversity. Ensuring stable HQ balancing economic development and ecological conservation in the study area is a pivotal challenge for the high-quality development of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration. In light of these concerns, this study aims to achieve the following objectives: (1) Assess the impact of spatiotemporal land use changes on HQ from 2000 to 2020 using the habitat assessment module of the InVEST model; (2) Utilize a bivariate spatial autocorrelation model to analyze the spatial correlation characteristics between HQ and various topographic factors, including elevation, slope, topographic relief amplitude (TRA), and topographic niche index (TNI); (3) Employ GIS spatial analysis methods to explore the topographic gradient effects on HQ within the study area; and (4) Provide recommendations for optimizing regional land use and enhancing the ecological environment. The results of this study aim to offer scientific evidence to support regional biodiversity conservation and spatial planning initiatives.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration is situated in central China (Fig. 1), representing a vital component of the middle reaches of the Yangtze River urban agglomeration. Comprising three cities—Changsha, Zhuzhou, and Xiangtan—it spans approximately 28,000 km2. The topography of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration is notably diverse, characterized by various landforms. The terrain exhibits a lower elevation pattern in the central area surrounded by higher elevations, transitioning from basins, mountains, and hills, ascending gradually from the center towards the surroundings. The regional climate has a subtropical monsoon climate, featuring vegetation consisting of subtropical evergreen broad-leaved forests. The Chang-Zhu-Tan area, situated in the middle reaches of the Yangtze River urban agglomeration, faces the critical task of balancing urban development with biodiversity conservation. This is crucial for ensuring sustainable development in the region.

Data sources

Land use data for 2000, 2010, and 2020 were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Resource and Environmental Science Data Center (https://www.resdc.cn), with a resolution of 30 m. The primary land use types include six main categories (arable land, forest land, grassland, water, built-up land, and unused land) and 25 secondary categories, of which the study area involves 18. Digital elevation model (DEM) data were acquired using the ASTER Global Digital Elevation Map (v 2) from the Geospatial Data Cloud with a resolution of 30 m(http://www.gscloud.cn/). The data on administrative regions, main roads, and main railways were from the National Basic Geographic Databases (https://www.ngcc.cn/ngcc/).

Bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis

A bivariate spatial analysis model was used to explore the spatial correlation characteristics between HQ and topographical gradients. Spatial autocorrelation analysis helps explore the clustering tendencies of geographical attributes across different spatial distributions, encompassing global and local spatial autocorrelation49. Utilizing global and local bivariate Moran’s I index helps reflect the overall spatial correlation between HQ and terrain gradients, capturing both local clustering and dispersion effects. Moran’s I index ranged from − 1 to 1, where values greater than 0 indicate positive correlation, less than 0 signify negative correlation, and values close to 0 imply a random distribution without spatial autocorrelation.

Results

Land use change

The land use distribution in the study area for 2000, 2010, and 2020 is illustrated in Fig. 2 and detailed in Table 4. From 2000 to 2020, forest and arable land were the predominant land use types in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration, accounting for approximately 90% of the total land area. During this period, there was a rising trend in built-up and water areas, while arable land, forest, and grassland showed a decreasing trend. Unused land depicted an initial increase followed by a decrease. From 2000 to 2020, built-up areas increased by 982.20 km2 (a 150.93% rise), while water saw an increase of 43.38 km2 (an 8.40% rise). Arable land decreased by 590.71 km2 (6.90%), forest by 423.27 km2 (2.36%), grassland by 14.74 km2 (3.33%), and unused land increased by a total of 3.14 km2 (77.14%). The primary changes in land use within the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration during this period involved the expansion of built-up areas and water along with the reduction in arable land and forest areas. The land use transition matrix (Table 5) elucidates the shift in land use, primarily involving mutual conversion between arable land and forest and their transformation into built-up areas. Over 20 years, 457.97 km2 of arable land was converted to forest, while 426.79 km2 of forest was converted to arable land. Furthermore, 558.36 km2 of arable land transitioned into built-up areas, and 466.19 km2 of forest into built-up areas. Consequently, the critical feature of land use change in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020 was the significant transformation of arable land and forest into built-up areas.

Spatio-temporal variation in HQ

The InVEST-HQ model generated spatial distribution maps for the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration across the three periods (Fig. 3). For comparative analysis, employing an equal interval classification method and considering previous scholars’ studies and the characteristics of the region35, the HQ index was categorized into five levels: low (0-0.2), relatively low (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), relatively high (0.6–0.8), and high (0.8-1). The habitat area and proportion for the three periods were assigned as shown in Table 6. The results indicate a continuous decline in the average HQ index over 2000, 2010, and 2020 with values of 0.651, 0.622, and 0.606, respectively. The distribution of HQ levels primarily comprises relatively high and relatively low levels, collectively accounting for over 67% of the area. During 2000–2020, relatively low-level habitat areas remained stable at around 31%, whereas relatively high-level habitat areas experienced notable fluctuations. Between 2000 and 2010, these areas decreased from 42.82 to 37.86%, then between 2010 and 2020 dropped from 37.86 to 36.29%. Areas of relatively low-level and relatively high-level habitats showed a gradual increase, starting from 2.32% and 9.29–4.19% and 12.85%, respectively, during 2000–2010, and continuing the upward trend to 5.83% and 12.98%, respectively, from 2010 to 2020. Moderate-level habitat areas remained relatively stable at around 14% throughout 2000–2020. Overall, the HQ in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibited a declining trend over the two-decade period, characterized by a reduction in relatively high-quality habitat areas and an increase in moderate-to-high and low- quality habitat areas.

From the spatial distribution of HQ, the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibits significant heterogeneity in the spatial distribution of different HQ levels. Relatively low-level habitats predominantly cluster around urban areas with intensive human activity, primarily at the junctions of Changsha, Xiangtan, and Zhuzhou. Over 2000–2020, as urbanization accelerated in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration, the continuous expansion of urban built-up areas replaced relatively high-level habitats surrounding them, while relatively low-level habitats expanded outward. Simultaneously, due to the construction of fundamental transportation infrastructure, serious substantial habitat threats began segmenting relatively high-level habitats, leading to fragmentation. In summary, the distribution of HQ levels in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibits a spatial pattern “lower in the central region and higher in the surrounding areas.”

Topographical gradient effects on HQ

Topography constitutes a crucial factor influencing the distribution and composition of biological communities. Changes in topography lead to different habitat conditions, thereby generating variations in biodiversity across topographical gradients. Based on Formula 8, the dominance indices for different topographic factors were calculated. The data for various habitat quality levels in the study area for the years 2000, 2010, and 2020 were then overlaid with these topographic factors. This analysis revealed the distribution of different habitat types across various topographic gradients (Fig. 5). The results indicate a significant topographical gradient effect on the spatial distribution of HQ in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020.

Topographic gradient distribution characteristics of HQ in the study area from 2000 to 2020.

The analysis of elevation gradient distribution indices revealed a trend of continuous decrease in low and relatively low-grade habitats as the gradient ascends. These habitats reach their maximum distribution dominance at gradient level I, wherein low-level habitats exhibit a notably higher distribution dominance, reaching 2.92 in 2020. Moderate-level habitats demonstrate significant distribution dominance at gradient levels I and II. In contrast, high and relatively high-level habitats display significant distribution dominance at gradient levels II, III, and IV. Over 2000–2020, high-level habitats gradually increased in distribution dominance at gradient level V, accompanied by a decrease in distribution dominance for relatively high-level habitats at this gradient, corresponding to the transformation of favorable habitats into high-level habitats over this period.

Analyzing the slope gradient distribution indices reveals substantial differences in the distribution of different habitat levels across slope gradients. Low and relatively low-level habitats exhibit significant distribution dominance at gradient level I, primarily due to this region being the main area for human activities and production within the study area, characterized by arable land and built-up areas with relatively poor habitat suitability. Habitats of moderate quality dominate significantly in gradient levels I and II. High and relatively high-level habitats demonstrate significant distribution dominance at levels II, III, IV, and V. From 2000 to 2020, changes in habitat distribution levels across slope gradients mainly show a decrease in distribution dominance for high-level habitats at levels IV and V. Simultaneously, distribution dominance for high-level habitats at these levels increased.

Analyzing the TRA gradient distribution indices reveals that low and relatively low-level habitats exhibit significant distribution dominance at levels I and II. Moderate-level habitats demonstrate considerable distribution dominance at levels I, II, and III. Relatively high-level habitats exhibit significant distribution dominance at levels III, IV, and V. High-level habitats demonstrate significant distribution dominance at levels IV and V. Over 2000–2020, changes in habitat distribution levels across TRA gradients mainly display an increase in distribution dominance for high-level habitats at level V, corresponding to the decrease in distribution dominance for relatively high-level habitats at this gradient.

From 2000 to 2020, an analysis of the TNI gradient distribution indices reveals distinct spatial variations in the distribution of different habitat levels within the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration. Notably, low and relatively low-level habitats demonstrate significant distribution dominance at gradient levels I and II, while moderate-level habitats exhibit considerable dominance at levels II and III. Furthermore, relatively high-level habitats display significant dominance at levels III and IV. High-level habitats exhibit considerable dominance at levels IV and V. Over this period, shifts in the distribution of habitat levels across TNI gradients primarily involve a progressive increase in the distribution dominance of high-level habitats at gradient level V, accompanied by a reduction in the distribution dominance of relatively high-level habitats at this gradient.

Throughout 2000–2020, the distribution of various habitat levels across topographic gradients in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration primarily manifests as sustained strong distribution dominance of low and relatively low-level habitats at gradient levels I and II. Concurrently, there is a decrease in the distribution dominance of relatively high-level habitats at gradient level V, contrasted by an increase in the distribution dominance of high-level habitats at this gradient. Overall, the distribution of HQ within the research area exhibits a significant topographic gradient effect, where relatively low-level habitats hold greater dominance at lower topographic gradients. In contrast, relatively high-level habitats demonstrate increased dominance at higher topographic gradients.

Discussion

Impact of land use changes on HQ

The influence of land use changes on HQ in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibited distinct spatial distribution characteristics closely tied to the distribution of land use types. The HQ assessment reveals a gradual decline from 2000 to 2020 within the urban agglomeration, showing significant regional disparities. While there is a substantial increase in the area of low-grade habitats, there is also an ongoing trend of transformation from relatively high to high-quality habitats. The rapid socio-economic development has led to the rapid expansion of urban areas in the Chang-Zhu-Tan metropolitan region. This has made the surrounding areas more appealing, resulting in continuous urban land expansion at the cost of agricultural and forestry lands, thereby worsening habitat degradation. Implementing ecological projects such as reforestation and land restoration has converted vast arable lands into habitats with better suitability, enhancing environmental quality in specific regions. Although habitat deterioration and improvement are both evident, the latter’s extent is smaller, ultimately indicating an overall declining trend in habitat quality. These ecological initiatives effectively mitigate the adverse impact of urban expansion on habitats, underlining the significance of reforestation and restoration programs. However, addressing habitat restoration amidst economic development remains a crucial challenge for urban areas.

Correlation analysis between topographic gradients and HQ

The distribution, structure, and functionality of habitats are closely connected to the features of the land. In the complex terrain of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration, land use practices are constrained by the topography, affecting the distribution of population and industries. The synthesis of various factors has resulted in distinct hierarchical distributions of HQ across different topographic factors. Previous studies on HQ and topographic gradients primarily employed singular topographic factors such as TNI. This study selected four topographic factors— elevation, slope, TRA, and TNI to conduct a vertical spatial analysis of habitat quality. Using a bivariate spatial autocorrelation model, the study examined the spatial correlation between habitat quality and four factors. It also delved into the effects of topographic gradient on habitat quality in the study area. Such an approach helps avoid errors arising from studying singular topographic factors. From the perspective of topographic gradients, there is a significant correlation between the topographic factors of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration and the spatial distribution of its habitats; it was observed that high-quality habitats dominate at higher topographic gradients (levels IV and V), while low-quality habitats dominate at lower gradients (levels I and II). In regions with gentle topography, high potential for development increases conflicts between anthropogenic activities and the environment, intensifying competition for land use and resulting in lower habitat quality. As the topographic gradient increases, the dominance shifts to forests and grasslands with fewer human activities, leading to abundant biodiversity and significantly enhanced HQ. Consequently, the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration exhibits marked vertical spatial differentiation in HQ. Therefore, considering the diverse resource endowments, socio-economic development positioning, and industrial structures across the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration, adopting precise and scientifically informed ecological strategies to address regional disparities and various developmental stages is crucial for sustainable socio-ecological development50.

Suggestions for sustainable HQ Development

In the context of ecological and green development, it is crucial to reconcile urban development with ecological preservation. The assessment of HQ in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration and the detailed analysis of its topographic gradient effects vividly demonstrate the characteristics of the habitat distribution. This understanding helps relevant authorities promptly manage delicate ecosystems, maintain regional stability, and ensure ecological security. Therefore, proactive measures are crucial to mitigate the adverse impact of urban development on the environment. In regions with low topographic gradients (levels I and II), such as the regions at the junction of Changsha, Xiangtan, and Zhuzhou cities, it is imperative to enhance the rational utilization of land, prevent the degradation of lakes, wetlands, and similar ecosystems, and concurrently execute reforestation in steeply sloped areas to avoid geological disasters. Attention in territorial spatial planning should focus on managing and safeguarding ecologically and landscape-sensitive areas such as rivers, lakes, wetlands, and green spaces while exploring active biodiversity conservation within urban areas, including sites like Changsha Songya Lake National Wetland Park and the ecological restoration project for abandoned open-pit mines in the Yangtze River Economic Belt in Zhuzhou. Additionally, despite the relatively low habitat quality in farmland, it is essential to promote eco-friendly agriculture to enhance the biodiversity levels of farmlands. In areas with moderate topographic gradients (levels II and III), usually near rivers, in a strip-like pattern, it is essential to preserve biodiversity stability. This can be done by enhancing the construction of ecological corridors using forest and grass belts, thereby improving the connectivity and overall protection capacity between protected areas. In areas with high topographic gradients (levels IV and V), such as the western parts of Changsha, western and southern parts of Xiangtan, and western and southeastern parts of Zhuzhou, the contribution of forest and grassland resources is more substantial. Establishing and expanding protected areas for ecosystem services emphasizing forest management, restoration, biodiversity conservation, and enhancing environmental carrying capacity is imperative. In summary, the development of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration should strictly adhere to territorial spatial planning, controlling urban expansion, achieving efficient land resource utilization, and improving HQ through natural recovery and ecological restoration measures. Simultaneously, developing scientifically informed ecological restoration plans, continuing the progress of land reclamation projects, restoring damaged habitats, and enhancing regional ecosystem services functionality are essential.

Limitations and future research suggestions

This study thoroughly described the spatiotemporal differentiation characteristics of HQ in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration, analyzed HQ distribution characteristics from a topographical perspective, and explored the spatial correlation and aggregation patterns between HQ and topographic gradients. The outcomes may serve as a reference for spatially differentiated biodiversity conservation policies. Despite that the InVEST-HQ model is relatively mature at assessing HQ at large to medium scales, the subjectivity in setting some parameters based primarily on model manuals and related studies impedes field testing and validation, requiring further enhancement through on-ground monitoring of biodiversity data35. Moreover, while this study is limited to the correlation between topographic factors and HQ, the factors causing changes in HQ are more complex, including natural elements like climate change, water system distribution, policy, and socio-economic factors22. As the focus on ecological construction continues to grow, the influence of relevant policy and socio-economic factors on habitat within the research area is expected to intensify. Future research should integrate natural, socio-economic, and policy factors, constructing a comprehensive HQ evaluation model involving multiple factors to investigate the mechanisms behind the evolution of HQ within the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration.

Conclusion

Based on the DEM and land use data from 2000 to 2020, this study utilized the InVEST-HQ model to assess the spatiotemporal distribution of land use and HQ within the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration. The study explored the spatial correlation between HQ and various topographic factors. The main research conclusions are as follows:

Forests and farmlands have dominated the land use structure in the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration over the past 20 years, accounting for approximately 90% of the study area. The spatiotemporal changes in land use are primarily caused by converting farmlands and forest lands into construction lands.

The spatiotemporal changes in land use are primarily caused by converting farmlands and forest lands into construction lands.

The average HQ index of the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration in 2000, 2010, and 2020 were 0.651, 0.622, and 0.606, respectively, showing a consistent decline in habitat quality. Considering the proportion of area covered by different HQ levels, high and relatively low HQ levels are predominant. The cumulative area of these two HQ levels exceeds 67%. The main changing trend is the decrease in high-quality habitats, leading to the increase in mid-high and relatively low-quality habitats. Spatially, the HQ distribution pattern within the Chang-Zhu-Tan urban agglomeration can be described as “low in the middle, and high in the surroundings”.

The global bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed a clear positive correlation between HQ and topographic gradients. Through local bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis, the spatial aggregation patterns between HQ and topographic gradients were primarily “high-high” and “low-low.” The “high-high” cluster was characterized by forest land use in high altitudes, and the “low-low” cluster was prevalently in low topographic areas, characterized by construction and farmland use.

Relatively low-quality habitats showed a significant advantage in lower areas, whereas relatively high-grade habitats exhibited a substantial advantage in higher topographies.
