Color Stability of Different Restorative Approaches for the Masking of Silver Diamine Fluoride after Thermal Aging
Authors: Jacob Ozuna, Christine Nguyen, Neamat Hassan Abubakr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe present study aims to evaluate the color stability of different masking modalities of silver diamine fluoride (SDF) on extracted carious teeth after thermal aging.Methods and resultsOne hundred and eight recently extracted carious permanent teeth were used. All sample teeth had SDF applied over the carious lesion. The teeth were then divided into six groups: group A (SDF, potassium iodide (KI), and packable composite resin), group B (SDF, flowable, and packable composite resin), group C (SDF, KI, resin-modified glass ionomer (RMGI), and packable composite resin), group D (SDF, KI, and RMGI), group E (SDF, hybrid glass ionomer (HGI), packable composite resin), and group F (SDF and RMGI). VITA Easyshade was used to evaluate color stability. Initial masking of the dark staining was improved in groups in curing light groups (groups D and F). Groups restored with RMGI demonstrated the greatest color changes after aging. The groups that showed the greatest color stability of the materials after 5 years of aging were groups A, B, and E. Highest color change was observed during the first clinical year for all groups.ConclusionKI is effective at reducing SDF staining. Composite (CMP) provides a long-term color-stable restoration. Thermocycling had the greatest effect on HGI restorations.Clinical relevanceOne of restorative dentistry's most important clinical parameters is the color stability of tooth-colored dental restorations. Choosing a suitable restorative material is essential to overcome SDF's staining effects. Applying KI and composite resin restoration was the most effective, long-term color-stable restorative material for masking the SDF staining.How to cite this articleOzuna J, Nguyen C, Abubakr NH. Introduction

Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic diseases in the world, affecting populations at all ages of life.1,2 From behaviorally complex children to bedridden patients, treatment for dental caries is a persistent challenge. A child may be too young or may not weigh enough to undergo general anesthesia, while an older bed-bound patient may not have access to a dental unit. Various treatment modalities have been proposed for the treatment and prevention of dental caries, such as sealants, topical application of fluoride, and silver diamine fluoride (SDF).3 SDF is a clear liquid applied topically to teeth as a cavity treatment. A recent umbrella review indicated that SDF is efficient in arresting carious lesions in the primary and permanent dentition, and the evidence supported that SDF had minimal side effects. While SDF has been shown to be an effective and relatively inexpensive treatment for dental caries, it can have an esthetic impact on the teeth due to its dark color. The efficacy of SDF in arresting coronal caries in primary teeth, as well as arresting and preventing root caries in the adult dentition, has been previously reported.4 The mechanisms of SDF include the disruption of cariogenic bacteria replication, hindrance of demineralization with fluoride ions, occlusion of exposed dentinal tubules with a silver precipitate, inhibition of collagen degradation, and therefore arresting and prevention of carious lesions.3

The ease of application and low cost of SDF make it a convenient option for application and treatment in dental health services.5 Application of SDF is an excellent treatment option for patients who are unable to be transported to a dental office, where access to care is difficult, or for hard-to-manage patients, because it is painless, fast, and relatively inexpensive.2 While the use of SDF is not as invasive as other dental treatments, a prominent drawback of SDF application is the permanent dark staining of carious enamel and dentin.1 When applied to teeth, SDF can cause staining of the tooth surface, transforming it to a black or dark brown color. This staining is the result of the silver ions reacting with the dentin and enamel of the tooth. While this discoloration may be seen as a negative esthetic impact, it can also be viewed as a positive in cases where the primary goal is to preserve the tooth structure. The discoloration acts as an indicator of caries arrest for the clinician. Previous investigations have shown that the application of SDF, in populations with a high risk for root caries, was considered to be the most effective and cost-saving treatment.6

While the esthetic impact of treatment may be secondary to the goal of preventing further decay and preserving the tooth, others consider esthetics primary. Potassium iodide (KI) has been offered as an adjunct to SDF application due to its reported ability to reduce SDF darkening.3,4 In addition to KI, there are other methods to minimize the esthetic impact of SDF. Many combinations of dental restorative materials have been offered as alternative treatments to mask SDF staining. These include the application of a composite (CMP) material layer over the SDF-treated tooth surface in an attempt to mask the staining. On the contrary, it was suggested that since the penetration of the SDF will occlude the dentinal tubule, this may lead to a lower bond efficacy of the adhesive, which, as a consequence, will decrease the composite resin restorations’ clinical success.7 It was recommended to seal the SDF by using glass ionomer-based materials, which is known as SMART (silver-modified atraumatic restorative treatment). SMART was introduced as a minimally invasive treatment of caries where the glass ionomer is applied over SDF.8 The SMART technique has several advantages: the SDF will have a double effect to inhibit the cariogenic activity9,10 and reduce hypersensitivity11; in addition, the glass ionomer will chemically bond to the tooth, release fluoride, and help in masking the SDF black discoloration. A recent research concluded that zirconia-reinforced glass ionomer was considered the best choice for masking the SDF discoloration.12

In addition to the esthetic considerations for formulating an ideal masking technique, there must be consideration for color stability. An evaluation of color stability can consist of restorative hardness, longevity, tensile strength, and tear strength throughout an aging process.5 Color stability is affected by various factors ranging from restorative material composition to the clinical technique itself.5,6 In a study aimed to analyze the additive effect of nanoparticles and color stability, it was found that the addition of nanoparticles, such as nanoparticle-reinforced silicone elastomers, at various concentrations could improve the color stability of a prosthesis, which ultimately influenced the long-term shade and color durability.5 The identification of an ideal masking technique, with respect to color stability, is critical to the prospective usage of SDF in clinical practice. This study aims to identify and quantify the color stability of a restoration after the application of SDF on extracted carious permanent teeth, the efficacy of KI in neutralizing SDF discoloration, and the potential effect of CMP and hybrid glass ionomers (HGI) on the masking of SDF discoloration.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Groups

A sample size of 108 permanent teeth was recently collected in the emergency dental clinic at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas (UNLV). The randomly collected teeth had caries to the dentin in the cervical portion of the crown and were deemed clinically unrestorable. After the teeth were collected and sterilized, the teeth were stored in artificial saliva (Pickering Laboratories, CA, USA) at room temperature. Teeth were randomly divided into six groups to receive the proposed treatment modalities as shown in Figure 1.

Experimental design

Sample Preparation

A small excavating spoon was used to remove the infected dentin and unsupported enamel. One drop of SDF (Riva Star, SDI) was applied to all teeth for 1 minute. Group A: KI was applied to the tooth directly after SDF according to the manufacturer's instructions until the white precipitate totally disappeared. The teeth were rinsed with water for 30 seconds and etched for 30 seconds, with 37% phosphoric acid (Best-Etch, Vista). They were rinsed with water, once again, and air dried. A universal self-etching adhesive (Scotchbond Universal, 3M) was applied and cured, by an LED polymerization lamp (Demi Plus, Kerr), for 10 seconds. A 4 mm layer of universal CMP restorative material, shade A2B (Filtek Supreme, 3M), was added in two increments of 2 mm each and light cured for 20 seconds between layers. Group B: The teeth were rinsed, etched, rinsed, and air dried. The first layer added was a 1 mm layer of flowable CMP restorative material, shade A1 (Natural Elegance, Henry Schein), then light cured. A 3 mm layer of universal CMP, shade A2 was added and light cured. Group C: KI was applied in the same manner as group A and rinsed. The dentin was conditioned (Cavity Conditioner, GC) for 20 seconds, rinsed, and air dried. A 1 mm increment of HGI restoration, shade A2 (EQUIA Forte, GC), was added. The teeth were etched, rinsed, and air dried. A universal self-etching adhesive was applied and cured. A 3 mm layer of universal CMP restorative material was added and light cured. Group D: KI was applied in the same manner as group A and rinsed. The dentin was conditioned, rinsed, and air dried. A 4 mm increment of HGI restoration was placed, followed by a top coat (EQUIA Forte Coat, GC), and cured for 20 seconds. Group E: The teeth were rinsed with water for 30 seconds, a 1 mm increment of resin-modified glass ionomer (RMGI) (Vitrebond Plus, 3M) was applied, and cured for 10 seconds. The teeth were etched, rinsed, and air dried. A universal self-etching adhesive was applied and cured. A 3 mm layer of universal CMP restorative material was added and light cured. Group F: The dentin was conditioned for 20 seconds, rinsed, and air dried. A 4 mm increment of HGI restoration placed, top coat applied, and cured.

Image and Shade Analysis

Photographs were taken using a digital microscope (VR-3100; Keyence). Color changes were recorded by a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade, Vita). CIELAB values L, a, and b were measured for each restoration. L-values represent the lightness of the restoration with the value of 0 = black and the value of 100 = white.5 +a-values represent the red and –a-values represent green, while the –b-values represent the blue and +b-values yellow.7 Initial recordings and photographs were made immediately after the placement of the restorations. The spectrophotometer was recalibrated according to the manufacturer between each restoration's readings, and the restoration was measured at its center. All restoration margins were subjected to thermocycling. The thermocycler water baths were set to 5°C and 55°C with 20 seconds at each temperature (Thermocycler, SD Mechatronik). Statistical Analysis

Stata software was used to calculate the sample size of 108 with a power of 90% and a confidence interval of 95%. Means and standard deviations were calculated for all values. A group ratio of 1:1 was used. The specimens were placed into identical unmarked test tubes by one investigator and were randomly assigned into the test groups by another investigator uninvolved in the experiment. SPSS 24 (SPSS, IBM) was used for statistical calculations. Analysis of the data was performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to detect changes between the groups and within the groups. In addition, Tukey's post hoc tests were used for comparing the different treatment groups at each interval. A p-value of <0.05 was set for the statistical significance for all tests.

Results

Colorimetric Changes

There was noticeable and immediate black staining on the specimens that were treated with SDF only and then subsequently light cured as seen in Figure 2. The application of KI reduced this immediate darkening that could be visualized with the unaided eye, brought on by the curing light. In addition, the initial masking of the dark staining was also improved in the groups where curing was not used, i.e., HGI only. However, after the aging process of 5 years, groups restored with HGI demonstrated the greatest color changes ΔE = 11 for group C, ΔE =14 for group D, and ΔE = 24 for group F. The groups that showed the greatest color stability of the materials after 5 years of aging were groups A, B, and E with ΔE values of 7.02, 8.01, and 6.31, respectively.

Clinical photographs pre- and post-thermal aging of all examined groups

Color Analysis

The ΔL for the test groups revealed that group F had a significant amount of darkening after the first clinical year when compared to groups A, C, and E. From the first year to the third year, the only significant difference in ΔL was between groups B and D. During the final interval, group F underwent more significant darkening when compared to groups C, D, and E. There were little differences in darkening for groups A, C, and E after the 5 clinical years.

The ΔA for the test groups revealed there were no statistical differences in color changes on the red and green scale for all the groups during the first clinical year. From year one to year three group D when compared to C had a change that was significant. During the last interval, group D had a ΔA that was significantly different from all other groups.

The ΔB for the test groups revealed color changes that were significant for group D when compared to B in color changes on the blue and yellow scale during the first clinical year. However, there were no significant changes in ΔB in subsequent clinical years between all the test groups. The ANOVA revealed there was a significant influence of shade stability after thermal aging.

Using the pairwise comparison, the greatest amount of color change was observed during the first clinical year for all groups and stabilized for most groups. However, group F continued to change color with each interval (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

Although the efficacy of SDF in arresting and preventing coronal caries has been well documented, the mechanism of action is still challenged.6 Currently, the proposed mechanism of SDF includes silver ions disrupting bacterial cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis, eventually inducing mitochondrial failure, and hence disrupting bacterial plaque biofilm.15 Fluoride ions in SDF aid in the promotion of the formation of fluorapatite, calcium fluoride, and silver phosphate, which all contribute to the remineralization of demineralized tooth structure.3 Patency of exposed dentin tubules from extensive demineralization can be reduced via silver ion precipitation and calcium fluoride.3 SDF has also been shown to have an inhibitory effect on proteolytic enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases and cathepsins, responsible for degradation of collagen during the caries process.16 Teeth treated with SDF generally result in black staining of carious tooth structure due to excess silver precipitate.3

As the interest and clinical application of SDF increase, so does the potential of KI in masking its adverse staining. In 2000, Knight et al. introduced the use of KI to remove the black staining of SDF.17 The application of saturated KI to SDF has been proposed to form silver iodide (AgI), which significantly reduces excess silver precipitate that is known to cause staining. AgI is a yellow and insoluble precipitate that could be easily washed away with water.18 Generally, from the unaided eye, groups that included sequential application of KI after application of 38% SDF (groups A, C, and D) resulted in esthetically less noticeable dark staining than application of solely SDF. A recent investigation concluded that immediate application of 20% KI had great reduction of the black staining of SDF, while saturated KI was more effective after one day of application.19 Subsequent questions arise concerning the potential interactions of KI and the efficacy of SDF. Currently, the effect of KI on the caries-arresting potential of SDF is unsettled.19 Some studies have suggested that the addition of KI to SDF not only reduces the dark staining but also further reduces patency of exposed dentin tubules, leading to inhibition of recurrent caries formation.3,4,7 Another study concluded that although staining of carious lesions was minimized, the caries-arresting potential of SDF was not affected by immediate application of saturated KI to SDF.19 On the contrary, a study done by Zhao et al. suggested that KI had a hindering effect on the SDF mechanism for preventing secondary root caries. The proposed explanation attributed KI playing a role in reducing the optimal amount of silver ions needed for the antimicrobial activity of SDF when blocking progression of caries.20

Thermal aging had a significant effect on the color stability of restorative material.21 In the present investigation, teeth restored with HGI experienced a continual darkening of the restorations. In addition, the HGI restorative material appeared to be washing out and developing surface voids. The HGI top coat began to develop a flaky appearance after the aging process. Specimens restored with CMP displayed better long-term color stability, and the restorations were fully intact. The present findings come in agreement with previous research which indicated that the color stability of SDF masked with CMP is minimally affected by suntest aging.22 The color masking ability of CMP withstood the aging process after the 5 simulated clinical years. Present results suggest that the greatest amount of color change will occur in the first year after the placement of the restoration. The use of KI in combination with any restorative material provided an esthetic masking of the dark SDF staining, as seen in other studies.23 However, if the restoration is required for a longer duration, then the use of CMP will provide a longer-lasting masking of the color.

Conclusion

Limitations of the study were the difficulty of being able to use the shade guide in the same spot at each interval. This was overcome by increasing the sample sizes for each group to give an average, which was used for the data analyses. It is recommended that further studies be performed on the use of more hydrophilic materials as an alternative to CMP. The use of CMP may not always be practical due to difficulties in isolation.
