Massive hemoptysis due to pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm in invasive aspergillosis in a patient with interstitial lung disease and suspected Aspergillioma: A case report
Authors: Niroshan Ranjan, Angela Ishak, Medha Cherabuddi, Seth Garrett, Radhika Sheth, Anish Wadhwa, Javier I. Diaz Mendoza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is a serious complication, particularly in patients with existing structural lung disease like interstitial lung disease (ILD). Angioinvasive IPA can lead to pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm (PAP), which is a rare but life-threatening source of massive hemoptysis.Case presentationWe present the case of a 78-year-old white American male with ILD (potentially from Agent Orange exposure) and chronic hypoxic respiratory failure, dependent on home oxygen, who presented with recurrent, massive hemoptysis. Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) showed a 1.5 cm PAP in the right upper lobe cavity. Despite no classical causative immunosuppression, a diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis was supported by bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) culture and galactomannan testing. Although invasive disease was suspected, the possibility of an aspergilloma with a semi-invasive component could not be excluded due to lack of prior imaging. Urgent coil embolization was performed to control the hemorrhage, followed by antifungal treatment with voriconazole.ConclusionThis case underscores the need for early suspicion of pulmonary aspergillosis in ILD patients, utilizing BAL and CTA for diagnosis, and combining endovascular embolization with antifungals. Given the absence of prior imaging and underlying fibrotic lung disease, distinguishing invasive disease from semi-invasive or chronic forms can be challenging.

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is usually associated with neutropenic populations (e.g., hematologic malignancy, chemotherapy) (1). However, non-neutropenic populations with underlying structural lung diseases, such as interstitial lung disease (ILD), can also be at risk because of impaired mucociliary clearance and local immune dysfunction (2). Changes in the incidence and prevalence of IA in previously underrecognized populations underline the importance of increased awareness and consideration of patients with IA. Studies have shown the risk of IA in ILD patients approaches up to 16.4 %, with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and ILD associated with rheumatoid arthritis being at higher risks (4).

The overall clinical presentation of ILD patients with IA usually includes progressive dyspnea (93.1 % prevalence), hypoxemia, and hemoptysis (8.6 %)(6). Hemoptysis is of particular interest, as it is often accompanied by angioinvasive disease or mycetoma formation (7). IA in ILD can cause life-threatening hemoptysis (>50 % mortality if untreated) (8), vascular invasion that leading to pseudoaneurysms (9), and possible dissemination (1). Our case emphasizes the crucial importance of high clinical suspicion for IA in non-neutropenic patients with structural lung disease who presents with progressive respiratory symptoms and hemoptysis.

Case Presentation

A 78-year-old man with a history of interstitial lung disease (ILD), believed to be related to Agent Orange exposure during military service in Vietnam, presented with hemoptysis. He required 2 L of supplemental oxygen at baseline and had comorbidities including hypertension, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (on apixaban), chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

The patient initially presented to an outside hospital with hemoptysis that was attributed respiratory infection adter it transient imporved with Amoxicillin/Clavulanate. Hemoptysis recurred 2 days later after he was discharged, prompting a readmission. Apixaban was held and a bronchoscopic alveolar lavage(BAL) performed, which revealed a blood-tinged BAL fluid, which grew Aspergillus fumigatus culture and the BAL galactomannan was positive at 11.65. Voriconazole was initiated. Computed Tomography (CT) chest (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) at the time showed a possible pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm (PAP) and a non-occlusive pulmonary embolism in the right upper lobe. A few days after discharge, the patient returned to the emergency department again at the outside facility after experiencing approximately 200 mL of bright red hemoptysis. He reported taking Apixaban earlier that morning. He was transferred to our facility's intensive care unit for a higher level of care for his hemoptysis and suspected aspergilloma. On arrival, he was alert, hemodynamically stable, and intermittently coughing, with maroon-colored blood.

Labs showed a drop in hemoglobin (from 10.0 to 8.4 g/dL), leukocytosis (WBC 16.8 K/μL) absolute neutrophil 10.40 K/μL, and absolute lymphocytes at 5.10 K/μL (baseline 0.80 K/μL). Coagulation studies after apixaban reversal with Andexanet Alfa showed INR 1.19 and PTT 34 seconds. Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of chest(Fig. 3 & 4) revealed a 1.5 cm contrast-enhancing focus in the right upper lobe cavity, concerning for PAP, appearing larger compared to prior imaging, with active extravsation. Pulmonary angiography was performed, confirming a 1.5 cm PAP arising from a segmental branch of the right upper lobe of the pulmonary artery. Coil embolization was performed using three 4 mm Ruby coils across and proximal to the lesion resulting in successful exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm.

Follow-up bronchoscopy showed no active bleeding. He was successfully extubated and weaned back to his baseline oxygen requirement. Antifungal therapy was continued with voriconazole (200 mg BID), and anidulafungin was added empirically. Anticoagulation remained held. He was transferred out of the MICU and continued antifungal treatment with a planned 12-week course as well as outpatient follow-up with infectious disease.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

This case highlights several key clinical aspects of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) in non-neutropenic patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD), with a particular emphasis on diagnostic challenges and rare but severe complications such as pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm (PAP). Traditionally considered a disease of the profoundly immunosuppressed, IPA is increasingly recognized in individuals with structural lung disease, even in the absence of neutropenia or high-dose immunosuppression [[1], [2], [3]]. However, we acknowledge the possibility that our patient's presentation may represent an aspergilloma with a semi-invasive component rather than frank invasive aspergillosis [4,5].

A striking feature of our case was the discordance between bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and serum galactomannan (GM) results (11.65 vs. 0.09). This finding is consistent with current literature demonstrating the superior sensitivity of BAL GM in non-neutropenic populations. A 2022 study of 120 IPA cases found BAL GM positivity in 83.2 % of non-neutropenic hosts, compared to 66.7 % in neutropenic patients [6]. Additionally, prior analyses have shown that BAL GM has a sensitivity of 81.3 % compared to only 25.7 % for serum GM in similar populations [7]. These findings suggest that BAL GM is a more reliable diagnostic tool in early-stage IPA when the infection is confined to the lungs and antigen release into the bloodstream is limited [4].

Another overlooked risk factor in such hosts is baseline lymphopenia. Our patient had a lymphocyte count of 800/mm3 on presentation. Recent evidence indicates that lymphopenia may independently increase the risk of invasive fungal disease, with one study showing an odds ratio of 2.745 for lymphopenic patients [2]. This observation invites a broader interpretation of immune dysfunction in structural lung disease and supports expanding IPA risk stratification beyond neutropenia alone.

Radiologically, IPA is classically associated with findings such as the halo sign (a nodule with surrounding ground-glass opacity) or the air-crescent sign (indicative of necrotic cavitation) [8]. These classic features were not observed in our patient. However, the detection of a 1.5 cm PAP within a right upper lobe cavity on CTA (Figs. 3 and 4) was a pivotal finding. PAPs are rare but highly distinctive markers of advanced angioinvasive disease. In this process, Aspergillus fumigatus invades and erodes the pulmonary artery wall, leading to vessel weakening and aneurysmal dilatation [9]. The organism's ability to produce elastase and other proteolytic enzymes enables it to degrade the elastic lamina of blood vessels [10], and this effect may be amplified in patients with fibrotic ILD, as was evident in our case (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The underlying fibrosis likely impaired local immune surveillance and conidial clearance, creating a permissive environment for fungal invasion. However, it is also plausible that this cavity was pre-existing and harbored an aspergilloma that progressed into a semi-invasive form with vascular involvement [4,5].

PAPs remain a rare complication of IPA, reported in fewer than 2 % of cases [5]. Fungal etiologies account for less than 5 % of all pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysms, particularly among patients without classical immunosuppression [11]. The presence of PAP in our patient, who had no history of transplantation, cytotoxic therapy, or neutropenia, underscores the expanding at-risk population. Our findings suggest that structural lung disease, compounded by low lymphocyte counts, may provide sufficient immune dysfunction to permit fungal angioinvasion [1,2]. That said, due to the lack of prior CT imaging, we cannot determine whether the cavity was new or pre-existing, limiting our ability to definitively distinguish invasive aspergillosis from chronic or semi-invasive forms [4].

Our patient's history of Agent Orange exposure further raises the question of environmental and occupational contributions to lung vulnerability and susceptibility to opportunistic infections. While definitive causal pathways are difficult to establish, prior environmental exposure may exacerbate immune dysfunction and structural lung injury, potentiating the risk of fungal invasion [12].

Management of PAP secondary to angioinvasive IPA is complex and requires multidisciplinary collaboration. First-line treatment for PAP-associated hemoptysis is transcatheter embolization by interventional radiology. Recent cohort studies have demonstrated a 100 % technical success rate for this approach [13]. Coil embolization, as performed in our case, effectively excludes the pseudoaneurysm from circulation while preserving adjacent pulmonary blood flow. Bronchoscopic techniques, including cautery, are not effective in this setting because the source of bleeding is the pulmonary artery, not the bronchial circulation [14]. This emphasizes the importance of accurate localization and appropriate intervention.

A case series of eight patients undergoing embolization for PAP reported complete resolution of hemoptysis in all cases [15]. However, management must also address concurrent anticoagulation, especially in patients with comorbid conditions like atrial fibrillation. In our case, hemoptysis recurred shortly after discharge—likely due to premature re-initiation of apixaban. The decision to restart anticoagulation must weigh thromboembolic risk against the potential for rebleeding, and in some cases, may warrant extended anticoagulation-free periods [16].

Antifungal therapy is a cornerstone of IPA management. Voriconazole remains the first-line agent per current guidelines [10]. Our patient was appropriately treated with voriconazole, and anidulafungin was added empirically, reflecting growing interest in combination antifungal therapy in critically ill patients or those with concern for azole resistance [1]. While most guidelines recommend a minimum of 12 weeks of antifungal therapy, the optimal duration in cases complicated by PAP remains uncertain.

Conclusion

This case advocates for multidisciplinary management and challenges traditional risk stratification in non-neutropenic hosts. It demonstrates that structural lung disease with baseline lymphopenia may predispose patients to invasive fungal disease with vascular complications, even without classical immunosuppression. However, given the radiographic findings and lack of prior imaging, we also considered the possibility of an aspergilloma with semi-invasive features leading to angioinvasion and pseudoaneurysm formation. The successful management through early diagnosis, endovascular intervention, and appropriate antifungal therapy provides valuable insights for similar complex cases.
