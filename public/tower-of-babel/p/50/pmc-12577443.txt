Investigation of region-of-interest-based functional connectivity within the default mode network among adolescents with depression complicated by obesity
Authors: Yutong Li, Xiuying Pan, Shaochen Cheng, Qiannan Yao, Jian Ji, Xinlin Huang, Hongyan Sun, Xiaobin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study utilized resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) combined with default mode network (DMN) region-of-interest (ROI)-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis to examine adolescents with depression complicated by obesity. Correlation analyses were conducted between the functional connectivity of distinct brain regions in the depression-with-obesity cohort and scores from the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) to examine potential associations. This study aimed to elucidate the underlying pathogenesis of depression complicated by obesity and identify potential imaging biomarkers for early diagnosis in this population.MethodsWe analysed rs-fMRI data from 37 adolescents with depression complicated by obesity (OMDD group, n = 37), 38 patients with depression (MDD group, n = 38), and 35 healthy controls (HCs group, n = 35). DMN FC was compared with whole-brain connectivity during rs-fMRI. Imaging data from the three groups were collected and analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Group differences in FC values were assessed, and correlations were examined between these values and clinical scale scores across patient groups.ResultsCompared with the MDD group, the OMDD group demonstrated significantly increased FC between the left parahippocampal gyrus (left PHG) and the right precuneus. Compared with the HCs, both the OMDD and MDD patients presented reduced FC between the left PHG and the left putamen, right putamen, and opercular part of the right inferior frontal gyrus. All findings were corrected using Gaussian random field (GRF) theory at a voxel-level threshold of P < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of P < 0.05 (minimum cluster size > 30 voxels). Analyses revealed significant negative correlations between the FC values of the right putamen in the OMDD group and the “interpersonal relationship” scores (r = − 0.373, P = 0.023).ConclusionCompared with healthy controls, adolescents with depression complicated by obesity demonstrated significant alterations in functional integration within the DMN. Specifically, the OMDD group exhibited aberrant FC between the left PHG and the right precuneus. This aberrant FC may underlie the pathophysiology of this disorder. These findings offer novel insight into the neural mechanisms of depression comorbid with obesity, enhancing our understanding of its pathogenesis.Keywords: Adolescent depression, Obesity, Magnetic resonance imaging, Functional connectivity, Default mode network

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a serious mental disorder characterized by low mood, lack of interest, and pessimistic thoughts [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 350 million individuals are affected by depression worldwide [2]. In China, the reported prevalence of depression among adolescents ranges from 5.8% to 17.0%, with evidence of a significant annual increase [3]. These trends have profound adverse effects on the physical and psychological well-being of this population. Clinical and epidemiological studies highlight that a significantly higher body mass index (BMI) and increased obesity indicators are observed in adolescents with MDD [4]. Obesity is a chronic and complex condition characterised by excessive fat deposition in the body that may be detrimental to health, and the degree of obesity is associated with potential health risks [5]. Like depression, the global prevalence of obesity is increasing annually [6]. A multinational meta-analysis of youth aged 10–19 years across 21 countries/regions revealed a pooled obesity prevalence of 27.7% [7]. Current projections suggest that the global obese population will surpass one billion individuals by 2030 [8].

Depression and obesity have emerged as significant challenges for global public health. Body mass index (BMI), an anthropometric index calculated from height and weight, serves as a key metric for assessing obesity in populations [9]. Notably, BMI may also predict depression risk [10]. A substantial body of research has revealed a positive correlation between the severity of depression in adolescents and their BMI, as well as obesity indicators [4]. Furthermore, studies have shown that the higher the BMI of obese adolescents, the greater their risk of developing depression in adulthood [11]. Additionally, research has revealed a significant positive correlation between obesity and the risk of depression, with obese children and adolescents being more susceptible to depression [12]. This mounting evidence underscores the existence of a bidirectional association between depression and obesity. Adolescents, as the future of the nation and the reserve force for society’s sustainable development, necessitate urgent attention to the prevention and treatment of depression and obesity.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) is a noninvasive, highly reproducible neuroimaging technique that has advanced rapidly in recent years. This method measures blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals, which reflect spontaneous neural activity in specific brain regions [13]. Based on this characteristic, rs-fMRI technology has been widely used in the field of neuropsychiatric imaging research [14], in which the functional connectivity (FC) analysis method provides a new way of thinking for the scientific research of depression. FC can noninvasively represent the correlation patterns between different brain regions and analyse the changes in effective FC in the brain so that the brain can be analysed in the same way. FC can noninvasively display the correlation patterns between different brain regions, and by analysing the changes in effective FC in the brain, characteristics of central nervous system-related diseases can be revealed [15]. In the resting state, the cerebral cortex exhibits widely distributed spatial correlation, persistence, and spontaneous low-frequency oscillatory BOLD signals, and the functional network constructed by the brain regions that generate these signals is defined as the default mode network (DMN) [16]. In a detailed study of this network, ANDREWS-HANNA et al. reported that the DMN contains 11 partitions [17], and existing studies have shown that abnormalities in the FC of this network are associated with depression and obesity [18, 19]. Few studies have explored depression and obesity after partitioning its. Therefore, partitioning the DMN and then exploring the FC of adolescent patients with depression complicated by obesity is important to further understand the neuroimaging basis of this condition and the underlying intrinsic mechanisms.

In this study, we selected the DMN partition template constructed by Andrews-Hanna et al. as the region of interest (ROI). The FC technique was used to compare differences in the temporal correlation between the DMN and whole-brain voxels in patients with depression complicated by obesity, in patients with depression alone, and in healthy controls. The left parahippocampal gyrus (left PHG) was selected as the ROI for this DMN-based analysis based on several critical considerations. First, the left PHG as a core structural and functional node within the DMN architecture, facilitating the integration of information between the hippocampus and other major DMN components, including the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) [20]. Second, the left PHG is engaged in multiple neurocognitive functions, such as memory consolidation, emotional regulation, and scene construction, which frequently demonstrate notable impairments in both depression and obesity [21, 22]. What’s more, as one of the core nodes in the DMN, the left parahippocampal gyrus (left PHG) has not received sufficient attention in previous studies. Existing research has demonstrated that abnormal FC of the PHG is present when depression or obesity occurs separately [23, 24]. However, few studies have explored whether there are abnormalities in the FC of this brain region in the context of comorbid depression and obesity, a state that may exacerbate or alter the aforementioned abnormalities Accordingly, by examining the FC between this specific DMN node—the left PHG—and the whole brain, we aimed to elucidate how dysfunctional communication originating from a pivotal DMN hub contributes to the pathophysiology of depression complicated by obesity, thereby enabling a precise and robust evaluation of DMN integrity Prior research has identified negative life events as established risk factors for mental health disorders, particularly among adolescents [25]. The Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC), validated in its Chinese adaptation, was developed to evaluate such stressful events [26]. Therefore, in this study, we performed a correlation analysis between the functional connectivity of different brain regions and ASLEC scores in patients with depression complicated by obesity. Based on the above background, the following hypotheses are proposed: ① Depression complicated by obesity is associated with functional connectivity within the DMN, which may lead to pathological alterations. Moreover, abnormal functional connectivity between the left PHG (a key node) and functional brain networks involved in reward processing and cognitive control may also be present in this group. ② The functional connectivity values of the differential brain regions in the group with depression complicated by obesity are significantly correlated with the scores of the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist. If the above hypothesis is valid, these findings may advance our understanding of the neuropathogenesis of depression comorbid with obesity and identify imaging biomarkers to facilitate early diagnosis and targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Subject recruitment

Adolescent patients with depression from Guangji Hospital in Suzhou City were selected, and the inclusion criteria for the patient group were patients who were structurally assessed by two professionally trained psychiatrists above the attending level using the Chinese version of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MIN) [27]. The following criteria were met: ①diagnostic criteria for depression (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition, DSM-5) [28]; ② Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [29] score ≥ 5; ③ a certain level of cognition, able to read and understand the content of the experiment and the scale; ④ an age range of 11–18 years; and ⑤ Han Chinese, right-handed (Han ethnicity is the most populous ethnic group in China) [30]. All subject groups are selected from the Han ethnicity to control genetic and cultural backgrounds to ensure homogeneity. Moreover, all participants were right-handed to maintain consistency in functional responses related to brain lateralization [31]. ⑥ First admission to the hospital and completion of magnetic resonance imaging within one week of admission, with no previous systematic drug therapy (no antidepressants or weight management medications were used). During the same period, 35 healthy controls matched for sex, age, and education level were selected. Healthy controls (HCs group) were enrolled based on the following criteria: ① no diagnostic criteria for any mental disorder and no family history of psychiatric illness; ② no history of drug or alcohol abuse; ③ no serious physical diseases, neurological disorders, etc.; ④ no contraindications to MR examination; ⑤ right-handedness, Han Chinese ethnicity; and ⑥ BMI in the normal range.

The BMI was calculated according to the formula “BMI = body mass (kg)/height (m)²”, which was combined with age, sex and BMI according to “Screening for Malnutrition in School-Aged Children and Adolescents” [32] and “Classification Criteria of Body Mass Index Values for Screening of Overweight and Obesity in School-Aged Children and Adolescents in China [33] (see Table 1). The study subjects were divided into a depression group (MDD group) and a depression complicated by obesity group (OMDD group). Among these subjects, 3 people with excessive head movements and 2 people who did not cooperate with the scale were excluded. Ultimately 75 individuals were included in the study, with 37 in the OMDD group and 38 in the MDD group.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① suffering from other types of psychiatric diseases or first-degree relatives suffering from depression; ② serious somatic pathology or dependence on drugs or alcohol; ③ risk of seizures; ④ recent participation in other clinical drugs or research topics; ⑤ contraindications to MRI; and ⑥ obesity due to drug effects. The study strictly adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was formally approved by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Guangji Hospital (Approval No. Su Guanglun Audit No. 2019–054). All subjects provided written informed consent. The subjects’ demographic information, including essential indicators such as sex, age, education level, and BMI, was also systematically collected and combined with relevant clinical data and rating scales.

Data acquisition

MRI data for all the subjects were acquired at Guangji Hospital (Suzhou, China) using a Siemens Skyra 3.0T MRI scanner equipped with a 20-channel head-neck coil. Prior to scanning, subjects were briefed on MRI safety protocols. During scanning, the subjects were placed in a supine position with their head within the scanner’s field of view. Headphones were provided to mitigate acoustic noise, and foam padding was used to immobilize the head. Participants were instructed to remain awake, keep their eyes closed, avoid focused thinking, and minimize head movement. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence. Functional imaging parameters included TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, 32 slices, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, flip angle = 90°, field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm², and 240 volumes. High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a 3D gradient-echo sequence: TR = 2530 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, flip angle = 7°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm², and 192 slices.

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing was performed on the MATLAB platform using DPARSFA (Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI Advanced) from the DPABI toolkit (http://rfmri.org/dpabi). The acquired DICOM images were converted to the NIFTI format. During preprocessing, the first 10 volumes of each subject’s functional data were discarded to account for magnetic field stabilization. Slice timing correction and head motion correction were subsequently applied. Subjects exhibiting translational motion > 2 mm or rotational motion > 2° were excluded. Functional data were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space. Data were resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm³ isotropic voxels. A bandpass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) was applied to mitigate low-frequency drift and high-frequency physiological noise. Spatial smoothing was performed using a Gaussian kernel with a 4-mm full width at half maximum (FWHM).

Functional connectivity analysis based on region of interest

Based on the results of the MNI study, the Andrews-Hanna template was selected in the DPABI software. The template was constructed by the Andrews-Hanna team, and the DMN was divided into 11 partitions, which were the brain regions of interest in this study. Their detailed divisions with the corresponding MNI coordinates are shown in Table 2. The average time series of the 11 regions of interest and whole-brain voxels were subsequently calculated, and the functional connectivity (FC) correlation coefficients were derived by Pearson correlation analysis. To optimize the analysis, the coefficients were processed using the Fisher Z-transformation, which was applied to correlation coefficients to enhance statistical comparability, enabling the generation of ROI-to-whole-brain FC maps.

Statistical analysis

General demographic and clinical data of the OMDD, MDD, and HCs groups were statistically analysed using SPSS 24.0 software. The measurement data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The two-sample t-test was used to compare two groups, one-way ANOVA was used to compare three groups, and further two-by-two comparisons between groups were made using the least significant difference (LSD) t test. The chi-square test was used for count data, the two-tailed test was used for statistical results, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Using the statistical analysis (SA) module of the DPABI software, FC values between brain regions were compared among the OMDD, MDD, and HCs groups using one-way ANOVA, and regression analyses were performed on covariates (sex, age, and years of education). Differences in FC values of the brain regions among the three groups were analysed using GRF correction thresholds set at P < 0.001 for the voxel level and P < 0.05 for the clump level, with voxel values >30 defined as statistically significant. The results were processed in DPABI using the Utilities tool for value lifting to extract the FC values of different brain regions among the three groups. The FC values were compared two by two post hoc, and the results obtained were Bonferroni corrected. The difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.016 (0.05/3). To reveal the practical significance of the research findings, it is necessary to calculate effect sizes for the statistical results. Cohen’s f was used to quantify the overall effect strength for differences between three or more groups [34], whereas Cohen’s d was used to measure and quantify the effect size of the difference between two groups [35]. A commonly used interpretation categorizes effect sizes as small (f from 0.10 to 0.25, d from 0.20 to 0.49), moderate (f from 0.25 to 0.40, d from 0.50 to 0.79), and large (f >0.40, d >0.80) [34]. Finally, SPSS 24.0 software was used to correlate the FC values of brain regions with statistically significant differences between groups with scores on each of the following assessment tools: the Adolescent Life Events Scale (ASLEC), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), Brief Coping Styles Questionnaire (SCSQ) scale, and Hypomania Check List-32 (HCL-32). Pearson’s correlation analysis was used for bivariate normal distributions, and Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for nonnormal distributions. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Functional connectivity analysis

We selected the DMN template constructed by Andrews-Hanna et al., which was divided into 11 brain regions as the ROIs. We subsequently used ANOVA to analyse the distribution of the FC between the DMN and the whole brain among the three groups. Statistically significant differences were observed in the FC values among the three groups only between the left parahippocampal gyrus (left PHG) (the only ROI with results) and the left putamen, right putamen, right inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis (rIFGop). Post hoc two-by-two comparisons of FC values in DMN brain regions versus the whole brain were performed for each of the three groups, and regression analyses were performed on covariates (sex, age, years of education). Comparing the OMDD group with the MDD group, we found increased FC values between the left PHG and the right precuneus. Compared with those in the HCs group, the FC values between the left PHG in both the OMDD and MDD groups and the left putamen, right putamen, and rIFGop were decreased. The differences among the above three groups were statistically significant. (GRF corrected, voxel-level P < 0.001, cluster-level P < 0.05, voxel value > 30). Discussion

In this study, we explored the differences in FC between DMN-related brain regions and whole-brain voxels in adolescent patients with depression complicated by obesity and those with depression and HCs by applying the FC technique of rs-fMRI. First, by comparing the FC of DMN-associated brain regions with the whole brain between the OMDD and MDD groups, we identified increased FC values between the left PHG in the OMDD group and the right precuneus. Second, in the comparison between the OMDD and HCs groups, decreased FC values were detected between the left PHG of the OMDD group and each of the left putamen, right putamen, and the rIFGop. Notably, identical differences emerged when the MDD group was compared with the HCs group. Finally, we found that FC values in the right putamen of the OMDD group were negatively correlated with the scores of the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) for “Interpersonal Relationships”. These findings indicate that FC analysis may serve as an objective neuroimaging biomarker for the early detection of adolescents at risk for depression complicated by obesity, enabling timely intervention and prevention.

Association between depression and obesity in PHG

As a crucial component of the outer arc of the limbic system, the PHG constructs a core interactive interface between memory processing and the perception of real-time experiences, relying on its close connections with the frontal lobe regions related to working memory and the hippocampus; the latter serves as the central structure for episodic memory encoding and spatial navigation and forms neural pathways through the perforant path. The PHG plays an indispensable role in the maintenance of working memory and the retrieval of episodic memory [36, 37]. Moreover, due to the multiple connections between the PHG and the hippocampus and amygdala, the PHG also plays an important role in emotional regulation [38]. A study on resting-state functional connectivity at the voxel level in the whole brain of patients with depression has shown that the FC of the memory system in the PHG and the medial temporal lobe is reduced in patients with depression [39]. Coincidentally, on the research data from the Human Connectome Project involving 1,017 participants, a significant correlation was observed between the enhancement of FC in the PHG and the decline in sleep quality as well as in the scores of depressive symptom [40]. Moreover, the function of the PHG is also inextricably linked to the occurrence of obesity. A meta-analysis revealed that obesity and eating disorders are characterized by abnormal activation of common brain regions, such as the hippocampus, PHG, and amygdala [41]. In recent fMRI studies, compared with healthy control participants, obese participants presented a significant decrease in neural activity in the medial temporal lobe region (covering the hippocampus and PHG) and some brain areas in the prefrontal cortex that are closely related to memory retrieval [22]. This result reveals that obesity may potentially interfere with memory-related cognitive processes by affecting the neural functions of these key brain regions. Based on the existing research evidence, the pathological mechanism of the depression complicated by obesity is highly likely to be strongly associated with the abnormal function of the PHG. The PHG may act as a crucial mediating factor in the bidirectional influence between the imbalance of emotional homeostasis and cognitive memory.

Association between depression and obesity in the precuneus

The precuneus, as a core functional area in the superior parietal lobe of the brain, occupies a pivotal position in higher-order cognitive neural mechanisms and plays a central regulatory role in cognitive dimensions such as visuospatial information processing, situational memory encoding and extraction, and self-awareness construction [42]. The precuneus is a crucial component of the DMN [43]. In general, functional connectivity between brain regions within the DMN is abnormal in depressed patients. A resting-state fMRI investigation focusing on the DMN in patients with severe depression revealed that, in contrast to healthy control participants, individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder demonstrated significantly enhanced functional connectivity between the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the precuneus during the resting state [44]. Consistent with this finding, Peng et al. reported a positive association between depression severity and the precuneus-dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) FC [45]. Obesity is similarly associated with precuneus dysconnectivity. For example, fMRI studies have shown increased FC between the precuneus and supplementary motor area (SMA), left postcentral gyrus, and precentral gyrus in obese individuals, which may reflect compensatory neuroadaptations in right precuneus-prefrontal circuits during effortful appetite regulation [46]. In addition, when we shift our perspective to depression complicated by obesity, we find that abnormalities in the precuneus exhibit similar changes. The latest research shows that in individuals with comorbid obesity and depressive mood, the functional connectivity between the SMA and the precuneus is increased [47]. Therefore, as shown by the abovementioned studies, it is not difficult to determine whether depression or obesity alone, or the comorbid state of the two, can lead to an increase in the functional connectivity of the precuneus. Our finding of altered FC between the left PHG and the precuneus (another major DMN node) suggests a specific disruption within the DMN in the OMDD group. This indicates that the pathological interaction between depression and obesity may target the functional integrity between specific key hubs within the DMN.

Association between depression and obesity in putamen and inferior frontal gyrus

The putamen, a key component of the basal ganglia, is critically involved in learning, motor control, and processes such as reward processing, cognitive function, and addiction [48]. The putamen has been implicated in diverse motor and psychiatric disorders, with MDD specifically linked to reduced putamen volume and functional deficits [48, 49]. One study reported a positive correlation between reduced FC in the putamen-right anterior cingulate gyrus pathway and depression severity [50]. Diminished FC between the left putamen and right amygdala exhibited a positive correlation with higher depression severity during emotional face processing tasks [51]. This finding is consistent with our results; that is, compared with those of the HCs, the FC values between the parahippocampal gyrus of both the OMDD group and the MDD group and between the left putamen and the right putamen are decreased. As a result, the functional imbalance of the putamen plays an important role in the development of both depression and obesity. In addition to its role in processing cognitively and emotionally relevant information and executing learned behaviours, the putamen modulates dopamine-mediated reward pathways [52]. Abnormalities in its functioning may lead to disturbed eating patterns, a shift to a reliance on short-term reward feedback from high-calorie foods, and overeating to increase dopamine levels in an attempt to derive pleasurable sensations from food, which in the long term can lead to weight gain and obesity [53, 54]. In a study using fMRI in obese patients, a reduced putamen response to palatable food was associated with deficits in BMI and reward prediction; furthermore, obese individuals demonstrated enhanced resting-state FC within the putamen compared with that in normal-weight controls [55]. Obesity also triggers a chronic inflammatory response throughout the body, which may exacerbate putamen dysfunction by affecting striatal dopamine receptor expression [56, 57]. In turn, dysfunction of the putamen may lead to the development of depression, which in turn leads to a vicious cycle. Therefore, when the putamen is dysfunctional, it may affect the function of other brain regions through functional connectivity, which may lead to an imbalance in mood and appetite regulation and increase susceptibility to depression complicated by obesity.

The inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis (IFGop), a frontal lobe region overlying the insular cortex [58], and the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) region are involved in complex attention and working memory processes [59]. Additionally, the IFGop is involved in high-level cognitive processes and plays a key role in executive control function [60]. The IFG is one of the brain regions that is activated when individuals present with anxiety and show stress induction [61]. Moreover, neurobiological evidence suggests that altered neural activity along the IFG-to-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) axis is correlated with antidepressant treatment efficacy [62]. A study revealed that the FC of numerous voxels in the IFGop differed between depressed patients and healthy controls. In most cases, FC was enhanced in this region and significantly greater in the right IFG than in the left IFG. Brain regions with FC to the IFGop include the inferior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, temporal pole, angular gyrus, cingulate cortex, precuneus, and hippocampus [63]. Notably, many of these regions overlap with the DMN [64]. In contrast, our results demonstrated reduced FC between the left PHG and the rIFGop in MDD patients. This discrepancy may arise from methodological variations, including sample size disparities and divergent software preprocessing methods. Nevertheless, these findings underscore a critical association between aberrant IFG FC and depressive pathology.

The IFG is closely associated with obesity, as evidenced by increased IFG activation in obese individuals during mealtime relative to lean cohorts [65]. A systematic evaluation revealed structural abnormalities in gray matter volume in obese patients, with a significant positive correlation between increased BMI and decreased inferior frontal gyrus volume in obese individuals [66]. In an analysis of functional connectivity in obese individuals, higher BMI was found to be associated with lower FC between the caudate nucleus and the right inferior frontal gyrus [67] in addition to reduced functional connectivity in the frontal gyrus in the default mode network [68]. In our study, we found that the OMDD group had lower FC values in the parahippocampal gyrus and the opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus than the HCs group, which is consistent with the decreasing trend in the results of the FC analysis in obese individuals described above.

Correlation of FC differential brain areas and clinical scales in OMDD groups

The present study found that the FC value of the right putamen in the OMDD group was negatively correlated with the ASLEC scores of “Interpersonal Relationships”. The ASLEC used in this study consists of 27 standardized items divided into six factors: interpersonal relationships, academic pressure, punishment, loss, health adaptation and other factors. It was specifically designed to assess stressful life events experienced by the adolescent population in the last 12 months. As required by the scale design, the adolescents read each event and rated it on a 5-point scale (1 = no impact, 5 = strong/extremely heavy impact), with higher total scores indicating that the subjects had experienced more intensive negative life event stimuli [69, 70].

Exposure to adverse environments during early adolescence constitutes a significant risk factor for depression in this population. Adolescents exhibit heightened sensitivity to interpersonal dynamics, wherein peer rejection and familial communication deficits emerge as salient stressors [71]. An interpersonal relationship fMRI study demonstrated that the putamen is critically involved in processing verbal stimuli related to interpersonal conflict, particularly during the perception of conflict-laden language [72]. At the same time, the putamen has also been implicated as an important structural underpinning of cognitive, affective, and motor processing [73], and in depressed adolescents with nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI), reduced putamen volumes correlate with dysregulated modulation of frustration and anger [74]. When individuals deal with stressful interpersonal relationships, poor patterns of emotion regulation often act as a risk factor for predisposition to depression [75]. Studies have examined the gender prevalence of the relationship between obesity and depressive symptoms, with poor interpersonal interactions as a mediating variable being significantly associated with both disorders [76]. Reduced social engagement in obese individuals is strongly associated with a depression diagnosis, with diminished social activity frequency increasing depression risk [77]. Therefore, in adolescents, putamen abnormalities may lead to the dysregulation of emotional functioning, which in turn leads to the development of poor interpersonal patterns and, ultimately, a significant psychological burden. The abovementioned consequences of putamen abnormalities may constitute a potential risk factor for the development of depression complicated by obesity in adolescents.

Obesity-depression interactive reinforcement: bidirectional influence pathways in comorbid States

The functional connectivity (FC) abnormalities identified in the OMDD group and MDD group are not viewed as independent phenomena but rather as manifestations of an interactive effect. Therefore, we propose the following mechanistic model for the interaction of comorbid depression and obesity within the DMN:

Depression-associated FC reductions (left PHG–Putamen/left PHG–rIFGop):​​ The PHG integrates emotional memories [36–38]. The putamen regulates reward processing and cognition [48]. The rIFGop mediates cognitive control and emotional regulation [60, 61]. The weakened FC between the left PHG and the putamen, along with that between the left PHG and the rIFGop, may disrupt the transmission of emotional memory–reward signals. For example, our study reported that FC values in the right putamen of the OMDD group were negatively correlated with “interpersonal relationship” scores. This finding suggests that individuals with depression may experience impaired reward processing during daily events and that negative emotional memories may not be effectively counteracted by normative reward signals, thereby maintaining a low mood state. Consequently, individuals may encounter greater difficulty in improving mood or regulating weight through adaptive behaviours (e.g., exercise, socializing), thereby indirectly contributing to obesity maintenance. Negative emotional memories are difficult to regulate effectively by the cognitive control function of the rIFGop, resulting in the inability to suppress negative thought rumination, which may be a key neural basis for depressive symptoms (depressed mood, psychomotor retardation).

The increased PHG–precuneus FC may exacerbate this state. Negative information integrated by the PHG (encompassing emotional memory and somatosensory perception) is continuously extracted into self-referential thoughts via the precuneus [36, 37, 81]. This information is subsequently fed back to the PHG through the DMN circuitry, establishing a closed loop characterized by negative information integration, self-referential reinforcement, and exacerbated low mood. This process not only intensifies depressive symptoms but also diminishes individuals’ confidence in and motivation for weight loss, ultimately establishing a vicious cycle: obesity → neural circuit abnormalities → exacerbated depression → worsening obesity.

In conclusion, this model suggests that the PHG may be regarded as a key functional node within the DMN. Specifically, the neural activity state of the PHG can not only regulate the overall activity level and functional patterns of it, but also, through its core role, participate in the coordinated implementation process of the DMN in key cognitive functions such as emotional memory encoding, somatosensory integration, and self-referential processing, thereby supporting the core functional value of this network in intrinsic cognitive activities. FC changes in different brain regions are associated with the combined effects of depression and obesity. The interaction between the two may be the core neural mechanism of the OMDD group within the DMN.

Limitations and perspectives

This study has several limitations. First, the limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of our findings, and future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to enhance statistical power. Second, the cross-sectional design inherently limits our ability to capture dynamic disease-related changes over time. Longitudinal investigations are necessary to delineate the temporal trajectory of symptom progression and evaluate neuroplastic adaptations to therapeutic interventions. Future longitudinal fMRI studies are critical to elucidate resting-state functional connectivity (FC) alterations in depression complicated by obesity and their clinical implications.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study revealed the presence of significant functional connectivity (FC) abnormalities between left PHG and the whole brain in patients with depression complicated by obesity. The largest FC abnormalities in these patients were concentrated in brain regions involved in learning, motor control, and cognitive control, particularly the precuneus, putamen and IFGop. These findings suggest that FC abnormalities in the left PHG and the above brain regions may be key driving nodes in the pathogenesis process of patients with depression complicated by obesity. The present study revealed that abnormal function of the DMN is the physiological mechanism underlying depression complicated by obesity. These findings not only expand the theoretical understanding of depression complicated by obesity but also lay an important physiological foundation for the subsequent development of precise intervention and prevention strategies.
