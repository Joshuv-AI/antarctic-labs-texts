Development of an H9N2 vaccine strain with enhanced neuraminidase immunogenicity via dual hemagglutinin and neuraminidase modification
Authors: Jin-Ha Song, Seung-Eun Son, Howon Kim, Seung-Ji Kim, Se-Hee An, Chung-Young Lee, Kang-Seuk Choi, Hyuk-Joon Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceThe Y439 lineage 01310 E20 H9N2 vaccine strain currently used in South Korea has undergone extensive egg adaptation, resulting in substantial changes, including an 18-amino acid neuraminidase (NA) stalk deletion. Additionally, both early and late 01310 passages inherently harbor an N-glycan at hemagglutinin (HA) residue 158-160 (HA158) that may interfere with virus-specific antibodies.ObjectiveWe aimed to develop a high-yield vaccine strain without egg passaging and to overcome the limitations of the conventional vaccine strain that may compromise immunogenicity.MethodsWe introduced a genetically modified 01310 PB2 gene (310-MVV: I66M, I109V, I133V) to increase replication in embryonated eggs and removed the N-glycan at HA158 and restored the NA stalk to improve immunogenicity. The resulting strain was assessed for egg replication and immunogenicity in chickens.ResultsThe resulting vaccine strain (310-SNS-193D-MVV) grew efficiently in embryonated eggs without repeated passaging. As restoring the NA stalk alone was insufficient to enhance NA-specific immunity, simultaneously removing the N-glycan at HA158 markedly increased NA-specific antibody responses and neutralizing antibody titers across multiple H9N2 lineages. Additionally, incorporating 310-SNS-193D-MVV into a bivalent formulation with a Y280 lineage strain conferred broader coverage without evidence of immune interference.Conclusions and RelevanceThese findings underscore how PB2, HA, and NA targeted genetic modifications can improve H9N2 vaccine productivity and immunogenicity. These strategies are not limited to our H9N2 strain and can be applied to other low propagating or NA stalk-deleted virus strains.Keywords: Influenza A virus, H9N2 subtype; vaccines, inactivated; neuraminidase; virus replication; cross protection

INTRODUCTION

H9N2 avian influenza viruses (AIVs) are divided into North American and Eurasian lineages, among which the Y439, G1, and Y280 lineages have caused significant economic losses in Asia, the Middle East, and Africa [1,2,3]. In South Korea, Y439 lineage H9N2 AIVs that were not adapted to chickens have been established in commercial poultry, creating both economic and public health concerns [4]. Consequently, the Korean government implemented a vaccination policy using an egg-passaged Y439 lineage H9N2 vaccine strain that reduced Y439 lineage outbreaks in domestic poultry. Despite the reduced incidence of Y439 lineage outbreaks in domestic poultry, Y280 lineage AIVs remain prevalent, and Y439 and North American lineage viruses continue to be detected in wild migratory birds in South Korea [5,6,7].

Serial passage in embryonated chicken eggs (ECEs) is a classical method for adapting vaccine strains that initially replicate poorly to increase their yield [8,9]. Although serial passaging in ECEs can increase viral yield for vaccine production, such adaptation often introduces unintended mutations at antigenic sites, potentially altering antigenicity and affecting vaccine efficacy [10,11]. The current Y439 lineage H9N2 vaccine strain used in South Korea, A/chicken/Korea/01310 E20/2001 (01310 E20), was passaged 20 times in ECEs to improve replication efficiency. During this process, it acquired a T133N mutation in hemagglutinin (HA), resulting in a newly introduced N-glycan, and underwent an 18-amino acid deletion in the neuraminidase (NA) stalk region [12]. NA stalk deletion is considered a chicken adaptation marker and has been linked to increased virulence in chickens and mammals, as well as enhanced viral fitness in human cell lines [13,14,15]. Such an increase in virulence raises biosafety concerns and may compromise the safety profile necessary for extensive use. Moreover, shortening the NA stalk can decrease NA immunogenicity by lowering its height relative to that of HA, thus limiting epitope exposure [16]. Although NA is often considered a subdominant antigen compared with HA, it undergoes fewer antigenic changes and has increasingly been recognized as a crucial immunogen in influenza viruses [17,18,19,20,21]. Additionally, unlike most recent H9N2 AIVs, both the early- and late-passage 01310 strains possess an N-glycan near the HA antigenic site (residues 158-160, H3 numbering), which may reduce virus-specific antibody induction when used as a vaccine (Supplementary Table 1) [22,23].

In this study, we sought to address the shortcomings of conventional H9N2 vaccine, particularly the truncated NA stalk and the presence of an N-glycan at HA residues 158-160 (HA158). Rather than relying on extensive serial egg adaptation, we introduced genetically modified PB2 (310-MVV) to increase viral replication in ECEs, while also removing the N-glycan at HA158 and restoring the full-length NA stalk to enhance vaccine immunogenicity [24]. Our resulting constructed virus strain, 310-SNS-193D-MVV, replicated robustly in ECEs, demonstrating improved growth properties and suitability for vaccine production. Serological assays revealed that simultaneously removing the N-glycan at HA158 and restoring the full-length NA stalk significantly increased cross-lineage NA-specific immunity and conferred broader neutralizing activity. Finally, formulating our vaccine strain with a Y280 lineage virus yielded an even broader immune response against diverse H9N2 lineages without immune interference. These findings highlight the interdependent nature of HA and NA immunogenicity and suggest that combining modifications to both can serve as a valuable framework for developing broadly protective H9N2 vaccine strains.

METHODS

Ethics statement

All experiments involving recombinant viruses constructed in this study, including in vitro assays, were conducted in a biosafety level 2 facility at the College of Veterinary Medicine, Seoul National University (Seoul, South Korea), and were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Seoul National University (approval number: SNUIBC-R230313-2). The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (approval number: SNU-231102-1). All animal experiments were performed in an animal biosafety level 1 facility within the same institution.

Viruses, plasmids, cells, and eggs

Four wild-type H9N2 viruses, A/chicken/Korea/01310/2001 (01310, Y439 lineage), which was passaged 4 times (01310 E4) or 20 times (01310 E20, T133 variant); A/chicken/Korea/SL20/2020 (SL20, Y280 lineage); and A/Greylag Goose/South Korea/SW21/2021 (SW21), which was confirmed to be of North American lineage, were used, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. We chose 01310 E20 (T133 variant) as the control virus to eliminate the additional glycosylation that would occur if residue 133 was Asparagine (N) (as in the 01310 E20 strains). By maintaining Threonine (T) at residue 133, we focused on the effects of removal of N-glycan at HA158 and NA stalk restoration. The recombinant H9N2 virus derived from the Y280 lineage, SL20-MVV, which was developed in a previous study, was utilized for the bivalent experiments [24]. The 293T, Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK), and Calu-3 cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium or Minimum Essential Medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C in 5% CO2. All the wild-type and recombinant viruses were replicated in 10-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicken eggs (VALO BioMedia GmbH, Germany) before use.

Establishment of recombinant viruses

To generate recombinant H9N2 viruses, we used the HA and NA genes derived from 01310 E4 virus and six internal genes (PB2, PB1, PA, NP, M, and NS) from A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8, H1N1) backbone. A modified PB2 gene containing the I66M, I109V, and I133V mutations (310-MVV) was incorporated to enhance viral growth in ECEs, as described previously [25,26]. All gene segments were cloned into the pHW2000 reverse-genetics vector.

To further modify the HA gene, site-directed mutagenesis was performed using a Muta-Direct kit (iNtRON, Korea) to introduce N158S and/or N193D mutations, targeting the N-glycosylation site at HA158. A 12-plasmid reverse-genetics system was employed to rescue recombinant viruses in 293T cell [27]. Rescued viruses were verified by hemagglutination assay using 1% chicken red blood cells (RBCs) and confirmed through full-genome sequencing via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction [28].

Sequence analysis

Seventy-four H9N2 HA sequences from Korean isolates were collected from the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID) [29]. Multiple alignments were performed with MAFFT (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/), and the frequency of potential N-glycosylation sites (p-NGSs) commonly present in HA was examined. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA11 software [30]. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using a general time-reversible nucleotide substitution model. Bootstrap analysis was performed with 1,000 replications with gamma distributions with invariant sites (G + I).

Structural modeling

The A/Swine/Hong Kong/9/98 HA crystal structure (PDB ID: 1JSD) and A/Tanzania/205/2010 NA structure (PDB ID: 4GZX) were used in this study. These models were used to visualize our vaccine design strategy in 3D. All rendering was performed with the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 3.1.0; Schrödinger, USA) [31].

Western blotting

Recombinant viruses were subjected to PNGase F (New England Biolabs, USA) treatment or mock treatment before electrophoresis on Bolt Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, USA). Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot 2 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After blocking, the membranes were incubated with either anti-H9N2 HA polyclonal antibody (Sino Biological, China) or anti-H9N2 NA polyclonal antibody (Sino Biological), followed by incubation with goat anti-rabbit IgG-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Bethyl, USA). TMB substrate (Surmodics IVD, USA) was used for color development.

Replication and growth kinetics of the recombinant H9N2 viruses

To evaluate replication efficiency in embryonated eggs, each recombinant virus was serially diluted (106 to 109) and inoculated into five 10-day-old SPF eggs. After 72 h at 37°C, a hemagglutination assay was performed to determine the 50% egg infectious dose, which was calculated by the Spearman-Karber method [32]. To evaluate mammalian infection risk, each virus was inoculated into MDCK or Calu-3 cells at a multiplicity of infection of 0.001. The supernatants were collected at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h and then titrated (50% tissue culture infectious dose [TCID50]/mL) in MDCK cells.

Solid-phase binding assay

Following a previously reported procedure, 96-well immunoplates (SPL, Korea) were coated overnight with 10 µg/mL Fetuin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 4°C [33]. After the plates were washed and dried, each virus (16 hemagglutination units [HAU] in phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] plus 2 µM NA inhibitor) was added for incubation overnight at 4°C. The plates were then washed, blocked with PBS containing 0.1% NA-treated bovine serum albumin for 1 h, and incubated for 1 h at 4°C with two-fold serial dilutions of biotinylated sialylglycopolymers (3′SLN-PAA or 6′SLN-PAA; GlycoNZ, New Zealand). Finally, HRP-conjugated streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied for 1 h of incubation, TMB substrate was used for color development, and the reaction was stopped with 0.1 M H2SO4. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Antigen preparation and immunization in SPF chicken

Four viral antigens were used for vaccine preparation: 01310 E20 (T133 variant), 310-MVV, 310-SNS-193D-MVV, and SL20-MVV. Each virus was adjusted to 64 HAU in PBS, inactivated by the addition of 37% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) at a 1:500 ratio, and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Monovalent vaccines were prepared by mixing the inactivated antigen with ISA78 (SEPPIC, France) at a 3:7 ratio, whereas bivalent vaccines contained equal volumes of two inactivated viruses at the same oil-adjuvant ratio. Each oil emulsion (0.5 mL) was injected intramuscularly into five 3-week-old SPF chickens. Sera were collected at 2, 3, and 4 weeks postvaccination and heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min.

Serological assays

In the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay, twofold serial dilutions of serum in 96-well V-bottom plates were mixed with 4 HAU of each antigen at a 1:1 ratio. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature, 1% chicken RBCs were added for an additional 40 min of incubation. The HI titer was defined as the highest serum dilution that prevented hemagglutination.

An enzyme-linked lectin assay was used as the neuraminidase inhibition (NI) assay [34]. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with 25 µg/mL fetuin (Sigma-Aldrich) by overnight incubation at 4°C. Additionally, two-fold serial dilutions of serum were incubated with antigen at the 90% effective concentration and then transferred to fetuin-coated plates for 2 h of incubation at 37°C. The plates were washed, 0.5 µg/mL PNA-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich) was added, and TMB substrate was added for color development. The NI titer was defined as the serum dilution that inhibited 50% NA activity.

A virus neutralization (VN) assay was conducted as previously described [35]. First, serial twofold dilutions of serum were mixed with 100 TCID50 virus at a 1:1 ratio for 1 h at 37°C. The virus/serum mixture was subsequently incubated with MDCK cells for 1 h, and then the medium was replaced with 50 µL of serially diluted serum plus 50 µL of infection medium. After 72 h, the supernatants were subjected to a hemagglutination assay with 1% chicken RBCs. The VN titer was defined as the highest dilution that completely neutralized the viruses, as detected by the absence of hemagglutination activity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, USA). The replication efficacies of the recombinant viruses in embryonated eggs were compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Additionally, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was employed to compare growth kinetics in mammalian cell lines and to analyze the data from the solid-phase binding assay and serological assays (HI, NI, and VN). For the serological assay, the titer values were converted to log2 values for statistical analysis. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Development and characterization of an H9N2 strain exhibiting a restored NA stalk and removal of the N-glycan at HA residue 158 by introducing the N158S mutation

We analyzed HA sequences from South Korean H9N2 isolates (n = 74) and determined the frequency of eight common p-NGSs (Table 1). Among the p-NGSs, residues 128-130, 158-160, and 210-212 are located in the HA globular head. The majority of Korean H9N2 isolates contain p-NGSs at residues 128-130 and 210-212, whereas a p-NGS at HA residues 158-160 (HA158) is rarely observed. However, the conventional vaccine strain (01310) currently used in Korea inherently harbors this relatively uncommon p-NGS at HA158, regardless of passage history (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). The additional N-glycan at HA158 can potentially mask critical antigenic epitopes or alter their conformation, thereby interfering with immune recognition of the virus. Moreover, the NA stalk of the 01310 E20 strain became truncated after repeated egg passaging, which may limit the exposure of the NA head and compromise NA-based immunity. Thus, the presence of an uncommon N-glycan at HA158 and the truncated NA stalk in the current vaccine strain highlight the need to overcome these antigenic limitations to enhance vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy.

We rescued six recombinant viruses featuring early-passage NA (01310 E4 NA) (Fig. 1A, Table 2). We also introduced an HA-N158S mutation to remove the p-NGS at HA158 (310-SNS HA) (Fig. 1B). Simply replacing the internal genes of the wild-type 01310 E4 virus with those of 310-(p) or 310-MVV increased viral replication in ECEs by approximately tenfold compared to the parental 01310 E4 strain (Table 2). However, removal of the p-NGS at HA158 markedly reduced replication efficiency, resulting in approximately 67-fold (310-SNS(p)) and 120-fold (310-SNS-MVV) reductions in viral titers compared to the 01310 E20 (T133 variant) virus. We hypothesized that the loss of this N-glycan near the HA receptor-binding domain might increase its affinity for α2,3-linked sialic acids (α2,3-SAs), thereby disrupting the HA/NA balance and impairing viral growth [36]. Indeed, a solid-phase binding assay revealed that removing the N-glycan at HA158 (310-SNS-MVV) clearly increased the affinity for α2,3-SAs compared with 310-MVV, whereas the affinity for α2,6-linked sialic acids remained negligible (Supplementary Fig. 2). To mitigate this heightened affinity for α2,3-SAs, we introduced the N193D mutation, which is known to reduce the affinity for α2,3-SAs, into 310-SNS-MVV, producing 310-SNS-193D-MVV [37]. This modification reduced the affinity for α2,3-SAs to the level of 310-MVV and fully restored egg replication, surpassing that of even 01310 E20 (T133 variant) (Supplementary Fig. 2, Table 2), indicating that N193D offsets the imbalance caused by glycan removal. Western blot analyses confirmed the removal of the N-glycan upon N158S mutation, as the HA0 band of 310-SNS-193D-MVV migrated slightly lower than those of the control viruses but aligned with those of the control viruses upon PNGase F treatment, indicating specific N-glycan loss (Fig. 1C). The NA stalk length of 310-SNS-193D-MVV was also restored to match that of the early-passage strain.

In summary, as illustrated in Fig. 1D, by restoring the full-length NA stalk, removing the N-glycan at HA158, and introducing the compensatory N193D mutation, we successfully generated a vaccine strain that replicates efficiently in ECEs without extensive egg passage.

Combining deglycosylation at HA158 and restoration of the NA stalk enhances cross-lineage NA antibody immune responses

To evaluate the in vivo immunogenicity of 310-SNS-193D-MVV strain as optimized H9N2 vaccine strain in SPF chickens, three inactivated viruses, 01310 E20 (T133 variant), 310-MVV, and 310-SNS-193D-MVV, were formulated as oil emulsion vaccines. Serum samples were collected at 2, 3, and 4 week post immunization, and HI, NI, and VN assays were performed against three H9N2 antigens: 01310 E4 (Y439 lineage), SW21 (confirmed to be of North American lineage; Supplementary Fig. 1), and SL20 (Y280 lineage). Sequence analysis revealed that 01310 shares 87.5%/95.5% HA1/NA amino acid identity with SW21 but only 81.6%/84.2% amino acid identity with SL20 (Table 3).

Furthermore, NI and VN assays were performed with the closely related 01310 E4 (Fig. 2D and G), and all three vaccine strains again produced strong responses, indicating effective induction of both HA and NA immunity. In contrast, the NI and VN titers against SW21 were notably greater for 310-SNS-193D-MVV than for 01310 E20 (T133 variant) or 310-MVV (Fig. 2E and H). Similarly, although the NI and VN titers against SL20 were lower overall in all the groups, 310-SNS-193D-MVV continued to outperform the other formulations (Fig. 2F and I).

Overall, while HI titers remained similar among vaccine strains, the NI and VN assays clearly demonstrated the advantage of 310-SNS-193D-MVV, particularly in terms of cross-lineage immunity. These findings suggest that the dual HA/NA modification synergistically induced robust cross-lineage NA-targeted immunity, thereby enhancing the overall breadth of neutralization.

The bivalent formulation consisting of 310-SNS-193D-MVV and a Y280 lineage H9N2 strain confers robust cross-reactive antibody immune responses

Despite the broadened immunogenicity of 310-SNS-193D-MVV, our data indicated relatively low neutralizing activity against the more antigenically distant SL20 strain, which is of Y280 lineage and is now dominant in the South Korean poultry population [5]. Given the heterogeneous nature of H9N2 viruses circulating in different regions, including those of the Y280 lineage in commercial poultry flocks and those of the Y439 lineage in some wild birds, we sought to address this gap by preparing a bivalent combination of inactivated viral antigens. Specifically, we combined 310-SNS-193D-MVV with SL20-MVV (engineered to harbor the 310-MVV PB2 gene with efficient egg propagation and reduced mammalian virulence [24]) to determine whether incorporating both vaccine strains could confer robust cross-lineage immune responses without immune interference.

Consistent with the results in Fig. 2, monovalent 310-SNS-193D-MVV produced robust HI, NI, and VN responses against 01310 E4 and SW21 but showed comparatively weaker reactivity against SL20 (Fig. 3). In contrast, monovalent SL20-MVV generated high HI, NI, and VN antibody titers against SL20 but was less effective against 01310 E4 and SW21. Notably, the bivalent formulation elicited high antibody titers (HI, NI, and VN) against all three antigens without evidence of immune interference, and the titers remained comparable to those of each monovalent vaccine against its homologous antigen. In summary, although 310-SNS-193D-MVV induced broader cross-reactive antibody responses than the conventional strain, it exhibited relatively low immunogenicity against the Y280 lineage, which was effectively addressed by adding SL20-MVV to formulate a bivalent antigen mixture.

DISCUSSION

The current Y439 lineage H9N2 vaccine strain (01310 E20 virus) used in South Korea has been serially passaged in ECEs to increase viral yield [12]. However, this process introduced an 18-amino acid deletion in the NA stalk, potentially reducing NA immunogenicity by limiting epitope exposure [12,16]. Additionally, both the parental 01310 strain and the E20 variant inherently harbor an N-glycan at HA158, a feature that is absent in approximately 90% of Korean H9N2 isolates (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). This N-glycan may shield critical antigenic sites, further diminishing vaccine-induced immunity [38]. Although 01310 E20 has effectively controlled Y439 lineage outbreaks, ongoing detection of Y439-like variants and North American lineage viruses in migratory birds underscores the need for an improved vaccine (Supplementary Fig. 1). In this study, by restoring the NA stalk, removing the N-glycan at HA residue 158 via the N158S substitution, and replacing the internal genes, we developed a vaccine strain with broader immunogenicity with higher yield in eggs without repeated passaging than the conventional vaccine strain.

N-Glycans on influenza HA are known to support structural stability, receptor specificity, and binding affinity [39,40]. Removing such an N-glycan near the receptor-binding domain can impose a fitness cost by increasing HA binding affinity, which in turn leads to overly strong attachment to nonproductive receptors (e.g., mucus), thereby hindering viral motility and necessitating more robust NA activity for detachment [41,42]. Indeed, deleting the N-glycan at HA158 in our experiments reduced replication in ECEs, suggesting a fitness cost that demanded further compensatory adaptation. Position 193 in HA is located within the receptor-binding site and is known to influence receptor affinity and host range [37,43,44]. Introducing the N193D substitution successfully counterbalanced excessive α2,3-binding, restoring efficient propagation in eggs, and demonstrating how a single change near the receptor-binding site can counteract the fitness deficit caused by N-glycan removal. Consequently, these findings underscore that when engineering recombinant vaccine strains, identifying compensatory changes is critical, and the removal of even a single glycan to improve immunogenicity can necessitate additional alterations to preserve fitness.

Simply restoring the full-length NA stalk did not significantly enhance NA-based immunity in our experiments; the key insight was the synergistic effect of combining both HA and NA modifications. Specifically, removing the N-glycan at HA158 alongside NA stalk restoration led to a marked increase in NA inhibition against heterologous H9N2 antigens. A previous study in mice reported that merely extending the NA stalk did not increase the induction of NI-reactive antibodies but increased the number of antibodies with antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, likely because it led to increased exposure of epitopes below or on the lateral surfaces of the NA head domain [16]. However, our results demonstrate the critical importance of additional removal of N-glycan at HA: this modification likely alleviated steric hindrance around the head domain of the extended NA, effectively exposing conserved epitopes to B cells and significantly enhancing NI antibody responses [19]. This finding emphasizes the unique and complementary role of HA N-glycan removal when combined with NA stalk extension, supporting previous observations that HA N-glycosylation and NA stalk length act as interdependent determinants of viral antigenicity [45].

NI antibodies play a critical role in blocking the release of viral particles and limiting the spread of infection between host cells [17]. Additionally, NA is more conserved than HA due to its lower mutation rate, and certain antibodies targeting specific NA epitopes have been reported to exhibit broad antigenic reactivity across multiple subtypes [21]. In line with these characteristics, NI antibodies induced by the 310-SNS-193D-MVV strain showed high titers against phylogenetically distinct H9N2 antigens, clearly demonstrating the cross-reactive nature of NA-based immune responses. Notably, while HI titers were comparable among vaccine groups, NI and VN titers showed a strong positive correlation, with higher NI responses consistently associated with higher neutralizing antibody titers against heterologous antigens. This suggests that NI antibodies contributed significantly to the observed neutralizing activity against heterologous antigens, providing a plausible explanation for the distinct differences in VN titers despite similar HI responses. Taken together, the 310-SNS-193D-MVV strain—engineered with both NA stalk restoration and HA glycan removal—elicited the strongest NA-based cross-reactive immune responses, including the induction of cross-neutralizing antibodies. These findings highlight its potential as a promising vaccine strain design strategy for addressing antigenic variation.

Immune interference, which occurs when the immune response to a dominant antigen can suppress the response to another, possibly resulting in an uneven immunogenic profile, is often a concern with bivalent vaccines [46,47]. Our prior research revealed that when recombinant viruses of Y439 lineage were combined with those of Y280 lineage in a bivalent antigen formulation, there was a reduced HI antibody response against the less immunogenic Y439 lineage [24]. However, our bivalent antigen formulation induced high antibody titers against all three H9N2 lineage viruses without evidence of immune interference. Thus, our bivalent approach provides a promising strategy to protect poultry flocks from multiple cocirculating H9N2 lineages while minimizing the risk of vaccine failure.

Despite the promising immunogenicity results, this study has a limitation. We did not perform a challenge experiment to directly evaluate protective efficacy in vivo, so our findings rely on immune correlates (antibody titers). However, previous studies have demonstrated that HI titers ≥ 64 reliably predict protective efficacy against H9N2 infection in chickens [48,49]. Given that our final bivalent antigen formulation elicited HI titers above this protective threshold against all three antigenically diverse H9N2 lineages tested, along with notably high NI and VN antibody responses, it is reasonable to infer substantial protective efficacy even in the absence of direct challenge data. Nonetheless, future studies are warranted to confirm this inference.

In conclusion, we successfully demonstrated the optimization of the conventional H9N2 vaccine strain to achieve high-yield and enhanced immunogenicity, particularly against NA, through strategic genetic modifications. Importantly, these optimization strategies may also be applicable to other low-propagating or NA-stalk-deleted virus strains beyond the H9N2 strain described here. Furthermore, the bivalent antigen formulation combining 310-SNS-193D-MVV and SL20-MVV showed high serological responses against various H9N2 lineages.
