DR.DEGMON: self-explainable deep neural network for drug-induced cell viability prediction incorporating differentially expressed genes and gene ontology
Authors: Wootaek Lim, Jitae Kim, Songhyeon Kim, Hyunsu Bong, Kwang-Su Park, Minji Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prediction of cancer drug responses is essential for advancing cancer treatment strategies and drug development. With the increasing availability of large-scale pharmacogenomic datasets, many deep learning models have been proposed to predict cancer drug responses. However, many existing models lack the capacity to offer critical biomedical insights, such as providing interpretability regarding the potential mechanism of action.MethodsWe propose DR.DEGMON (Drug Response prediction using Differentially Expressed Genes with Multi-layer perceptron integrating gene Ontology Network), a self-explainable deep neural network designed to predict the viability of pan-cancer cell lines in response to drug treatments by utilizing differentially expressed genes. DR.DEGMON leverages prior biological knowledge by incorporating Gene Ontology (GO) into the hierarchical structure of a multi-layer perceptron. The architecture of DR.DEGMON highlights key genes and GO terms that contribute to drug responses through layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP), suggesting potential biological pathways associated with specific drugs.ResultsDR.DEGMON achieved a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.8568 for cell viability prediction, outperforming all baseline models. The model also showed robust generalization performance on external datasets, including GDSC, PRISM, and CCLE. In addition, we employed layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) to obtain relevance scores for input genes and nodes representing GO terms.ConclusionDR.DEGMON shows high performance in predicting drug responses and provides interpretable results. The integration of GO and LRP enabled the model to suggest the underlying biological processes involved in drug responses, making it a valuable tool for predicting outcomes and discovering new biomedical knowledge in cancer pharmacogenomics. This approach offers both practical utility in drug development and a method for improving the understanding of cancer biology.Keywords: Cancer drug response prediction, Self-explainable artificial intelligence, Gene Ontology, Differentially expressed genes

Background

Predicting cancer drug response (CDR) is crucial for developing anticancer drugs and understanding cancer biology [1]. The development of large pharmacogenomic databases, such as the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) [2], Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) [3], Profiling relative inhibition simultaneously in mixtures (PRISM) [4] and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [5], has greatly advanced CDR prediction. These databases encompass drug responses to hundreds of compounds across thousands of human cancer cell lines, enabling researchers to link molecular signatures (genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic) to drug responses by using artificial intelligence (AI) models.

Various deep learning architectures have been proposed to improve the accuracy of CDR prediction. Most existing approaches aim to maximize predictive performance by integrating drug information, such as chemical structures, with omics data, including basal gene expression profiles. Representative examples include DeepCDR [1], a hybrid graph convolutional network that incorporates genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic data from cell lines and the chemical structures of drugs. DeepCDR is trained on the CCLE multi-omics data and externally validated using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Similarly, Precision Medicine Prediction using an Adversarial Network for Cancer Drug Response (PANCDR) [6] is an adversarial network that uses gene expression data of samples from GDSC and TCGA to reduce the gap between preclinical and clinical data in the CDR prediction task. More recent models further extend this paradigm by exploring different architectural designs. PASO [7] integrated pathway-based difference features with Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) using a transformer-based encoder to capture complex interactions between omics and chemical structures. In addition, Multi-Modal Drug Response Predictor (MMDRP) [8] is a multi-modal deep learning framework designed to address the data scarcity and skewness often observed in pharmacogenomic datasets. Also, Graph Neural Network with multi-task learning for Drug Response Prediction (GNNDRP) [9] utilized graph neural networks combined with self-supervised learning tasks to improve drug molecular representations from response networks. While achieving robust performance in predicting intrinsic sensitivity through architectural advancements, these models predominantly rely on static molecular profiles of cancer cells and chemical features, limiting their ability to capture the dynamic transcriptomic changes essential for inferring the drug's mechanism of action (MoA). Understanding the MoA of compounds is important for increasing the success rate of drug development by identifying toxicity or potential side effects before the clinical trial stage [10]. However, previous CDR prediction models that use gene expression, mutations, or target information lack sufficient information within the data to suggest mechanistic hypotheses regarding drug responses.

This limitation can be addressed by utilizing differential gene expression to predict the CDR [11]. Since cancer drugs induce transcriptomic changes by targeting proteins and affecting gene interaction networks, differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the changes in gene expression before and after treatment, can offer valuable insights into how drugs induce cell death [12]. Although basal gene expression can provide important information regarding the basic cell state and function for CDR prediction, DEGs following drug treatment offer additional possibilities for understanding the MoA of drugs [13]. Furthermore, compounds with similar MoA tend to cause similar transcriptomic changes, enabling the prediction of the CDR and MoA using the DEGs induced by unknown compounds [14]. DEGs affected by various compounds are available from the library of integrated network-based cellular signatures (LINCS)-L1000 [15], wherein there are over three million gene expression profiles following pharmacological or genetic perturbation of cell lines. In recent years, numerous deep learning models have been introduced, but most rely on integrating chemical structure information and basal gene expression data to predict half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values. While IC50 serves as a standard metric representing the overall potency of a drug, it is a derived statistical value that abstracts away dose-dependent dynamics. In contrast, predicting cell viability at a specific concentration and time captures the state of cells under defined treatment conditions. Using the DEGs from LINCS-L1000 and cell viability data, Lu et al. proposed WRFEN-XGBoost [16], a model that predicts drug-induced cell viability. They adopted a weighted fusion algorithm based on random forest and elastic net to select key genes, which were used to train the XGBoost model. Although the model trained on the CTRP dataset achieved good performance on a randomly selected internal test set and was validated using external datasets such as CCLE and NCI-60, it lacks interpretability for revealing biomedical information on the MoA of drugs.

To overcome the lack of interpretability in models, explainable AI techniques can be applied for CDR prediction tasks to discover biomedical knowledge regarding the MoA of drugs. Explainable AI techniques, such as local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) [17], Deep Learning Important FeaTures (DeepLIFT) [18], and layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) [19] calculate the influence of features or nodes in models on predictions. By introducing these explainable AI techniques with prior biological knowledge into neural network models, it is possible to build self-explainable models that provide human-understandable explanations for their predictions as an intrinsic part of their operation [20]. In this context, prior biological knowledge data refers to the existing information and understanding of biological systems, processes, and interactions. Using the previously published popular biological prior knowledge such as Gene Ontology (GO) [21], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [22], Reactome [23], Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) [24], and self-explainable multi-layer perceptron (MLP) models mimicking these architectures of knowledge have been proposed and are widely used. For example, DCell [25] introduced the concept of Visible Neural Networks (VNNs), which explicitly maps the neural network architecture to the hierarchical structure of the GO to predict cell growth phenotypes from genotypes. Building upon this interpretable framework, DrugCell [26] extended the VNNs approach to predict drug responses. It employs a dual-branch architecture that combines VNNs for modeling cellular genotypes with a separate neural network branch for processing drug molecular fingerprints. Similarly, Deep GONet [20] is a self-explainable, fully connected neural network that uses gene expression data to predict cancer phenotypes. Deep GONet is based on an MLP that integrates GO data into the hierarchical architecture of a neural network, introducing them to the adaptive regularization term. Deep GONet also utilizes LRP to calculate the importance of nodes. P-Net [27] predicts cancer state in patients with prostate cancer based on their genomic profiles using a deep learning model that incorporates prior hierarchical biological knowledge from the Reactome pathway dataset. P-Net employs DeepLIFT to compute the importance scores and understand the decisions of the model. These models demonstrate how they operate and make decisions, and the biological interpretation of the results helps researchers understand the disease. Therefore, it is necessary to build a self-explainable model for drug response prediction and to understand how drugs affect a specific disease.

In this study, we propose a deep neural network named DR.DEGMON (Drug Response prediction using Differentially Expressed Genes with Multi-layer perceptron integrating gene Ontology Network). This self-explainable deep neural network predicts the viability of pan-cancer cell lines based on drug-induced DEGs data collected from CTRP and LINCS-L1000. Our model incorporates prior biological knowledge from GO data and introduces a term-to-term hierarchy in the context of biology into the hierarchical structure of the MLP. We used external datasets, including GDSC, PRISM, and CCLE, to evaluate the performance of the model. Moreover, key genes and GO terms for specific cancer cell lines and drugs were identified by calculating the relevance score of each input feature and node in the neural network using LRP.

Methods

Dataset

To train a deep learning model to predict cell viability using data on DEGs, we integrated the LINCS-L1000 and CTRP datasets. We collected DEGs from LINCS-L1000 level 5 data (24-h treatments only) and information on the viability of cell lines treated with drugs from CTRP. Cell viability ranged from 0 to 1.442, with a lower value indicating a lower survival rate and better drug response. We attempted to match samples from both datasets based on drugs, cell lines, treatment hours, and dosages. However, it was difficult to match the samples with the dosages in an exact manner. Therefore, as shown in Eq. (1), we considered samples with the same cell line and drug from each dataset as the same when the absolute difference between log10(CTRP dose) and log10(LINCS-L1000 dose) was less than or equal to 0.2 [16]. Finally, 40,777 samples covering 91 cell lines and 407 drugs were obtained; the dataset was randomly split into 80% for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing.

In addition, to evaluate the model using external validation datasets, we matched the LINCS-L1000 dataset with other drug response datasets, such as GDSC, PRISM, and CCLE, as described above. For additional case studies involving shRNA, we matched shRNA-based genetically perturbed DEGs from LINCS-L1000 with RNA interference log fold-change data from DepMap [28]. We utilized the GO biological process data (GO-BP; October 2019), which consists of 19 levels, to structure the MLP. In this hierarchy, lower numerical GO levels correspond to more general biological processes, whereas higher numerical levels represent increasingly specific functional concepts. Incorporating the entire GO hierarchy is inefficient because the lowest levels contain highly specific terms with sparse gene associations, while the highest levels are too general to capture specific drug responses. Therefore, GO levels were treated as a structural hyperparameter. We evaluated various level combinations as detailed in the hyperparameter search space (Additional file 1 Table S1). Based on the model performance, we selected GO terms corresponding to levels 7, 6, 5, and 4 where the number of GO terms at each selected level directly determined the number of nodes in the corresponding hidden layer. Consequently, the model connects input genes to the more specific terms at level 7, which then feed into progressively more general terms up to level 4. Finally, 12,014 genes that were common to the LINCS-L1000 data and GO were used as inputs to the model.

Model architecture

Our MLP model consists of four fully connected layers, which takes 12,014-dimensional DEGs levels as the input (Fig. 1). We used L2 regularization in the loss function to apply the hierarchical structure of GO to the model, which is also used in Deep GONet [20]. In this context, connections between genes and GO terms, or between GO terms in GO, are referred to as GO connections; otherwise no-GO connections. The weights of the no-GO connections result in penalties in L2 regularization. Given the incompleteness of prior biological knowledge, we penalized the no-GO connections rather than removing them entirely. This approach ensures that the model considers all connections, thereby avoiding the loss of potentially valuable information regarding unknown gene functions.

Overview of DR.DEGMON. The input of DR.DEGMON is the corresponding DEGs data from LINCS-L1000 with cell viability data from CTRP. The structure of DR.DEGMON incorporates prior biological knowledge from GO, where each node represents a GO term, and the edges contain information on biological connections between genes and GO terms or between GO terms themselves. After model training, the relevance score of each node for each sample is calculated using LRP on the trained model. A higher relevance score represents a higher contribution to the prediction. Model interpretation

To interpret the trained model easily, we retrained it for binary classification. Cell viability values were binarized based on 0.8 [10]. Although the overall structure of the model remains the same, the binarized model uses a binary cross-entropy loss function and a sigmoid function for the output layer.

To interpret the results of our model, we used LRP, which is a backpropagation-based approach that calculates relevance scores for input features and nodes within the layers for each sample. These scores indicated the contribution of each element to the prediction of the model. Results

Model evaluation and comparison

Our comprehensive literature review indicates that WRFEN-XGBoost is currently the only existing study that predicts cell viability solely from drug-induced DEGs, excluding chemical structural inputs. Therefore, to ensure a rigorous evaluation, we compared DR.DEGMON against WRFEN-XGBoost as well as four standard machine learning models: random forest, PCA-SVR, k-nearest neighbor, and elastic net. The hyperparameters of the baseline models are listed in Additional file 1 Table S3. For models with random initialization, we trained the models three times, determined the average performance and standard deviation of the results, and reported the average training time. To evaluate the performance of the model, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), coefficient of determination (R2), Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC), and root mean squared error (RMSE) between the predicted and actual cell viability values. As shown in Table 1, DR.DEGMON consistently demonstrates the highest performance in all measurements, achieving the PCC of 0.8568, R2 of 0.7332, SCC of 0.6462, and RMSE of 0.1496. Compared with the results obtained using WRFEN-XGBoost, DR.DEGMON showed a 3.35% increase in the PCC, 6.96% increase in R2, 6.74% increase in the SCC, and 7.88% decrease in RMSE. Furthermore, compared with random forest and WRFEN-XGBoost, DR.DEGMON offers substantial time advantages. It took 3 min and 50 s to train DR.DEGMON, which was 62-fold faster than WRFEN-XGBoost, which required 240 min. Additional results obtained using DR.DEGMON for binary classification are shown in Additional file 1 Table S4. Overall, the results show that DR.DEGMON predicted cell viability in response to a specific dosage based on DEGs alone and outperformed the baseline models.

Evaluation on external datasets

To evaluate the generalizability and scalability of DR.DEGMON trained on the CTRP dataset, we conducted external evaluations using independent datasets: GDSC, PRISM, and CCLE. Each dataset was selected to serve a distinct evaluation purpose. GDSC, which primarily consists of anti-cancer drugs similar to CTRP, was used to assess predictive performance within the oncology domain. In contrast, PRISM, a large-scale repurposing dataset comprising a diverse collection of FDA-approved drugs, was utilized to evaluate the model’s generalization to real-world drug discovery scenarios. All datasets were processed using the same preprocessing procedure described in the Methods section to ensure compatibility with the LINCS-L1000 dataset. For the GDSC dataset with 74 cell lines and 260 drugs, our model achieved a PCC of 0.6520 for all GDSC samples, with 0.6822 obtained for samples of the observed cell lines and drugs. For samples of unseen cell lines or drugs, the PCC values were 0.6737 and 0.6029, respectively, supporting the model's ability to generalize to previously unobserved cancer cell lines. For PRISM, which included 52,487 samples of 70 cell lines and 1,197 drugs, the overall PCC was 0.4865. Although this performance was lower than that observed for GDSC, likely reflecting the higher chemical and functional heterogeneity of compounds in PRISM, DR.DEGMON exhibited relatively robust predictive performance on unseen drugs compared to the overall average and seen drugs. Given that PRISM includes a substantial proportion of non-oncology and repurposing drugs, this result suggests that DR.DEGMON is less dependent on compound-specific patterns encountered during training. Instead, by leveraging DEGs, the model captures transferable biological responses, enabling it to generalize effectively to diverse and novel compounds. Interpretation of the Results

Example 1: Gefitinib treatment of A549 cell line sample

In this section, we describe the interpretation of the results obtained with DR.DEGMON using LRP (Fig. 3). We calculated the contribution of each node in each layer to the output of each sample. Note that for the evaluation, we retrained the model for binary classification because we aimed to interpret the samples in which drugs were sensitive to cell lines with viability values close to zero. Thus, we binarized the cell viability values by assigning a label of 1 to drug-sensitive samples. We selected a test set sample of gefitinib, an EGFR inhibitor, as an example to interpret the outcomes obtained using DR.DEGMON. Gefitinib is among the most well-known medicines used for cancer treatment, and numerous studies have been conducted to determine its molecular MoA. Therefore, the results for gefitinib from DR.DEGMON act as the best example for comparing published and predicted data. For gefitinib, five samples were collected from five different cell lines in the test set. We observed the top 10 nodes from layers 1 and 2 and the input layer based on their LRP scores. Among them, we focused on the A549 lung cancer cell line because gefitinib is approved by the FDA as a first-line treatment for non-small cell lung cancer.

Bar plots illustrating the top 10 LRP score input genes and nodes for the A549-gefitinib sample in the CTRP test dataset. (a) and (b) Top 10 scoring nodes representing GO terms for layers 1 and 2 of the model, respectively. c Top 10 scoring genes among the inputs, with their DEGs values represented by different colors. Colors closer to blue indicate lower DEGs values, whereas those closer to red indicate higher average DEGs values

Previous studies showed that gefitinib regulates the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways [30]. As expected, most outcomes from DR.DEGMON were highly correlated with these signaling pathways. Several interesting outcomes were identified from the results of layer 1. For example, the folic acid response (GO: 0051593) and folic acid biosynthesis process (GO: 0009396) showed high LRP scores, which were closely correlated with the JAK/STAT signaling pathway [31]. According to previous studies, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway upregulates folate receptor expression. Thus, gefitinib can downregulate folate receptor levels, resulting in an increase in folic acid biosynthesis and response to folic acid. Additionally, we observed negative regulation of macrophage activation (GO: 0043031) and positive regulation of interleukin-1β production (GO: 0032731). Gefitinib causes mitochondrial damage, resulting in reactive oxygen species generation, which stimulates the maturation of interleukin-1β. Thus, macrophages are activated by matured interleukin-1β [32].

We also identified some cell death-related GO terms, such as protein deglycosylation (GO: 0006517), regulation of mitochondrial membrane permeability (GO: 1902445), and exit from mitosis (GO: 0010458). As expected, these GO terms showed high LRP scores because gefitinib ultimately led to cancer cell death. The results of previous studies support these cell death-related signals, including changes in glycosylation levels [33], alterations in mitochondrial membrane permeability [34], and inhibition of mitosis [35], following gefitinib treatment. Other top 10 GO terms, such as pyridoxal phosphate catabolic process (GO: 0032361), phylloquinone catabolic process (GO: 0042376), and negative regulation of leucine-tRNA ligase activity (GO: 1903634), were highly correlated with metabolism. Although no studies have directly demonstrated metabolic changes after gefitinib treatment, gefitinib may alter the mTOR signaling pathway, leading to changes in cancer cell metabolism [36].

The results from layer 2 also provide interesting GO terms, although they were not as strong as those from layer 1. Particularly, the protein repair GO term (GO: 0030091) showed the highest gefitinib-induced LRP score. Gefitinib may cause cellular stress that triggers a protein repair system [37]. Additionally, T-tubule organization (GO: 0033292) showed a high LRP score, which may correlate with changes in tubule length after gefitinib treatment [38]. Furthermore, similar to the results from layer 1, the apoptotic process GO term (GO: 0097473) in retinal rod cells showed a high LRP score, suggesting that gefitinib affects retinal rod cell death, although there is no clear evidence to support this prediction.

Another interesting GO term was the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) overload response (GO: 0006983). ER trafficking plays an important role in EGFR transport [39]. After gefitinib treatment, cancer cells may attempt to transport more EGFR because they lack functional EGFR, potentially causing ER trafficking overload. Some GO terms that have not been thoroughly studied, such as postsynaptic density organization (GO: 0097106), negative regulation of neural precursor cell proliferation (GO: 2000178), and postsynapse to nucleus signaling pathway (GO: 0099527), showed high LRP scores. To date, the effects of gefitinib on the neural system, including synaptic and postsynaptic processes, have not been examined, representing an unexplored area, as suggested by DR.DEGMON.

In contrast to the results from layers 1 and 2, most genes from the top 10 input nodes lacked clear evidence of a correlation with the EGFR signaling pathway after gefitinib treatment. This is because the results from the top 10 input nodes highlighted specific gene names rather than GO terms. However, DR.DEGMON predicted some genes of interest. Specifically, AMIGO2 had the highest LRP score and was predicted to negatively correlate with gefitinib treatment. Although AMIGO2 and EGFR were weakly connected, the EGFR downstream signaling pathway may be associated with AMIGO2 expression, as AMIGO2 regulates the Akt signaling pathway [40]. Additionally, several reports suggested that the PCNA and MSN genes are correlated with the EGFR signaling pathway. Studies have indicated that phospho-PCNA is regulated by the EGFR signaling pathway [41] and that MSN is highly correlated with the Wnt signaling pathway [42]. Another report supported a close link between the Wnt and EGFR signaling pathways [43]. Collectively, these genes are likely affected by gefitinib treatment. Thus, they may represent potential new genes affected by EGFR inhibitors in A549 cells. Additional results for vorinostat treatment in the MCF7 cell line are shown in Additional file 1 Figure S2.

Example 2: EGFR Knockdown in A549 cell line sample

We conducted additional interpretation using shRNA data to extend our study beyond compound-perturbed data to genetically perturbed data. We matched the DEGs data from LINCS-L1000 with shRNA data from DepMap. For model analysis, we utilized the log-transformed fold-change scores of the effects by comparing the pre- and post-shRNA treatment data. For each correctly predicted sample, we interpreted the results using LRP based on the top five nodes in the first layer of the model (Fig. 4). This experiment was performed using a sample of shRNA targeting EGFR in the A549 cell line, similar to our previous experiment with gefitinib treatment of the A549 cell line.

Bar plots illustrating the top 5 layer-wise relevance propagation score nodes for the A549-EGFR knockdown sample in the shRNA dataset

The results from layer 1 highlight several compelling outcomes. Slow endocytic recycling (GO: 0032458) was the highest-scoring GO term. EGFR plays an important role in the cell endocytic pathway, in which internalization of EGFR is followed by endocytosis [44]. During this process, a subset of EGFR is recycled back to the cell surface via endocytic recycling [45]. Actin filaments are crucial components of the cytoskeleton for actin filament branching (GO: 0090135). They are involved in cell shape maintenance, movement, and division. EGFR signaling can influence cytoskeletal reorganization [46], which may affect dynamic changes in actin filaments, such as branching. The last compelling GO term was positive regulation of mitochondrial organization (GO: 0010822). EGFR signaling can influence cell survival and metabolism, potentially affecting mitochondrial organization and function [47]. Additional results for MYD88 shRNA knockdown in A549 cells are shown in Additional file 1 Figure S3. These results demonstrated that the interpretability of our model is not limited to drug-induced perturbations and is applicable to DEGs data generated by other perturbation techniques. Our approach suggested key genes or GO terms, providing insights into the mechanisms of genes in cancer cell lines.

Discussion

While the comparison with WRFEN-XGBoost, which is the only existing study sharing our specific modality of predicting viability solely from drug-induced DEGs, confirmed the efficacy of our model architecture, to further validate the superior predictive ability of using DEGs across diverse model architectures, we extended this comparative assessment to the four machine learning baselines including PCA-SVR, KNN, random forest, and elastic net using the same CTRP dataset framework presented in Table 1. We benchmarked the models trained on drug-induced DEGs against those trained on standard static inputs, comprising averaged L1000 level 3 DMSO-treated gene expression profiles, which represent the basal cell state, drug ECFP fingerprints, and dosage. Consistently, the DEG-based approach yielded superior performance across all models compared to the static feature set, which achieved PCCs of 0.5479 in PCA-SVR, 0.7520 in KNN, 0.7972 in random forest, and 0.7673 in elastic net.

Most recent state-of-the-art models utilize multi-modal inputs that combine pre-treatment omics data with drug chemical features to primarily predict IC50 values. In contrast, DR.DEGMON is designed to operate solely on drug-induced DEGs, focusing on the dynamic transcriptomic changes induced by the treatment to predict cell viability at a specific dosage and time point. Although this fundamental difference in inputs and prediction targets poses a challenge for direct comparison with the state-of-the-art models, we selected PANCDR as a representative baseline to demonstrate the superior informational value of DEGs data over static basal gene expression features. For a fair comparison, we slightly modified the PANCDR architecture and adapted its training and evaluation framework to align with the cell viability prediction task. We standardized the data to the 24-h treatment time point and integrated dosage information into PANCDR's input features to enable concentration-specific predictions. To ensure robustness, we trained and evaluated both models on the GDSC dataset, performing 10 independent trainings with random initialization. Under these conditions, DR.DEGMON achieved a PCC of 0.7470 (standard deviation = 0.0079), significantly outperforming PANCDR, which achieved a PCC of 0.6435 (standard deviation = 0.0289) with a p-value of 3.81e-7.

These results indicate that DEG profiles provide richer, context-specific information regarding drug efficacy than the combination of basal expression and chemical structures. Furthermore, unlike basal expression models that infer MoA indirectly, the use of DEGs allows DR.DEGMON to directly pinpoint the biological processes driven by drug treatment. Specifically, whereas PANCDR relies on SMILES information combined with basal gene expression, DR.DEGMON uses only drug-induced DEGs as input yet achieved superior performance. This confirms that dynamic transcriptomic data are more informative for the CDR task than static cell line features and drug structural information.

The results provided by our model are interpretable and can provide biological insights through LRP. We transformed our model into a classification model to apply the LRP. LRP is a backpropagation-based interpretation technique that calculates relevance scores based on output values. Given that low cell viability indicates high sensitivity, we focused on nodes with low LRP scores for interpretation, as they contribute to minimizing the output values and are thus considered significant in the regression model. Therefore, we retrained our model for binary classification, where the output was the probability of sensitivity. Moreover, we employed LRP-ϵ, which adds an epsilon term to the denominator of the standard LRP equation to reduce noise. Several LRP rules are available, such as the z-rule, γ-rule, and αβ-rule, and different rules may return different results.

We analyzed A549 cell line samples treated with gefitinib and identified genes and GO terms related to the cell lines and drugs showing the highest LRP scores. Additionally, by interpreting the shRNA datasets, we extended our study to data generated using other experimental techniques. However, the GO terms identified in LRP analysis should be further verified experimentally to confirm the MoA of the drugs. In this context, if researchers identify a sensitive drug through screening but its MoA is unknown, DR.DEGMON can provide mechanistic clues to guide subsequent experimental validation.

Our study has several limitations. DR.DEGMON relies on GO-BP, last updated in October 2019. Consequently, the biological insights provided by the model are constrained by the state of knowledge at that time, potentially omitting recently discovered gene functions or pathway associations. Furthermore, the coverage of GO is inherently incomplete; while it offers a robust hierarchical structure, specific functional modules, gene–gene interactions, and complex signaling pathways relevant to drug response may not be fully represented compared to other curated resources. To mitigate this limitation, our study may be enhanced by incorporating other prior biological knowledge data, such as KEGG, Reactome, or STRING, and by integrating more drug response data. In addition, due to the limitations of the L1000 assay, which predicts 11,350 expression levels based on 978 landmark genes, errors may exist in the DEGs data. Even though there are several limitations, DR.DEGMON achieved enhanced performance in predicting cell viability using DEGs, which enabled the interpretation of results at the gene level and facilitated the inference of potential biological processes underlying the drug response.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed DR.DEGMON, a self-explainable deep learning model for predicting cell viability in response to drug treatments using only DEGs. DR.DEGMON incorporates prior biological knowledge by building an MLP structure based on GO data, and customizing the L2 regularization. Our model enhances prediction accuracy and provides interpretable insights into drug response-related biological processes. It outperformed existing baseline models in terms of both prediction accuracy and computational efficiency, as demonstrated by evaluations of the CTRP dataset. Through LRP, our model identified key genes and GO terms that strongly contributed to its predictions, offering biological insights into the potential MoA of drugs. The ability of DR.DEGMON to maintain stable performance in unseen cell lines and drugs highlights its robustness and potential for broader applications in cancer pharmacogenomics. Moreover, DR.DEGMON’s interpretability enables researchers to gain biomedical insights into drug development and personalized medicine. This approach bridges the gap between predictive AI models and biological interpretation, thereby serving as a valuable tool for elucidating potential mechanisms underlying drug activity and facilitating the discovery of new therapeutic targets.
