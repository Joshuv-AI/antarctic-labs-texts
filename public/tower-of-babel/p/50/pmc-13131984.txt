Artificial intelligence-enhanced electrocardiogram for the detection of rheumatic heart disease
Authors: Raeesa Bhorat, David M Harmon, Kan Liu, Jennifer Dugan, Christie Ngo, Paul A Friedman, Herman Bagula, Mpiko Ntsekhe, Zachi I Attia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains the most prevalent acquired cardiovascular disease among young adults in developing countries, and there is a critical need for an efficient, affordable, and available screening strategy to detect RHD in these resource-limited areas. The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is a ubiquitous, inexpensive diagnostic tool, and application of artificial intelligence to the ECG (AI-ECG) has allowed for screening of various cardiovascular pathologies.Methods and resultsPatients aged >18 years with severe RHD with at least one transthoracic echocardiogram and one standard 12-lead ECG performed at Groote Schuur Hospital, Cape Town, within 180 days of each other were eligible. A total of 920 RHD patients were identified, with 2199 corresponding controls from the same medical centre. Patients were allocated to derivation (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) groups, respectively. The AI-ECG to detect RHD from a standard 12-lead ECG was built using the Keras framework under Tensorflow backend. In the test group, the AI-ECG for RHD exhibited strong algorithm performance with an AUC of 0.890 (95% Confidence Interval 0.848–0.940) and an accuracy of 83.8%. The algorithm remained robust between RHD presentation: mitral valve alone (AUC 0.913) and mixed mitral and aortic valve disease (AUC 0.858). There were minor performance differences in the subgroup analysis of patient sex, age, and ECG characteristics.ConclusionIn this study, an AI-ECG algorithm was successfully developed to screen for advanced RHD from a standard 12-lead ECG, and the AI-ECG performance remained acceptable across most subgroups, including RHD manifestation, patient sex, age <70 years, and ECG characteristics.Keywords: rheumatic heart disease, Artificial intelligence, Electrocardiogram, Neural network

Introduction

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains the most prevalent acquired cardiovascular disease (CVD) among young adults in developing countries, carries a high mortality rate, and contributes significantly to CVD-related disability in endemic regions.1–3 Where available, valve procedures, such as surgical replacement and percutaneous trans-mitral commissurotomy, can significantly reduce the burden of RHD-related heart failure and mortality in patients with haemodynamically significant valve disease.3,4 In patients with both haemodynamically significant valve disease and an indication for anticoagulation (i.e. atrial fibrillation), vitamin K antagonists substantially reduced heart failure-related mortality, stroke, and systemic embolism compared to other oral anticoagulants in a recently completed large trial.5

While access to these therapeutic interventions remains a major barrier to care, even in countries where availability is not an issue, long delays to diagnosis and referral for treatment contribute significantly to poor outcomes. Additionally, there is a lack of ultrasound technology and echocardiographic expertize in high-RHD burden community health facilities, both of which are necessary for effective disease screening and diagnosis.6,7 While a recent focus has explored the impact of community screening for early, milder forms of RHD,8 there remains a critical need for an effective, affordable, easily available, and simple-to-use screening strategy to improve the diagnosis of advanced RHD for people living in disease-endemic regions.6,7

Although not currently on the World Health Organisation (WHO) Essential Diagnostics Test list for primary health care facilities, the electrocardiogram (ECG) is a simple, cost-effective, point-of-care test that can be added to the list, provided enough evidence of clinical utility is made available.9 In recent years, the application of artificial intelligence to a standard 12-lead ECG (AI-ECG) has facilitated the detection of cardiovascular pathology beyond what is able to be seen by manual ECG interpretation (i.e. left ventricular systolic dysfunction, non-rheumatic multi-valvular disease, and electrolyte abnormalities).10–13 Some of these algorithms have also demonstrated translatable utility for use on portable, handheld ECG devices.14–16

While these AI-ECG algorithms have shown promise in academic medical settings, there have only been limited, successful applications of this type of AI-ECG technology where RHD is endemic or in other resource-limited medical settings.12,17 In an effort to fill this knowledge gap, we describe the derivation, validation, and examination of an AI-ECG algorithm to diagnose advanced RHD utilising data from a patient population and community where this disease is endemic (Cape Town, South Africa).

Methods

The institutional review board at Mayo Clinic (Rochester, Minnesota, USA; IRB:18–008992) and at University of Cape Town’s Faculty of Health Sciences (Cape Town, South Africa; Human Research Ethics Committee: 306/2022) approved this minimally invasive, retrospective cohort study.

Patient data

We identified patients aged >18 years with a confirmed clinical and echocardiographic diagnosis of symptomatic, severe RHD between the years 2003 and 2022 who had at least one transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) and one, standard 12-lead ECG performed at Groote Schuur Hospital, Cape Town, within 180 days of each other, consistent with our prior AI-ECG algorithm development timeline for other progressive valve diseases.18 This ‘diseased’ cohort had confirmed RHD on TTE interpreted by cardiologists at Groote Schuur Hospital according to current imaging practice guidelines, and all of these RHD patients were scheduled for valve replacement surgery.19 In this RHD pre-surgical cohort, ECGs were routinely collected after the diagnosis of RHD had been established, and subsequent TTEs were obtained for surgical/interventional planning, with ECG-TTE pairs most frequently occurring within a few days of each other. Patients with prior cardiothoracic surgery, valve prostheses, incomplete TTE reports, or ECG-to-TTE times greater than 180 days were excluded.

A corresponding cohort of 2199 control patients aged >18 years with 12-lead ECGs and no RHD diagnosis were identified at the same academic medical center (Groote Schuur Hospital, Cape Town, South Africa) over the same time-period (2003–2022), yielding a disease to control ratio of 1:2.4. Control size was determined based on ECG/medical record availability from the Groote Schuur Hospital medical archives. There was limited comorbidity data available from the control cohort, as medical records during this time period were not digitized, requiring physical location and manual data extraction. While the majority of these records were inaccessible, the medical records of 100 consecutive control patients were located and manually reviewed to identify significant comorbidities (i.e. hypertension, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease) and to confirm the absence of RHD by TTE. The left ventricular function on echocardiogram was also reviewed for each of these consecutive control cases.

The final cohort was assigned through random sampling to training, validation and testing subsets 80%, 10% and 10%, respectively. None of the patients were assigned to more than one group (Figure 1). A small group of RHD patients (n = 64) were excluded due to unusable or incomplete ECG files.

Patient flow diagram.

All ECGs were acquired as digital standard 12-lead ECGs on a Marquette ECG machine and were stored using the MUSE database (GE Healthcare, Marquette, WI, USA) for later acquisition.

Artificial intelligence model

The convolutional neural network (CNN) model was built using the Keras Framework under Tensorflow backend (Google, Mountain View, CA, USA). The 12-lead ECGs were acquired at 500 Hz for 10 s, which contains 5000 data points for each lead; therefore, the input ECG shape for each patient is 5000 × 12 × 1. The CNN was built using a total of 8 stacked blocks of 2D convolutional layers, max pooling, batch normalisation and dropout layers, followed by two fully connected layers and a final output layer activated using a Softmax function to generate 2 outputs (negative and positive). To better fit the model’s shape manipulation, all the ECGs were zero-padded from 5000 × 12 × 1 to 5120 × 12 × 1 to maintain an even dimension after each pooling layer, dropout rate was used at 0.5, and 30 epochs were used for training the network, while saving the model weights for the epoch that achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC ROC) on the internal validation set. We trained our model on a GPU cluster with an Nvidia GeForce Tesla V100 graphics card. An optimal diagnostic threshold of 0.8, where sensitivity was equal to specificity, was selected to categorize ‘high likelihood’ of disease for results above that threshold. Please refer to our Supplemental Methods and Supplemental Figure for further details regarding network training and validation. In an effort to increase the transparency and reproducibility of our work, we have completed a European Heart Rhythm Association Artificial Intelligence (EHRA AI) checklist, which is included in our supplement (Supplemental Checklist).20

Outcome and statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the ability of the AI-ECG to accurately identify patients with advanced RHD. All presented statistical measures of performance are based on the testing data. Applying a threshold determined in the validation data indicating a positive screen, standard measures of diagnostic performance (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy) were computed. The AUC 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for all measures of diagnostic performance using the large sample approximation of the DeLong method. Further subgroup analysis was completed to assess the validity of the AI-ECG model on the basis of specific RHD manifestation (i.e. aortic valve disease only, mitral valve disease only, combined mitral and aortic valve disease) and patient characteristics, including age, sex, and ECG characteristics (i.e. QRS width and presence of PR interval). The focus of the subgroup analysis was to provide descriptive data on the performance of the algorithm over the range of patient profiles identified above. This subgroup analysis was intended to be descriptive; no formal hypothesis testing was configured to test for differences from the original area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. A small number of control patients did not have sex (n = 6) or age (n = 8) available from recorded ECG MUSE data and were not included in these respective subgroup analyses.

Manual ECG analysis

A total of 100 unlabeled ECGs (25 each from true positive, true negative, false negative, and false positive groups) from the test cohort were manually reviewed by a senior Cardiology Fellow (DMH) to identify potential arrhythmia patterns which may have impacted algorithm performance. ECGs were characterized as sinus rhythm (including normal sinus rhythm, sinus bradycardia and sinus tachycardia), atrial fibrillation/flutter, and junctional rhythm. Machine-derived ECG intervals were also compared between these groups (PR, QRS, QT).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 920 cases with confirmed RHD were identified, and 856 of these cases had valid ECGs and were included in the training, validation, and testing of the AI-ECG algorithm. The average age of the RHD cohort was 45 (+/−13) years old, and 72% (n = 671) of the cohort was female (Table 1). Nearly one quarter (24%) of these patients had hypertension, and 6.4% had diabetes mellitus. The control cohort was older (56 +/− 13 years) with a lower female population (45%; n = 674). From a limited, manual comorbidity review of consecutive control patients (n = 100), there was a higher prevalence of hypertension (37%) and a similar rate of diabetes mellitus (5%; Table 1). The average left ventricular ejection fraction by TTE was slightly lower in the RHD cohort (56.5% vs. 60%; Table 1).

Valve disease characteristics

In the RHD cohort, the majority (55.5%; n = 511) of patients had combined mitral valve and aortic valve disease. The remaining patients had isolated mitral valve disease (39%) and isolated aortic valve pathology (5.4%; Table 1). The vast majority of RHD patients had moderate to severe (>75%) manifestation of their respective valve pathologies, many of these patients exhibiting ‘mixed’ regurgitation and stenosis of the aortic (28%) and mitral (43%) valves due to the inflammatory nature of the disease (Table 2).

Algorithm performance

In the test group, the AI-ECG for RHD exhibited excellent algorithm performance with an AUC of 0.890 [95% Confidence Interval (CI) 0.848–0.940] and an accuracy of 83.8% (Figure 2A). The sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV of the algorithm were 82.6%, 85.3%, 92.0% 70.2%, respectively. When evaluating the algorithm performance by specific valve pathology, the AI-ECG demonstrated good performance for combined aortic and mitral valve pathology (AUC 0.858) as well as isolated mitral valve disease (AUC 0.913) and isolated aortic valve disease (AUC 0.877; Figure 2B and Table 3). While the AUC was high for isolated aortic valve disease, the number of RHD patients in this subgroup was very low (n = 2; Supplementary material online, Table S1). The algorithm performance for isolated aortic valve disease was likely reflective of a high negative predictive value; however, this subset size, with only 2 RHD patients, is insufficient for reliable statistical inference. It was interesting to note that while the majority of RHD cases exhibited mixed valve pathology (55.5%; Table 1), in the testing subset, isolated mitral valve disease was the primary manifestation of disease (58%; n = 50; Supplementary material online, Table S1).

Sex, age, ECG interval subgroup evaluation

We performed subgroup analysis on the basis of sex, age, and ECG characteristics to assess the AI-ECG algorithm for potential performance bias. The model exhibited robust performance in both male and female patients (AUC 0.895 and 0.89, respectively; Table 4). The AI-ECG demonstrated some variation in performance amongst age groups at 10-year intervals, with patients under age 40 years having the best performance (AUC 0.93), and patients aged ≥70 years demonstrated weaker performance (AUC 0.677; Table 4). Amongst patients with QRS intervals less than 120 ms, the algorithm demonstrated excellent performance (AUC 0.895), which declined with widening of the QRS interval (AUG 0.836 for QRS > 120 ms; Table 4). There was only a small decline in performance for patients with a documented PR interval on ECG (AUC 0.867) vs. those without a PR interval (AUC 0.854; Table 4), as these individuals were most likely in a non-sinus rhythm (i.e. atrial fibrillation or junctional rhythm).

In the true positive, false positive, and false negative subgroups, the underlying rhythm characteristics (sinus rhythm vs. atrial fibrillation/flutter) were similar. Heart rate and ECG interval characteristics (QRS, QT, QTc) were also similar between these subgroups, with false positives exhibiting slightly longer QT intervals in comparison. The true negative cohort had less atrial fibrillation and longer QRS and QT intervals compared to the other three subgroups with a similar heart rate (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we successfully derived and tested an AI-ECG algorithm for the screening of advanced RHD. We were able to train this model in a patient population where RHD is endemic (Cape Town, South Africa) with heterogeneous valve involvement, and, as anticipated, the AI-ECG exhibited robust performance with an overall AUC ≥0.89 across a wide array of advanced disease presentations. Although overall performance was strong, it is notable that the ability of the algorithm to rule out disease (NPV >90%) was much better than its ability to rule in disease (PPV 70%). In subgroup analysis, the algorithm maintained its acceptable performance with only mild variability between patient differences in disease manifestation (isolated mitral valve and mixed valve disease), sex, age less than 70 years, and ECG characteristics, highlighting the potential use of the algorithm as an accessible screening tool for individuals receiving community health care in RHD endemic countries where the limited capacity for diagnosis of advanced cardiac disease carries significant implications.

As the use of AI-ECG algorithms to detect cardiovascular pathologies has increased exponentially in recent years, multiple groups, including our own, have successfully validated algorithms to detect various valve pathologies, including regurgitant and stenotic lesions of the aortic and mitral valves.12,18,21 While these previous works may have similarities to this study, no group to date has specifically derived an algorithm with RHD as the diagnostic target. Notably, most of these groups had a small population of RHD or of mitral valve stenosis, and algorithm validity in this disease subset (mitral stenosis) was either not analysed at all or was validated on a small disease population.12,18 The natural history and presentation of RHD are quite different from those of other, non-inflammatory valvular pathologies evaluated by these groups, and we anticipate that our AI-ECG algorithm is observing distinct ECG characteristics in determining an advanced RHD diagnosis. Our algorithm demonstrated similarly strong performance characteristics compared to these analogous AI-ECGs for valve disease, highlighting the novelty and robustness of our work to accurately detect this unique, non-uniform, complex pathology in a younger, at-risk population.

In the subgroup analysis of our work, we were able to demonstrate the largely acceptable performance of our algorithm between sex and age groups. Male and female patients exhibited minimal difference in algorithm performance (AUC 0.895 vs. 0.89; Table 4). The algorithm performance was strongest in the youngest patient subgroup (< 40 years; AUC = 0.93), likely representative of the higher disease prevalence in these younger patients. The algorithm performance was also acceptable amongst other age groups 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and 60–69 of age (AUC 0.875, 0.853, 0.872, respectively), with a notable performance drop in patients ≥70 years (AUC 0.677) likely representative of the low prevalence of disease in this population (i.e. only 4 cases ≥70 years in the test cohort; Supplementary material online, Table S2). The robust performance in these younger patients, who carry the highest burden of disease, will be crucial to identify, and they may benefit from expedited referral for appropriate RHD care, distinguishing them from individuals with alternative diagnoses.

As this disease is largely prevalent in a younger population, it would be worthwhile to further validate this algorithm on a younger patient population (18–40), as this group was quite small in our present study (n = 34 cases in the test cohort; Supplementary material online, Table S2), with no paediatric patient representation. While we saw a drop in performance for patients greater than age 70 years, there was only a very small population of RHD cases in these testing subgroups, which may have impacted the algorithm performance (see Supplementary material online, Table S2). This variability in AI-ECG algorithm performance has been documented in other similar studies when there was a mismatch between test group age and the age of patients in the original training environment.22,23

The ECG characteristics sub-analysis was fascinating, as our algorithm demonstrated the strongest performance for patients with a normal QRS interval (less than 120 ms) and with a documented PR interval (indicating sinus rhythm). This suggests that our AI-ECG for RHD can distinguish the subtle differences between normal sinus rhythm for patients with RHD and those without disease. In the manual ECG analysis, this hypothesis was confirmed as true positive, false negative, and false positive patients had similar ECG characteristics (Table 5). While the algorithm appears to perform best in ruling-out disease for patients in sinus rhythm on manual ECG evaluation, there does not appear to be any clear ECG characteristic biases between these other (TP, FP, FN) subgroups. Unsurprisingly, the AI-ECG experiences a performance drop with ECG characteristics of a wide QRS (>120 ms), as oftentimes bundle branch blockade can result in unreliable AI-ECG analysis, even in patients prone to conduction system disease (i.e. cardiac amyloidosis).23 While a similar performance decline was observed in patients without a documented PR interval, this was to a lesser extent, with a still acceptable AUC of 0.854.

Multiple efforts have been made over recent years to effectively utilize AI-enhanced diagnostic tools for screening purposes in resource-limited populations.15,17 However, many of these AI-enhanced tools were not developed within these at-risk populations, and, as a result, the performance of these algorithms may vary with this use due to unintended population bias.15,24 In this present study, we were fortunate to train, validate, and test this AI-ECG in a population of interest where RHD is endemic. While this is a crucial first step prior to large-scale screening, external validation will remain an absolute necessity to ensure performance accuracy across other populations and geographies where RHD is endemic. Looking forward, we aim to complete both external and prospective validation efforts in order to optimize algorithm use for advanced RHD disease screening in these endemic populations.

Limitations

Our work is best understood in the context of its limitations. The majority of participants in the study had symptomatic, severe/end-stage disease, as ECGs were collected from patients in the pre-surgical setting. Therefore, by design, the algorithm’s performance in patients with mild to moderate disease and asymptomatic severe disease remains unknown. Specifically, the results from this study should not be extrapolated to employ this algorithm as a screening tool in community programs aimed at early detection for antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent disease progression.25 The RHD population in this study had a mean age of 45, and there was only a limited number of young (<40 years of age) test subjects. Regardless of the high performance in this group (AUC 0.93), future studies aimed at algorithm optimisation would require further validation and recalibration in younger patient populations (< 40 years of age, including paediatric patients < 18 years of age) from geographically diverse regions where the burden of advanced forms of the disease is highest.26

The inability to obtain complete data on comorbidities in the control cohort for comparison with the RHD cohort was an additional study limitation. Control participants consisted of a diverse population of hospitalized patients with no known RHD. However, as these medical records were non-digitised (physical paper charts), we were only able to locate and manually verify comorbidities and confirm the absence of RHD by TTE in a sample of 100 participants, and not the entire control cohort. As such, there may have been crossovers of RHD patients within the control group, which may have impacted algorithm performance. Similarly, during the limited control cohort echocardiography review, no significant valvular disease was identified; however, we did not exclude or plan to exclude patients with significant non-RHD valve pathology. While there is a possibility that both RHD and non-RHD valve disease patients were included in the control data, it is impossible to conclude whether the AI-ECG algorithm identified the specific inflammatory substrate of RHD vs. a more generalized volume overload/hypertrophy common to other valve pathologies from this data, though we suspect the former.

This model was trained and tested on a patient cohort from a single academic centre, and the results from this study may not be widely applicable. On a similar note, the disease to control population of our study was at a ratio of 1:2.4 due to medical record/ECG availability, with a small testing subset (n = 86). While this ratio and testing number are sufficient for initial model development, it would be beneficial to assess model performance in a larger population, with an adequate number of test subjects, with a disease prevalence more representative of the intended population (i.e. closer to 1:50–100). Finally, our AI-ECG algorithm does not offer full explainability in its decision-making as described above. While not evaluated in this study, a model with more transparent results (i.e. explainable AI) is an area of future study.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed an AI-ECG algorithm to accurately screen for advanced RHD from a standard 12-lead ECG. This algorithm was developed in a population where RHD is endemic, and the AI-ECG performance remained acceptable across most subgroups, including varied disease presentation (isolated mitral valve and mixed valve disease), age at presentation (under 70 years), patient sex, and ECG characteristics. This low-cost, point-of-care, AI-enhanced clinical tool may offer a solution for widespread, accessible screening for this prevalent, highly morbid cardiac disease, which remains endemic in much of the developing world.

Requests for patient-related data not included in the paper will not be considered. Any data and materials that can be shared will be released via a Material Transfer Agreement.
