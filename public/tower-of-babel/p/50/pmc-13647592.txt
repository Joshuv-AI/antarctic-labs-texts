Effects of core strength training on athletic performance in handball players: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fulin Hang, Lihe Chen, Jia Shen, Ruoyang Li, Yang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA Statement (registration number: CRD420261366106) to systematically evaluate the effects of core strength training (CST) on the competitive performance of handball players and to provide evidence-based references for the scientific implementation of sport-specific core strength training in handball. Literature searching and screening were performed independently by two researchers. Eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, and their methodological quality was assessed using the PEDro scale. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4 software. A total of 8 RCTs involving 226 handball players were ultimately included. The results indicated that, based on currently available limited evidence, CST combined with conventional training significantly improved players’ overall throwing ability compared with conventional handball training alone (mean difference [MD] = 3.45, 95% confidence interval [CI] [2.50, 4.40], P < 0.00001). Specifically, statistically significant improvements were observed in standing throw and running throw, whereas no significant difference was found in jump throw. CST combined with conventional training also significantly enhanced athletes’ jumping ability (MD = 3.76, 95% CI [1.58, 5.94], P = 0.0007); however, no significant intervention effect on linear sprint performance was detected (P = 0.34). Findings from the included studies suggest that training duration and frequency may be associated with intervention outcomes, and an 8-week intervention cycle may yield measurable improvements in sport-specific performance.ConclusionBased on current research evidence, CST can improve the competitive performance of handball players across multiple dimensions. It is recommended that sport-specific core training, delivered 2–3 times per week for a minimum duration of 8 weeks, be progressively integrated into routine handball training programs in accordance with the specific demands of handball.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01847-x.Keywords: Handball, Core strength training, Sports performance, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Core strength training (CST) was initially applied in the field of sports rehabilitation. Leveraging its prominent value in rehabilitation interventions and its proven practical effectiveness, CST has gradually been introduced and adopted in competitive sports. Currently, CST has been widely integrated into the training systems of various competitive sports disciplines [1–5]. The sports science community has established a standardized definition of the human core: the core refers to the entire trunk region below the shoulder joints, above the hip joints, and encompassing the pelvis, including the associated muscle groups of the lower back, abdomen, and pelvis. Anatomically, the core muscles form a box-shaped functional unit, with the abdominal muscles constituting the anterior aspect, the lower back and gluteal muscles forming the posterior aspect, the diaphragm serving as the superior boundary, and the pelvic floor and gluteal muscles providing inferior support. From a motor function perspective, the core muscles occupy a central position in the human kinetic chain, serving as the key structure responsible for maintaining spinal and pelvic stability. They provide critical proximal stability for distal limb movements during both daily activities and athletic performance.

Handball is a team competitive sport that integrates speed, explosive power, high-intensity physical confrontation, and specialized technical skills [6–8]. It is characterized by rapid transitions between offense and defense, as well as complex and diverse movement patterns. Competitive performance in handball is highly dependent on coherent and efficient force generation throughout the whole-body kinetic chain, as well as precise neuromuscular control of compound technical movements. During competition, sound trunk postural control and superior dynamic and static balance can effectively reduce energy dissipation during force transmission, thereby optimizing movement quality and mechanical efficiency. The development and maintenance of these core capacities are fundamentally supported by the stabilizing function of the core musculature [9–11].For instance, in technical actions such as standing throws and running throws, the complete force production sequence involves lower limb push-off and loading, trunk rotational compensation, and upper limb whipping movements, all of which heavily rely on trunk muscle coordination and dynamic balance. Directional dribbling under high-intensity physical confrontation requires the core muscles to limit excessive trunk sway, stabilize the center of mass, and thereby facilitate rapid deceleration and directional changes. Similarly, jump shots and defensive blocking movements demand substantial core muscle engagement. During the airborne phase of jump shots, without ground support, coordinated core muscle activation is essential for postural regulation. In defensive blocking, the core musculature absorbs external physical impacts, resists perturbations, maintains bodily stability, and ensures consistent execution of both offensive and defensive movements.

CST has been shown to effectively improve athletes’ athletic performance and is applicable across a wide range of sports disciplines. However, the theoretical framework of CST within the field of handball remains underdeveloped. Existing review studies are generally characterized by conflicting evidence and notable methodological limitations, highlighting the urgent need to supplement and refine relevant research in accordance with the sport-specific characteristics and practical competition demands of handball. Based on the aforementioned research gaps, this study systematically collates and synthesizes current experimental findings on the effects of CST interventions on the athletic performance of handball players, and conducts a comprehensive theoretical analysis. This study aims to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which CST influences the athletic performance of handball players, thereby establishing a solid theoretical foundation for the evidence-based implementation of core strength training within handball-specific training systems.

Methods

Compliance with standards

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement [12], and was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under registration number CRD420261366106.

Retrieval strategy

Literature searching and screening were performed independently by two researchers. The following databases were searched: PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCOhost, Elsevier, Cochrane Library, and Scopus, with supplementary searches conducted via Baidu Scholar and Google Scholar. Search terms were derived from reviews of prior literature, encompassing synonyms and related terms for core strength training and handball. In addition to the primary database search, a secondary search was performed during the literature screening process. A total of 207 articles were identified, including one article retrieved from Baidu Scholar. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to April 12, 2026. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were established in accordance with the PICOS framework [13]: (1) Participants: healthy handball athletes with no restrictions on nationality, ethnicity, sex, age, or competitive level; (2) Interventions: the experimental group received core strength training as the primary intervention; (3) Comparisons: the control group participated in routine handball training only; (4) Outcomes: outcome measures were required to include at least one athletic performance indicator, such as throwing velocity, sprint speed, or agility; (5) Study design: only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were eligible for inclusion, irrespective of allocation concealment or blinding procedures.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) duplicate publications or studies with unavailable full texts; (2) studies lacking the required outcome indicators; (3) review articles or meta-analyses; (4) non-controlled trials; (5) animal experiments; (6) letters to the editor, invited commentaries, or errata (Fig. 1).

Literature quality evaluation

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the evaluation criteria outlined in the Cochrane Handbook. Specifically, the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale was employed to score each included study, with a maximum total score of 10 points (Table 2). The PEDro scale comprises 11 items, with scores interpreted as follows: less than 3 points indicates low quality, 4–5 points indicates moderate quality, 6–8 points indicates high quality, and 9–10 points indicates very high quality. Quality assessments were conducted independently by two researchers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. Of the 8 included studies, 6 were rated as high quality [14–19] and 2 as moderate quality [20, 21].

Publication bias

A total of 8 studies were included in this meta-analysis. In accordance with methodological standards, the limited number of included studies resulted in insufficient statistical power for publication bias testing, rendering the results of such tests unreliable; therefore, formal quantitative assessment of publication bias was not performed. Furthermore, several individual studies contained multiple correlated sub-indicators derived from the same dataset, resulting in data non-independence. For this reason, bias assessment based on disaggregated sub-indicators was not conducted, and the potential risk of publication bias in small-sample studies cannot be completely ruled out.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4. All outcome indicators included in this study were continuous variables. The standardized mean difference (SMD) or mean difference (MD) was used as the effect size measure, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) calculated for each outcome. Specifically, MD was selected when all included studies reported outcomes using identical measurement units, whereas SMD was applied when different measurement tools or units were used for the same outcome across studies. Heterogeneity among the included studies was evaluated using the chi-square (χ²) test and the I² statistic. When P > 0.1 and I² < 50%, heterogeneity was considered low, and a fixed-effects model was employed. When P ≤ 0.1 and I² ≥ 50%, substantial heterogeneity was indicated; in such cases, the sources of heterogeneity were first explored, after which a random-effects model was adopted.

The value of the coefficient R is 0.5.

Results

Characteristics and quality assessment of included studies

Profile and methodological quality of included studies. A total of 8 randomized controlled trials were included in this meta-analysis, published between 2011 and 2025. All studies adopted a parallel-group controlled design. The experimental groups received a combined intervention consisting of conventional handball training (HT) and core strength training (CST). Among the control groups, seven studies applied conventional handball training alone, and one study [21] employed no intervention. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the PEDro scale, with scores ranging from 5 to 7 points. Specifically, two studies scored 7 points (high quality), four scored 6 points (relatively high quality), and two scored 5 points (moderate quality). Overall, the included studies were of moderately high methodological quality, and the findings were considered reliable.

All participants were adolescent and young adult handball athletes aged 16.5 to 25 years, with the majority being competitive athletes aged 16 to 19 years. Regarding sex distribution, four studies recruited female athletes, three recruited male athletes, and one study [20] did not clearly report the sex of participants. The sample size per group ranged from 8 to 20, with total sample sizes per study ranging from 18 to 40, indicating generally small sample sizes. The body mass index (BMI) of participants ranged from 21.34 to 25.6, which is consistent with the physical characteristics of athletes with normal weight or mild overweight.

Characteristics of intervention protocols. Core strength training was applied as a supplementary intervention alongside conventional handball training, with the content and intensity of routine training remaining unchanged across all studies. The intervention parameters were as follows: training duration ranged from 6 to 10 weeks, comprising two studies with a 6-week program, five with an 8-week program, and one with a 10-week program [16]. Training frequency ranged from 2 to 4 sessions per week, with three studies arranging 2 sessions per week, four arranging 3 sessions per week, and one arranging 4 sessions per week [16]. The specific exercise selection, loading intensity, inter-set rest intervals, and duration of each set were not reported uniformly across the included studies.

Outcome measures and main results. The included indicators covered five core dimensions of handball athletes’ competitive performance: (1) sport-specific throwing performance (throwing velocity, 7-meter penalty throw, 9-meter running shot, etc.); (2) strength and explosive power (vertical jump, standing long jump, jump height, reactive strength index, etc.); (3) speed and agility (40-meter sprint, Illinois Agility Test, etc.); (4) balance and flexibility; and (5) core and joint function indicators, including core chain function and shoulder joint stability.

The overall results indicated that the combined intervention of CST and conventional handball training yielded greater improvements in most performance indicators compared with conventional handball training alone. With respect to sport-specific throwing performance, the combined intervention group demonstrated more pronounced increases in throwing velocity and shooting ability, whereas declines in throwing indicators were observed in some control groups receiving conventional training only. Jump and explosive power indicators were markedly improved across all experimental groups, while control groups showed limited gains and occasional declines in certain indicators. The combined intervention also produced notable targeted improvements in core function indicators. Speed and agility were generally improved, albeit with moderate inter-study heterogeneity. Additionally, asymmetries between dominant and non-dominant limbs were identified in several indicators. For instance, decreased left knee flexor strength was observed in the experimental group of Amrinder Singh [20], and reduced jump contact time of the non-dominant limb was reported by A. Ferri-Caruana [19].

Effects of CST on throwing ability in handball athletes

Among the included studies, throwing performance was assessed across three categories: standing throw, running throw, and jump throw.

Four studies investigated the effect of CST on standing throw performance in handball players (Fig. 2), with 57 participants in the experimental groups and 53 in the control groups. All outcome measures were reported in kilometers per hour (km/h). No significant between-study heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0%, P = 0.76), and a fixed-effects model was therefore applied. Discussion

Comprehensive discussion and mechanism analysis of the effects of core strength training

The 8 included studies in Table 2 had PEDro scores ranging from 5 to 7, indicating moderately high overall methodological quality. All studies consistently showed that conventional handball training combined with core strength training was significantly superior to handball training alone or a no-intervention control across the majority of outcome measures, providing consistent empirical evidence for the effectiveness of core strength training as a supplementary approach to handball sport-specific training. For core sport-specific indicators, all 5 studies that directly measured throwing velocity revealed significant improvements in the experimental group across different movement patterns, including the 7-meter penalty throw, 9-meter run-up standing throw, and jump throw. In contrast, only a small number of control groups showed minor improvements, while most exhibited a declining trend. This finding challenges the traditional perception that throwing ability is solely determined by upper limb strength, suggesting that the benefits of core strength training on throwing performance may not exclusively derive from direct strengthening of upper limb muscles. Instead, such improvements may be achieved by optimizing the transmission efficiency of the whole-body kinetic chain, which follows the sequence of lower limb push-off → trunk transmission → upper limb force generation. As the pivotal link of the kinetic chain, core muscles may reduce energy dissipation at the trunk segment and enhance terminal output power through improved contraction timing and coordination capacity. However, the aforementioned mechanisms have not been directly or quantitatively verified in the included studies. Results from studies measuring jumping ability align with the direction of this hypothesis. Explosive power indicators such as vertical jump and standing long jump all improved in the experimental group, whereas only one control group showed improvement and the rest exhibited no significant change or decline. This suggests that enhanced core stability may more efficiently convert the force generated by lower limb push-off into vertical and horizontal translational kinetic energy, which may facilitate key technical movements such as jump shots and defensive blocking in handball players.

The comprehensive improvement of trunk muscle function induced by core strength training may serve as the potential basis for its multidimensional training effects. Results of the Bourban test showed that core endurance of the ventral chain, dorsal chain, and bilateral lateral chains all improved significantly in the experimental group. In the control group, by contrast, core endurance of the ventral chain and bilateral lateral chains decreased, with only the dorsal chain showing a minor increase. This difference partly reflects the potential imbalance in core muscle development associated with conventional handball training: repetitive throwing and change-of-direction movements in routine handball training primarily activate the dorsal chain muscles, while the ventral and lateral chains receive insufficient stimulation. Over time, this may lead to anterior-posterior trunk muscle strength imbalance and subsequently increase the risk of sports injuries. In contrast, systematic core strength training may comprehensively activate and enhance the coordinated function of the anterior, posterior, and bilateral trunk muscles, helping to correct muscle strength imbalance. The between-group differences in shoulder joint range of motion and stability, as well as the extension range of the throwing and non-throwing arms, are also consistent with the hypothesis that core stability supports upper limb sport-specific function. The significant improvements in shoulder joint stability and throwing arm extension range in the experimental group may reduce shear load on upper limb joints during high-speed throwing movements, while lengthening the force arm to increase work distance. This potential pathway may explain why significant improvements in throwing velocity can still be observed with combined core strength training, even without additional upper limb sport-specific training. Furthermore, between-group differences in physical fitness indicators such as 40-meter sprint speed and Illinois Agility Test results suggest that core strength training may also improve athletes’ locomotion speed and change-of-direction ability. The underlying mechanism may be that core muscles maintain a neutral trunk position during rapid changes of direction and acceleration, ensuring effective transmission of lower limb force and stable control of body posture, thereby avoiding energy waste and movement distortion caused by trunk sway.

Some heterogeneous results across the included studies provide important clues for further understanding the mechanisms underlying the effects of core strength training. In the study by Amrinder Singh [20], left hamstring strength decreased in the experimental group. In the study by Ferri-Caruana [19], no significant between-group difference was found in the Reactive Strength Index of the dominant and non-dominant sides. These findings suggest that exercise selection, load configuration, and the proportion of unilateral training in core training programs may directly affect the uniformity of intervention effects. If a training program overemphasizes bilateral symmetric exercises while neglecting unilateral core stability training, it may fail to effectively improve bilateral muscle strength asymmetry in athletes. Meanwhile, the difference in intervention effects across sexes warrants further investigation. Studies on female athletes showed that the magnitude of improvement in throwing velocity and jumping ability induced by core strength training was generally greater than that in male athletes, which may be associated with the relatively lower baseline core strength levels and greater room for training adaptation in female athletes. The potential dose-response effect of training duration and frequency is also reflected in the results: a 10-week training program with 4 sessions per week yielded greater improvements in sport-specific throwing indicators than a 6-week program with 2 sessions per week. This suggests that both neuromuscular and morphological adaptations to core strength training may require sufficient accumulation of stimulation duration and frequency, and short-term, low-frequency core training may be unlikely to produce significant gains in sport-specific ability. Overall, the results of the studies included in this meta-analysis demonstrate that conventional handball training combined with core strength training can comprehensively improve the sport-specific performance and physical fitness of handball players. These effects may be associated with multiple pathways, including optimized kinetic chain transmission, enhanced trunk stability, and balanced muscle development, which have corresponding physiological and biomechanical theoretical foundations. However, the specific mechanisms remain to be directly verified by targeted mechanistic studies in future research.

Effects of CST on throwing ability in handball athletes

The results of the meta-analysis demonstrate that core strength training combined with conventional handball training significantly improves the overall throwing ability of handball players. Subgroup analysis further reveals that improvements in standing throw and run-up throw performance are statistically significant, whereas the between-group difference in jump throw ability fails to reach statistical significance. The PEDro scores of the studies included in this section are concentrated in the range of 5 to 7, indicating moderately high overall methodological quality. The outcomes of literature screening and data pooling present high reliability, providing a credible evidence-based reference for investigating the association between core strength training and throwing ability in handball players.

Between-group comparisons indicate that the control group, which received only conventional handball training, generally showed minimal improvements in sport-specific indicators such as standing throw and run-up throw, and some studies even reported slight declines in performance, suggesting that sport-specific training alone is insufficient to drive sustained advancement in throwing ability. In contrast, the experimental group that received conventional training plus core strength training exhibited steady positive growth across all throwing indicators, implying that core strength training exerts an independent beneficial effect on handball-specific throwing performance and cannot be fully replaced by conventional handball training. The intervention effects of core strength training vary notably across different throwing movement patterns. Standing throws and run-up throws rely on ground support to complete the full kinetic chain, and the stabilizing and regulatory role of core strength may be more fully exerted, which may serve as a potential reason for the significant between-group differences in effect sizes across these movement types. Based on the limited available evidence, it is speculated that jump throws, performed in an aerial state without ground support, are affected by multiple confounding factors including aerial posture control, flight time, and individual sport-specific technical style, which may attenuate the intervention effect of core strength training to a certain extent and ultimately result in no statistically significant improvement in jump throw ability. The included studies present mild heterogeneity, mainly stemming from differences in training intervention parameters and testing environments. The included studies involve training durations of 6 to 10 weeks and training frequencies of 2 to 4 sessions per week. Differences in intervention duration and frequency may be important factors contributing to the varied magnitudes of improvement in athletes’ throwing ability, with an overall trend of more prominent beneficial effects from longer-duration, higher-frequency training. Meanwhile, some studies adopted goalkeeper interference scenarios, while others employed static testing without interference. Coupled with inconsistent assessment indicators such as the 7-meter penalty throw and 9-meter run-up shot, these factors further contribute to minor discrepancies in study results.

Regarding the characteristics of the study population, both male and female handball players can benefit from core strength training, and the overall magnitude of improvement in throwing ability is greater in female athletes than in male athletes. This difference may be associated with the weaker baseline core strength and greater room for training adaptation in female athletes, suggesting that core strength training has positive intervention value for throwing performance in athletes of both sexes, with only population-level differences in the magnitude of improvement. Taken together, the available research evidence indicates that the improvement of throwing ability in handball players by core strength training presents certain movement specificity: there is relatively sufficient evidence supporting the beneficial effects on ground-supported throwing movements, whereas based on the currently included studies, it cannot be confirmed that conventional core training produces a significant improvement in jump throw performance in an aerial state.

Effects of CST on jumping ability in handball athletes

The included studies adopted vertical jump and standing long jump as outcome measures for jumping ability. The results showed that core strength training combined with conventional handball training yielded positive improvements in jumping-related indicators among both male and female handball players, while the control groups that received no core strength intervention mostly exhibited stagnation or even slight declines in jumping ability, suggesting that core strength training serves as an important intervention approach for improving jumping performance in handball players. The included studies had PEDro scores ranging from 5 to 7, with sound overall methodological quality. The intervention outcomes were comparable across samples of different sexes and ages, providing an evidence-based foundation for exploring the intrinsic association between core strength and jumping ability.

Based on the available study findings, it is hypothesized that the facilitative effect of core strength training on jumping ability may be associated with the synergistic optimization of the biomechanical kinetic chain. The completion of handball jumping movements relies on lower limb push-off force generation, trunk core stability, and the sequential transmission of whole-body force. As the pivotal hub for force transmission between the upper and lower limbs, core muscles may maintain trunk posture stability at the instant of take-off, reduce ineffective energy dissipation at the trunk segment, and ensure more complete transfer of lower limb explosive power to the whole body, thereby increasing vertical jump height and standing long jump distance. However, the aforementioned mechanistic pathways have not been directly or quantitatively verified in the included studies. The study by T. Mohan Kumar [21] demonstrated that after 8 weeks of core strength training at 3 sessions per week, male athletes achieved significant improvements in standing long jump performance, while the corresponding indicators declined in the control group with no additional intervention. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis that strengthened core muscles can optimize jumping force generation patterns and compensate for the deficiencies of sport-specific training alone in terms of trunk control.

Differences in the duration and frequency of intervention protocols may be correlated with the magnitude of improvement in jumping ability and the effects of training transfer. All currently included studies adopted an 8-week intervention cycle, with training frequencies set at either 2 or 3 sessions per week. The uniform intervention duration facilitates cross-sectional comparison of effect differences. At a core training frequency of 3 sessions per week, athletes showed more pronounced improvements in indicators such as vertical jump and standing long jump, suggesting that higher-frequency training stimulation may exert a more sustained effect on the adaptive remodeling of core muscles. Meanwhile, moderate-frequency core training can also achieve positive growth in jumping ability, indicating that the beneficial effect of core strength training on jumping performance has a certain range of frequency adaptation. Based on the available evidence, it is speculated that an 8-week intervention cycle may be sufficient to induce sport-specific adaptation of core strength, which can then be translated into measurable improvements in jumping ability. Higher training frequencies may further amplify the intervention benefits, but this dose-response relationship remains to be verified by more studies.

Sex differences and variations in control group intervention designs also contribute to differentiated patterns of change in jumping ability. Female athletes achieved steady growth in vertical jump performance after adding core strength training to conventional handball training. Young male athletes showed not only significant improvements in standing long jump but also synchronous enhancement of whole-body explosive power. Differences in control group settings also exerted an impact on the results: in some studies, the control groups only participated in routine handball training without any additional physical fitness intervention, and their jumping indicators barely improved or even declined. In studies using conventional handball training as the control, the control groups showed slight improvements in jumping ability, but the magnitude of improvement was far lower than that in the core training experimental groups. Synthesizing the available results, it is inferred that the jumping ability of both male and female handball players can benefit from core strength training. Conventional sport-specific training can hardly replace the potential shaping effect of core training on trunk stability and force transmission, which may be an underlying reason for the significant divergence in jumping performance between the experimental and control groups. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms still need to be confirmed by further targeted research.

Effects of CST on sprint ability in handball athletes

Meta-analysis results showed no statistically significant difference in the improvement of linear sprint ability among handball players between core strength training combined with conventional handball training and control interventions (MD = − 0.11, 95% CI [− 0.33, 0.12], P = 0.34), indicating that the currently limited evidence does not support the claim that core strength training can significantly improve linear sprint performance in handball players. Heterogeneity testing revealed mild heterogeneity across the included studies (Chi² = 1.56, df = 1, P = 0.21, I² = 36%). The pooled results generated using the fixed-effects model demonstrated good internal consistency, and the conclusions possessed a certain degree of stability.

Differences in intervention protocols and control group designs across the included studies may be important factors influencing the pooled results. Although both studies adopted an 8-week core strength training intervention, there were marked differences in control group designs: in the study by T. Mohan Kumar [21], training was administered 3 times per week, and the control group received no additional intervention; in the study by Amrinder Singh [20], training was also delivered 3 times per week, but the control group received the same volume of conventional handball training as the experimental group. This difference in study design may account for the divergence in effect sizes between the two studies: Amrinder Singh [20] reported a more favorable improvement trend in the experimental group relative to the control group, whereas the between-group difference in T. Mohan Kumar [21] was minimal. The high weight (76.7%) of the latter study further reduced the overall pooled effect size, which may be a key reason why the overall result failed to reach statistical significance. Additionally, the core training exercise combinations, load intensity, and progressive protocols were not clearly standardized across the two studies, which may have resulted in varying degrees of improvement in core muscle strength and endurance, thereby affecting the transfer efficiency to sport-specific performance.

Based on the available evidence, it is speculated that the non-significant effect of core strength training on linear sprint ability in handball players may not stem from the ineffectiveness of the intervention itself, but rather from the dual limitations of study design and indicator selection. First, an 8-week intervention cycle may be insufficient to achieve the full transfer of core strength adaptations to sport-specific sprint ability. According to the kinetic chain hypothesis, core muscles serve as the hub for force transmission between the upper and lower limbs, and improvements in their stability and strength may require long-term neuromuscular integration training to translate into enhanced energy transmission efficiency during running. Short-term interventions may only induce adaptations at the level of basic strength, making it difficult to manifest statistically significant differences in linear sprint indicators that are highly dependent on lower limb explosive power. However, the aforementioned mechanistic pathways have not been directly or quantitatively verified in the included studies. Second, the inherent limitations of the linear sprint indicator may mask the true sport-specific value of core strength training: sprints in handball are mostly accompanied by direction changes, sudden stops, and physical contact, and the stabilizing and balancing roles of core muscles in such compound movements may theoretically be more prominent than in simple linear acceleration. The included studies only adopted a single linear sprint indicator, which may be insufficient to fully reflect the actual impact of core strength training on sport-specific sprint patterns.

Limitations

Insufficient sample size and representativeness: Some outcome indicators (e.g., horizontal jump, 60-yard sprint) were covered by only two or fewer studies, resulting in inadequate statistical power. The included studies predominantly enrolled adolescent handball athletes, and most lacked long-term follow-up. Therefore, the generalizability of the research findings to high-level professional athletes is limited.

Conclusion

A total of 8 randomized controlled trials were included in this study. The PEDro scale was applied to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, with scores ranging from 5 to 7 points. The overall methodological quality was moderately high, and the research results were reliable. The combined intervention of core strength training and conventional handball training was significantly more effective than conventional handball training alone, and could improve the competitive performance of handball athletes across multiple dimensions, including sport-specific throwing, jumping explosive power, and core muscle function.

Recommendations

Based on the limited available evidence, a systematic core strength training cycle of ≥ 8 weeks at 2–3 sessions per week is recommended for handball players in daily training. A 10-week, 4-session-per-week regimen may be trialed for enhanced sport-specific throwing performance, but with relatively limited supporting evidence. Cycles of ≤ 6 weeks only serve basic neuromuscular adaptation and barely yield significant gains in competitive ability. Generic core exercises should be replaced with handball-specific drills (standing throws, run-up throws, jump take-offs, and change-of-direction movements), theoretically guided by the potential of core training to optimize whole-body kinetic chain force transmission. Training should prioritize standing throw, run-up throw, and vertical jump capacity. Current evidence does not support core strength training as a primary intervention for linear sprint improvement. Programs should target balanced development of ventral, dorsal, and lateral trunk muscles, supplemented by unilateral and dominant/non-dominant side core drills. Training loads can be differentiated by baseline core strength levels across sexes. Routine integration of core training into handball-specific training systems is advised to sustain improvements in trunk posture control and sport-specific force generation efficiency.
