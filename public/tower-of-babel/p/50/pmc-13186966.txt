Study on physico-mechanical properties and damage constitutive model of sandstone-like materials
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Weiguo Qiao, Weijie Song, Mingbin Wang, Haozheng Wang, Yue Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scientific and rational selection of rock-like materials plays a critically important role in geotechnical model tests. This study conducted physical and mechanical property tests on three types of sandstone, analyzing their microstructural characteristics and physical-mechanical behaviors. A similar material simulating sandstone, composed of cement, quartz sand, iron concentrate powder, gypsum, and pure water, was proposed. Uniaxial and triaxial compression tests were performed on specimens of this sandstone-similar material. The optimal mix proportion of the similar material was determined, and a damage constitutive model for it was established. The results indicate that the clay mineral composition of all three sandstones is dominated by quartz, feldspar, and rock fragments, with the fine-grained sandstone exhibiting the highest quartz content. The pore structure of coarse-grained and medium-grained sandstones underwent significant changes after water absorption, with their dense flaky structure becoming notably loosened, whereas the pore structure of fine-grained sandstone showed little difference after absorption. The optimal mix proportion for the sandstone-similar material was determined as follows: aggregate (quartz sand + iron concentrate powder) accounting for 70% of the solid mass (quartz sand + iron ore concentrate + cement + gypsum), the aggregate component ratio (quartz sand : iron ore concentrate) being 2:1, the binder component ratio (cement : gypsum) being 1:0, and a water mixing ratio of 25%. A strain-softening damage statistical constitutive model for the sandstone-similar material was established, and verification shows that this model accurately reflects the stress-strain relationship of the material under triaxial compression conditions.

Introduction

Physical similarity model tests are vital in geotechnical engineering for simulating complex mechanical environments and failure processes of structures1–3. These tests enable the study of stress evolution from elastic deformation to ultimate failure, making them indispensable for analyzing surrounding rock behavior in tunnels, slopes, and other critical infrastructures4–6.

A key challenge lies in developing rock-like materials that accurately replicate the physico-mechanical properties of natural rock. While granular cemented materials (such as sand-gypsum or sand-cement blends) are widely adopted due to their adjustable strength and ease of fabrication7–9, two critical gaps persist: (1) most studies prioritize mechanical property similarity while neglecting microstructural fidelity and constitutive modeling10–12; (2) limited research integrates damage thresholds into constitutive models for strain-softening behavior under triaxial compression13.

To address these gaps, a novel sandstone-like material was developed and its damage constitutive model was established. Firstly, the fundamental physical and mechanical properties of deep-well sandstone were investigated, analyzing the particle size distribution, mineral composition, microstructure, and hydrophysical characteristics of coarse, medium, and fine sandstone. Subsequently, the composition of the sandstone-like material was proposed. Orthogonal experimental design was employed to conduct uniaxial and triaxial compression tests, along with water absorption tests on the material, thereby determining its optimal proportion. Finally, based on the triaxial compression test data, a damage constitutive model for the sandstone-like material was established.

Literature review

Current similarity materials predominantly use cemented granular composites. Xu et al.14 used iron powder, barite powder, and quartz sand as aggregates, gypsum as the binder, and latex powder as the modifier to prepare soft rock-like materials. Experimental results show that a low dosage (less than 2%) of latex powder can enhance the density, compressive strength, and Poisson’s ratio of the soft rock-like material. Li et al.15 used sand and talc powder as aggregates, paraffin wax as the binder, and developed a new type of fluid-structure coupling similarity material by combining talc powder and hydraulic oil. This material was applied in tunnel water inflow model tests, effectively revealing the variation patterns of multi-field parameters such as displacement, stress, and seepage pressure. Luo et al.16 conducted uniaxial compression tests on specimens of two different similarity materials (sand-gypsum and sand-cement-gypsum) with varying mix ratios to improve the simulation accuracy of similarity material model tests. They analyzed their mechanical properties and failure characteristics and discussed the sources of error in the preparation of similarity material models and the testing of specimens. Li et al.17 developed a new fluid-structure coupling similarity material composed of sand, barite powder, talc powder, cement, vaseline, silicone oil, and an appropriate amount of mixing water, which has been successfully applied in geomechanical model tests. Shi et al.18 developed a new rock-like material using quartz sand, barite powder, cement, sodium silicate, rosin, and glycerin as raw materials, aiming to incorporate hydro-mechanical and dynamic performance. They also proposed a preparation scheme for the similarity material.

In summary, research on the development methods and mechanical property testing of similarity materials continues to evolve and innovate, with thorough validation of aggregate and binder selection19–21. Despite advances, systematic studies linking microstructure, water absorption, and damage mechanics remain scarce.

Materials

Sampling of sandstone in deep well

The sandstone specimens were collected from the surrounding rock of the − 990 m level haulage roadway in the Tangkou coal mine, belonging to the Permian strata. Core samples obtained through borehole drilling at the Tangkou coal mine were classified into three lithological types: coarse-grained sandstone, medium-grained sandstone, and fine-grained sandstone. After laboratory processing (cutting and grinding), standard cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm were prepared, as shown in Fig. 1.

Raw materials for preparing sandstone-like materials

To ensure the similarity in physical and mechanical properties between the sandstone-like materials and the prototype sandstone, quartz sand was selected as the aggregate, while a mixture of ordinary Portland cement and gypsum was used as the binder to fabricate the sandstone-like specimens. Due to the high unit weight of natural sandstone, iron concentrate powder with a fineness of 100 mesh was incorporated as a density modifier to regulate the unit weight of the similarity materials. Microstructure and mineral composition of sandstone

To obtain the microstructure and mineral composition of coarse-, medium-, and fine-grained sandstone, rock thin sections were prepared and observed under an optical microscope. The specific procedure was as follows: the three types of sandstone specimens were cut into approximately 3 mm thick slabs using a rock saw, then ground with silicon carbide abrasive and water to thin slices of 0.03 mm thickness. The slices were polished on glass plates with fine-grained alumina powder and finally mounted on glass slides using balsam, as shown in Fig. 3. The mineral composition and grain structure of the sandstone were observed using an optical microscope, and the experimental results are presented in Fig. 4.

As shown in Fig. 4, the coarse-, medium-, and fine-grained sandstones are primarily composed of quartz, feldspar, and lithic fragments, with all three types exhibiting poorly rounded grains. The coarse-grained sandstone features a grain-supported texture dominated by point-to-line contacts and minor sutured contacts, with grains ranging from subangular to subrounded (occasionally subrounded) and sizes of 0.45–1.2 mm. Its composition includes 25% quartz, 45% chert, 5% feldspar (K-feldspar and albite), 3% volcanic rock, 19% quartzite lithic fragments, and ~ 3% mica. The medium-grained sandstone, characterized by subangular grains (0.25–0.5 mm), is similarly grain-supported with predominant line contacts, comprising 45% quartz, 7% chert, 5% feldspar (mainly K-feldspar), 40% quartzite lithic fragments, and ~ 2% mica. The fine-grained sandstone displays grains of subangular to subrounded morphology (minor angular) with sizes of 0.03–0.25 mm, also grain-supported with line contacts, and consists of 48% quartz, 8% chert, 10% feldspar (K-feldspar and plagioclase), 5% metamorphic rock, 20% quartzite, 4% phyllite lithic fragments, and ~ 5% muscovite.

Based on the sieve-based particle separation method described in reference22, 5 kg samples each of coarse-, medium-, and fine-grained sandstones were collected. These samples were separately boiled in a 5 wt% NaOH solution to progressively disintegrate the sandstone particles. Subsequently, particles of varying sizes from the original sandstone specimens underwent drying, sieving, mass measurement, and statistical analysis. This process ultimately yielded the particle size distribution curves of the sandstone, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The particle size ranges shown in Fig. 5 demonstrate close alignment with the results obtained from optical microscopy analysis.

As shown in Fig. 7, the pore structure of coarse-, and medium-grained sandstones undergoes significant changes after water absorption, with their dense flaky structures becoming notably looser. In contrast, fine-grained sandstones exhibit minimal differences in pore structure post-water absorption, although montmorillonite particles experience volume expansion due to water uptake. This phenomenon occurs because clay minerals filling the gaps between coarse-, and medium-grained sandstone particles react chemically with water, leading to detachment of larger framework particles and subsequent filling of larger pores by water, which further enlarges micro-pores and enhances their connectivity. Meanwhile, cementing materials and other substances within the rock pores may partially dissolve, break apart, and migrate due to water movement, creating expanded and smoother migration pathways, resulting in increased pore size and improved connectivity.

Water absorption characteristics of sandstone

The water absorption rate of rock refers to the ratio of the mass of absorbed water to the solid mass of the specimen, which indirectly reflects the internal pore structure of the rock26,27. To investigate the temporal evolution of water absorption in sandstone, free immersion tests were conducted on three types of sandstone. The curves depicting the variation of water absorption rate with time are presented in Fig. 8.

Fig. 8Curves of water absorption rate over time and fitting curves for sandstone.

Curves of water absorption rate over time and fitting curves for sandstone.

As shown in Fig. 8, the water absorption rates of the three sandstone specimens all increase with time and stabilize after approximately 8 h. During the initial stage of the test, the coarse-grained sandstone exhibits the fastest absorption rate, while the medium- and fine-grained sandstones show similar absorption rates, both following a near-linear relationship with time. This rapid increase is attributed to water initially filling larger pores within the rock. Simultaneously, the dissolution, fragmentation, and migration of cements within intergranular pores expand transport pathways, enhance pore connectivity, and accelerate water absorption. Subsequently, the absorption rate gradually decreases, and the incremental water absorption slows. By the later stage of the test, the absorption rate approaches zero, with minimal further increases in water content, indicating that the sandstone specimens have reached a saturated state. This stabilization occurs because the pores are predominantly water-filled, and the remaining narrow pore channels hinder further moisture migration, establishing equilibrium.

A comparison of the water absorption rates among the three sandstones reveals significant differences in their maximum water absorption capacities. The coarse-grained sandstone exhibits the highest absorption capacity, stabilizing at 5.94%; the medium-grained sandstone follows with a stabilized absorption rate of 4.76%; and the fine-grained sandstone has the lowest capacity, stabilizing at 4.14%. This disparity arises because the coarse-grained sandstone, with its larger grain size, features looser intergranular contacts and wider pore throat diameters compared to the other two types, thereby enabling greater water absorption. As shown in Table 2, the UCS of the coarse-grained sandstone is 39.53 MPa, with a corresponding peak axial strain of 3.76 × 10− 3; the medium-grained sandstone has a UCS of 37.07 MPa (peak axial strain: 5.01 × 10− 3); and the fine-grained sandstone exhibits a UCS of 39.62 MPa (peak axial strain: 3.52 × 10− 3). It can be observed that the UCS values of the three sandstone specimens are relatively close, but their corresponding peak strains differ. The peak strains of the coarse- and fine-grained sandstones are similar in magnitude, yet both are smaller than that of the medium- grained sandstone. In triaxial compression tests, increasing confining pressure significantly enhances the strength of sandstone, with the strength gain observed over the 0–5 MPa confining pressure range being markedly greater than that over the 5–10 MPa range. Under 10 MPa confinement, peak stress σmax increased by 95.2% (coarse), 63.4% (medium), and 91.6% (fine) versus uniaxial compression. Medium sandstone exhibited anomalous strain at 5 MPa (εmax = 13.02 × 10− 3), attributable to montmorillonite-induced ductility. Conversely, fine sandstone’s low εmax (11.08 × 10− 3 at 10 MPa) reflected quartz-dominated brittleness.

Analysis of Fig. 12; Table 5 indicates that among the nine groups of coarse-grained sandstone-like material specimens, the LC-1 group exhibited the highest uniaxial compressive strength of 57.72 MPa, with triaxial compressive strengths of 78.17 MPa at 5 MPa confining pressure and 84.29 MPa at 10 MPa confining pressure. The LC-9 group showed the lowest uniaxial compressive strength of 15.98 MPa, with triaxial compressive strengths of 31.88 MPa at 5 MPa confining pressure and 44.55 MPa at 10 MPa confining pressure. Furthermore, as the confining pressure increased, the growth rate of ultimate compressive strength for specimens in groups LC-1, LC-6, and LC-8 was significantly lower than that of the other six groups. The primary reason lies in the effect of the binder composition ratio (Factor C): the six groups other than LC-1, LC-6, and LC-8 contained varying proportions of gypsum. Under gypsum’s influence, these specimens exhibited lower ultimate compressive strength under low confining pressures, while their ultimate compressive strength growth rate increased substantially with rising confining pressure. Further analysis reveals that the six groups excluding LC-1, LC-6, and LC-8 also demonstrated more gradual post-peak strength degradation—another effect attributable to the addition of gypsum.

From the stress-strain curves, it can be observed that during the initial loading phase, the specimens are in the elastic phase, with the stress-strain curve exhibiting a linear relationship. As the normal stress gradually increases, the specimen stress progressively rises to the peak strength, and the peak strength demonstrates a gradual upward trend with increasing confining pressure. During the post-peak phase, the stress begins to decline gradually while the strain continues to increase, and the stress reduction rate slows under higher confining pressures. Compared to the triaxial compression tests, the four stages of uniaxial compression—compaction, elasticity, yield, and failure—are more pronounced in the specimens, with a prolonged compaction phase. Although the four stages are also discernible in triaxial compression, the compaction phase is shorter, the yield phase becomes more distinct, and the peak point is significantly delayed.

Selection of mix design schemes for sandstone-like materials

Based on the test results, a comparison of the physical and mechanical properties between the sandstone-like materials and natural sandstone revealed that groups LC-8, LZ-8, and LX-8 most closely matched the respective indices of coarse-grained sandstone, medium-grained sandstone, and fine-grained sandstone. The uniaxial compressive strengths of natural coarse-, medium-, and fine-grained sandstones were 39.53 MPa, 37.07 MPa, and 39.62 MPa, respectively, while the corresponding sandstone-like materials LC-8, LZ-8, and LX-8 exhibited uniaxial compressive strengths of 37.70 MPa, 35.27 MPa, and 36.50 MPa—indicating minimal divergence. Additionally, differences in parameters such as triaxial compressive strength, unit weight, elastic modulus, and water absorption rate were all constrained within narrow ranges. Therefore, the 8th group mix ratio is determined as the optimal scheme, with an aggregate-to-solid ratio of 70%, an aggregate composition ratio (sand: iron concentrate powder) of 2:1, a binder composition ratio (cement: gypsum) of 1:0, and a water addition rate of 25%.

Conclusions

In this study, a sandstone-like material composed of cement, quartz sand, iron concentrate powder, gypsum, and pure water is proposed to simulate sandstone. The optimal mix ratio of the sandstone-like material is determined, and a damage constitutive model for the sandstone-like material is established. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The main clay mineral composition of sandstone is primarily composed of quartz, feldspar, and lithic fragments, among which fine-grained sandstone has the highest quartz content, reaching 48%. All three types of sandstone are grain-supported, where coarse-grained sandstone features predominantly point-to-line contacts between grains, with a minor proportion of sutured contacts, while medium- and fine-grained sandstones are characterized by predominant line contacts between grains.

(2) The water absorption rate of sandstone was tested using the free immersion method. The water absorption rates of the three sandstone specimens stabilized after 8 h: coarse-grained sandstone exhibited the highest water absorption capacity (5.94%); medium-grained sandstone followed with an absorption rate of 4.76%; and fine-grained sandstone showed the lowest absorption capacity (4.14%). Coarse- and medium-grained sandstones displayed significant pore structure changes post-water absorption, with their dense flaky structures becoming noticeably looser, while the pore structure of fine-grained sandstone remained largely unchanged, although its surface montmorillonite particles expanded in volume due to water absorption.

(3) The optimal mix ratio of sandstone-like materials was determined by comparative analysis of the results from triaxial compression tests, water absorption tests, and the physical and mechanical properties of sandstone. The optimal mix ratio is as follows: the aggregate-to-solid ratio (A) is 70%, the aggregate composition ratio (B) is 2:1, the binder composition ratio (C) is 1:0, and the water addition rate (D) is 25%.

(4) Based on triaxial compression test data of sandstone-like materials, and from the perspective of considering damage threshold effects, a statistical constitutive model for strain softening damage was established by introducing damage threshold parameters and statistical damage theory. Verification by comparing with experimental data showed that the model effectively reflects the stress-strain relationship of sandstone-like materials under triaxial compression.

Perspectives

Future work will focus on three strategic directions: (1) Scalability optimization: scaling production via automated extrusion systems to reduce strength variability while cutting costs by 25%; (2) Multi-hazard resilience: embedding 0.1–0.5 wt% graphene oxide to enhance fracture energy by 33% under cyclic loading (f = 1–5 Hz, simulating seismic/blasting impacts), with field trials planned in Xinhe Coal Mine; (3) Sustainable binders: replacing 40–60% Portland cement with geopolymers to reduce the CO₂ footprint by 0.51 kg CO₂/kg (−62%) while maintaining UCS > 35 MPa.
