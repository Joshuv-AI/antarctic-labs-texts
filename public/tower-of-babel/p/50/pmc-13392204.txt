Integrating Behavioral Health Services for Individuals With Rheumatic Diseases: A Quantitative Analysis of a Pilot Study
Authors: Shannon Teaw, Jessica Link‐Malcolm, Michelle Ghebranious, Dorothy Jet Patterson, Jenny Foster, Sofia Bereket, Urooj Wahid, Puneet Bajaj, E. Blair Solow, Linda S. Hynan, Jennifer L. Barton, Una E. Makris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveChronic pain, depressive and anxiety symptoms are common comorbidities among persons with rheumatic disease (RD) and contribute to increased symptom burden and reduced quality of life (QoL). Behavioral health (BH) interventions to motivate behavior change and promote self‐management can effectively address these conditions. Despite potential benefits, efforts to integrate BH services into rheumatology clinics have been limited. We developed and piloted a BH program integrated within an academic rheumatology clinic to evaluate feasibility and impact on patient‐reported outcomes (PROs).MethodsParticipants were adults with RD who chose one of three primary BH goals: increase physical activity, improve mood and/or anxiety, or manage pain. The BH specialist met via telephone with participants for up to eight sessions, which included psychoeducation, motivational interviewing, and mindfulness meditation, combined with supplemental apps and videos. Feasibility data included recruitment, retention, and intervention process data. PROs evaluated change from baseline to end assessment for pain interference, depression and anxiety, pain catastrophizing, QoL, and PRO Measurement Information System measures of fatigue, sleep disturbance, social satisfaction, and physical function. Sign‐rank tests or paired Student's t‐test were used to compare baseline and postintervention scores.ResultsTwenty‐eight participants completed the program. Most (n = 15, 54%) chose the goal of improving mood and anxiety. Participants completed a mean of 6.86 (SD 1.56) sessions. Mean change scores demonstrated statistically significant improvements from baseline to end assessment across all PROs (P < 0.05), except social satisfaction and physical function (P > 0.05).ConclusionIntegrating BH services within a rheumatology clinic was feasible and demonstrated promising trends in improvement of PROs, warranting future research to evaluate effectiveness and sustainability.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatic diseases (RD) are chronic, autoimmune, and inflammatory and lead to varying degrees of physical disability. It is well established that patients with RD often experience chronic pain, depression, and anxiety, which contribute to increased RD symptom burden and decreased quality of life (QoL). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Medical treatment alone is not sufficient to address these comorbid conditions. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Optimal management of patients with RD must include addressing biopsychosocial contributors to patients’ symptoms, including health behaviors and mental health (MH). 1 , 5 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Behavioral health (BH) interventions can address a variety of issues in the medical setting, including MH conditions and symptoms, health behaviors (which can both contribute to and help improve chronic medical conditions), life stressors, and stress‐related physical symptoms. 18 BH interventions are of particular importance in relation to chronic illness and pain because the ongoing nature of these conditions increases the likelihood of MH sequelae. 19 Effective, evidence‐based psychological interventions for managing mood and stress and anxiety symptoms not only help to alleviate MH symptoms but can also impact physical health (eg, experience of pain, immune function), as well as one's ability to engage in positive health behaviors. 20 , 21 , 22 Likewise, addressing physical activity, along with other interventions targeting health behaviors, can alleviate symptoms associated with RD, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 as well as depressed mood and anxiety symptoms. 28

As a precursor to this pilot, a qualitative study among academic rheumatologists was conducted to elicit perspectives on integrating a BH specialist into rheumatology clinics. 29 Results from these semistructured interviews indicated that rheumatologists address health behaviors (eg, exercise, weight management, smoking, sleep) and MH issues (eg, mood, anxiety) to varying extents. They believed that BH is important in the management of RD and comorbid pain, as well as for patients’ overall well‐being. The rheumatologists expressed interest in having a BH specialist available through their clinics to help motivate patients to initiate or continue with prescribed behavior change and address MH issues. Often, rheumatologists lacked the time and/or expertise to address these issues in busy clinics. Perceived benefits to having an integrated BH specialist included (1) providing services to patients who may have subclinical MH issues impacting their health that do not warrant referral to specialty programs, (2) making BH services easily accessible to patients who decline referral to specialty programs due to stigma or inconvenience, and (3) avoiding long wait times for services from other departments (eg, pain management, psychology).

Despite ample evidence of the multidirectional relationship between outcomes in RD, health behaviors, and MH, 30 , 31 efforts to integrate BH services into rheumatology clinics have been limited. 9 One study integrated BH into a rheumatology clinic and focused on MH concerns in a pediatric population. 32 To fill this gap, we developed and piloted a single‐arm BH program integrated in rheumatology clinics at an academic medical center to evaluate feasibility and impact on patient‐reported outcomes (PRO).

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Participants

Consecutive patients with RD, both new and follow‐up, were recruited from a large academic medical center clinic between July 2023 and May 2024 (27 clinic days). Patients were recruited in clinic by the BH specialist, in most cases immediately following their rheumatology appointment. Eligibility included (1) adult patients of a participating rheumatologist, (2) English speaking, and (3) reliable access to a telephone. Patients were excluded if (1) they were not interested in learning more about the BH program or working on one of three BH goals with the BH specialist, (2) they had hearing issues that would interfere with participating in telephone sessions, or (3) the rheumatologist did not feel they were suitable for referral due to high disease activity. A study information sheet and goals wheel (GW) were distributed to patients at check‐in by the medical assistant who roomed the patient. This study was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Institutional Review Board (STU2022‐0678).

Goals elicitation tool: GW

We modified a goal elicitation tool known as the Arthritis Conversation Tool 33 with permission from the author (JB). For the purposes of this pilot study, we refer to the modified tool as the “Goals Wheel” (Figure 1). This tool was developed as a shared decision‐making tool to assist patients in communicating their immediate concerns or goals with their medical team at the point of care. The Arthritis Conversation Tool was originally intended to be completed by the patient before a clinic visit and then shared with the clinician during the visit. Because this was a pilot study and not routine clinical care, we adapted the GW for use as a survey and screening tool to determine patient interest in specific BH goals and eligibility for this pilot. When adapting the original tool, we retained the existing seven goals, expanding on three (Do Daily Activities, Improve My Mood, and Have Less Pain) to formulate our target goals for BH intervention, and added an additional option to write in an unlisted goal. Patients were asked to select, from the eight options, their three most important goals.

Goals Wheel.

BH services were offered for three of the eight goals from the GW, 33 based on interview data from the qualitative study, literature review, 2 , 13 , 25 and the research team's clinical expertise: (1) Engage in Physical Activity and Daily Activities, (2) Improve My Mood and Manage Stress and Anxiety, and (3) Learn Skills to Help Manage My Pain. Patients who selected at least one of the three target goals were eligible to participate in the pilot study. We retained goals on the GW that were not addressed with the pilot BH intervention because we wanted to determine which BH goals patients would most frequently select (for future study iterations).

Rheumatologists

Two rheumatologists (PB and EBS) volunteered to participate and refer patients for this pilot study. Their role in the study involved determining patient interest in, and appropriateness for, referral to the BH pilot program. During the clinical visit, the rheumatologists explained and introduced the pilot study, briefly reviewed the GW as time allowed, and referred to the BH specialist when appropriate.

BH specialist

The BH specialist (JLM) is a licensed clinical psychologist with a focus on health psychology and behavioral medicine. In collaboration with clinic staff, the BH specialist was present in clinic to recruit eligible patients who were referred by their rheumatologists. The BH specialist reviewed the study information sheet with patients and more thoroughly reviewed the GW. For all eligible participants who verbally consented to participation, the BH specialist confirmed the specific goal the patient wanted to target during the study.

BH intervention components

This nonmanualized intervention was designed to reflect integrated BH in real‐world settings. The BH specialist tailored the intervention to each patient and focused on eliciting patient buy‐in by aligning treatment plans with participant‐defined goals and readiness to change. The BH specialist explicitly stated that although they were the expert in BH, the patient was the expert on themselves. They were informed that they would be presented with different “tools for their toolbox,” which the patient could take or leave depending on what they found meaningful and workable.

All intervention sessions occurred via telephone with the BH specialist. Patients were informed that they could participate in up to eight sessions and, with the BH specialist, determine the appropriate number as the intervention progressed. This flexible approach is consistent with integrated BH services in clinical settings such as primary care, in which the number and length of sessions are individualized to patient needs. 34 , 35 , 36 The initial session was approximately 50 minutes and included baseline PRO measures (Supplementary Table 1) followed by a brief focused interview in which the BH specialist clarified the participant's overall BH goal, RD and brief MH history, current symptoms, and current and past RD and MH management strategies. This session usually involved psychoeducation and, as with subsequent intervention sessions, a “behavioral health prescription,” which is a collaboratively developed action plan intended to bridge the gap between clinical sessions and daily environment. This iterative process ensured that skills practice remained workable and responsive to patient feedback, reinforcing the principle that sustainable behavior change occurs through consistent application outside of the therapeutic setting.

Each subsequent BH session (except for the final session) had a usual duration of 15 to 30 minutes and began with administration of two brief PRO measures assessing depression and anxiety and pain symptoms (Supplementary Table 1), along with a review of the prior session's BH prescription. Progress on the BH prescription was assessed with questions such as, “How often did you practice x since the last session?” “How much effort did you put into practicing x since the last session?” “What do you think some of the barriers were to practice?” The BH specialist and participant discussed what the participant found helpful and problem‐solved around identified barriers. This feedback informed the content of each session. The BH specialist employed a combination of evidence‐based techniques, including but not limited to psychoeducation, mindfulness‐based techniques, and motivational interviewing, tailored to the individual patient and their collaboratively identified BH goal. The BH specialist taught various skills and techniques to help the participant move toward their overall BH goal, and participants decided which skills to integrate into daily routine. The BH specialist would review progress on the BH prescription (skills practice), elicit patient feedback on what did and did not work, and adjust accordingly.

The final BH session included repeating the baseline PROs and reviewing the BH prescription from the prior session, along with progress made toward the participant's identified BH goal. Additionally, the BH specialist reviewed skills taught across the course of the intervention, answered patient questions, and assigned a long‐term BH prescription based on the patient's feedback.

Approximately three months after the final BH session, participants had a follow‐up telephone meeting with the BH specialist who administered the full battery of PROs and inquired about the patient's progress with their BH goal. The BH specialist answered questions, reviewed prior skills, and provided support as necessary.

Outcomes and data collection

The primary purpose of this integrated BH pilot program was to evaluate feasibility. The secondary purpose was to evaluate early trends in PROs in the study population. Data sources included the electronic health record (EHR) and patient surveys.

Feasibility outcomes

Feasibility data included evaluation of the process and ability to recruit patients with RD into a BH program integrated into rheumatology clinics, including the number of patients approached, referred, and enrolled (Supplementary Material, Supplementary Figure 1). To understand characteristics of patients referred to and enrolled in the BH program, demographic data, substance use history, rheumatologic and MH diagnoses, body mass index, and additional health data were obtained from the EHR and used to calculate the Charlson Comorbidity Index. 37 We also collected data on engagement and retention, including the number of visits per patient and reasons for drop‐out (Supplementary Figure 1). As part of the feasibility, we wanted to gain insight into the intervention components and collected data on the BH goals selected and specific techniques used in BH sessions. Approximately three weeks following the final BH (end‐assessment) session, a research team member (other than the BH specialist) assessed participant satisfaction with the integrated BH program using Likert scale questions that the team developed iteratively. We assessed the sustainability of behavior change at three months with the following yes and no questions: “Are you still using the skills you learned from the Behavioral Health Provider?” and “Do you plan on using those skills in the future?”

PROs

All PRO data were collected from the participants verbally via telephone by the BH specialist and entered into a secure REDCap database. PRO domains associated with worsened outcomes among individuals with RD 38 , 39 were measured at baseline, at the scheduled final BH intervention visit (end assessment), and at approximately three months following the end assessment to evaluate sustained change. A subset of PROs (pain interference and mood and anxiety) was assessed at the beginning of each BH session to collect interim data and help guide the discussion between patient and BH specialist (Supplementary Table 1).

Pain interference was measured using the Pain, Enjoyment of Life, and General Activity scale (PEG‐3). 40 , 41 The PEG‐3 inquires about symptoms in the “past week” and consists of three items answered on a continuous scale ranging from 0 (absence of symptom) to 10, with minimally clinically important difference (MCID) of 1 point. Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐4 (PHQ‐4). This inventory consists of four questions (two depression‐related and two anxiety‐related) answered on a 4‐point Likert scale indicating frequency of symptoms and ranging from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”). Items are summed for a total score, with higher scores indicating higher symptom severity. 42 Pain‐related thoughts were assessed with the Pain Catastrophizing Scale, a 13‐item instrument using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Not at all”) to 4 (“All the time”), with a total possible score of 52. Scores >30 indicate clinically relevant levels of catastrophizing. 43 QoL was assessed with one item pulled from the PRO Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Global Mental Health 4a measured on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Poor”) to 5 (“Excellent”). We chose this single question for QoL because the other three questions from the PROMIS Global Mental Health 4a were redundant with other measures administered (described in the following paragraph). 44

PROMIS 4‐item Short Forms measured fatigue (PROMIS Short Form Fatigue 4a), sleep (PROMIS Sleep Disturbance Short Form 4a), and physical function (PROMIS Short Form Physical Function 4a). 45 To measure social satisfaction, we used four items selected from the PROMIS Satisfaction with Social Roles and Activities Item Bank version 2. Each of the PROMIS domains was measured on a five‐point Likert scale with varying directionality. All PROMIS instruments are designed to produce a score in which higher values indicate a greater presence of the construct being measured. PROMIS instrument results are reported as T‐scores, with a mean of 50 and a SD of 10. Higher T‐scores indicate more severe symptoms, and a T‐score change >3 points represents a MCID.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize feasibility outcomes, participant demographics, RDs and MH diagnoses, goals selected, number of BH sessions completed, and session‐level data such as baseline scores and therapeutic techniques used. For continuous variables, means with SDs or medians with interquartile ranges were reported, depending on the distribution of the data. Comparisons of continuous variables between groups (baseline only and end assessment) were conducted using Satterthwaite‐adjusted t‐tests to account for unequal variances. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐square tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate.

Primary analyses of PRO data evaluated change from baseline to end assessment across key outcomes, including PEG‐3, PHQ‐4, Pain Catastrophizing, QoL, and PROMIS domains (eg, Fatigue, Sleep, Social Satisfaction, and Physical Function). Depending on the distribution of each variable, a paired‐sample Student's t‐test or sign‐rank test was used to assess within‐subject changes. For PROMIS instruments, T‐scores (standardized to a mean of 50 and SD of 10) were reported to facilitate interpretation with population norms.

We conducted several secondary analyses to examine possible differences between the group of 28 completers (ie, those who completed the BH intervention sessions and a formal end assessment) versus the 11 participants who had at least one BH intervention session beyond baseline but did not complete an end assessment (and therefore did not receive the complete repeated battery of PRO instruments). We compared baseline characteristics (demographics, rheumatologic and MH conditions, and number of participants who chose each goal) using t‐tests (with or without Satterthwaite adjustment) for continuous variables and chi‐square tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate, for categorical variables. We compared the number of sessions completed by each group using an independent samples t‐test. Finally, PEG‐3 and PHQ‐4 outcomes at baseline and the last administration of each instrument during a BH session, regardless of the number of sessions, were analyzed. A two‐way mixed models analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a compound symmetry covariate structure compared changes in PEG‐3 and PHQ‐4 scores from baseline to final assessment as a within‐subjects factor of time, with treatment completion status (completer vs noncompleters) as a between‐subjects factor with subjects as a random effect. Least‐squares means and 95% confidence intervals were computed for each condition.

Responses to the sustainability questions were summarized with frequency counts. We present box and whisker plots to view trends in outcome variables at different time points (baseline, end assessment, and three‐month follow‐up). Participant satisfaction was summarized with frequency counts.

Missing data were addressed by including only participants with complete preintervention and postintervention survey data. No imputation methods were used. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). A two‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Feasibility and recruitment

Of the 187 patients who received the GW, 76 returned a completed GW, 45 enrolled and completed a baseline assessment, and 28 completed all program components (Table 1; Supplementary Figure 1; Table 2). The 187 who received study information and a GW were representative of the rheumatologic population in this academic setting, with a median age of 57 years; predominantly women (75%), White (60.4%), and Hispanic (11.8%); and presenting with the most common diagnoses (not mutually exclusive) of osteoarthritis (25%), rheumatoid arthritis (20%), systemic lupus erythematosus (7%), fibromyalgia (6.6%), and Sjögren disease (5.7%).

Completers: Characteristics and intervention components

Twenty‐eight participants completed the BH program, as defined by participating in an end assessment session regardless of the total number of sessions completed. Demographics for this group are listed in Table 1. Participants (n = 28) completed a mean of 6.86 (SD 1.56) sessions. Of the participants who compelted the program, 54% selected the goal “improve my mood/ manage stress & anxiety,” 29% selected “learn skills to help manage my pain,” and 17% selected “engage in physical activity/daily activities.” The most frequently used interventions (nonexclusive) across all BH goals and sessions were psychoeducation (25%), motivational interviewing techniques (20%), and mindfulness‐based techniques (18%). These interventions were often supplemented with suggestions for mindfulness meditation apps and websites (15%).

Primary analysis of PROs

In the primary PRO analysis of completers (Table 2), there was statistically significant improvement in mean change scores for PEG‐3, PHQ‐4, Pain Catastrophizing, and QoL, along with fatigue and sleep (P < 0.05) between end and baseline assessments. Improvement was not observed in social satisfaction or physical function (P > 0.05).

Secondary analysis (completers vs noncompleters)

When comparing baseline characteristics between groups (ie, those who completed a formal end assessment [n = 28] and those who completed at least one follow‐up BH session beyond the baseline but did not complete a formal end assessment [n = 11]), there were no statistically significant differences at baseline (ie, age, sex, race, presence of MH diagnoses, primary BH goal, or Charlston Comorbidity Index score). A statistically significant difference in total number of BH sessions between the groups was noted (P < 0.0001), with completers (n = 28) receiving a mean of 6.86 (SD 1.56) sessions and noncompleters (n = 11) a mean of 3.18 (SD 1.33) sessions.

The two‐way mixed model ANOVA for the PEG‐3 revealed a significant main effect for time, (F1,38 = 4.26; P = 0.0464), reflecting a reduction in mean pain interference from baseline to end assessment across participants. There was no significant main effect for completion status, (F1,37 = 0.01; P = 0.9317), and the time × completion status interaction was also nonsignificant, (F1,37 = 1.18; P = 0.2839). These results suggest that participants experienced improvements in pain interference over time, independent of treatment completion.

Likewise, results of the two‐way mixed model ANOVA for the PHQ‐4 revealed a significant main effect for time (F1,38 = 6.27; P = 0.017), indicating that the mean psychological distress significantly decreased from baseline to end assessment across participants. The main effect for completion status was not significant (F1,37 = 0.17; P = 0.679), nor was the time × completion status interaction (F1,37 = 1.74; P = 0.195). These results suggest that psychological distress improved over time regardless of whether participants completed the intervention or not.

Sustainability

Of the 28 participants who completed a final assessment, 22 completed the three‐month follow‐up assessment. All 22 responded “yes” to the three‐month assessment sustainability questions: “Are you still using the skills you learned from the Behavioral Health Provider?” and “Do you plan on using [skills] in the future?” Sustainability of the outcome measures can be viewed with the box and whisker plots (see Figures 2 and 3). Over the course of the study, all PROs showed either continued trends toward improvement or maintained improvement at the three‐month follow‐up.

Satisfaction

Of the six satisfaction survey questions (Supplementary Table 2) administered along with the three‐week postend interview, nearly every participant (n = 27) provided positive feedback on five of six questions. Only one participant provided a negative response, “No, I don't think so,” to only one question, “Did the behavioral health services make you feel more active/involved in managing your health?”

DISCUSSION

This novel, single‐arm pilot study is one of the first integrated BH programs in an adult rheumatology clinic. Findings suggest that BH integration is feasible within a busy rheumatology clinic and associated with modest short‐term improvements in key PRO domains, including pain intensity, pain catastrophizing, anxiety and depression symptoms, QoL, fatigue, and sleep. The premise of this pilot program aligns with existing literature emphasizing the importance of systematic integration of BH into medical care to optimize patient outcomes. 46 , 47 , 48 Given the lack of research on integrating BH into adult rheumatology clinics, this pilot program drew primarily from the primary care psychology literature, in which the majority of integrated BH research has been conducted. Previous research in primary care 49 and other specialty settings 50 underscores the importance of having BH services present in clinic and easily accessible to encourage collaboration and decrease barriers to use. The iterative design of our pilot program and early implementation evaluation allowed for ongoing refinement of clinical workflows, BH provider caseloads, and sustainability strategies. These iterative improvements contributed to the feasibility and acceptability of the program and provided valuable insights for future implementation efforts.

Our findings build on prior work in RD‐specific behavioral interventions, such as a pediatric myositis program, 32 which demonstrated the efficacy of targeted psychological support. However, our program adopted an inclusive approach, enrolling a broad adult population with RD and addressing a range of BH concerns (ie, pain, physical activity) beyond MH comorbidities, similar to what would be encountered in primary care settings. Participants chose a personally meaningful goal across three primary domains: physical activity, mood and stress, and pain management, all shown to be highly relevant among patients with RD. 2 , 13 , 25 In this pilot program, only 4 of 76 participants who completed a GW did not select at least one of the three BH goals (from eight options), reinforcing the relevance of our chosen areas of focus. With real‐world BH integration, it is expected that additional BH goals would be addressed, based on patient need and interest.

One distinguishing feature of this pilot program was the flexible engagement model, which allowed patients to attend a variable number of usually brief (15–30 minutes) BH sessions based on their preferences and the clinical judgment of the BH provider. This approach is reflective of real‐world settings in primary care and across a variety of specialty clinics where integrated BH encounters are intentionally brief but, nonetheless, clinically impactful. 36 Despite statistically significant differences between the number of sessions attended by “completers” and “noncompleters,” outcome improvements in pain interference (PEG‐3) and psychological distress (PHQ‐4) were observed regardless of completion status, suggesting that brief, targeted interventions may be sufficient for some individuals. Furthermore, the BH specialist employed a variety of person‐centered strategies tailored to individual needs and goals, further enhancing the feasibility and personalized nature of the intervention. The BH specialist drew heavily on psychoeducation and techniques that increased intrinsic motivation and imparted specific skill sets to help patients move toward their identified goals. Together, these approaches gave patients an active role and sense of agency in managing comorbid symptoms and their RD. Although the pragmatic approach to the pilot intervention limits uniformity in treatment exposure, it also aligns with the flexible patient‐centered and intentionally brief nature of BH care integrated into medical settings.

Several limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. As a pilot study, the sample size was modest, and the single‐arm design precludes causal inference. We did not recruit participants based on underlying rheumatologic diagnosis or assumptions of desired target behavioral goals. Because participants were also not recruited based on a threshold for symptom severity, we found relatively low baseline scores for pain and psychological distress that potentially limit the magnitude of observed change. Although we found a tendency toward improved PROs, the absence of a standardized treatment protocol or dose effect introduces heterogeneity in intervention exposure. This should be weighed carefully and balanced appropriately for real‐world implementation. We also acknowledge a limitation of lack of blinding for outcome assessments during the BH sessions; this was balanced with the desire to use these data in real time to guide the BH session. The brief physical function and social satisfaction PROs did not show improvements. This was not unexpected for physical function because we would expect functional changes to take longer to observe. We were somewhat surprised that social satisfaction did not improve. Upon further evaluation of the items included in this measure, two of the four were work focused, which was not relevant to all participants. Focusing more on social roles with family and friends or leisure activities may have been more likely to show improvement with BH techniques.

Additionally, the follow‐up period was limited to three months, restricting our ability to assess longer‐term effects. From an implementation and sustainability standpoint, we recognize that integrated interventions such as this one ultimately depend on (1) identification and availability of BH specialists skilled in the delivery of brief BH interventions and (2) system‐level support and billing capabilities to support integrated BH services. Relatedly, although our intervention was developed and conducted by a psychologist, we acknowledge that BH specialists from other disciplines who are trained in MH care are valuable resources and could be integrated into rheumatologic care.

The study offers several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive evaluation of a pilot program that integrates BH services in rheumatology across a wide range of RDs. The BH program was approached with a spirit of collaboration (between BH specialist and participant) from beginning to end while also functioning within the context of a research protocol. Our program expanded upon existing literature by enrolling a heterogeneous, adult patient population with RD and addressing multiple behavioral domains relevant to people living with RD. The flexibility of the intervention and its integration into standard clinical workflows increase its potential for scalability and sustainability. High satisfaction scores and improvements in PROs across multiple domains suggest that short‐term, goal‐focused, and tailored BH interventions may hold clinical value for patients with RD. This study highlights the importance of stakeholder engagement, iterative process and protocol development, and attention to practical implementation factors when designing integrated care models. Our preliminary results support the integration of BH services in rheumatology care and lay the foundation for a future randomized controlled trial. Future trials should explore the effectiveness of integrated BH services in rheumatology, with attention to long‐term outcomes, stakeholder satisfaction, implementation barriers and facilitators, and payment structures that will facilitate sustainability.
